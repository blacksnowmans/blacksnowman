Packing for the Impossible: What It Actually Takes to Survive a Winter Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

The romance of the Camino de Santiago draws hundreds of thousands of travelers to northern Spain every year, promising quiet reflection, ancient stone paths, and a simplified existence measured only in footsteps. But scrolling through travel blogs under the warm glow of a screen is a far cry from standing on a frost-covered trail in the middle of January. When the temperature drops and biting winds sweep down from the Pyrenees, the romantic fantasy of the medieval pilgrimage gives way to a much harsher reality. For anyone foolish—or brave—enough to attempt the winter route, a poorly packed bag is not merely an inconvenience; it is a fast track to misery, hypothermia, or worse.

To understand what it genuinely takes to endure hundreds of kilometers of freezing European winter with a lightweight setup, we can look at the meticulous preparation of a traveler standing just 163 centimeters (about 5 feet 4 inches) tall, captured on video the night before flying from Paris to Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, the traditional French starting point of the trek. Her approach offers a masterclass in pragmatic survival: stripping away every ounce of vanity and excess weight while fiercely protecting the vulnerable points of the human body against cold, dampness, and fatigue.

The Anatomy of a Minimalist Pack: Osprey, Decathlon, and Layers

The foundation of any long-distance trek is the vessel carrying your life on your back. For this winter journey, the choice of pack is the Osprey Kyte 46L in a deep purple shade that shifts toward navy depending on the light. At 46 liters, it is a modest volume for a winter excursion, forcing a strict adherence to minimalism. The guiding philosophy is simple: you do not survive a winter Camino by piling on three heavy winter coats. Instead, you rely on a technical layering system.

The clothing roster is ruthlessly streamlined. The base layer consists of a simple black turtleneck paired with fleece-lined leggings. On mild days, a windbreaker is enough. If the chill sets in, a short-sleeve shirt is added over the turtleneck. When a bitter freeze grips the trail, a lightweight down jacket—specifically a Montbell piece—goes on underneath the windbreaker. That is the entire wardrobe: a black turtleneck, a short-sleeve shirt, fleece-lined leggings, a lightweight down jacket, and a windbreaker, supplemented by a Nike cap, a Jordan headband to manage sweat, and a face mask worn at night to keep unfamiliar hostel pillows clean.

When it comes to gear, budget-friendly practicality trumps brand obsession. Purchased at a Decathlon store in Paris, an Arpenaz 40L Poncho in black, sized XL to fit individuals up to 190 cm, was chosen deliberately oversized. Even for a 163 cm traveler, the extra fabric prevents rain from splashing up onto the legs—a common cause of rapid core temperature drops in northern Spain's notoriously volatile weather. Weighing just 340 grams with a 2,000mm waterproof rating, it acts as a primary shield against the elements. Similarly, a pair of Decathlon Forclaz T100 trekking poles, picked up for a meager €6 each (about $6.50), embodies a liberating philosophy for long-distance walkers: if they get too heavy, you simply throw them away.

Protecting the Engine: Blisters, Joints, and Hostel Hygiene

On the Camino, your feet are your engine, and the moment a blister forms, a walking meditation turns into a forced march through hell. To prevent this, the packing list includes two pairs of toe socks and two pairs of knee-high socks, rotating to keep moisture at bay. Joint protection is handled by a BodySafe ankle brace (costing about $9) and a spare knee brace. Rather than traditional hiking boots with laces that can snap on the trail, the choice is made to trust a dial-based Boa lacing system, praised by retail staff for its reliability and ankle support.

The medical and hygiene kit borders on an emergency room supply cache: petroleum jelly, Nexcare hydrocolloid blister dressings, alcohol swabs, Fucidin ointment, and povidone-iodine. In lieu of heavy gaiters to keep mud out of boots, simple plastic bags and rubber bands serve as an ingenious, weightless alternative. After a grueling day of walking, the only sanctuary is the shower. Having learned the hard way on a past trip to the UK—where she tried to save weight by using a thin neck towel that refused to dry and made hair-washing a nightmare—she packed a proper sports microfiber towel alongside a Lush shampoo bar and Super Milk hair primer.

Because severe dry skin is aggravated by freezing winds, anti-aging ampoules, facial creams, multi-purpose balm, and hand cream made with camellia oil are non-negotiable additions. To survive the notoriously noisy multi-bed dormitories known as *albergues*, high-grade earplugs are treated as absolute top-tier priorities. Toss in a bottle of aroma oil for psychological comfort and a specialized bedbug spray, and the kit covers both physical survival and mental preservation.

Fueling the Soul and Securing the Digital Lifeline

One of the most underestimated obstacles of a multi-week European journey is culinary fatigue. When local cuisine stops tasting comforting, morale plummets. The solution? Five to six squeeze tubes of Korean red pepper paste (*gochujang*) and packets of spicy instant broth base. Stirred into hot water at a freezing mountain hostel, these portable flavors act as a potent psychological antidote to homesickness and physical exhaustion. Tucked into a hip pocket alongside a single tube of lipstick—offering a touch of normalcy and self-confidence—these small items maintain a sense of personal identity on the road.

Administrative and digital preparedness is equally tight. A secure money belt worn against the body safeguards passports and cards, with paper currency divided into separate envelopes across a 21-day itinerary to match local cash-handling realities. Connectivity is secured via a 20GB tourist SIM card, backed by a 10,000mAh power bank and a multi-port USB adapter to prevent the dreaded dead-battery panic. Add four laundry clips, a portable clothesline, a roll of paper detergent, and a multi-mode headlamp, and the 46-liter bag achieves zero functional gaps.

Yet, no amount of gear can erase the genuine dangers of a winter crossing. The French route over the Pyrenees regularly exposes hikers to sudden blizzards, sub-zero temperatures, and severe hypothermia. Tragically, every year hikers succumb to acute medical emergencies like heart attacks or strokes along isolated stretches of the trail. The ultimate winter shield is not a better jacket or a lighter pole; it is the mental flexibility to halt an itinerary, wait out a storm, or take public transit when nature demands it.

What the Winter Trail Teaches Us About Letting Go

Watching someone systematically pare down their life into a 46-liter backpack reveals a profound philosophical truth: the liberation of realizing that everything does not have to be under control. Modern life trains us to over-prepare for every hypothetical variable, stuffing our schedules, minds, and homes with safety nets that ultimately weigh us down. Walking over 800 kilometers forces a brutal audit of what actually matters.

You quickly learn that unnecessary worries are the heaviest baggage of all. By letting go of the obsession with absolute perfection—accepting a worn-out sleeping bag, planning to discard cheap trekking poles if they become a burden, and facing the trail's unpredictability with a squeeze of spicy pepper paste—the journey transforms. It stops being an exercise in accumulation and becomes an exercise in subtraction.

When the traveler finally touches down in Paris and steps out into the crisp mountain air of Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, her pack will not just be a container for gear. It will be a physical manifestation of a quiet, stubborn decision: to trust oneself, to travel light, and to walk forward into the cold, one deliberate step at a time.
