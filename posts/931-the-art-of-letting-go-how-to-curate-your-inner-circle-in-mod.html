The Art of Letting Go: How to Curate Your Inner Circle in Modern Korea

October 10, 2026

We have all been there: sitting across from someone who is smiling, nodding, and offering kind words, yet feeling an inexplicable, nagging sense of dissonance. Is this person’s kindness genuine, or is it a mask? As we navigate the complexities of life in South Korea—a society often defined by rigid social hierarchies and the exhaustion of maintaining extensive professional networks—this question becomes increasingly urgent. As we age, the task of maintaining relationships seems to grow harder, not easier. We find ourselves yearning to prune the "social fat" and hold onto only those who are truly our people.

Rhyu Si-min, a prominent South Korean author and public intellectual, recently addressed this phenomenon on the YouTube series Intellectual Invitation. His insights offer a profound look at why our social circles inevitably shrink, and how we can shield ourselves from the pervasive culture of performative kindness and digital toxicity that defines the current era.

The Litmus Test for True Character

When we seek to understand who a person really is, we often turn to the most mundane of activities: sharing a meal or a drink. Rhyu argues that this intuition is spot on. While words are easily manipulated, a person’s behavior in everyday settings is the most honest reflection of their character.

This is no small matter for the modern Korean professional. According to recent workplace surveys, as performance pressure mounts, the stress stemming from internal interpersonal relationships accounts for a significant portion of employee burnout—often jumping from 18% to nearly 28% as organizational performance declines. When an individual’s professional life is already under immense strain, toxic personal connections become an unbearable burden.

This is why the "waiter test" is so critical. If someone is unfailingly kind to you but dismissive or rude to service staff, your internal alarm bells should be ringing. Rhyu emphasizes that the way a person treats someone from whom they have nothing to gain is the ultimate indicator of their true self. Those who speak down to shop clerks or waiters are often transactional, viewing every relationship as a vehicle for their own benefit. Once you see this pattern, continuing to invest time and emotional labor into that relationship is not just exhausting—it is unnecessary.

Self-Respect and the "Inferiority Trap"

At the root of such mistreatment of others, Rhyu identifies a lack of self-esteem and a warped sense of inferiority. He puts it succinctly: "Only those who respect themselves are capable of respecting others." Individuals with high self-worth do not need to belittle those around them to feel whole. Conversely, those trapped by deep-seated insecurities often look down on others to puff themselves up.

We do not have the luxury of spending our lives trying to "fix" or reform these individuals. Life is far too short to spend it with people who do not treat us as peers. While business settings may occasionally require us to wear a "social mask," we should never allow our private lives to be consumed by such emotional labor. If a relationship requires constant management or feels like an endless drain, the healthiest choice is to follow the principles of Dale Carnegie’s How to Win Friends and Influence People: if the person cannot even be bothered to show basic gratitude or sincerity, it is time to create distance.

Mirroring the Age of Hatred

The internet, for all its promise, often feels like a sprawling landscape of rage and division. In South Korea’s highly digitized society, social media platforms and news portals are frequently flooded with vitriolic comments, and even public figures often find themselves at the mercy of anonymous hate campaigns.

Rhyu’s perspective on this is firm: "Malicious comments are a mirror reflecting the character of the person writing them; they have nothing to do with the person being targeted." Despite this truth, many victims find themselves trapped in a cycle of self-censorship, wondering if the hatred directed at them has some kernel of validity. The speed at which rumors and insults spread in the mobile era amplifies this pain, turning what would have once been private whispers into public spectacles.

The solution is not to engage, but to detach. Searching for one’s own mention in a sea of hate is a form of self-destruction. While hate and prejudice are constants in human history, their power is only as great as the attention we grant them. The only effective counter-strategy is to continue building a life defined by accurate information and positive, meaningful connections. In a world where echo chambers and confirmation bias are rampant, refusing to play the game of outrage is a radical act of self-preservation.

Designing a Life of Purpose

Now in the later stages of his life, Rhyu describes his daily existence as being built around two pillars: reading and eating. The physical act of turning a page, the smell of ink, and the texture of paper are experiences that digital devices simply cannot replicate. His philosophy is simple yet unshakable: you are what you consume, both in terms of the food that nourishes your body and the texts that feed your mind.

His goal for the years ahead is not grand or ambitious. It is to remain mobile, independent, and autonomous for as long as possible. True freedom, he suggests, is the ability to walk on one's own two feet and fill one's day with choices that reflect one's own values rather than the expectations of others.

Ultimately, the "self" is not a treasure waiting to be discovered in some remote corner of the world; it is something we forge through the daily struggle of living well. If you feel embarrassed by who you were in the past, that is not a cause for despair—it is the clearest evidence of your growth. You are able to see your past limitations only because your perspective has widened.

In a world of noise and judgment, the path forward is straightforward: stop scavenging for the scraps of others' opinions. Instead, sit down for a warm meal with a friend who accepts you exactly as you are, and open a good book. It is perhaps the most elegant way to navigate the turbulence of modern life.
