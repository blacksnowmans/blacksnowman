The Ghost of Yalta in Shenzhen: Why a US-China-Russia Summit Leaves South Korea on Edge

November 16, 2026

Diplomatic channels across Asia and Europe went into overdrive on November 15 when Moscow officially confirmed what had previously sounded like geopolitical fiction: US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin will sit down together at a single table in Shenzhen on November 19. The unprecedented trilateral summit, arranged on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) forum opening in China’s tech capital on November 18, marks the first time in history that the leaders of the world's three primary superpowers will meet in a dedicated three-way format.

For observers outside Asia, the summit might look like a dramatic attempt to tackle global instability head-on. But in foreign ministries from Kyiv to Taipei—and especially inside the presidential Blue House in Seoul—the news has triggered deep unease. Analysts and media across East Asia are already invoking the "Ghost of Yalta," referencing the 1945 conference where the Allied powers carved up post-WWII spheres of influence behind closed doors, often at the expense of smaller nations. For South Korea, navigating this sudden realignment among giants represents the most critical foreign policy test yet for President Lee Jae-myung and his administration.

An Unprecedented Gathering in Shenzhen

A summit of this configuration has no modern precedent. While multilateral gatherings like the G20 or the United Nations General Assembly frequently bring world leaders into the same building, creating a isolated, trilateral negotiating table exclusively for Washington, Beijing, and Moscow breaks entirely new diplomatic ground. Moscow’s official confirmation on November 15 sealed weeks of intense, covert preparations between foreign ministry protocol teams, who have been scrambling to settle everything from seating arrangements to agenda control in China's southern tech hub.

Interestingly, potential economic stumbling blocks appear to have been intentionally cleared to make room for sweeping security discussions. Reports surfaced on the morning of November 15 that President Trump’s delegation had decided against raising sensitive semiconductor trade disputes, including ongoing controversies surrounding Nvidia and its CEO Jensen Huang, during the meeting with President Xi. By side-stepping micro-level tech trade friction and corporate controversies, the US administration signalled a preference for high-stakes geopolitical topics: nuclear arms limitations, ending the war in Ukraine, and setting global security boundaries.

This deliberate clearing of the agenda underscores the sheer weight of what is being attempted. With individual corporate issues off the table, the Shenzhen summit is primed to focus almost exclusively on grand strategy and global balance of power negotiations.

Three Leaders, Three Divergent Calculations

Despite the grand imagery of three strongmen coming together, the meeting is defined by conflicting motivations—a classic case of sharing the same bed while dreaming three completely different dreams.

President Donald Trump approaches the summit seeking a headline-grabbing diplomatic triumph: bringing China into a comprehensive nuclear arms control architecture. Historically, bilateral treaties like New START governed nuclear parity strictly between Washington and Moscow. However, China’s rapid expansion and modernization of its nuclear arsenal have rendered bilateral frameworks obsolete in Washington’s eyes. Trump aims to leverage this three-way encounter to pressure Beijing into accepting strategic constraints, framing it as a vital victory for global safety.

President Xi Jinping finds himself in a far more delicate position despite hosting the event on home soil. China’s nuclear warhead stockpile remains significantly smaller than those of the US or Russia. Entering formal arms reduction negotiations at this stage threatens to freeze China’s strategic deterrent at a permanent disadvantage. Beijing fears being trapped in a framework engineered by Washington, while simultaneously worrying that visible alignment with Russia could intensify Western trade containment against China's economy. Consequently, Chinese strategists harbor deep skepticism that the meeting will yield substantive benefits, fearing an outcome of high diplomatic pressure with little strategic return.

President Vladimir Putin views the summit as a major victory before it even begins. Facing years of Western economic sanctions and diplomatic isolation following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, standing shoulder-to-shoulder with the American and Chinese leaders restores Russia’s status as an indispensable global superpower. Putin intends to use the platform to push for an end to the war in Ukraine on terms favorable to Moscow—securing territorial gains and sanction relief—while dangling potential cooperation on nuclear issues as leverage against Trump.

The Strategic Mismatch in Shenzhen: Trump wants to constrain China’s nuclear growth; Xi wants to avoid being boxed into an asymmetric arms control deal; Putin wants an exit from diplomatic isolation and a favorable resolution in Ukraine. With goals so fundamentally opposed, substantive consensus will be remarkably difficult to achieve.

The Ghost of Yalta and Peripheral Anxiety

The prospect of three superpowers privately negotiating global security has revived fears of great-power bargaining at the expense of middle and smaller powers. The reference to the 1945 Yalta Conference—where Franklin D. Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Joseph Stalin mapped out post-war Europe—is not merely academic rhetoric; it reflects immediate panic in vulnerable capitals.

In Ukraine, officials worry that Washington and Moscow might strike a deal over territorial boundaries and security guarantees without Kyiv's consent. In Taiwan, security analysts fear that American commitments could become bargaining chips in exchange for Chinese concessions on nuclear limits or economic trade. When international order pivots toward stark realism, smaller nations historically pay the toll.

Beyond territorial concerns, the Shenzhen summit is slated to address modern security frontiers, including the militarization of artificial intelligence and automated command-and-control systems. As AI technology increasingly integrates into defense infrastructure, the risk of accidental escalation or automated miscalculation has alarmed military strategists in all three nations. Establishing baseline rules for AI in nuclear command systems is a rare area of shared concern, though creating enforceable verification mechanisms between deeply distrustful powers remains a formidable challenge.

What Is at Stake for South Korea and Seoul’s Foreign Policy Test

For South Korea, the Shenzhen gathering carries direct and immediate implications for national security. The primary anxiety in Seoul centers around the concept of "Korea Passing"—a term widely used in South Korean political discourse to describe scenarios where major powers negotiate the fate of the Korean Peninsula without South Korean participation or input.

With North Korea aggressively cementing its military alliance with Russia—supplying munitions and troops in exchange for technological assistance—the balance of power in East Asia has grown increasingly fragile. If the trilateral summit touches on Northeast Asian security, there is a serious risk that the three powers might settle for a "nuclear freeze" or status-quo management regarding Pyongyang, rather than pursuing complete denuclearization. Such an outcome would implicitly cement North Korea’s status as a nuclear-armed state, presenting a permanent security nightmare for Seoul.

This dynamic presents a pivotal challenge for President Lee Jae-myung and his administration. President Lee, representing South Korea's liberal Democratic Party of Korea, has championed a doctrine of pragmatic diplomacy aimed at maintaining strategic autonomy. Rather than aligning strictly with a single bloc, Lee’s foreign policy framework seeks to preserve strong security ties with Washington while keeping economic and diplomatic channels open with Beijing and Moscow.

At the APEC summit, President Lee faces a delicate balancing act:

  • Preventing Isolation: Ensuring South Korea's voice is heard on North Korean denuclearization and preventing any great-power compromises that undermine Seoul's security.
  • Economic Safeguards: Mitigating supply chain vulnerabilities amidst ongoing US-China tech competition, particularly as South Korea's semiconductor giants navigate global trade restrictions.
  • Diplomatic Flexibility: Engaging constructively with China as the host nation while reassuring the US of South Korea's commitment to the bilateral alliance.

If great-power bilateralism replaces multilateral norms in Shenzhen, South Korea's leverage could diminish rapidly. Seoul cannot afford to be a passive bystander while decisions affecting the Korean Peninsula are discussed behind closed doors.

What to Watch Next

As the November 19 summit approaches, several key indicators will reveal whether this meeting marks a real shift in global politics or merely high-stakes diplomatic theater:

  • The Final Communiqué: Pay close attention to whether a joint statement is issued. If the three leaders fail to produce a signed document, it will confirm that divergent interests prevented even basic consensus.
  • Language on North Korea: Watch closely for any references to the Korean Peninsula. Any shift from traditional "complete denuclearization" phrasing toward "risk reduction" or "stability" would signal a worrying shift toward accepting Pyongyang's nuclear status.
  • Ukraine and Taiwan References: Note whether Ukraine or Taiwan are explicitly mentioned or noticeably absent, which will indicate whether side-deals were struck or tabled entirely.
  • President Lee Jae-myung’s Side Meetings: Track South Korea’s bilateral engagements on the sidelines of APEC, particularly potential one-on-one sessions between President Lee and President Xi, to gauge Seoul's success in preserving its diplomatic maneuverability.
