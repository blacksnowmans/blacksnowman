Beyond Mainstream Polling: Why the Korean Left is Rallying Behind "Fighter" Politicians

October 13, 2026

Imagine a late-night scene in a bustling Seoul apartment. The glow of a smartphone illuminates the face of a dedicated supporter of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s current ruling party. They aren’t watching a traditional news broadcast or reading a mainstream editorial. Instead, they are glued to a YouTube live stream, one of several high-traffic political channels that have become the digital town squares of South Korean politics. As the host launches a real-time poll, thousands of votes pour in within seconds. The names at the top of the list aren’t necessarily the ones the mainstream media discusses, but they are the ones who make the hearts of the party’s base beat faster.

This is the world of "hybrid politics" in South Korea—a space where official statistics from established polling agencies meet the raw, unvarnished passion of digital communities. While mainstream polls often focus on "electability" or broad public appeal, these YouTube-driven surveys reveal a different kind of data: the desire for "fighters." In a political landscape still reeling from the turbulent years of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration, which ended in his impeachment and current imprisonment, the liberal base is not looking for mediators. They are looking for the vanguard of reform.

The YouTube Phenomenon and the Rise of the Reformist Vanguard

In South Korea, political YouTube channels like Park Si-young TV—hosted by a prominent political strategist—have evolved into powerful influencers that can rival major TV networks in terms of engagement. For outside observers, it might be easy to dismiss a YouTube poll as a mere "echo chamber" effect. However, for the Democratic Party of Korea, these channels represent the "active base"—the people who volunteer, donate, and show up at the ballot box. To ignore them is to ignore the engine of the party.

During a recent broadcast that spanned over 90 minutes, the conversation turned to the future: Who will lead the next generation of the liberal movement? The names that surfaced repeatedly were Choe Kang-wook, Choo Mi-ae, Jung Chung-rae, and Cho Kuk. These are not just politicians; in the eyes of their supporters, they are symbols of resistance against what many describe as the "judicial violence" of the previous era.

The host of the show noted a conspicuous absence in mainstream presidential preference polls: Choe Kang-wook, a former lawmaker known for his uncompromising stance against prosecutorial overreach. To test the waters, the channel launched an impromptu live poll. The result was a jolt to the system for anyone used to the sanitized rankings of traditional media. Choe Kang-wook didn't just appear on the list; he dominated it.

"In a world where politicians often try to play it safe, the voters are gravitating toward those with the sharpest edges and the most combat power."

This sentiment captures the current mood of the DPK membership. They are tired of "middle-of-the-road" politics. After witnessing the collapse of the previous administration under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the liberal base believes that only "선명성" (seon-myeong-seong)—a term meaning "clarity" or "boldness of conviction"—can prevent a return to the past.

The Numbers Behind the Passion: A Real-Time Snapshot

The results of the live poll were telling. In the first round of voting, Choe Kang-wook secured a commanding 33% of the vote. He was followed by Choo Mi-ae, the former Justice Minister who famously clashed with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during his time as Prosecutor General, at 27%. Jung Chung-rae, a senior DPK lawmaker known for his fiery rhetoric, took 21%, while Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party (a staunchly liberal splinter party), received 19%.

As the broadcast continued, the numbers shifted slightly but the hierarchy remained firm. A later tally showed Choe at 30%, Choo at 28%, Jung at 22%, and Cho at 21%. While the host joked that such lopsided results might make the channel’s viewers look "unreliable" to outsiders, the underlying message was serious. These figures represent the "hard core" of the reformist movement—voters who value a politician's history of struggle over their polished public image.

The significance of Choe Kang-wook’s 1st place finish cannot be overstated. Despite not holding a seat in the current National Assembly, his popularity remains sky-high among the base. Supporters remember him as one of the few who stood on the front lines when the prosecution service—which many liberals view as an unaccountable "deep state" in Korea—was at its most aggressive during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. For these voters, Choe is a "방골" (bang-gol)—a traditional term for someone with a "rebellious bone" who refuses to bow to unjust authority.

  • Choe Kang-wook (33%): Viewed as the ultimate fighter against prosecutorial overreach.
  • Choo Mi-ae (27%): Respected for her early warnings and direct confrontation with the previous administration.
  • Jung Chung-rae (21%): A veteran communicator who keeps the party’s message sharp and aggressive.
  • Cho Kuk (19%): A symbolic figure of the "judicial reform" movement whose personal tribulations have turned him into a martyr-like figure for the left.

The Disconnect Between the Establishment and the Grassroots

Why does the mainstream media often exclude figures like Choe Kang-wook from their polls? The answer lies in the friction between "institutional politics" and "movement politics." The establishment—including major newspapers and even the inner circles of the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition—often views these figures as "polarizing" or "too radical."

However, the live poll participants argue that this exclusion is a form of erasure. They believe the establishment is trying to "manage" public opinion by narrowing the choices to those who are more palatable to the corporate and judicial elites. When the guests on Park Si-young TV questioned why Choe is left out of official candidate lists, they were tapping into a deep-seated frustration with a political system that they feel is perpetually trying to "tone down" the will of the people.

This tension is particularly relevant now, even with President Lee Jae-myung in the Blue House. While the Lee Jae-myung government has begun the work of dismantling the "prosecutorial republic" left behind by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the base feels the job is far from over. They see a judiciary and a media landscape that are still largely hostile to the liberal agenda. In this environment, "moderation" is seen not as a virtue, but as a weakness that allows the opposition to regroup.

The high polling numbers for Choo Mi-ae and Cho Kuk further reinforce this. Both served as Justice Ministers and both were instrumental in the attempt to reform the Supreme Prosecutors' Office. Their enduring popularity suggests that for a large segment of the Korean electorate, the "reform of the powerful agencies" remains the single most important issue on the national agenda.

What the "Hammer of Reform" Means for President Lee’s Future

For President Lee Jae-myung, these poll results are both a blessing and a challenge. On one hand, they show that his party’s base is highly energized and ready to support a bold reform agenda. On the other hand, the demand for "warriors" puts pressure on the administration to maintain a high-conflict posture with the opposition and the remaining remnants of the old guard.

The voters are effectively asking for a "hammer." They want politicians who aren't afraid to get their hands dirty in the legislative and judicial trenches. This explains why Jung Chung-rae, who often uses blunt and uncompromising language in the National Assembly, remains a perennial favorite. He speaks the language of the base, unfiltered by the diplomatic niceties of Yeouido (Korea's equivalent of Capitol Hill).

The "human connection" mentioned by the host of the YouTube show is also vital. These voters don't just support these politicians for their policies; they support them because they feel a sense of "debt" to them. They watched these individuals endure what they perceive as "judicial massacres" and "character assassinations" during the years Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol held power. This emotional bond creates a "hardened" support base that does not easily fluctuate based on daily news cycles.

The "Fighter" Candidates: A Quick Guide

Choe Kang-wook: Former lawyer and lawmaker. He was a key architect of the push to strip the prosecution of its investigative powers.

Choo Mi-ae: Former DPK chair and Justice Minister. She is often credited with being the first to identify the dangers of a "prosecutor-led state."

Jung Chung-rae: A high-ranking member of the DPK’s Supreme Council. He is known for his ability to mobilize the party's grassroots through social media.

Cho Kuk: Former Justice Minister and law professor. His Rebuilding Korea Party serves as a "flank" to the DPK, pushing for even more radical reforms.

The Road Ahead: Watching the "Real" Public Opinion

As South Korea moves further into the Lee Jae-myung era, the influence of these "YouTube-certified" leaders will be a key metric to watch. While they may not always be the darlings of the mainstream press, their ability to move the needle within the Democratic Party of Korea is undeniable. They are the ones who determine the party's internal temperature and the direction of its legislative priorities.

The next few months will be crucial. We should watch whether these "fighter" archetypes are given formal roles in upcoming party leadership elections or if they continue to operate as influential outsiders. Their performance in future polls—both the "official" ones and the "raw" YouTube ones—will indicate whether the Korean public is ready for a period of political stability or if the hunger for "uncompromising reform" will continue to drive the national narrative.

In the end, the numbers on a YouTube live screen are more than just digital noise. They are a signal from a segment of the population that feels it has finally found its voice. In the high-stakes game of Korean politics, where the transition from "Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s era" to the "Lee Jae-myung era" was paved with unprecedented legal and social conflict, the rise of the "fighter" politician is not just a trend—it is a survival strategy for the Korean left.

What to watch next:

  • Internal DPK Dynamics: Will President Lee Jae-myung bring these "vanguard" figures closer into the center of government to satisfy the base?
  • Mainstream Poll Inclusion: Watch for whether polling agencies begin to include Choe Kang-wook and others in their "Next President" surveys due to public pressure.
  • Legislative Reform: The "warrior" politicians will likely be the ones leading the charge on the next phase of judicial and media reform in the National Assembly.
