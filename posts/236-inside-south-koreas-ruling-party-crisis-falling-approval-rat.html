Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Crisis: Falling Approval Ratings and Internal Strife

September 07, 2026

For anyone watching South Korean politics closely, the current mood in the capital feels increasingly surreal. The approval rating of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is hovering uncomfortably in the mid-30% range—a concerning figure for a government that came to power with high ambitions for reform. Yet the most alarming warning sign is not coming from conservative strongholds, but from the party's own heartland. Recent polling data reveals that the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party, has suffered a sharp double-digit drop in its support base across the southwestern Jeolla region, including Gwangju and South Jeolla Province—traditional powerbeds of the progressive movement. For a party that relies on these voters as its political bedrock, this sudden erosion of trust has prompted urgent questions about what has gone wrong.

The Roots of Disillusionment: When the Party Turns Inward

The precipitous drop in poll numbers cannot be easily dismissed as mere political noise or blamed entirely on external factors. Instead, it points to a widening chasm between the party leadership and the grassroots supporters who built it. For months, rank-and-file members have watched with growing unease as internal accountability has withered. Lawmakers who should be offering constructive criticism have largely muted themselves, choosing to align with factional loyalties rather than speaking hard truths to power. Meanwhile, official party communication channels, such as online media platforms operated under the party's banner, have frequently leaned into divisive rhetoric, alienating the very people they are meant to unite.

This dynamic has left core supporters feeling abandoned. When traditional voters begin quietly unsubscribing from party channels and withholding their public backing, it serves as a powerful indicator that the official narrative no longer resonates. The danger for the ruling party is that these mounting fractures are no longer peripheral; they are actively consuming the political capital necessary to govern effectively.

Kim Min-seok and the Dilemma of "Expansion" Versus "Cohesion"

Much of the current internal friction has crystallized around senior figures like Representative Kim Min-seok, whose political maneuvers have sparked fierce debate within progressive circles. Kim has championed an "expansionist" strategy aimed at broadening the party's appeal toward moderate and conservative voters, even establishing a grand-integration initiative to bridge traditional political divides. However, this outreach has come at a steep cost to internal harmony.

On critical legislative fronts—most notably real estate policy, a perennial lightning rod in South Korean politics—Kim’s positions have frequently clashed with core reform agendas, leading critics to question whether his strategy serves the public interest or merely his own political positioning. Seasoned observers point out a paradox: attempting to court outside voters while failing to maintain basic solidarity within one's own ranks is political building on sand. Without internal cohesion, efforts to broaden a coalition risk fracturing the foundational support that keeps the party viable in the first place.

Compounding these strategic disputes are more serious legal and procedural concerns. Allegations involving party registry management and personal data compliance have pushed certain internal controversies out of the realm of ordinary political disagreement and into potential legal exposure. When a party founded on democratic principles faces accusations of compromising data protection and fair internal competition, it strikes directly at its core identity.

Procedural Fairness and the Erosion of Democratic Norms

The malaise currently affecting the Democratic Party extends far beyond personality clashes; it touches upon the fundamental question of internal democracy. Recent controversies surrounding voting procedures at national party conventions have drawn sharp criticism from members who argue that critical rules were altered or implemented without sufficient transparency. For a party that frequently positions itself as the guardian of procedural justice on the national stage, any perception of unfairness in its own internal elections carries a heavy political price.

Discontent of this scale rarely stays contained. As local elections approach, these simmering factional tensions risk ossifying into permanent divisions, transforming strategic disagreements into fundamental questions about the party's identity. Critics within the progressive camp argue that regaining the trust of voters—and pushing approval ratings back toward the 45–50% threshold—will require more than clever public relations. It will require restoring a culture where dissenting voices are welcomed rather than penalized, and where internal procedures are above reproach.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's political landscape prepares for upcoming electoral cycles, several key developments will indicate whether the ruling party can course-correct:

    How the party leadership handles internal investigations and demands for transparency regarding convention procedures and data-handling controversies. Whether senior figures like Representative Kim Min-seok adjust their legislative strategies to reconcile moderate outreach with core reform commitments. Upcoming regional polling trends in the Jeolla provinces to see if the recent double-digit drop in support represents a temporary protest or a lasting shift in voter loyalty. Changes in tone and strategy among official party media channels and communication arms, which will signal whether the leadership is serious about curbing internal division.

Ultimately, the current dip in approval ratings serves as more than just a momentary fluctuation in poll numbers. For a party built on principles of reform and representation, it stands as a stark reminder that public trust, once fractured by internal discord, is exceedingly difficult to rebuild.
