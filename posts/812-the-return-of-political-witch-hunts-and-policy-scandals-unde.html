The Return of Political Witch Hunts and Policy Scandals Under South Korea’s Progressive Government

October 07, 2026

To watch South Korean politics today is to experience a dizzying sense of deja vu. For years, the progressive camp—now in power under President Lee Jae-myung—heavily criticized the rough-edged, vindictive style of political investigations that targeted family members to silence opponents. Yet, as the Lee administration settles into power, critics argue that the exact same tactics are being recycled, casting a harsh light on how quickly absolute power can corrupt its holder's ideals.

The latest flashpoint centers on a civic group known as Judicial Justice Citizens' Action (Sasehaeng), which recently filed a criminal complaint against lawyer Lee Jong-keun. While the legal charges—ranging from abuse of power to violations of the Attorney Act—sound technical, the political subtext is glaringly obvious to anyone following Seoul's power corridors. Lee Jong-keun is the husband of Park Eun-jung, a lawmaker with the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's main liberal party, who has increasingly broken ranks to criticize the administration's personnel and governance choices. By targeting a politician's spouse to muzzle her dissent, critics say the administration is reviving a playbook of intimidation that many hoped had been left behind.

When Progressive Power Resembles the Old Regime

The decision by Sasehaeng’s standing representative, Kim Han-me, to file complaints against lawmaker Park’s husband has raised immediate questions about timing and motive. Why target the spouse of a dissenting ruling-party lawmaker rather than someone else? To many observers, the move is a thinly veiled attempt to tame Park, echoing the aggressive tactics once deployed against former Justice Minister Cho Kuk and his family.

Politics is supposed to be a contest of policies and philosophies. Instead, what is unfolding resembles a calculated effort to bludgeon internal critics into silence. Lawmaker Park has found herself in the crosshairs not because of a sudden awakening of civic virtue by a watchdog group, but because the new inner circle surrounding President Lee—often dubbed the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction—demands absolute ideological conformity. When a progressive administration adopts the very same heavy-handed, family-targeting methods it once decried under conservative rule, it forfeits its moral authority.

The irony is bitter. Under previous liberal administrations led by figures like Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in, utilizing the justice system to excise political opponents within one's own broad coalition was rarely, if ever, so brazenly attempted. By weaponizing investigators and allied civic organizations, the current government risks becoming indistinguishable from the conservative administrations it replaced.

The 54 Trillion Won Leverage ETF Disaster

Beyond political infighting, the administration is facing severe criticism over major economic policy failures. Chief among them is the botched rollout of a leverage exchange-traded fund (ETF) policy that wiped out about 54 trillion won (roughly $39 billion) in market value. The initiative, pushed through hastily as a populist measure ahead of local elections, was implemented without even a single meeting minute being recorded. When questioned, key government bodies hid behind the shield of "confidentiality."

The public fallout has been immense. Everyday retail investors watched their savings evaporate, yet accountability has been virtually nonexistent. Key policy architects have vanished from public view or been quietly reassigned to positions shielded from parliamentary audits. Calls for a special independent counsel investigation are mounting, as the public grows increasingly furious over an administration that preaches transparency while stonewalling inquiries into one of the largest financial policy botches in recent memory.

When the government treats tens of trillions of won in public wealth as disposable political capital and then scrambles to cover its tracks, it crosses the line from mere incompetence to institutional recklessness.

Lavish Meals and the Moral Decay of the Judiciary

The rot, critics argue, extends far beyond the executive branch and into the judiciary. Recent disclosures regarding Chief Justice Cho Hee-de have sparked widespread public outrage. According to records made public by opposition lawmakers, the head of South Korea's highest court utilized official expense accounts to fund high-end omakase dinners and expensive course meals, alongside purchasing 108 million won (about $78,000) worth of wine—including 26 bottles bought over a single two-day period.

The judiciary’s unconvincing defense that the purchases were for "official conference rooms" insults the intelligence of a citizenry currently enduring severe economic hardship. While ordinary people struggle to pay bills and navigate a cooling economy, the top figure in the judicial branch appears to be living in a world of casual luxury funded by taxpayers.

When the judiciary is plagued by such glaring moral laxity, public trust in the rule of law inevitably collapses. The media, too, stands accused of dereliction of duty—either fawning over favored political figures or looking the other way while public funds are squandered at the top.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward upcoming political battles and legislative showdowns, several critical indicators will determine the trajectory of the Lee administration:

  • The Momentum for Special Counsels: Watch whether public pressure forces the ruling party to accept an independent investigation into the 54 trillion won leverage ETF disaster, or if the administration continues to successfully block subpoenas and shield key aides.
  • Internal DPK Fractures: Pay attention to how the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction handles internal dissent. Further attempts to sideline figures aligned with traditional liberal values could trigger a formal fracture within the ruling party ahead of legislative elections.
  • Approval Ratings and Rhetoric: Monitor whether the president's sliding approval numbers prompt a genuine shift in governing style, or if the administration doubles down on aggressive messaging and internal purges.
  • Judicial Accountability: Observe if parliamentary committees follow up on the Chief Justice's expense controversies with binding oversight, or if the allegations are quietly swept under the rug.

South Korea’s democratic institutions were hard-won through decades of civic sacrifice. As the current administration tests the limits of executive power, financial transparency, and political tolerance, the ultimate question is whether the public and independent watchdogs can hold the powerful accountable before the erosion of democratic norms becomes irreversible.
