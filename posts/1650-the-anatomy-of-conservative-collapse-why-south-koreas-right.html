The Anatomy of Conservative Collapse: Why South Korea’s Right Keeps Imploding

October 29, 2026

For South Korea’s conservative movement, history has not merely rhymed; it has repeated itself with devastating precision. Less than a decade after former President Park Geun-hye was stripped of power and removed from office following massive public demonstrations, the conservative establishment found itself trapped in the exact same nightmare with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Once again, an administration that arrived on a wave of ardent conservative expectations spiraled into constitutional catastrophe, culminating in impeachment, removal, and imprisonment. For conservative voters who believed their movement had learned its lessons from 2017, the second collapse was not just a political defeat—it was an existential shock.

Yet in the halls of the People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative party, now relegated to the opposition benches, the prevailing mood often appears oddly detached from reality. Whenever approval ratings for the current ruling Democratic Party or President Lee Jae-myung show signs of strain, voices within the conservative camp prematurely declare a comeback. They treat their survival as an inevitability, assuming that the public’s frustration with the government in power will automatically deliver them back into office. To understand why this assumption is dangerously flawed, one has to look past short-term polling and examine the structural pathology that brought South Korean conservatism to ruin—a dynamic memorably described decades ago by the British historian Arnold Toynbee.

Two Impeachments, One Lingering Trauma

South Korea’s modern political history offers few parallels to the double trauma suffered by the conservative establishment. When former President Park Geun-hye took office, she carried the immense symbolic weight of her lineage as the daughter of Park Chung-hee, the authoritarian leader who oversaw South Korea’s rapid postwar industrialization. Many of her supporters assumed that growing up inside the presidential Blue House gave her a natural grasp of statecraft. When her administration crumbled under the weight of influence-peddling scandals and procedural rot, the betrayal felt by the conservative base was profound.

Even so, the party attempted damage control during Park’s impeachment. It rebranded, sought to compartmentalize the fallout, and eventually clawed its way back into the presidential office behind Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. But the crisis that erupted during Mr. Yoon’s tenure was of an entirely different order of magnitude. Rather than an administration slowly unraveling over cronyism, the country was plunged into an unprecedented insurrection crisis that shook the nation's foundational constitutional order. The wreckage left behind was far more severe than the fallout from 2017; it inflicted a near-fatal blow to the conservative party's claim to be a steward of the republic.

Despite this ruinous record, the People Power Party has repeatedly relied on superficial stopgaps rather than deep, painful soul-searching. Instead of confronting how the party allowed a second constitutional rupture to occur on its watch, its leadership has focused on dodging accountability. Now sitting as the primary opposition to President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, the party frequently confuses the normal friction of governance—temporary drops in presidential numbers or routine political fatigue—with an endorsement of its own conduct. History does not forgive constitutional breaches so easily, and a temporary dip in an opponent's standing is a mirage, not a mandate.

Toynbee’s Mirror: How Creative Minorities Decay

To diagnose why South Korea’s conservative leaders repeatedly stumble at the very same threshold, Arnold Toynbee’s study of civilizational rise and fall offers an illuminating lens. Toynbee argued that societies progress under the leadership of what he termed a "creative minority"—a dynamic group that devises novel, effective responses to the urgent challenges of their era. Because their vision commands genuine respect, the broader public willingly follows their lead.

The tragedy, Toynbee observed, begins once that minority takes power and solidifies into an entrenched elite. Confronted with a new generation of problems, they fail to innovate. Instead, they fall prey to what Toynbee called the "idolization of the ephemeral self"—the hubristic belief that because their methods succeeded in the past, those same methods must remain permanently valid. When conditions evolve, the ruling group refuses to adapt, blindly clinging to outdated formulas that no longer fit the world around them.

This dynamic captures the core failure of South Korea’s recent conservative rule. The conservative establishment built its historical legitimacy on the rapid economic mobilization and strong-arm discipline of the late twentieth century. But the challenges of modern South Korea demand open communication, institutional consensus, procedural legitimacy, and protection for the vulnerable. Rather than rising to these contemporary demands, conservative governance under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol reverted to old authoritarian muscle memory. When faced with political deadlock or social pushback, the administration reached for coercion rather than dialogue, trying to impose its will through raw institutional power. It treated twenty-first-century citizens as passive subjects of rule, mistaking retrograde force for political strength.

The Echo Chamber and the Loss of Public Consent

When a creative minority stops creating, Toynbee noted, it degenerates into a "dominant minority"—a faction that rules not through moral authority or shared vision, but through sheer coercion and the defense of its own privileges. Neurologists and psychologists often describe a similar pattern as the trap of being a victim of one's own success: power feeds a desire for even greater power, breeding an acute intolerance for dissent.

During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, this isolation became acute. The mechanisms of internal feedback broke down completely. Critical advice was tuned out, dissenting perspectives were purged, and leadership surrounded itself with sycophants who echoed only what the leader wished to hear. Legitimate critics were treated as political enemies, and attempts were made to muzzle unfavorable press coverage. Deprived of the critical braking mechanisms that keep executive authority grounded, the administration accelerated into self-inflicted isolation.

Power only retains its vitality when it is shared, mediated, and disciplined by institutional humility; when it is hoarded and privatized as an end in itself, it inevitably constructs its own collapse.

The predictable consequence of becoming a dominant minority is the wholesale desertion of the public. Citizens will follow a leadership that offers genuine answers to historical crises, but when rulers turn power into a personal fortress to protect their privileges, the public’s patience snaps. The mass mobilizations that brought down both conservative administrations were not historical accidents; they were the constitutional immune system of a sovereign citizenry rejecting rulers who attempted to govern through force rather than consent. Once the electorate turns its back on an elite that has broken faith with democratic norms, superficial apologies cannot restore that lost legitimacy.

The Mirage of Rebound and What to Watch Next

Today, the People Power Party faces a reckoning it continues to postpone. Banking on public fatigue with the ruling Democratic Party is an intellectual and strategic dead end. When a political force has presided over two separate impeachment crises—one of which involved a treasonous disruption of the constitutional order—it cannot rebuild public trust simply by waiting for the incumbent government to make mistakes. A party cannot cure internal decay by changing its banner or reshuffling its public relations messaging; it must excise the roots that generated the rot.

As South Korea’s political map settles around President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and an embattled conservative opposition, the road ahead will be defined by whether the right can genuinely break with its past. In the months and years ahead, close observers should track several key indicators:

  • Accountability for the Insurrection Crisis: Watch whether the People Power Party formally and unambiguously breaks with the figures and factions directly implicated in the constitutional disruption during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, or whether it continues to offer them tacit protection.
  • The Battle Over Political Grammar: Observe whether conservative leaders continue to rely on the rhetoric of authoritarian coercion and polarization, or if a new generation of reformers emerges capable of articulating a modern, democratic conservatism that respects procedural norms.
  • The Depth of Internal Reform: Monitor whether the opposition engages in structural institutional reform, or merely pivots between cosmetic rebrandings designed to capture temporary polling bumps.

Toynbee’s warning was never confined to ancient empires. It remains a living principle of political survival. A movement that remains captive to the idols of its past, mistaking brute dominance for leadership, cannot escape the judgment of history. Until South Korea’s conservative establishment acknowledges why its methods failed, its path back to credible governance will remain firmly blocked.
