The Democratic Party’s Crisis: Internal Rebellion and the Quest for Reform

October 28, 2026

For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the liberal party currently holding the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung—the hallways of the National Assembly have become a pressure cooker. As public opinion polls show a slide in support, the party’s internal cohesion is fraying. Veteran lawmakers, concerned that the current leadership is steering the party toward an electoral catastrophe, have begun to openly discuss a move that is usually reserved for the gravest of political emergencies: the formation of an Emergency Response Committee (ERC).

To an outside observer, this might look like routine political maneuvering. But in the context of South Korean politics, the call for an ERC is a signal that the party’s senior figures have lost faith in the current leadership’s ability to govern or manage the national agenda. This is not just a disagreement over policy; it is a fundamental challenge to the party’s direction at a time when the government’s momentum is beginning to stall.

Cracks in the Democratic Party’s Foundation

The recent plenary meetings of the DPK have been marked by unusually sharp criticism from senior members. At the heart of this unrest is a lack of confidence in the current leadership, specifically surrounding Kim Min-seok. Critics argue that the leadership has failed to address the deepening divide within the party, a divide that is increasingly visible to the public.

Lawmaker Ahn Gyu-baek voiced the concerns of many when he remarked that the sight of internal infighting leaking into the public sphere is "truly regrettable." Similarly, Cho Seung-rae issued a stern warning: "If the leadership wavers, the party members lose trust, and ultimately, the party will collapse." For these senior figures, this is a matter of institutional survival. The party is finding that when the leadership appears disconnected or weak, it loses the ability to set the national agenda, effectively paralyzing the administration’s ability to enact its platform.

The call for an Emergency Response Committee is essentially a "break-glass-in-case-of-emergency" measure. Traditionally, an ERC is a temporary body tasked with steering a party through a transition or a major crisis. Today, however, it is being positioned as a final card—a way to force a top-to-bottom renewal. The central question remains: is a change in structure enough, or is the party failing to provide the substantive, livelihood-focused governance that voters are demanding?

The Rhee Si-woo Case: Defamation and Legal Pragmatism

Parallel to the turmoil in the DPK, the legal landscape surrounding prominent political commentator Rhee Si-woo has shifted. Rhee, who had been facing defamation charges, recently saw his case dismissed by the police after the prosecution failed to proceed. The legal reasoning is straightforward yet politically significant: the charges were dropped because the "victim"—President Lee Jae-myung—formally requested that no punishment be leveled against Rhee.

Under South Korean law, defamation is a "crime against the victim's will," meaning that if the victim explicitly states they do not wish for the accused to be punished, the case cannot move forward. While some critics speculated that this was a political decision, it is, in fact, a correct application of the law. By choosing not to pursue the defamation claim, the President has effectively neutralized a source of friction, allowing the administration to move away from petty legal battles and focus on broader political agendas.

Rhee has used this outcome to signal a proactive stance, vowing to "fact-check" the various allegations that have surrounded him, particularly regarding the Shilla Jen scandal. By clearing these hurdles, Rhee aims to reclaim his influence as a key voice within the liberal bloc, positioning himself to provide what many call "critical support" for the Lee Jae-myung administration.

Kim Keon-hee and the Limits of Judicial Patience

While the DPK navigates internal friction and legal clean-ups, the judiciary continues to keep a spotlight on the past administration. The recent extension of a detention warrant for Kim Keon-hee—the spouse of the incarcerated former leader Yoon Seok-youl—is a significant event in South Korean judicial history.

Kim, who is embroiled in a "buy-and-sell office" scandal (the illegal trading of political positions), had previously received a five-year prison sentence in a lower court. The decision to keep her detained rather than releasing her on bail signals that the judiciary is unlikely to show leniency. Many analysts believe this is a clear indication that a final guilty verdict is imminent. Had the court not issued this additional warrant, Kim would have likely been released on October 31. By keeping her in custody, the court has signaled to the public that despite concerns regarding the current judicial system’s pace—often criticized as being too cautious or overly sensitive to political pressure—it remains committed to holding key figures from the previous administration accountable.

Looking Ahead: The Path to Integration

For the Democratic Party, the way forward is clear but difficult. The infighting between various factions must stop if the party is to regain the trust of the public. Supporters of President Lee Jae-myung are waiting to see if the party can move beyond the "pro-Seok" vs. "anti-Seok" labels and focus on the practical realities of daily life—inflation, housing, and job security.

What to watch next:

  • The Fate of the ERC: Whether the DPK actually forms an Emergency Response Committee or opts for a less drastic cabinet reshuffle will be the first test of whether the leadership is willing to listen to its veteran members.
  • The "New Lee" Strategy: Watch how the administration and its media allies, including voices like Rhee Si-woo, align to support the President’s agenda after the recent legal clearances.
  • Judicial Developments: The continued detention of Kim Keon-hee will remain a barometer for public trust in the judiciary. Any sign of a premature release would likely trigger massive public backlash and further weaken the party’s standing.

The Democratic Party stands at a crossroads. It can either continue its internal squabbling, risking its status as the ruling power, or it can undergo a painful but necessary renewal. As the party looks toward the future, the message from its own ranks is consistent: govern for the people, or expect the voters to turn their backs.
