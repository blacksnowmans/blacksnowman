Descending into the Deep: Why the World’s Elite Divers are Racing to South Korea

November 21, 2026

Imagine the light fading from a bright turquoise to a bruised purple, and then finally to an absolute, crushing ink. Your heart rate slows to a rhythmic thud—perhaps 30 beats per minute—as your body enters the "mammalian dive reflex." Your lungs, which were full of air at the surface, are now compressed by the weight of the ocean to the size of small oranges. At 124 meters (407 feet) below the surface, the pressure is more than 13 times that of the atmosphere at sea level. There is no tank, no lifeline, and no room for a single panicked thought. There is only the line, the silence, and the singular goal of returning to the surface on a single breath.

This was the reality for Nataliia Zharkova, an elite Ukrainian diver who recently shattered the world record in the Constant Weight (CWT) discipline. Her descent into the abyss has captured the imagination of the global diving community, but it is particularly resonant in South Korea. While once a niche hobby for the adventurous, freediving has transformed into a cultural phenomenon in the peninsula. From the halls of international climate conferences to the pixelated oceans of upcoming video games, the "silent sport" is currently experiencing a massive surge in influence, and South Korea is sitting at the very center of this deep-blue expansion.

Resilience in the Abyss: The Ukrainian Record and the Human Limit

The headline of the week in the marine leisure world is undoubtedly Nataliia Zharkova’s 124-meter world record. Competing in an official AIDA (International Association for the Development of Apnea) event, Zharkova utilized a monofin—a single, large fin that makes the diver look like a dolphin—to reach a depth that was once thought physiologically impossible for the human body.

To understand the gravity of 124 meters, one must understand the risks involved. Divers at this level must contend with "lung squeeze," where the extreme pressure can cause capillaries in the lungs to leak fluid or blood if the diver is not sufficiently relaxed. Then there is nitrogen narcosis, often called "rapture of the deep," which can cause a sense of euphoria or disorientation similar to alcohol intoxication. Reaching this depth requires more than just physical strength; it requires a level of psychological mastery that allows the diver to remain calm while their body is being physically crushed by the water column.

The achievement is even more poignant given the background of the athletes. Both Zharkova and her compatriot, Kateryna Sadurska—who also set a world record this week—hail from Ukraine. The international diving community has noted the incredible mental fortitude required to train at a world-class level while their home nation remains embroiled in conflict. This sense of resilience was echoed by Croatian diver Vitomir Maričić, who noted in a recent interview that "fear is not something to be thrown away, but something to be converted into willpower." For these athletes, the silence of the deep is not an escape, but a forge for the human spirit.

While the ocean depth records grab the headlines, the sport is also flourishing in controlled environments. In Turin, Italy, the Italian Indoor Championships saw a flurry of national records, particularly from the Sardinia team, which took home nine medals. These indoor events, which focus on "Dynamic Apnea" (swimming as far as possible horizontally on one breath), provide a laboratory for the technical strokes and efficiency that eventually allow divers to go deeper in the open ocean. The synergy between indoor precision and outdoor endurance is what is currently pushing the limits of the sport further than ever before.

The Korean Market: A New Mecca for Tropical Tourism

If the world records represent the "peak" of the sport, South Korea represents its most explosive growth engine. This was clearly visible at the PADI Freediving & Mermaid Festival 2026, a major industry event held in Korea. One of the most prominent participants was the Philippine Department of Tourism, which has identified the Korean market as its most vital demographic for marine leisure.

Why is South Korea so obsessed with diving? To a foreign observer, it might seem counterintuitive. The waters surrounding the Korean peninsula, particularly the East Sea, are often cold, dark, and subject to harsh seasonal changes. However, this is exactly what drives the market. Korean divers are famously enthusiastic and well-equipped, but they are constantly seeking "blue water"—the warm, clear conditions found in Southeast Asia. This has turned destinations like Cebu, Bohol, and Boracay into "Korean diving colonies," where resorts are specifically tailored to the needs of Korean trainees and recreational enthusiasts.

Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, there has been a concerted effort to strengthen "soft power" ties with Southeast Asian nations. This geopolitical alignment mirrors the economic reality: as disposable income in Korea remains high and the desire for "experiential" travel grows, the marine leisure industry has become a major bridge between Seoul and Manila. The Philippine government isn't just selling a vacation; they are selling the infrastructure for Korean divers to achieve the certifications they can't easily get in the chilly waters of the North Pacific.

The Rise of the "Mermaid" Economy

Within this market growth, a fascinating sub-genre has emerged: Mermaid Diving. While serious "depth" freedivers might focus on records and physiology, Mermaid Diving focuses on aesthetics, grace, and performance. Using specialized "mermaid tails" that function as monofins, participants perform underwater dances and poses.

In South Korea, this has become a massive social media trend. Driven by platforms like Instagram and TikTok, the visual appeal of "underwater artistry" has attracted a younger, predominantly female demographic that might have been intimidated by the hardcore image of traditional freediving. For the Korean leisure industry, this is a goldmine. It requires specialized equipment, pool rentals, and photography services, turning what was once a hobby into a full-fledged creative industry. The Philippine Department of Tourism notably highlighted its "Mermaid-friendly" resorts at the PADI festival, recognizing that for many modern travelers, the quality of the underwater photo is just as important as the depth of the dive.

Global Frontiers: From Kyrgyz Lakes to Ethical Crossroads

The expansion of freediving is not limited to tropical oceans. In a surprising move, the government of Kyrgyzstan has announced plans to build a professional freediving center on the shores of Lake Issyk-Kul. Located at an altitude of 1,600 meters (5,249 feet), Issyk-Kul is the second-largest alpine lake in the world.

This project highlights the growing interest in "Altitude Diving." Because the atmospheric pressure is lower at high altitudes, the physiological demands on the diver’s body—particularly regarding decompression and equalization—are much more complex. A training center here would serve as a high-altitude base for European and Asian divers looking to sharpen their skills in a unique environment. It signals that freediving is moving away from being a "vacation activity" and toward being a sophisticated, year-round athletic discipline.

However, as the sport grows, it faces significant ethical challenges. The diving community was recently rocked by an incident in Indonesia, where a diver was arrested for illegally poaching and slaughtering endangered sea turtles for food. The backlash from the global community was swift and severe. For many, the incident highlighted a dark side of the industry's growth: as more people enter the water, the risk to fragile ecosystems increases.

"The ocean is not a hunting ground for human greed. Those who do not respect the sanctity of marine life have no right to call themselves divers."

This sentiment was echoed on a much larger stage at the COP31 climate summit. Sahika Ercumen, a world-class Turkish diver and UNDP (United Nations Development Programme) Goodwill Ambassador, delivered a powerful message to global leaders. Having spent thousands of hours underwater, Ercumen has a literal "front-row seat" to the effects of climate change, from coral bleaching to the proliferation of microplastics. She argues that divers are the "sentinels" of the sea—the first to witness the symptoms of a dying ocean. Her activism serves as a reminder that the sport of freediving is inextricably linked to the health of the planet; without a living ocean, the "silent sport" has no home.

The Future: Safety, Gaming, and Mainstream Integration

As we look toward the end of the year, the conversation in the diving world is shifting toward safety and mainstream culture. The industry is currently pushing for a stricter "Buddy System" and "Blackout" prevention protocols. Freediving, by its nature, involves pushing the body to the edge of consciousness. "Blackouts" (loss of consciousness due to hypoxia) can happen even to experts. The cardinal rule of the sport—never dive alone—is being reinforced through new educational guidelines from major agencies like PADI and AIDA, aiming to ensure that the sport's rapid growth doesn't lead to a spike in preventable accidents.

Interestingly, the sport is even crossing over into the digital realm. Recent news that Rockstar Games’ upcoming Grand Theft Auto VI (GTA 6) will feature highly detailed scuba and underwater exploration mechanics has caused a stir. While it may seem trivial, the inclusion of realistic diving in one of the world's most popular entertainment properties often serves as a "top-of-funnel" interest generator. Just as movies like The Big Blue inspired a generation of divers in the 1980s, virtual experiences are expected to drive a new wave of curious beginners into real-world diving centers.

What to Watch Next

  • The Asian "Blue Corridor": Watch for more partnerships between the South Korean government and Southeast Asian tourism boards. As travel demand peaks, expect "exclusive" Korean diving zones and specialized travel packages to become the norm.
  • Climate Activism: Look for more elite athletes like Sahika Ercumen to take center stage at political summits. The "diver as witness" narrative is becoming a powerful tool in environmental lobbying.
  • Technological Integration: With the rise of "Mermaid" and "Aesthetic" diving, watch for new underwater drone technology and specialized camera gear tailored for the Korean "content creator" market.

Whether it is a Ukrainian athlete defying the limits of biology at 124 meters or a Korean office worker practicing their mermaid kick in a heated pool in Seoul, the world of freediving is deeper and more interconnected than ever. It is a sport that demands total presence in a world of constant distraction—and that, perhaps, is the secret to its enduring and growing appeal.
