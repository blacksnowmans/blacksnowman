Why South Korea’s Liberal Base Is Demanding the Impeachment of the Chief Justice

October 22, 2026

Under the biting night wind outside the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) headquarters in Yeouido, Seoul, a bizarre and solemn spectacle unfolded. Dozens of protesters sat cross-legged on the frozen pavement, wrapped tightly in silver thermal emergency blankets. From a distance, their foil-draped shapes resembled Hershey’s Kisses chocolates—a visual irony that earned them the nickname the "Kisses Brigade." Holding candles aloft, the group chanted a single, uncompromising slogan through the cold air: "The courts are a mess! Impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae!"

To an outside observer, the scene might seem perplexing. South Korea’s political landscape recently underwent a seismic realignment. President Lee Jae-myung, candidate of the liberal DPK, now holds executive power following the impeachment, removal, and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Suk-yeol after his failed attempt to declare martial law on December 3. The DPK commands a commanding majority in the National Assembly. Yet, despite controlling both the executive and legislative branches, the liberal base feels backed into a corner—not by the opposition, but by an entrenched judicial establishment that they believe is actively sabotaging democratic reform.

At the center of their fury is Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae. To the grassroots activists freezing on the streets of Yeouido, Jo represents a legal cartel that shields figures from the previous regime while applying draconian standards to progressives. What began as quiet frustration within parliamentary corridors has erupted into an open revolt, exposing a deep divide between the DPK’s passionate base and its cautious legislative leadership.

A Tale of Two Sentences: Insurrection vs. a Commendation Letter

The immediate catalyst for the protest was a stunning appellate decision by the Seoul High Court regarding Park Sung-jae, who served as Minister of Justice under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Park was a central figure in the December 3 martial law attempt, accused of preparing logistical support to detain lawmakers and shut down Parliament. In his initial trial, a lower court sentenced Park to 25 years in prison for his role in the insurrection.

However, during the second-instance trial, a special panel at the Seoul High Court drastically reduced Park’s sentence by 16 years, handing him just 9 years in prison. The appellate judges acquitted Park on charges of abuse of authority, ruling that there was insufficient evidence that his directives to draft administrative documents or review prison capacity during the emergency amounted to criminal abuse of power. In effect, the court reframed an active effort to dismantle constitutional democracy as little more than a procedural oversight.

For South Korea's liberal voters, this ruling shattered whatever trust remained in the judiciary. The leniency granted to Park stands in painful contrast to the prosecution and conviction of Chung Kyung-sim, a university professor and wife of prominent liberal politician Cho Kuk. Chung was sentenced to four years in prison for her involvement in forging a single college commendation certificate for her daughter. The prosecution had demanded a seven-year sentence for paperwork irregularities, and the courts obliged with a harsh prison term that devastated her family.

The Appellate Disparity: Former Justice Minister Park Sung-jae saw his 25-year sentence for supporting an anti-constitutional military coup reduced to 9 years. Meanwhile, Chung Kyung-sim served four years in prison over a single forged university certificate. To the DPK base, this double standard proves the judiciary is fundamentally compromised.

To the activists draped in silver foil, this stark contrast illustrates a broken scale of justice. A major figure involved in an illegal attempt to subvert the republic receives a 16-year sentence reduction on technicalities, while a liberal family faces total destruction over an academic certificate. In their view, Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae’s tenure has provided umbrella coverage for conservative elites under the guise of judicial independence, leaving legislative impeachment as the only constitutional tool left to restore systemic balance.

Internal Friction: Caution in Parliament versus Urgency on the Street

While the street campaign grows louder, the ruling Democratic Party finds itself caught in an embarrassing internal rift. President Lee Jae-myung has pushed an ambitious agenda focused on economic recovery and administrative reform, but DPK leadership in the National Assembly has hesitated to table an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice.

This hesitation has infuriated party elders and reformist lawmakers. Senior DPK lawmaker Park Ji-won—a veteran political operator and former intelligence chief—publicly lambasted the current floor leadership led by Kim Min-seok. In broadcast interviews, Park minced no words, accusing the executive board of "losing its nuts and bolts" and warning that its passive "prudence" was draining the government's momentum. Former party head Jung Chung-rae similarly urged immediate action, declaring that "politics is about timing" and calling on the party to haul Jo Hee-dae before a legislative vote.

The debate highlights an enduring conflict within South Korea's ruling party:

  • The Moderate Leadership: Figures like DPK lawmaker Jeong Seong-ho advocate for political moderation, warning that launching an unprecedented impeachment against a sitting Chief Justice could trigger a political backlash, alienate undecided voters, and invite accusations of legislative overreach.
  • The Grassroots and Reformist Wing: Activists and lawmakers like Kim Byung-joo argue that the DPK's massive parliamentary majority was granted specifically to dismantle entrenched legal cartels. They view "prudence" as an excuse for cowardice that allows conservative legal networks time to regroup.

This internal friction mirrors past political cycles. DPK supporters remember painfully when the party held 180 seats in Parliament during previous administrations but failed to pass decisive judicial reforms due to endless internal deliberation, ultimately paving the way for conservative counter-offensives. The Kisses Brigade sitting outside party headquarters is determined not to let history repeat itself.

Completing Judicial Reform: Strip Prosecutorial Powers

Beyond Chief Justice Jo’s immediate fate, the anger on the streets is fueled by a desire to complete the long-delayed separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers in South Korea. For decades, South Korean prosecutors possessed the rare dual authority to both investigate crimes and indict suspects—a structural arrangement that criticism alleges allowed the Prosecution Service to act as an unchecked political weapon.

The DPK base is demanding comprehensive reform legislation that would permanently dismantle this structure:

  • Complete Structural Separation: Stripping the Prosecution Office of all direct investigative authority, converting it strictly into a "Prosecution Office" under the Ministry of Justice dedicated solely to indictments and trial maintenance.
  • Independent Investigative Agencies: Transferring primary investigative duties to a specialized Major Crimes Investigation Agency under the Ministry of Interior and Safety.
  • Abolishing Supplementary Investigations: Eliminating the legal loop-holes that allow prosecutors to request "supplementary investigations" as a pretext to maintain control over police inquiries.

When moderate lawmakers suggested that pursuing these measures reflected "narrow partisan logic," reformists reacted sharply. Former Justice Minister Chu Mi-ae, a longtime champion of prosecutorial reform, recently faced attacks over clipped remarks regarding municipal fire department funding. Conservative media outlets accused her of disparaging firefighters; however, when the full parliamentary audit video was released, it revealed she was arguing for the central government to take on greater fiscal responsibility for local fire services—a position subsequently affirmed by fire labor unions and DPK leadership.

For DPK supporters, the swift distortion of Chu’s remarks was another example of how conservative media and legal networks attempt to smear reform-minded figures. The pattern reinforces their conviction that half-measures will fail. In their eyes, as long as prosecutors retain backdoor investigative powers and the judiciary remains shielded by figures like Jo Hee-dae, any liberal government remains vulnerable to judicial overreach.

The Cultural Sphere and the Road Ahead

The struggle over judicial reform is also playing out in public civil society. Prominent progressive figures outside elected office continue to rally public sentiment. Author and commentator Rhyu Si-min, alongside veteran broadcasting anchor Byun Sang-wook, recently announced a public engagement event for October 31 at the Seongsu-dong branch of "Agio"—a social enterprise shoe manufacturer that employs disabled workers. Participants can join for a 50,000 KRW (about $37) registration fee, or attend for free with a shoe purchase.

Opponents have tried to undermine Rhyu’s public return by attempting to revive old corporate allegations involving Sillajen, a biotech firm whose stock trading scandal dominated headlines years ago. However, these attempts ignore established legal facts: South Korean courts fully acquitted Sillajen’s former executives of undisclosed trading charges, and past prosecutorial investigations under Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure found zero financial links between the firm and Rhyu. The recycling of settled allegations underscores how deep the political polarization runs across South Korean society.

As winter sets in, the immediate focus shifts back to Yeouido. What happens next will depend on three concrete indicators:

  • The DPK Floor Strategy: Whether Kim Min-seok and the DPK floor leadership bend to internal pressure and formally introduce an impeachment bill against Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae in the National Assembly.
  • Prosecutorial Reform Bills: The speed at which the DPK-controlled legislature introduces formal bills to completely eliminate prosecutors' supplementary investigative rights and set up a standalone Major Crimes Investigation Agency.
  • Grassroots Mobilization: Whether the "Kisses Brigade" sit-ins outside the DPK headquarters grow into broader weekend candlelit rallies, signaling whether the liberal electorate will tolerate parliamentary compromise.

For the protesters shivering under silver foil sheets in front of the DPK headquarters, the stakes could not be higher. They view their vigil not as an attack on the government they elected, but as an intervention to force their leaders into action. Until the National Assembly acts, the silver-clad protesters intend to remain on the asphalt, waiting for what they call the bell of judicial accountability to ring.
