Border Wars, Ballot Shocks, and Meme Politics: What the Global Far-Right Surge Means From Europe to East Asia

November 20, 2026

In a German state election tally room, strategists for conservative leader Friedrich Merz looked at the numbers and called the result a "disaster"—their camp’s worst postwar state election showing. Across the English Channel, a British party leader was trading mock videos set inside the world of Grand Theft Auto 6 to dramatize migrant boat crossings, while in Madrid, campaigners braced for the final week of a deadlocked snap general election defined by migration fears. For policymakers in Washington, Brussels, and Seoul, these moments are no longer isolated political sideshows.

According to a November 19 survey of 72 international and South Korean media reports—including dispatches from DD News, Bloomberg, and Politico Europe—far-right and nationalist movements are expanding their institutional reach while simultaneously grappling with internal infighting, street-level extremism, and diplomatic backlash. While much of the electoral drama is centered in Europe, South Korean newsrooms such as Chosun Ilbo, Hankook Ilbo, and broadcaster SBS News tracked the same currents alongside intensifying friction between China and Japan, as well as Tokyo’s refusal to grant entry to a South Korean politician. Together, the reports illustrate how grievance over immigration, welfare budgets, and national identity is testing democratic norms and cross-border alliances around the globe.

Germany and France expose cracks inside Europe’s far-right surge

The clearest electoral shock on November 19 came from Germany. DD News reported that the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) climbed into first place in a state election, dealing a severe blow to Merz’s Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and weakening the traditional conservative establishment’s regional base. Bloomberg wrote that a major, seemingly unstoppable far-right party has now cemented its place in Europe, underscoring how deeply Germany’s political landscape has shifted. Commenting on the vote, U.S. President Donald Trump told DD News that the German far right’s victory was driven by immigration.

Yet the AfD’s rise has unfolded alongside acute social tension and controversy. UCANews criticized the party for rebranding St. Nicholas chocolates and politicizing religious tradition, while outlet Flying Penguin reported claims that German funds were backing far-right memes spreading antisemitism. Security anxieties also flared: Turkey’s Anadolu Agency reported that German authorities detained a far-right extremist suspected of plotting an attack on a mosque. On the same day, Deutsche Welle noted that German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius visited the Auschwitz memorial during a trip to Poland, while Israeli outlet Ynetnews reported that Berlin’s far-left Die Linke party was embroiled in a scandal involving a mosque linked to Hezbollah.

Similar pressures are reshaping France’s presidential race, where traditional centrists and conservatives are losing ground. Regional outlet Mezanet reported that Marine Le Pen of the far-right National Rally (RN) and hard-left leader Jean-Luc Mélenchon are emerging as the likely finalists for a presidential runoff. South Korean daily Chosun Ilbo highlighted that leading contenders are campaigning on populist economic pledges, including lowering the retirement age back to 60. Meanwhile, The Straits Times reported that France’s Green Party leader proposed backing Mélenchon to unify the left, and Yahoo News Canada noted that former Prime Minister Édouard Philippe—once an ally of President Emmanuel Macron—introduced a new conservative agenda to salvage the center-right. On the streets, Greek newspaper To Vima examined the roots of ongoing riots in France, while Jacobin reported violent far-right responses to public demonstrations.

Crucially, the European far right is far from monolithic. Defence24 reported that Germany has already moved to shield its financial and military aid for Ukraine in case Le Pen wins the French presidency. At the same time, Yahoo noted that a German AfD politician fiercely attacked Le Pen, accusing her of hollowing out her party’s identity to chase mainstream appeal. In Britain, The Telegraph published an editorial warning that the UK Conservative Party must maintain zero ties with the AfD—demonstrating how toxic parts of Germany’s far right remain even to fellow European conservatives.

Welfare numbers, Brexit, and meme warfare in Britain

In the United Kingdom, Nigel Farage’s Reform UK spent November 19 driving an aggressive anti-immigration offensive centered on welfare spending and policing. Reform UK released its own figures claiming that foreign recipients of Universal Credit, Britain’s main welfare benefit, reached a record 1.26 million. The Economist observed that the party continues to keep the burqa—worn by some Muslim women—at the center of political debate, while the Watford Observer reported Farage accusing authorities of "two-tier policing" over the arrest of a pastor who criticized Islam.

That rhetoric has begun drawing defectors from the governing centre-left Labour Party, though not without internal friction. The Nottingham Post reported that a Nottinghamshire Labour local councillor, previously suspended for liking a Reform UK social media post, formally joined Farage’s party. Yet Left Foot Forward noted that Reform UK faced mockery after senior party figure Zia Yusuf claimed former Conservative (Tory) MPs were excluded from shaping its anti-immigration platform. Across the broader UK-EU debate, GB News reported that Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham opened the door to Rwanda-style third-country deportation deals as European Union states explore similar models, a trend New Internationalist magazine condemned as crossing a human rights red line.

Farage also revived old battles over Britain’s exit from the EU. Politico Europe and Mezanet reported that he traveled back to Brussels to counter Burnham’s push for closer EU cooperation, while writing in the Clacton Gazette that he would fight any attempt to reverse the Brexit referendum. On the same day, Japan Today noted that London and Brussels were holding a summit to mend post-Brexit ties, prompting Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch to warn the prime minister in The Telegraph not to reopen Brexit divisions.

Outside traditional policy debates, Reform UK’s digital campaign tactics drew widespread ridicule—and highlighted a transatlantic style of viral politics:

  • Video game parodies: The Independent and the Clacton Gazette reported that Reform UK lawmakers were mocked for portraying themselves as Grand Theft Auto (GTA) characters in a launch-day clip, while Newsweek noted Farage shared a parody video of himself playing GTA 6 to stop migrant boats in the English Channel.
  • AI backlash: The Mirror reported that a close Farage ally posted a bizarre, low-quality AI video dubbed "Grand Theft Jenrick" featuring Conservative figure Robert Jenrick; when criticized, Reform figures told detractors to "get a life," according to Yahoo News UK.
  • Transatlantic networks: The New European noted Farage took aim at actor Robert Pattinson’s outside ventures, DeSmog traced ties linking Farage, Trump, and a UK MAGA network, and Declassified UK detailed how an investigative story concerning Reform deputy Richard Tice was hit with a reporting ban.

From tight ballots in Spain to security threats across Europe

The convergence of migration anxiety and far-right activism extends well beyond Europe’s largest economies. In Spain, Politico Europe reported that polling ahead of the final week of a snap general election campaign shows narrowing gaps between parties, setting up a razor-thin finish. InfoMigrants flagged immigration as a decisive issue for Spanish voters, while The Japan Times warned that an anti-immigration government could drag down Spain’s economic growth. Analyst Dave Keating questioned whether Madrid is eroding its own influence in Brussels. On the domestic front, Spanish outlet Demócrata reported warnings from regional leader María Chivite that civil rights could roll back under a center-right or far-right coalition, even as Basque party EH Bildu’s Oskar Matute reaffirmed opposition to all violence, including that of the defunct separatist group ETA. Underscoring the darker undercurrent in Spain, Searchlight Magazine reported that a Nazi leader died outside a Spanish courthouse shortly after a police raid.

Across the continent, authorities are confronting violent underground networks and security alarms:

  • Extremist infiltration: NL Times reported that the Dutch far-right party Forum for Democracy (FvD) expelled members tied to a neo-Nazi martial arts tournament. In Bulgaria, Novinite reported that a murder investigation into the killing of Georgi Kuzev uncovered a white supremacist group calling itself the "Ku Klux Klan - Plovdiv Division."
  • Terror threats: Japanese wire service Jiji Press reported that a shooting in Krakow prompted Poland to declare national mourning and open a terrorism investigation, while Moya Norwegia reported that Norwegian security services kept their national threat level at 3 out of 5, warning of a high probability of an attack within a year.
  • Parliamentary friction: According to Philenews, Cyprus Mail, and Politis, authorities in occupied northern Cyprus denied entry to a delegation of far-right Members of the European Parliament (MEPs)—a sharp reversal from past welcomes given to the AfD—with the European Parliament clarifying the trip was not an official mission. Separately, EUobserver accused the European Parliament of violating EU law by gutting its own ethics body.

Analysts cited across policy circles—including the Goodman Project, the Almendrón Association, and the Middle East Council on Global Affairs—argue that local welfare pressures, the political risks of military rearmament, and fraying social contracts over migration remain the core engines driving these movements forward.

Exclusionary politics and diplomatic friction beyond Europe

Outside Europe, the November 19 reports point to a parallel rise in exclusionary politics, human rights crises, and bilateral tensions stretching from the Middle East to East Asia and the Americas. In Israel, Deccan Chronicle and Middle East Eye reported a surge in anti-Palestinian violence, including mosque arson and vehicle-ramming attacks on civilians; Israeli opposition figures blamed Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s "incitement" for the mosque attack, according to Deccan Chronicle. Arabic outlet Al-Quds reported intensifying right-wing efforts to delegitimize Arab lawmakers in Israel’s parliament, the Knesset, while Social Europe warned that only a two-state solution can prevent long-term catastrophe. Elsewhere, AllAfrica reported that Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) warned of a healthcare crisis in South Africa fueled by anti-immigrant hostility, and Anadolu Agency reported that a United Nations judicial body ruled Myanmar’s crackdown on the Rohingya constitutes genocide—the first genocide ruling issued against a state.

For readers in Seoul and across Asia, the sharpest resonance came from East Asia’s deteriorating political atmosphere. The New York Times reported that China is escalating verbal clashes aimed at Japan, while South Korea’s SBS News detailed controversy over Beijing’s cold and "rude" diplomatic treatment of Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi. At the same time, Tokyo faced domestic and regional scrutiny: South Korean daily Hankook Ilbo reported that a Japanese cabinet minister came under fire over a "90,000-yen lobby event" where attendees paid to meet government officials, and South Korean outlet Choiboshik's Media examined Tokyo’s decision to deny entry to South Korean politician Cho Kuk, a former party leader.

In the Americas, big money and social media populism mirrored Europe’s trends. Outlet Harici reported that billionaire Elon Musk said he felt "pretty stupid" after spending $442 million on U.S. midterm elections without achieving his desired return. Further south, Valor International reported the rise of a far-right social media star in Brazil’s lower house race, while teleSUR English noted that Argentina aligned with the United States in abstaining from a United Nations vote addressing violence against women.

Several concrete flashpoints will determine whether this populist wave consolidates power or fractures under the weight of governing:

  • Spain’s election outcome: Watch whether tight final-week polling produces an anti-immigration coalition in Madrid, and how financial markets and Brussels respond.
  • Franco-German defense coordination: Watch whether Merz’s battered conservatives and Berlin’s government can lock in Ukraine aid protections before Le Pen and Mélenchon test France’s runoff math.
  • Britain’s EU reset: Track whether the UK-EU summit yields practical agreements on migration and trade despite Farage’s Brussels campaign and Badenoch’s Brexit warnings.
  • East Asian diplomacy: Watch whether China’s friction with Prime Minister Takaichi, Tokyo’s domestic lobbying scandal, and Japan’s border exclusions involving figures like Cho Kuk harden diplomatic walls across Northeast Asia.
