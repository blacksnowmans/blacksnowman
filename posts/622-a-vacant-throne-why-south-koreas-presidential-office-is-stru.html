A Vacant Throne: Why South Korea’s Presidential Office Is Struggling to Retain Staff

October 03, 2026

For those watching South Korean politics, the mood in Yeouido—the island in Seoul that serves as the country’s political heartbeat—has soured significantly. A common refrain echoed across news communities is, “How did this administration end up in such a state?” It is a rare and embarrassing spectacle: less than 18 months into his term, the President is finding that his most critical gatekeeper, the Chief of Staff—often called the "No. 1 position for all state affairs"—has become a job that high-profile candidates are actively running away from.

The incumbent, Kang Hun-sik, signaled his intent to resign quite some time ago. Since then, potential successors have been quick to publicly distance themselves, issuing statements that essentially amount to: "It’s not me, please print a correction!" It begs the question: why has the center of power, a position that should be the ultimate political prize, turned into a role that everyone is desperate to avoid?

The Fateful Timing of a Resignation

The drama began in earnest when Kang Hun-sik submitted his resignation on the very day the President was scheduled to depart for a diplomatic tour of the U.S. and Mexico. In South Korean politics, the Chief of Staff is the anchor that maintains the Blue House (the presidential office) during the head of state’s absence. To walk away at the moment of departure is a clear signal that the internal machinery of the administration has broken down beyond simple repair.

Initially, it was assumed the resignation would be processed swiftly upon the President’s return, with a new appointment following immediately. Names like Assemblyman Han Jun-ho were floated as likely successors. However, the reality has been far more stagnant. More than two weeks have passed, and the position remains effectively vacant. While some minor reshuffling of policy staff has occurred, the most vital seat in the cabinet has been left open for over 11 days. This is no longer a mere administrative delay; it is a testament to the "power leakage" currently plaguing the administration. It indicates that the administration is suffering from a desperate talent drought, as the political class senses that the ship is taking on water.

The Calculus of Political Survival

The refusal of figures like Han Jun-ho to step into the role is highly symbolic. When the media began suggesting Han as a candidate, his camp reacted with defensive speed, explicitly stating that he had no interest in the role and demanding retractions. They understood what many casual observers might miss: power, by its nature, has a trajectory. In the current climate, where approval ratings are faltering and the "lame duck" phase is appearing earlier than expected, becoming the Chief of Staff is not an honor—it is a political liability.

Aspiring politicians know that hitching their wagon to an administration on the decline can be terminal for their careers. This is why we are seeing a trend of "every man for himself" within the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition party, which currently holds the presidency). The downfall of figures like former Vice Minister Kim Yong, who was once considered a core powerhouse of the administration, has left his former allies scrambling to distance themselves. The cold reality of Yeouido is that when a political center begins to collapse, loyalists are the first to pivot toward their own survival.

A Structural Crisis: The "Shadow" Gatekeepers

The struggle to replace the Chief of Staff is not just about the person; it is about the office’s toxic internal structure. It is widely whispered that any potential successor would demand a total overhaul of the Presidential Annex (the office that handles the President's personal scheduling and access). Specifically, there is immense pressure to remove the "Seongnam Line"—a group of aides close to the President dating back to his time as mayor of Seongnam City.

As long as figures like Kim Hyun-ji hold real, unofficial power while nominally reporting to the Chief of Staff, the position remains a hollow shell. No competent administrator wants to be a "figurehead president" who takes the blame for policy failures while having no actual control over the staff executing those policies. Given the President's well-known temperament—his rigidity and refusal to compromise—it is highly unlikely he will grant a new Chief of Staff the mandate to purge his inner circle. This creates a trap: a Chief of Staff without authority is a scapegoat in waiting.

What to Watch Next

As the administration forces through controversial appointments, such as the new head of the Investigation Bureau for Serious Crimes, it is doubling down on a strategy of confrontation rather than reconciliation. This "my-way" approach is further alienating the moderate political class and deepening the isolation of the presidential office.

Moving forward, keep an eye on these three developments:

  • The "Placeholder" Appointment: If the administration eventually fills the Chief of Staff position with a political lightweight or a loyalist rather than a seasoned heavyweight, it confirms that the administration has abandoned attempts at reform and has retreated into a bunker mentality.
  • Internal Fractures: Watch for more "independent movements" by senior party members like Kim Min-seok. If they begin publicly contradicting the President’s core policies, it indicates that the central command of the Democratic Party has lost its ability to enforce discipline.
  • The Erosion of the "Seongnam Line": Any news regarding the resignation or reassignment of key unofficial aides will be the ultimate barometer. If the President refuses to cut these ties, the administrative paralysis will likely continue until the end of his term.

The tragedy of this administration is that it has traded systemic stability for absolute loyalty, only to find that loyalty is a flimsy shield against political failure. The search for a new Chief of Staff is no longer just a personnel issue—it is a stress test for a government that is rapidly running out of both time and options.
