Beyond the Streets: How Youth Protests and "Student Lists" are Redefining Democracy

October 28, 2026

Imagine a railway station in a mid-sized European city, recently renovated and touted as a symbol of modern progress. Suddenly, the roof collapses, crushing bypassers and burying families under tons of concrete. In the aftermath, as the dust settles, the public discovers that the renovation was part of a massive, opaque international infrastructure deal, plagued by kickbacks and a total disregard for safety standards. For the people of Serbia, the 2024 Novi Sad railway station tragedy was not just an accident; it was a visceral manifestation of systemic corruption that had been rotting the state from within for over a decade.

For a Korean reader, this scene feels hauntingly familiar. It evokes the same bone-deep fury and "helplessness" experienced during the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol 씨 (who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment). During his time in office, South Korea grappled with several social tragedies where the state seemed more interested in avoiding responsibility and concealing the truth than in protecting its citizens. This shared sense of betrayal—where the government serves as a shield for the privileged rather than a guardian of the public—is currently fueling a global wave of youth-led political movements that are no longer content with just protesting. They are winning elections.

The Novi Sad Tragedy and the Global Resentment Against Corruption

The collapse of the Novi Sad railway station in November 2024 served as the final straw for a Serbian public already weary of the twelve-year rule of the Vučić administration. The project was linked to China's "Belt and Road Initiative," an international infrastructure strategy involving trillions of dollars in investments. In Serbia, however, this deal became synonymous with "behind-the-scenes" money and astronomical levels of corruption involving the central government. The tragedy stripped away the regime’s facade, revealing that public safety had been traded for political and financial gain.

This is where the local becomes global. We are seeing a pattern in countries like Indonesia, Bangladesh, and Nepal, where student-led protests are no longer mere "cries in the street." In Bangladesh, student movements recently forced the resignation of the Prime Minister. In Indonesia, despite a history of successfully maintaining direct presidential elections after toppling a corrupt dictatorship, students have returned to the streets to fight what they perceive as a "democratic backsliding."

The common denominator is the structural failure of establishment politics. When politicians prioritize private interests over the welfare of the people and dismantle the fairness of institutions, the youth feel they have no choice but to "flip the table." To dismiss these movements as the "reckless outbursts of the young" is a dangerous mistake. These students are reacting to a reality where democracy has become nominal—a system that exists on paper but has essentially been declared dead in practice because it no longer serves the many.

"Student Lists": A World-First Experiment in Bottom-Up Politics

What makes the recent Serbian experience unique is not just the protest, but the "Student List" (Studentski Spisak)—a political organization that has shattered traditional political logic. Unlike traditional parties, which usually have a "central headquarters" and a "party leader" who issues top-down commands, the Serbian students built a horizontal, bottom-up system. This structure is reminiscent of the "self-management" systems of the old socialist era, where decisions bubble up from the base rather than being handed down from an elite core.

This is a historic achievement: students directly identifying candidates, forming their own lists, and successfully overturning a regime. In South Korea, we have a history of civic groups like the People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy (PSPD) leading "rejection campaigns" to influence general elections by blacklisting corrupt candidates. However, the Serbian case goes a step further. These youth did not just act as watchdogs for the voters; they became the political subjects themselves. They selected 250 parliamentary candidates based on two rigid, uncompromising criteria: integrity and expertise.

"Any individual with even a whiff of corruption or a history of representing the interests of the establishment was strictly excluded. The list was filled only with capable, clean individuals."

To those entrenched in old-school political engineering, this "leaderless" organization seemed like a ghost. Yet, it was precisely this horizontal structure and the refusal to engage in "clique politics" that allowed them to break through the walls of the establishment. They proved that you don't need a massive, corrupt political cartel to win an election; you need a system that voters can actually trust.

The Technocrat Trap: Efficiency vs. Representation

While the success of the Serbian "Student List" offers a beacon of hope, it also carries a subtle and dangerous trap. As the public grows to despise "politicians" as a class of corrupt elites, a new sentiment often emerges: "Why not just hand the country over to the experts?" This is the lure of Technocracy—the idea that government should be run by technical experts and bureaucrats rather than elected representatives.

In Serbia, and indeed in many countries undergoing similar shifts, there is a risk that "politics" will be reduced to a matter of administrative efficiency. This is a perilous path. Politics is not just about "fixing things"; it is about the difficult, often messy process of balancing competing interests and giving a voice to the marginalized. A government of technocrats, no matter how "expert" they are, lacks the democratic mandate and the accountability that comes with political representation. Without the "interference" of democratic processes, rule by experts can quickly devolve into a new form of dictatorship—one where the citizens have no control over the bureaucrats who manage their lives.

Koreans know all too well the damage that can be done by "elite bureaucrats" who feel they are above the law or the people. Whether it was the mismanagement of state affairs during the era of Yoon Suk-yeol 씨 or the historical struggles against authoritarian planners, the lesson is clear: expertise should serve politics, not replace it. Distinguishing between "using experts to fix politics" and "handing politics over to bureaucrats" is perhaps the most significant challenge facing these new movements.

What to Watch Next

The "Student List" experiment in Serbia is just beginning, and its ripples are already being felt across the Western Balkans, in countries like Bulgaria and Albania. The success of this model suggests that the era of the "unbeatable establishment" may be coming to an end, provided that youth movements can transition from the streets to the halls of power without losing their integrity.

In the coming months, there are three key developments to watch:

  • Institutionalization: Can these bottom-up movements maintain their horizontal structure once they enter parliament? The pressure to centralize power to "get things done" is immense. If they become just another top-down party, they will lose the very trust that brought them to power.
  • The Technocratic Balance: Watch how these new leaders interact with the existing civil service. Will they succeed in using experts as tools for democratic goals, or will they succumb to the "efficiency" of bureaucratic rule?
  • Regional Contagion: Keep an eye on neighboring countries. If the Serbian model proves stable, it could provide a blueprint for youth in other struggling democracies to bypass traditional parties entirely.

Democracy is not a machine that runs on its own. It is a living thing that backslides the moment we stop guarding it. The tears and the anger of students in Novi Sad, Dhaka, and Jakarta remind us that the true owners of democracy are those who are willing to fight for it. For South Korea, now under the leadership of President Lee Jae-myung and his administration, these global events serve as a reminder that the path toward a more accountable and transparent state requires constant vigilance from the ground up. History does not favor those who wait; it favors those who have the courage to change the game.

The global wave of youth resistance is a signal that the old ways of "doing politics" are no longer sufficient. Whether these movements can build a lasting, mature democracy remains to be seen, but they have already achieved the impossible: they have shown the world that even the most entrenched corruption can be toppled by a list of names and a demand for truth.
