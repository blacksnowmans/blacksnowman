South Korea's High-Stakes Balancing Act at the Shenzhen APEC Summit

November 18, 2026

Inside a banquet hall in Shenzhen, China, moments before the formal sessions of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit got underway, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and First Lady Kim Hye-kyung crossed paths with U.S. President Donald Trump. The two leaders stood face to face, shook hands, and spoke for roughly fifty seconds. What they actually said to each other remains undisclosed. Yet in the closely watched choreography of multilateral summits, fifty seconds was enough to send diplomats and reporters scrambling to read the room.

The Shenzhen gathering has quickly evolved from a routine economic forum into a high-stakes arena for Northeast Asian diplomacy. According to on-site reporting by South Korean journalist Park Hyun-kwang and commentary circulating in Seoul's political press, the central question hanging over the summit is whether a bilateral meeting between President Lee and Russian President Vladimir Putin is now imminent. After Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov publicly stated that Putin "will meet President Lee Jae-myung," Korean observers began treating an encounter between the two leaders as a matter of timing rather than speculation—and as a potential turning point for easing tensions on the Korean Peninsula.

At the same time, remarks by President Trump hinting at a possible meeting with North Korean leader Kim Jong-un, paired with Russian media reports suggesting Putin could also meet Kim, have fueled speculation about broader diplomatic maneuvering behind closed doors. Together, these overlapping signals put Lee's self-described "pragmatic diplomacy" to an immediate, public test.

Fifty Seconds With Trump and a Domestic Diplomatic Pivot

On the surface, a fifty-second greeting before a state dinner looks like little more than diplomatic courtesy. Park Hyun-kwang, however, characterizes the brief encounter as deliberate "ice-breaking" ahead of more formal bilateral engagement between Seoul and Washington. In a room filled with world leaders, making direct eye contact and exchanging unscripted pleasantries—with First Lady Kim Hye-kyung accompanying the president—served as an initial gesture of rapport aimed at a U.S. leader known for personal, unconventional diplomacy.

For readers outside South Korea, the scene in Shenzhen carries added weight because of the dramatic political shift that brought Lee to office. Following the impeachment, removal from office, and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol, Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea—now South Korea's ruling party, with the conservative People Power Party serving as the main opposition—assumed the presidency pledging to pursue "national-interest-first pragmatic diplomacy."

South Korean political commentators sympathetic to Lee's approach draw a sharp contrast between his poised, carefully staged appearance in Shenzhen and what they criticize as humiliating, poorly prepared diplomatic missteps during Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office. In their view, Lee's outreach to Trump is designed to secure South Korea's footing within a shifting U.S. foreign policy framework without locking Seoul into a one-dimensional posture that focuses exclusively on Washington. At the same time, critics and cautious observers inevitably note that balancing alliance expectations with outreach to rival powers leaves little margin for error, meaning the real measure of those fifty seconds will depend on what follows at the working level.

Moscow's Opening: Sanctions Versus Arctic Shipping

If the handshake with Trump was about maintaining momentum with a traditional ally, the signal from Moscow points toward repairing a relationship that has been pushed to the brink. Since the outbreak of the war in Ukraine and the subsequent deepening of military and political ties between Russia and North Korea, relations between Seoul and Moscow have been severely strained.

That is why Peskov's announcement that Putin intends to meet Lee drew immediate attention in Shenzhen. South Korean analysts interpret the Kremlin's public readiness to talk not as empty politeness, but as a pragmatic calculation. Even amid its closer alignment with Pyongyang, Moscow faces strategic costs if it completely alienates South Korea, one of Northeast Asia's major industrial and economic powers. Whether the two presidents sit down for a formal bilateral summit or hold an informal "pull-aside" conversation on the margins of APEC remains uncertain, yet commentators in Seoul view Peskov's remarks as evidence that Russia is responding to Lee's effort to keep diplomatic channels open.

According to Park Hyun-kwang's analysis, any conversation between Lee and Putin would revolve around two interconnected tracks—one focused on immediate security risks, and the other on long-term economic incentives:

  • Curbing military cooperation with Pyongyang: Seoul's primary urgency is addressing Russian military support for North Korea. South Korean officials and analysts warn that Russian technology and resources flowing to the North threaten security across the Korean Peninsula, making firm commitments to restrain that cooperation central to any talks.
  • Cooperation on the Arctic shipping route: On the economic side, South Korean commentary points to the opening of northern maritime corridors driven by climate change. Pairing South Korea's advanced shipbuilding industry with Russia's access to Arctic waters and natural resources is framed by Lee's supporters as a bold effort to turn security friction into commercial opportunity.

Pursuing both tracks simultaneously is a delicate exercise. Rather than relying solely on condemnation, advocates of Lee's strategy argue that Seoul must leverage what Moscow wants economically to extract security concessions—though doing so while navigating international sanctions tied to the Ukraine war and North Korea remains a complex test of diplomatic precision.

Whispers of a Trump-Kim Encore in Shenzhen

Beyond bilateral ties with Washington and Moscow, Shenzhen has been swept up in speculation over North Korea. President Trump recently raised eyebrows by mentioning the possibility of Kim Jong-un visiting China and hinting at a potential meeting with the North Korean leader. Around the same time, Russian media outlets reported that Putin could also meet Kim. Taken together, those statements have revived talk of a high-stakes convergence involving Washington, Moscow, and Pyongyang.

Park Hyun-kwang reports from the sidelines that diplomats from multiple countries are quietly tracking each other's movements in Shenzhen, working to arrange even brief, unannounced contacts. In his assessment, the fact that Trump's comments and Russian media reports point in the same direction suggests that behind-the-scenes groundwork may already be underway.

Any hint of a Trump-Kim reunion inevitably recalls the dramatic diplomatic shift of 2018 and the subsequent collapse of talks in Hanoi. For South Korea, moments when Washington, Moscow, or Beijing weigh direct talks with Pyongyang always carry an acute anxiety known domestically as "Korea passing"—the fear that major powers could strike deals affecting the peninsula's future while sidelining Seoul. South Korean commentators backing Lee argue that his multi-directional diplomacy is specifically intended to prevent that outcome, positioning South Korea as an active mediator and direct stakeholder rather than a passive bystander.

Mount Paektu's Symbolism and What to Watch Next

Adding another layer of intrigue to the diplomatic activity in Shenzhen is recent news that Kim Jong-un visited Mount Paektu, the volcanic peak on the North Korea-China border that holds central symbolic importance in North Korean political mythology. As Park Hyun-kwang explains, a visit to Mount Paektu by the North Korean leader is rarely a routine inspection; historically, it has functioned as a political ritual preceding major national decisions, whether nuclear tests or sweeping shifts in foreign policy.

Some analysts caution that the sheer geographic distance between Mount Paektu and Shenzhen makes it unlikely that Kim will appear in person during the APEC summit. Yet Korean observers note that modern summit diplomacy does not always require physical presence. Whatever decision Kim reached or signaled during his trip to Mount Paektu could still be relayed to the negotiating tables in Shenzhen through special envoys, or indirectly through Putin and Trump.

Whether the quiet maneuvering in Shenzhen produces tangible breakthroughs or stalls behind competing interests will become clearer in the days ahead. Here are the concrete developments to watch next:

  • The format and readout of a Lee-Putin encounter: Watch whether Moscow and Seoul confirm a formal bilateral summit or settle for a brief pull-aside meeting, and look closely at whether any official statement addresses Russian military support to North Korea alongside economic projects like Arctic shipping.
  • Follow-up between Seoul and Washington: Pay attention to whether the fifty-second greeting between Lee and Trump leads to scheduled working-level consultations or an announcement of a formal U.S.-South Korea summit.
  • Clarification from Pyongyang: Watch for diplomatic signals—whether through state media, envoys, or third-party comments from Moscow and Washington—indicating whether Kim Jong-un's Mount Paektu visit points toward renewed dialogue or a harder line after APEC concludes.
