The Shadow Behind the Throne: How a Resurfaced Factional Machine Is Reshaping South Korea’s Ruling Camp

October 19, 2026

Late at night inside an unremarkable neighborhood Chinese restaurant in suburban Seoul, an economist and an intensely private political operative sat over shared glasses of liquor. The operative, Jeong Jin-sang, was not a household name to the wider public, yet an accompanying colleague treated him with the rigid, deferential respect usually reserved for an elder statesman. Across the table, Jeong laid down his business card and made an extraordinary proposition: he wanted the scholar, Professor Choi Bae-geun, to serve as their modern-day “Sambong Jeong Do-jeon”—the 14th-century philosopher-statesman who drafted the legal, administrative, and ideological blueprint for the founding of the Joseon Dynasty. Their organization, Jeong promised, would handle the real machinery of power and elevate him to design a new republic.

That meeting, detailed in recent revelations by Choi himself, has pulled back the curtain on one of the most polarizing and poorly understood networks in modern South Korean politics: the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance (literally the "Eastern Gyeonggi Coalition"). What began decades ago as an underground student activist network rooted in the industrial satellite city of Seongnam has traveled a long, contested path to the very core of South Korea’s current government under President Lee Jae-myung. For foreign observers watching South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea navigate sharp internal friction, Choi’s testimony provides an indispensable map of the factional plumbing operating behind the headlines.

The Midnight Pitch: Courting an Architect for Power

Professor Choi Bae-geun is no ordinary academic spectator; he is a well-known progressive economist who had direct, personal exposure to the inner circle surrounding Lee Jae-myung during Lee's tenure as governor of Gyeonggi Province. The central figure in Choi’s disclosures is Jeong Jin-sang, long regarded by insiders as President Lee’s absolute confidant, having worked alongside him since Lee’s days as an outspoken civil rights lawyer and activist in Seongnam.

Within South Korean political circles, the benchmark for behind-the-scenes influence has long been figures like Yang Jeong-cheol, the legendary political architect who served as the indispensable right-hand man to former President Moon Jae-in. Yet during that late-night dinner, those introducing Jeong made a staggering claim: Jeong's standing within Lee's orbit surpassed even Yang’s historical influence. Choi observed a disciplined, semi-covert organizational culture. When asked direct questions about their long-term institutional goals, Jeong remained evasive, offering only that they would discuss details later.

As the overtures continued, the transactional nature of the alliance became clear. Key intermediaries, including policy planning chief Lee Han-joo, pressed Choi to join, insisting that if a suitable formal post did not exist, one would be invented for him. Concrete inducements followed: the camp offered to link research initiatives to funded public projects across Gyeonggi Province and its state-backed subsidiaries, even proposing to appoint one of Choi’s doctoral students to a senior civil service post (equivalent to Grade 3, a high-ranking directorship) under the pretext of maintaining smooth communications.

Choi flatly turned down the offers, warning that such maneuvers represented an improper privatization of public personnel authority. To Choi, the vision presented was not a collaborative pursuit of democratic governance, but an effort by an entrenched faction to capture intellectual prestige as window dressing while holding onto the operational levers of power.

From Activist Underground to State Machinery: The Seongnam Foothold

To grasp why this recruitment effort alarmed seasoned observers, one must look back to the origins of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance. Emerging from the radical student movements of the 1980s and 1990s, the group established its operational base in Seongnam, a working-class city southeast of Seoul. Unlike other progressive factions anchored in traditional labor union hubs like Ulsan or Changwon, this network maintained a distinct organizational philosophy: prioritize discipline, seize control of party machinery, and systematically leverage state apparatuses.

Choi recalled having broken ties with figures connected to this circle around 2004, during the early years of the Democratic Labor Party (a pioneer left-wing party). Even then, their roadmap was focused less on broad ideological pluralism and more on consolidating internal party hegemony. While the formal organization eventually dissolved from public view, its personnel and tactical methods found fertile ground in Seongnam municipal politics.

The decisive breakthrough arrived during the June 2010 local elections. Lee Jae-myung, then a little-known human rights attorney running as the candidate for the main liberal opposition party, struck an eleventh-hour electoral pact with Kim Mi-hee, the candidate representing the Democratic Labor Party. Kim withdrew, enabling Lee to secure a razor-thin victory as mayor of Seongnam.

The dividends of that consolidation became visible almost immediately:

  • Transition Committee Leadership: Kim Mi-hee was named head of Mayor-elect Lee’s mayoral transition committee.
  • Key Appointments: Han Yong-jin, a former co-chair of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance, was officially appointed to the transition committee, embedding factional personnel directly into municipal governance.
  • The Rise of the "Seongnam Faction": Confidants like Jeong Jin-sang, Kim Yong, and Kim Hyun-ji consolidated their status as Lee’s core operational team, transitioning from municipal activism to municipal administration.
  • Public Resource Allocation: The arrangement soon faced scrutiny over patronage, exemplified by "Nanum Hwan-gyeong," a social enterprise cleaning contractor founded by Gyeonggi Dongbu-linked figures that secured lucrative municipal waste management contracts from the city.

While municipal officials defended these moves as legitimate efforts to foster social enterprises, critics saw the template of an entrenched faction converting municipal procurement and administrative posts into organizational sustenance—a blueprint later expanded when Lee won the governorship of Gyeonggi Province.

The Ghost of 2012: The KINTEX Brawl and the Politics of Purge

For South Korea’s liberal establishment, the specter of the Gyeonggi Dongbu network carries acute trauma. The defining flashpoint occurred on May 12, 2012, at the KINTEX convention center in Ilsan, during a national central committee meeting of the now-defunct Unified Progressive Party. The party had been torn apart by allegations of widespread ballot rigging in its proportional representation primary, designed to favor candidates affiliated with the dominant Gyeonggi Dongbu faction.

When party reformers attempted to pass structural clean-up measures, factional loyalists stormed the stage. In chaotic scenes broadcast live across the country, party co-chair Rhyu Si-min was grabbed by the lapels and had his glasses knocked off, while fellow co-chair Cho Jun-ho was brutally assaulted, suffering serious neck injuries. Cho Kuk, then a prominent legal scholar at Seoul National University, publicly denounced the faction, warning that if internal democracy and procedural legitimacy were abandoned, the moral foundation of progressive politics would cease to exist.

"When a political party can no longer tolerate dissenting voices, what remains is not unity, but forced obedience."

Prior to that physical meltdown, the dominant faction had approached Rhyu Si-min with an enticing offer: step forward as the party leader or future presidential standard-bearer, and the organization would mobilize its disciplined ground game to carry him to power. Rhyu unequivocally rejected the deal, concluding that serving as a figurehead for an unaccountable faction would destroy party democracy. Choi Bae-geun’s recent revelations suggest that the playbook offered to Rhyu in 2012—offering the mantle of leadership in exchange for factional patronage—was precisely the model extended to him years later.

"Mun-Jo-Teol-Rae-Yu" and the Fracturing of the Democratic Mainstream

This historical pattern explains the visceral nature of the internal conflict currently roiling the ruling Democratic Party. Among hardline online loyalists of the administration, a derisive portmanteau has gained currency: "Mun-Jo-Teol-Rae-Yu." The label stitches together the names of five historic pillars of South Korea's progressive ecosystem:

  • Mun: Former President Moon Jae-in
  • Jo: Cho Kuk, former Minister of Justice
  • Teol: Kim Ou-joon, influential progressive media host (nicknamed for his facial hair)
  • Rae: Jung Chung-rae, veteran Democratic Party lawmaker
  • Yu: Rhyu Si-min, prominent author, commentator, and former minister

That these figures—each having played instrumental roles in mobilizing the progressive base and electing past administrations—are now targets of sustained vitriol from within their own camp illustrates the zero-sum logic of factional supremacy. In Choi’s analysis, this hostility is driven by a deep institutional insecurity. Figures like Rhyu Si-min and Kim Ou-joon possess independent platforms, loyal followings, and intellectual weight; they cannot simply be directed by backroom factional managers.

When external intellectuals refuse to act as passive packaging for internal factional designs, the organizational instinct is not compromise, but delegitimization. By turning their fire inward against former allies, the dominant grouping risks isolating the ruling party from moderate voters and eroding the pluralistic coalition that historically carried the South Korean left to power.

What to Watch Next

The public re-examination of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance and the Seongnam faction comes at a critical juncture for President Lee Jae-myung's administration, facing an aggressive challenge from the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. As the administration moves forward, several concrete indicators will reveal how deeply this factional battle affects the state:

  • Key Personnel Vetting: Watch upcoming appointments within the presidential office and public agencies. Nominations of figures with roots in the Seongnam administration or historical ties to the 2012 Unified Progressive Party apparatus will signal whether factional patronage continues to override institutional checks.
  • Legislative Party Elections: Monitor parliamentary committee leadership contests and party steering elections inside the Democratic Party. The treatment of non-mainstream lawmakers will show whether the party accommodates diverse viewpoints or accelerates purges of those outside the inner circle.
  • Public Backlash Among Intellectual Allies: Choi Bae-geun's public break marks a rare breach of silence from a respected policy figure. Watch whether other progressive academics, policy advisers, and civil society leaders follow suit by distancing themselves from closed-door decision-making.

In South Korea's high-stakes presidential system, the line between cohesive political leadership and factional capture is notoriously thin. As long as the shadows of past factional machinery linger over current governance, the health of the country's party democracy will remain an urgent open question.
