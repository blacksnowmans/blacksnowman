"One Team" Against the Government: Inside Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon and the Conservatives' Budget War

November 07, 2026

When Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon walked into the National Assembly on November 6, it was not merely for a routine municipal funding session. Meeting with Jang Dong-hyeok, the leader of the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative opposition party), the atmosphere inside the conference room felt less like administrative coordination and more like a war council. Marking their third private and public meeting within a single week, Jang declared unequivocally that the conservative party and the Seoul Metropolitan Government are now operating as "one team."

In South Korean politics, such language carries heavy institutional weight. Following the dramatic political reshuffling that saw former conservative president Yoon Suk-yeol impeached and imprisoned, the progressive Democratic Party of Korea now holds the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung. The conservatives find themselves pushed into the parliamentary minority, searching for political leverage. In Mayor Oh Se-hoon—a four-term mayor governing a metropolis of nearly ten million people—the People Power Party has found its most potent executive weapon against the central administration.

The Battle of the Balance Sheets: 104 Trillion Won Apart

At the immediate center of this confrontation is the annual budget review, the period each autumn when the National Assembly dissects the national spending plan for the coming fiscal year. The divide between the administration and the conservative opposition is cavernous. President Lee Jae-myung's government has submitted an austerity-tinged draft budget totaling 821 trillion won (approximately $615 billion). The People Power Party, conversely, is pushing for an alternative fiscal blueprint reaching roughly 925 trillion won (about $690 billion)—an extraordinary gap of 104 trillion won (around $78 billion).

This macro-level impasse reached ground level when Seoul's budget figures were placed on the table. According to Mayor Oh, the central government slashed Seoul’s requested state allocations by 1 trillion won (about $750 million), starving critical municipal projects of planned central co-funding. Oh minced no words during the consultative meeting, publicly branding the central government a "fiscal Nolbu administration."

"The central government behaves like Nolbu, expanding populist cash welfare programs on paper while quietly pushing the crippling financial burdens down onto municipal balance sheets."

For international readers unfamiliar with Korean folklore, Nolbu is the archetypal greedy older brother who hogs the family inheritance, starves his virtuous sibling, and exploits everyone around him for personal gain. By invoking Nolbu, Oh accused the Lee administration of taking political credit for nationwide welfare promises while sticking local taxpayers with the bill. Seoul officials pointed specifically to an estimated 3.6 trillion won (about $2.7 billion) in additional administrative costs forced upon the capital to fund welfare mandates decreed by the central government.

Municipal Governance as an Opposition Weapon

Why is a metropolitan budget dispute escalating into a national political confrontation? In South Korea's unitary system of government, local authorities possess limited tax-raising powers and rely heavily on central government transfers and matching grants to fund large infrastructure initiatives. When the ruling party in the Blue House and the municipal leadership in City Hall belong to opposing political camps, these negotiations invariably become friction points.

For the People Power Party, Seoul’s plight provides the perfect real-world exhibit to challenge the Lee Jae-myung administration's economic management. Conservative lawmakers, led by party leader Jang Dong-hyeok and floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik, intend to use Seoul's 3.6 trillion-won unfunded mandate claim to portray the central government's fiscal strategy as reckless and punitive. By framing the dispute not as Seoul begging for special treatment, but as an unfair administrative squeeze on urban residents, the opposition seeks to rally public skepticism against the administration's broader fiscal direction.

In exchange, Oh Se-hoon desperately needs the People Power Party’s legislative muscle. Critical municipal initiatives—chief among them aggressive urban redevelopment and high-density housing supply programs designed to stabilize runaway real estate prices—rely on national statutory support and fiscal subsidies. Without conservative lawmakers fighting for line-item amendments during the legislative review, Seoul's development agenda risks stalling.

Internal Conservative Math and the Road to Power

The sudden closeness between Jang Dong-hyeok and Oh Se-hoon cannot be understood in isolation from internal conservative party politics. As the main opposition party seeks to rebuild its identity in the post-Yoon Suk-yeol era, power struggles are simmering beneath the surface. The party remains split over lingering ideological debates, internal leadership factions, and the potential political return of figures like former interim leader Han Dong-hoon.

In this unsettled landscape, Oh Se-hoon occupies an enviable position. As the elected chief executive of the nation’s capital, he possesses real administrative power, budget authority, and daily media visibility—luxuries that parliamentary backbenchers lack. For Jang Dong-hyeok’s party leadership, securing an ironclad alliance with Oh anchors the party’s credibility in the massive Seoul metropolitan area, which typically decides national elections.

For Oh, cementing a unified front with the party apparatus solidifies his standing as the premier conservative standard-bearer. By positioning himself as the courageous executive standing up to the central government's "fiscal bullying," Oh elevates his stature far beyond City Hall. Yet critics caution that this strategy carries immense risks. If municipal administration becomes entirely subsumed by partisan combat, routine city governance can quickly be paralyzed by political trench warfare.

The Citizen Cost of Budget Warfare

Lost amid the rhetorical exchanges between City Hall and the National Assembly are the ordinary residents of the capital. While politicians posture over macroeconomic doctrine, real municipal programs hang in the balance. When central subsidies fall short, city halls face an unpalatable choice: abandon infrastructure pledges, cut local municipal services, or take on municipal debt.

The friction over cash welfare mandates is particularly acute. Centralized welfare expansions often require statutory matching funds from regional governments. When national programs expand without commensurate transfer adjustments, affluent cities like Seoul see their capital investment budgets squeezed. Crucial projects—such as flood prevention tunneling, subway infrastructure upgrades, and subsidized housing supply—inevitably find themselves competing with mandatory welfare transfers.

Yet framing the debate in purely adversarial terms threatens to erode the fundamental purpose of intergovernmental fiscal coordination. When budget deliberations are treated as a rehearsal for the next general or presidential election, compromise becomes synonymous with surrender.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly moves deeper into committee-level budget reviews, several concrete flashpoints will determine how this confrontation unfolds:

  • The 1 Trillion Won Restoration Battle: Watch whether conservative lawmakers on the Special Committee on Budget and Accounts successfully attach riders restoring the 1 trillion won ($750 million) cut from Seoul's infrastructure and housing allocations, or whether the ruling Democratic Party strikes them down.
  • Expansion to Other Conservative Municipalities: The People Power Party leadership has signaled that Seoul is merely the first stop. Watch for coordinated budget protests from other conservative-led provincial and municipal governments, creating a nationwide local-versus-central fiscal front.
  • Legislative Deadlock and Delay Tactics: With the opposition threatening filibuster-style resistance and demanding major expansions toward their 925 trillion won target, monitor whether the legal December budget deadline is breached, risking an unprecedented political impasse heading into the new year.

The clash between Jang Dong-hyeok's party apparatus and the central government proves that while the conservatives lost the presidency, they still hold powerful executive redoubts. Whether Mayor Oh’s aggressive stance secures the funding Seoul needs—or merely turns the capital’s civic budget into collateral damage—will be the defining story of this legislative season.
