Black Snowman

Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Infighting and the Telegram Leak Threatening Unity

September 07, 2026

When you watch South Korean politics up close, the self-absorption is hard to miss. The grand ideals of building a better nation and serving the public often vanish, replaced by a desperate scramble for political survival and personal turf. That bitter reality hung heavy over Representative Han Min-soo recently when a leaked phone screen flashed four simple words: "Seizing control of Jung Cheong-rae's faction." The long, weary sigh Han exhaled captured the quiet exhaustion of watching a political party consume itself from within.

South Korea's main liberal opposition, the Democratic Party of Korea, recently wrapped up its national convention, installing Kim Min-seok as party leader alongside a slate of five supreme council members: Choi Min-hee, Park Sun-won, Seo Mi-hwa, Lee Seong-yun, and Han Min-soo. Yet behind the celebratory speeches and raised hands lies a brutal backstory of internal factional warfare, fought under a newly minted voting rule weighted heavily toward ordinary dues-paying members. As the party preaches unity and reform, observers are left wondering whether these slogans are designed to secure the party's future or simply establish a fresh pecking order for rival camps.

The National Convention and the High Cost of Party Reform

Factional Labels and the Shadow of Internal Betrayal

The party convention felt less like a contest of policy ideas and more like a survival of the fittest. Han Min-soo managed a surprise second-place finish in the conservative stronghold of Daegu, ultimately securing his seat on the supreme council, but his path was anything but smooth. Members of certain factions lashed out, branding him a "traitor" for refusing to toe a rigid ideological line. Such attacks are routinely packaged as deep concern for the party's wellbeing, but the underlying arithmetic is transparent: tearing down a rival is often the easiest way to secure an open slot for oneself.

When reporters pressed the architects of these smear campaigns for a justification, the answers were remarkably hollow. Mumbling lines like "Someone told us to do it," these foot soldiers demonstrated how deeply entrenched obedient cronyism remains in South Korean politics. Attaching a "disloyal" label to a lawmaker who spent years working closely alongside President Lee Jae-myung and veteran politician Jung Cheong-rae was enough to spark genuine anger among rank-and-file members.

What truly infuriated the party base was the emergence of two toxic dynamics: self-anointed insiders who exploit personal proximity to the presidency for private leverage, and exclusionary gatekeepers weaponizing factional purity tests. Han acknowledged this reality in his victory speech, calling himself a "supreme council member created by ordinary party members." It was an implicit admission that amid a sea of calculating political operators, the grassroots base was his only reliable shield.

A Knife-Edge Race Under the Banner of Member Sovereignty

Final vote tallies from the party's election management committee revealed just how tense the contest was. In the leadership race, Kim Min-seok edged out Jung Cheong-rae with 54.08% to 45.92%. The race for the supreme council was even tighter, with Han Min-soo securing fifth place by a razor-thin margin of just 0.60% over the sixth-place finisher, Kim Yong. While delegates heavily favored Kim Min-seok, public opinion polls leaned toward Jung Cheong-rae, highlighting a persistent gap between the party establishment and the broader public.

This convention marked the debut of a "one member, one vote" system championed by Jung Cheong-rae. By dismantling an old, heavily weighted delegate system that gave party elites disproportionate influence, the reform was a historic step toward strengthening party democracy. Yet changing voting rules overnight could not eradicate decades of deep-seated factionalism.

Instead, the compressed math turned the race into a high-stakes thriller where thousands of votes separated winners from losers. Desperate candidates unleashed negative messaging against rival camps, inadvertently undermining the party's overall cohesion. The lesson is plain: advanced voting systems mean little if the politicians using them remain wedded to archaic, tribal mentalities.

Parliamentary Confirmation Hearings and Opposition Tactics

Recycling Prosecutor-Era Playbooks Against Reform Nominees

South Korea's National Assembly has once again tumbled into a loop of partisan warfare, draining policy confirmation hearings of any meaningful oversight. The conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, has largely abandoned substantive policy verification in favor of character assassination. Their coordinated attacks on nominees Kim Seung-won and Yong Hye-in have crossed well past normal parliamentary bounds.

The case against Kim Seung-won relies heavily on investigative records from the era when current conservative figures held immense prosecutorial power under the previous administration. It is a stunning irony: using archived investigative tools once wielded by their own allies to bludgeon nominees of the current progressive government. When conservative critics attempt to reframe routine constituent service—such as forwarding public grievances to government agencies—as illicit lobbying, the rationale collapses entirely.

Prosecutors previously threw immense resources at these exact matters without finding criminal wrongdoing, even clearing civil servants involved. Reviving a minor disciplinary note to generate political smoke is less about accountability and more about cutting off lawmakers from the communities they represent.

Double Standards on Presidential Prerogative and Judicial Independence

As part of the ruling coalition, defending the president's constitutional right to make cabinet and agency appointments is a basic prerequisite for administrative stability. President Lee Jae-myung's willingness to tap figures from outside traditional party lines mirrors a distinct governing philosophy. Irresponsible demands for resignations from within the broader political sphere only distort the nature of governance and damage institutional stability.

A deeper institutional crisis lies in the behavior of the judiciary and the conservative opposition, who invoke judicial independence selectively. The argument that the Supreme Court Chief Justice's right to recommend candidates holds equal weight to the president's appointment power fundamentally misunderstands South Korea's constitutional framework. The final appointment power rests with the executive, while recommendation mechanisms exist as a historical check against authoritarian overreach.

When senior court administrators appear before parliament to blur those distinct constitutional lines—and allegedly pressure nominees to step down—they trade judicial neutrality for political leverage. Rather than guarding the bench, parts of the judiciary risk becoming tools for political disruption.

Old-School Politics and Internal Fractures

Conservative Pilgrimages to Disgraced Former Presidents

While the ruling bloc grapples with internal friction, the conservative opposition remains mired in the past. Recent visits by conservative figures to imprisoned and disgraced former presidents—offering deep bows and praising them as "proud leaders"—offer a grim snapshot of the conservative party's identity crisis.

Listening to conservative lawmakers critique current administration picks while paying homage to figures convicted of corruption raises a basic question: do these politicians understand the meaning of reform? As Han Min-soo colorfully described it, the current state of the conservative opposition resembles a winter sweet potato left too long in the storage cellar—rotten at the core, giving off a stale odor, yet clinging stubbornly to survival.

Rather than reckoning with past misrule, conservative politicians lean into yesterday's playbook, ensuring continued public backlash.

The Telegram Leak and the Threat Within

Still, the Democratic Party cannot afford to point fingers without cleaning its own house. A leaked text exchange between a deputy head of the party's ethics inspection unit and Representative Park Sun-won exposed deep fissures within the liberal camp. Blunt phrases like "seizing control" and "executing operations" against specific figures revealed that internal watchdog bodies risk being repurposed into instruments of factional warfare.

When key figures tasked with maintaining party discipline harbor such partisan agendas, the damage to internal trust is severe. For supporters watching from the outside, few sights are more discouraging than watching politicians chase narrow survival tactics when the party should be marshalling its strength for upcoming local elections and broader administrative reform.

What to Watch Next

  • Internal Disciplinary Oversight: Monitor whether the Democratic Party leadership takes concrete steps to audit and reform its ethics units, ensuring investigative bodies operate independently of factional agendas.
  • Confirmation Hearing Fallout: Watch how the ruling coalition handles ongoing parliamentary battles over key nominees, which will signal whether the administration can maintain legislative discipline against conservative pressure.
  • Grassroots Mobilization: Keep an eye on how newly empowered ordinary party members utilize their voting weight in upcoming regional primaries, serving as a barometer for whether bottom-up reform is taking permanent root.

Politics is ultimately shaped by people, and a politician's choices reveal where their true loyalties lie. Beyond telecommunication leaks and backstage maneuvering, the fundamental question remains whether South Korea's political class can remember why they entered public service in the first place. The coming months will test whether the Democratic Party can transcend its internal divisions and deliver on the promise of reform.
