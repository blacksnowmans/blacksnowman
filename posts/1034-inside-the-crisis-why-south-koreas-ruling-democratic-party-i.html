Inside the Crisis: Why South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party is Watching Its Approval Ratings Freefall

October 12, 2026

Walk down the bustling streets of Seoul’s political district in Yeouido right now, and you can practically feel the tension radiating from party headquarters. South Korea is under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, who swept into power with a mandate to reform and rebuild after years of turbulent conservative rule. Yet, the very party that carried him to the Blue House—the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s main liberal party—is staring down an alarming reality. Recent tracking polls from major pollsters like Realmeter show a severe, unexpected plunge in public support. For a ruling party meant to serve as the bedrock for the president's agenda, this isn't just a routine mid-term dip. It has critics and insiders whispering about a collapse in party discipline, leaving foreign observers and domestic analysts alike asking a vital question: How did the ruling party manage to alienate its own base so quickly?

A Leadership Vacuum Under Kim Min-seok

At the center of this storm is Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party. Across political circles, the criticism is biting. Detractors are openly labeling his leadership as dangerously passive, with some calling it one of the most ineffectual tenures in the party's recent history. Even previous leadership periods that were criticized as in-name-only ruling parties are now looked back upon with relative tolerance compared to the current stagnation.

Instead of aggressively seizing the political narrative or rallying public enthusiasm, the current leadership has leaned heavily on what they call "prudence"—a cautious, wait-and-see approach that is rapidly exhausting the patience of core progressive voters. Inside the party, a pervasive culture of deference insists that lawmakers should not rock the boat or challenge Kim Min-seok’s authority, often framed as a matter of "political duty and courtesy." But critics argue that political etiquette does not put food on the table, nor does it rescue a sinking government's momentum.

This paralysis has left progressive voters adrift. The leadership has seemed incapable of deciding whether to confront or collaborate with the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, agile progressive splinter party—making the party's strategy look less like careful calculation and more like cowardice. Voters who turned out for a clear, combative opposition-style posture are instead watching their leadership drift without a compass.

The Rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party and the 10% Threat

Poll numbers do not lie, and the data paints a grim picture for the Democratic Party's monopoly on the liberal electorate. Recent automated response (ARS) surveys show the gap between the Democratic Party and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, narrowing to a razor-thin margin within the margin of error. Concurrently, the Rebuilding Korea Party is surging, climbing past 8% in ARS metrics and showing steady upward momentum in phone-interview polls as well.

This is not a random statistical noise; it is a clear migration of disillusioned liberal voters. When a splinter party begins capturing this much of the progressive base, it serves as a flashing red light for the ruling establishment. If the Rebuilding Korea Party crosses the psychological and political threshold of 10% support, it will cease to be a mere minor voice and will instead secure ultimate kingmaker status in the National Assembly.

History shows that Korean politics is unforgiving to major parties that take their base for granted. The electorate is tired of sluggish responses and is moving toward political entities willing to speak louder and faster. If the Democratic leadership fails to recognize this as an existential threat, they are demonstrating a fundamental loss of political intuition.

The Echo of Jung Chung-rae’s Absence

To understand just how stark the current leadership vacuum is, one only has to look back at the early days of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Back then, presidential and party approval ratings remained robust, largely due to the fierce, high-profile presence of lawmaker Jung Chung-rae. Acting as a human shield for the administration and relentlessly pushing core issues like prosecutorial reform, Jung kept the conservative opposition on the defensive and galvanized the progressive base.

When Jung stepped back from the frontline following a tense party convention, the party's broad-appeal sharpness vanished almost overnight. While core loyalists in phone-interview surveys still express strong internal approval, the ability to persuade moderates and independents completely evaporated. What replaced Jung's aggressive, forward-leaning brand of politics was a dull defensive shell.

Jung has maintained a strict silence since stepping back, largely out of a sense of political propriety—having lost a tight race, he chose to give the new leadership room to govern. Yet, this very silence has fueled speculation. Some party insiders suggest he is biting his tongue to protect rising progressive figures from friendly fire within a suspicious party mainstream. Regardless of the motivation, the contrast is stark: where there was once a dynamic champion driving the narrative, there is now an administration weighed down by defensive stagnation.

What to Watch Next

As the Democratic Party navigates this self-inflicted crisis, the coming weeks will determine whether the current leadership can salvage its agenda or if a broader internal rebellion is brewing.

  • Polling Thresholds: Watch closely to see if the Rebuilding Korea Party decisively breaks the 10% ceiling in national surveys. Doing so will fundamentally alter legislative negotiations and isolate the Democratic leadership.
  • Internal Dissension: Pay attention to the "silent majority" of backbench Democratic lawmakers. Right now, only a vocal faction defends the status quo. If approval ratings continue to slide, survival instincts will kick in, and the currently muted majority may begin openly demanding a change in course.
  • Shifts in Tone: Look for any signs that party leaders are abandoning their passive "prudence" in favor of the sharp, combative messaging that historically energizes the progressive base in South Korea.

The numbers are cold, and the public mood is growing increasingly frosty. President Lee Jae-myung’s administration cannot succeed if its supporting party remains paralyzed by internal caution and tactical timidity. The leadership now faces a stark choice: continue hiding behind empty calls for party unity as approval ratings drain away, or wake up, find their voice, and start fighting for their political future.
