Inside South Korea's Progressive Civil War: The Clash Between the 'New Lee' Faction and Traditional Supporters

September 13, 2026

Scroll through a political YouTube channel or an online community in South Korea today, and you are immediately struck by a sense of exhaustion. Spaces that were once dedicated to fierce, policy-driven debate have curdled into arenas for digital vigilantism—places where ideological purity is enforced through targeted harassment, doxxing, and online dogpiles. At the center of this transformation is a bitter internal conflict within the country's progressive camp: the uneasy marriage between the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction and the Democratic Party's traditional base. To dismiss this as a mere squabble among hyper-online partisans would be a mistake. The rift cuts to the core of South Korea’s main opposition party, raising an existential question about whether these two factions can forge a functional alliance or if they are steering toward an irreversible fracture.

The core dilemma facing progressives can be boiled down to a single, uncomfortable question currently echoing through political talk shows: Can a chemical bond ever form between the so-called "New Lee" movement and the party's historical supporters? On the surface, everyone is marching toward the same grand political goals. But look beneath the banner, and the fractures in methodology, tone, and political culture are widening by the day.

The Case for Divorce: Why Critics Argue Coexistence Is Impossible

When this question was posed on a recent political commentary program, one panelist did not hesitate. Without a second of doubt, they declared that harmony between the two groups is simply "impossible." Their diagnosis of the "New Lee" phenomenon was startlingly blunt: these are not partners for coalition, the panelist argued, but a faction that should be "expelled" from the party altogether. The grievance goes far beyond mere policy disagreements; critics view this new political current as a toxic element actively corroding the party's institutional health.

This hardline stance stems from a deep cultural disconnect. The Democratic Party's traditional base—voters who have stood by the party for decades—pride themselves on a shared history, core progressive values, and a baseline of polite, democratic discourse. To these long-haul loyalists, the rising wave of "New Lee" supporters does not look like a fresh, much-needed reform movement. Instead, it resembles a foreign, corrosive scar tissue threatening the foundations of the party.

As a result, this is no longer just a run-of-the-mill factional fight. It has become a struggle for survival over what the Democratic Party stands for. When two distinct groups sharing a single party banner can no longer even recognize each other as comrades, the warning lights are flashing red.

The Import of Toxic Political Culture

At the heart of the "incompatible" argument lies an import of foreign political tactics—borrowed, critics say, from the darkest corners of the internet. Commentators have increasingly drawn uncomfortable parallels between the online behavior of some modern progressive factions and the toxic digital culture once pioneered by far-right, reactionary Korean online communities known for systematic mockery, targeted harassment, and personal destruction.

For a political camp that has historically prided itself on self-correction and democratic ideals, the infiltration of these slash-and-burn tactics has been deeply demoralizing. Rather than trying to persuade opponents through debate or policy arguments, segments of the new political base favor silencing and burying adversaries. The traditional progressive values of procedural democracy, inclusivity, and open debate have been pushed aside, replaced by a defensive tribalism and naked hostility.

The most alarming part of this shift is that it is no longer confined to anonymous basement forums or late-night message boards. Lawmakers themselves are frequently caught winking at these digital mobs, riding the wave of online fervor rather than checking its excesses. When elected officials pander to the extremes instead of acting as a stabilizing force, a political party ceases to function as a public institution and begins to act as a hostage to its most radical fringes.

When Disdain and Derision Replace Democratic Debate

In politics, language is a window into a movement's soul. Unfortunately, the rhetoric driving the "New Lee" current is frequently soaked in mockery and contempt. Observers have noted that the aggressive language deployed online is no longer staying on social media; it is bleeding directly into the policy discussions and strategic posture of the broader progressive movement.

A healthy political party is designed to aggregate diverse interests, harmonize competing voices, and forge rational compromises. But when extreme, zero-sum logic dominates decision-making—and when mainstream lawmakers are too intimidated by their own base to speak truth to power—the party's public function hollows out. The entire ecosystem shifts toward a structure where you must demonize your opponent to survive. The irony is bitter: a political force meant to represent the public ends up amplifying societal division rather than healing it.

Progressive politics was built on the recognition of difference and procedural legitimacy. Winning by crushing your opponent only lays the groundwork for harsher retaliation down the road. If the new wave of supporters cannot see that mimicking the tactics of their political enemies makes them indistinguishable from those they claim to fight, the progressive movement risks losing its moral compass entirely.

The Road Ahead: Boundaries or Self-Destruction

The Democratic Party now faces a grueling stress test: Can it manage this internal ideological split, or will it fracture under the weight of its own contradictions? One side preaches broad-tent expansion through chemical unity, while the other warns that such unity is merely a slow suicide that sacrifices the party's identity.

Disagreements are natural in any major political coalition, but when the *methods* used to advance those disagreements become anti-democratic and rooted in cyber-harassment, the nature of the game changes. If the party leadership continues to coddle radical factions out of fear of losing short-term engagement or digital backing, it risks forfeiting its reason for existing in the first place.

🔥 Politics Fueled by Hatred Always Consumes Itself

The greatest threat to a democratic movement rarely comes from external enemies; it is the rot that takes root within. A political culture that wins by demonizing opponents, doxxing critics, and silencing debate is a disease that eats away at democracy from the inside out. Handing the keys of the party to those who weaponize malice is not coalition-building—it is capitulation.

Progressives must draw a sharp line. If the party cannot summon the courage to cut ties with those who profit off division, it will permanently squander public trust. True solidarity is forged through shared values, never through shared hatred.

What to watch for next will reveal the future trajectory of South Korea's liberal opposition:

  • Leadership Signals: Watch whether senior Democratic Party figures begin actively condemning targeted online harassment, or if they continue to court the digital influencers driving the polarization.
  • Institutional Pushback: Look for signs of pushback from veteran party members and regional chapters who feel alienated by the shift in political tone.
  • Electoral Strategy: Pay attention to how the party positions itself ahead of upcoming legislative battles—whether it doubles down on base mobilization or attempts to recapture moderate, centrist voters who are turned off by toxic political rhetoric.

Ultimately, the current debate over the "New Lee" faction and South Korea's traditional progressives is a litmus test for the country's political culture as a whole. Politics driven purely by dopamine hits and online tribalism may offer a fleeting sense of victory, but it ultimately acts as a political boomerang. Whether the Democratic Party can find its way back to a culture of reasoned debate and common sense remains the heaviest, and most consequential, unanswered question in Korean politics today.
