Cracks in the Foundation: Internal Power Struggles and the Crisis of Confidence in South Korea’s Democratic Party

September 09, 2026

Watching the latest episode of a popular political commentary program, one could feel the tension radiating through the screen. When a guest joked that “the moment you become a regular panelist on Park Si-young TV, you are branded an anti-Lee Jae-myung element,” it wasn’t just a quip to elicit a laugh. It reflected a stifling reality for those who support the current administration: the feeling that communication within the ruling party has become so rigid and hyper-politicized that even loyalists are beginning to crack under the pressure of constant internal surveillance.

This is not just about a few hurt feelings; it is a symptom of a party that has become so obsessed with internal hierarchy and the elimination of "disloyal" elements that it is losing touch with the public it was elected to serve. As the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the primary liberal party in South Korea, faces declining support, the recent spectacle of a leaked memo from a key party leader has laid bare the deep, systemic fractures within the movement.

The Memo Scandal: Power Politics in the National Assembly

On September 9, 2024, a photographer captured a glimpse of a notebook held by Kim Min-seok, a Supreme Council member of the DPK, during a plenary session at the National Assembly. The handwritten notes were damning: alongside names of political opponents, the notebook explicitly read, “Jung Chung-rae for Seoul Mayor” followed by the word “OUT.”

Jung Chung-rae, a former party leader and prominent figure, did not take this lightly. He took to social media to vent his frustration, asking why he was being "hit" with such tactics after surviving a grueling primary. The "OUT" notation triggered widespread speculation. Was this a genuine endorsement for the Seoul mayoral race, or a calculated strategy to set up an ally to fail in a notoriously difficult district—thereby clearing the board of potential rivals?

For a party that preaches cooperation, the blatant use of a strategy aimed at sidelining internal competition signaled a cold reality: the leadership is prioritizing the consolidation of power over the unity of the party. The Seoul mayoral race is a critical benchmark for the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. Using it as a chessboard to purge rivals exposes a culture where internal "purity" is monitored with the intensity of a McCarthyist trial.

The Erosion of the “We” Identity

The DPK was once a movement held together by a strong sense of collective identity. However, since the rise of the current leadership, that "we" is being systematically dismantled. A culture of censorship has taken root, where anyone who deviates from the party line is immediately branded an "anti-Lee" (ban-myung) or a "rebel."

This has manifested in bizarre, counterproductive behaviors. Recently, the party’s official YouTube channel faced backlash for uploading an AI-generated video created by a user whose profile explicitly mocked former liberal presidents. When caught, the party’s defense was not one of embarrassment or correction, but of arrogance. The official response—that the content was "high quality" and therefore justified keeping it up despite the vitriolic messaging—showed a complete disconnect from the sensibilities of the average voter.

Politics is the art of winning hearts, but the DPK currently seems to be mastering the art of alienating them. By pandering to a "core" group of extreme online supporters while alienating the broader coalition of voters who brought them to power, the party is shrinking its own tent. The irony is that in their attempt to fortify their grip on power, they are weakening the very foundation—public support—that sustains it.

The Warning Signs: Stagnant Approval Ratings

The data does not lie. Recent polling from Hangil Research shows that President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating is hovering at about 36.4%. Even more alarming is the fact that within the DPK’s own support base, the negative assessment of the President has reached 36.7%. When a party’s core supporters begin to express as much dissatisfaction as the general public, the administration is in deep trouble.

This situation mirrors the low points of the previous administration, yet the current leadership seems to be in a state of denial. Instead of acknowledging that the public is tired of arrogance, broken promises, and the lack of meaningful communication on issues like real estate policy, the party continues to blame the media or "dissident" factions for their woes. They are repeating the exact mistakes that cost the previous conservative government its mandate.

What to Watch Next

  • The "Succession" Narrative: Watch to see if President Lee Jae-myung makes a clear, public statement to end the speculation surrounding "consecutive terms" or constitutional changes. The public is currently fatigued by these rumors, and a definitive, "no-term-extension" pledge is likely the only way to stabilize the current political mood.
  • Internal Personnel Shakes: Will the party leadership finally recognize that their current communication strategy is failing? A genuine shift in cabinet or party leadership, moving away from "loyalist-only" appointments, will be the first concrete sign that the party is listening.
  • Policy Reversals: Pay close attention to the upcoming housing and real estate policies. If the administration continues to backtrack on previous reform promises, the disillusionment among their base—particularly the young voters who were once their strongest supporters—will likely become irreversible.

The DPK stands at a crossroads. They can continue to indulge in the "meme politics" of mockery and internal purges, or they can return to the basics: governing for the public good with humility and transparency. Power built on intimidation and insular strategy is a sandcastle—it looks solid until the tide comes in. Unless the party changes course, they may find that the public's patience, once exhausted, is nearly impossible to regain.
