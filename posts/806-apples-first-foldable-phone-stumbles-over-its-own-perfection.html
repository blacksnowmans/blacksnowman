Apple's First Foldable Phone Stumbles Over Its Own Perfectionism

September 18, 2026

Unfolding Apple’s debut foldable device for the first time feels a bit like stepping into the future while someone gently holds your ankles. Announced with the kind of theatrical reverence usually reserved for historical milestones, the newly released device—weighing 254 grams and starting at an eye-watering $2,400 (3.29 million won)—has sent the global tech community into a tailspin of both awe and exasperation. Apple is calling it the most significant hardware leap since the original iPhone, but early adopters pulling it out of their boxes in coffee shops and subways are finding that the hardware's sleek engineering hides a labyrinth of arbitrary software walls.

To understand the device is to understand Apple's eternal internal conflict: the desire to push the boundaries of form factor colliding headfirst with a stubborn refusal to let go of absolute control. Behind the polished hinge mechanism and the clever cover screen lies a series of design choices that ask whether a $2,400 luxury gadget should make the user bend to the software, or the other way around.

The Clever UI Solution and the Left-Handed Dilemma

When engineering its first folding screen, Apple faced a daunting geometry problem. To keep the device feeling natural on the outside, the cover screen was widened and given an asymmetrical D-shape with gentle curves. To prevent existing iPhone users from feeling lost in this new physical reality, software designers made a bold decision: they swept all core navigation elements—the Home screen dock, app navigation, and control toggles—onto the right side of the screen, merging the Dynamic Island, battery, and network icons into a single, neat circle.

This leaves the rest of the display as a pristine, familiar rectangular workspace. For a right-handed user staring at the screen, it feels seamlessly intuitive. The software behaves just like an ordinary iPhone, cleverly masked inside an entirely new piece of hardware.

The illusion shatters the moment you try to use it with your left hand. For true left-handed users, or anyone crammed into a crowded subway car holding a coffee with their right hand, the right-shifted user interface is practically out of reach. Naturally, one might ask: why not simply move the Dock to the left? Herein lies Apple's design dogma. The moment you shift the dock left, the main content is forced into the asymmetric, rounded-corner zone that Apple's perfectionism was built to avoid.

It is a stark reminder that Apple's design philosophy views physical ergonomics and user diversity as secondary to visual symmetry. Millions of left-handed users are left staring at a high-tech wall, wondering why a device costing the price of a used car assumes everyone's body works the exact same way.

Guarding the Hinge, Choking Multitasking

Unfolding the device reveals a 7.6-inch interior display that offers genuine surprises. The crease is remarkably faint, visible only when light catches it at an awkward angle, thanks to an intricate hinge system that relies on artificial intelligence algorithms to align more than a hundred tiny components with laser precision. The outer glass feels like a standard iPhone, but the inner screen features a subtle nano-texture finish that cuts down glare and feels pleasingly tactile, like an expensive e-reader.

Yet, once you dive into the expanded screen real estate—which offers 50% more area than a standard Pro Max model—the software immediately puts you in a straitjacket. Try dragging a fifth app icon into the vertical Dock, and you will find it stubbornly refuses to budge, despite plenty of empty space above and below. Apple clings to its traditional four-icon Dock limit, a relic of smaller screens that feels absurdly restrictive on a tablet-sized folding display.

The multitasking constraints don't stop there. The Split View feature, which splits the screen in two, locks you into a rigid 50-50 split. You cannot drag the border to resize the windows to your liking. While competitors like Samsung's Galaxy Z Fold series let users juggle floating windows and arbitrary split ratios, Apple forces you to stick to its prescribed proportions—presumably to keep the cover screen's UI scaling intact.

"When hardware gives you a canvas, software shouldn't hand you a ruler and tell you not to draw outside the lines."

By prioritizing developer ease and visual uniformity over user freedom, Apple has built a magnificent cage. The device's multi-tasking limitations show that the ecosystem's famous "seamlessness" can quickly curdle into a lack of user choice.

Steve Jobs' Ghost and the Half-Baked Stylus

In 2007, Steve Jobs famously mocked styluses from the stage, asking who on earth would want a stylus when God gave us ten fingers. That pronouncement became foundational dogma for the entire Apple ecosystem.

Nearly two decades later, reality has finally chipped away at that dogma. Faced with a massive folding screen, Apple has relented and added stylus support—but in the most begrudging way imaginable. The high-end Apple Pencil Pro is barred from working on the device; users must settle for the cheaper USB-C Pencil model. Even then, you are paying a massive premium for a stylus that lacks pressure sensitivity, haptic feedback, double-tap gestures, and even Apple's "Find My" tracking capabilities. It is essentially a glorified plastic stick capable of detecting tilt, and the software to support even this rudimentary feature won't arrive via update until later this year.

For early adopters shelling out thousands of dollars, being handed a stripped-down accessory months after launch is becoming an increasingly frustrating pattern.

The $2,400 Price Tag and the Missing Telephoto Lens

The financial barrier to entry is staggering. Starting at roughly $2,400 for the base 256GB model and climbing past $3,700 for the 2TB version, the device outprices nearly every competitor on the market. Yet, for all that money, compromises abound. To achieve a remarkably slim 5.2mm profile, engineers had to ditch the telephoto lens and variable aperture modules.

It creates a strange paradox: you are buying the most expensive phone Apple has ever made, yet you are accepting a camera system inferior to the standard Pro models. Sacrificing core photographic utility purely in the pursuit of thinness feels like a miscalculation for a device marketed as an ultimate productivity tool.

For international buyers, particularly in markets like South Korea, the hardware compromises are compounded by infrastructure hurdles. The device ships entirely without a physical SIM tray, forcing users onto eSIM. In regions where mobile carriers still tack on activation fees for every digital profile switch or require cumbersome customer service calls for minor changes, abandoning physical SIM cards turns what should be a two-minute setup into a bureaucratic headache.

What to Watch Next

Apple's first foldable entry is a technical marvel wrapped in software paternalism. It proves the company can build a durable, stunning folding screen, but it also exposes the limits of an ecosystem that struggles to accommodate user agency.

As this device lands in consumer hands, watch for three critical developments over the coming months:

  • Software Updates: Monitor whether Apple quietly backtracks on its rigid 50-50 split-screen rules and Dock limits in upcoming iOS patches.
  • Competitor Counter-Moves: Observe how Samsung and other Android manufacturers respond to the hardware slimness and hinge durability benchmarks set here.
  • Sales Resilience: Watch whether the $2,400+ price tag combined with missing camera hardware causes buyers to hesitate, or if the Apple logo alone is enough to normalize the compromises.

The ultimate verdict on this new form factor will not be written by tech reviewers, but by the quiet choices consumers make when deciding whether a luxury badge is worth giving up the freedom to use their own devices the way they want.
