The Erosion of Trust: South Korea’s Judiciary Under Fire

October 08, 2026

For observers of South Korean politics, the current state of the judiciary has become a source of profound disillusionment. The institution that serves as the final bastion of the rule of law—the judiciary—is increasingly viewed by the public not as a protector of the separation of powers, but as a walled fortress designed to shield its members from accountability. From the refusal of the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court to take a standard oath at a parliamentary audit to a series of scandals involving ethical lapses and preferential treatment, the perception is growing that South Korea’s legal elite considers itself above the law.

The Chief Justice’s Defiance and the Myth of Judicial Independence

The tension between the legislative and judicial branches reached a breaking point on October 6, 2026, during a national audit by the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee. Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court—South Korea’s highest judicial office—refused to take the mandatory witness oath. While his counterparts and other officials stood to swear in, Cho remained defiantly seated, later arguing that testifying under oath would undermine the principle of judicial independence and the separation of powers.

Critics, including lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party (South Korea’s liberal ruling party), were quick to point out the irony. The Chief Justice, tasked with upholding the law, set a precedent of open disregard for legal protocol. The logic—that answering questions about judicial administration somehow encroaches on independence—is seen by many as a cowardly excuse. It suggests an elitist, condescending attitude toward the public. By shielding himself behind the concept of "judicial independence," Cho is using a vital democratic principle as a convenient shield against legitimate oversight regarding his recent appointment processes and administrative transparency.

"Omakase" Scandals and the Culture of Privilege

The frustration with Chief Justice Cho extends beyond his courtroom conduct to his handling of internal ethics. During the audit, lawmaker Park Eun-jung questioned the court's use of public funds, specifically regarding "encouragement funds" and expenses for lavish meals. Cho’s defense—that he does not personally manage the cards and that the staff "wished to eat food they hadn't tried before"—rang hollow for a public grappling with economic uncertainty.

This "let them eat luxury" attitude is particularly galling given the judiciary's own history of harsh rulings against ordinary citizens. When a public servant is fired for embezzling as little as 800 won (about 60 cents), the court shows no mercy. Yet, when senior judges are accused of misusing tax dollars or accepting questionable hospitality, the standard of justice seems to shift. This double standard reinforces a growing public sentiment that the judiciary is a self-serving guild, protected by the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition), which frequently dismisses these scandals as "political attacks" rather than issues of basic fairness.

A Cycle of Impunity: When Judges Break the Law

The moral decline is not limited to the top. Recent cases involving lower-court judges have exposed deep-seated corruption. For instance, Judge Ji Ki-yeon was embroiled in a scandal involving a high-end liquor bar, where he allegedly received hospitality worth over 4 million won (about $3,000) from lawyers. Despite evidence of the meeting—which included paid hostesses—Ji dismissed the incident as a brief visit, and the Supreme Court’s disciplinary response has been widely criticized as a "slap on the wrist."

Similarly, the case of Senior Judge Kim Min-taek highlights a pattern of evidence tampering. After being investigated by customs for allegedly using a proxy to purchase luxury clothing at a 90% discount, Kim promptly replaced his mobile phone. While he avoided the most severe charges, the revelation of suspected overseas golf trips and business-class ticket gifts further stained the judiciary's reputation. In contrast to regular civil servants, who often face immediate dismissal for similar conduct, judges frequently escape with temporary suspensions or salary reductions, sheltered by the current Judicial Disciplinary Act.

The Double Standards of Political Justice

Perhaps most damaging to the public's faith is the judiciary’s handling of high-profile political cases. The acquittal of former President Yoon Seok-yeol in the second trial regarding the "Myung Tae-kyun public opinion poll" scandal—overturning a previous prison sentence—has sparked widespread outrage. The court ruled that the opinion poll data, despite having monetary value, did not constitute illegal political funding, but was merely "political assistance."

To the average citizen, this legal hair-splitting appears to be a deliberate attempt to protect powerful figures. This is particularly relevant as the same judicial panel oversaw the trial of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon. The case against Oh involves complex allegations regarding campaign finance and the use of edited transcripts. Despite compelling evidence and testimony, the court’s perceived leniency toward those connected to power stands in stark contrast to the harsh sentences handed down to ordinary citizens. If the law is applied differently based on one's political connections, the concept of "equality before the law" ceases to exist.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward the remainder of the year, several developments will signal whether the judiciary can begin to repair its severely damaged credibility:

  • KBS Leadership Change: With 28 candidates vying for the presidency of the Korean Broadcasting System (KBS), the selection process on October 20 will be a litmus test for media independence. A neutral, qualified leader is essential to restoring public trust in a media landscape currently plagued by partisan "copy-paste" journalism.
  • Judicial Reform Pressure: Increased scrutiny from the National Assembly on judicial budgets and disciplinary procedures suggests that the "sanctuary" of the judiciary will face further challenges. Watch for potential legislative pushes to reform the Judicial Disciplinary Act to ensure that judges are held to the same standards as the public they serve.
  • Public Sentiment and Accountability: The backlash against recent films and political rhetoric suggests that South Korean citizens are increasingly active in defending democratic values. Whether the courts continue to issue "lenient" rulings for political insiders will likely dictate the intensity of public protests and the pressure for institutional overhaul.

The judiciary sits at a crossroads. It can either continue to function as a closed, privileged class, or it can submit itself to the same rigors of accountability that it expects from the rest of the country. Without a fundamental shift toward transparency and equality, the current erosion of trust may lead to a permanent delegitimization of Korea's legal foundation.
