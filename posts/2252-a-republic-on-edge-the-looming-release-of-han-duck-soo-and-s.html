A Republic on Edge: The Looming Release of Han Duck-soo and South Korea's Battle Over Judicial Accountability

November 12, 2026

Inside South Korea's political and legal corridors, tension is reaching a breaking point over what critics describe as an impending rollback of justice. Han Duck-soo, who served as Prime Minister during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency, faces potential release around November 20. Han was sentenced to 15 years in prison and taken into court custody for his involvement in the December 3 martial law declaration—an episode now formally characterized by state investigations and prosecutors as an illegal insurrection. Yet, rather than serving as the definitive closing chapter on the illicit power grab, his trial has exploded into an existential conflict between the ruling progressive bloc and the nation's highest court.

For international observers watching Seoul navigate the turbulent aftermath of Mr. Yoon's impeachment and subsequent imprisonment, this standoff reveals the fragile equilibrium of South Korea's democratic institutions. At the heart of the storm sits Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the Supreme Court of Korea. Progressive lawmakers and civic groups charge that the apex court is deliberately running out the clock to grant safe passage to figures involved in the insurrection, setting up an unprecedented legislative showdown: whether to move forward with the impeachment of the Chief Justice himself.

The Double Standard: Why Han's Release Sparks Fury

The controversy surrounding Han Duck-soo is not merely about whether an elderly former senior official should receive bail or see his detention suspended. In the eyes of his detractors, freeing a man convicted of a 15-year offense strikes at the core of accountability for the December 3 crisis. Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok of the Democratic Party of Korea—the nation's current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung—has framed the prospective release as nothing short of a "declaration of war" by the judiciary against the public.

Critics argue that the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Cho has operated with glaring asymmetry. During the 2025 presidential election cycle, the Supreme Court demonstrated astonishing bureaucratic velocity, reviewing a massive case file of approximately 70,000 pages in just nine days to overturn an appellate ruling that targeted political figures. In stark contrast, when tasked with addressing the legal aftermath of the December 3 insurrection, judicial proceedings have slowed to a crawl. In one notable instance, a trial handling these grave constitutional crimes convened only once over a three-month span.

This sluggish pace has fostered an environment where defendants appear emboldened. Observers note that figures implicated in the insurrection have entered courtrooms smiling, waving, and signaling hearts to supporters as if celebrating a political victory rather than answering for subverting the constitution. For the ruling party and civil society, this optics disaster reflects a deeper reality: defendants believe the upper echelons of the judiciary remain sympathetic to the conservative establishment from which they were appointed.

The Squeeze on Lower Courts and the Supreme Court En Banc

The institutional friction is not purely ideological; it is playing out in bitter procedural maneuvers within the judiciary itself. One of the few judges praised by government watchdogs for strictly enforcing accountability is Presiding Judge Lee Jin-kwan. However, lawmakers warn that Judge Lee has been systematically sidelined by the Supreme Court’s procedural foot-dragging.

A prime example involves former Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister Choi Sang-mok. A formal recusal motion filed to remove Judge Lee from hearing Choi's case has languished at the Supreme Court for more than five months. Under standard judicial practice, such motions are resolved within about a month. Lawmakers, including Representative Kim Hyung-yeon of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, argue that the five-month delay is a deliberate maneuver to paralyze Judge Lee's bench and send a chilling signal to other lower-court magistrates: handle the insurrection cases too aggressively, and your courtroom authority will be neutralized.

An even more contentious maneuver occurred in August, when the Supreme Court abruptly transferred Han Duck-soo’s case to the En Banc division (Jeonwonhab-uiche)—a full-bench panel composed of all Supreme Court justices, reserved for foundational questions of law. By law, the final verdict should have been delivered by early August. By kicking the case to the full court after the statutory deadline, the Supreme Court opened a legal backdoor to re-examine the core arguments behind Han's conviction.

Legal analysts following the case warn that the full bench could attempt to parse Han's actions—such as presiding over Cabinet meetings to grant a veneer of administrative legitimacy to the martial law decree—as separate from the violent mechanics of an insurrection. If the court rules that lending bureaucratic legitimacy does not constitute direct complicity in rebellion, the 15-year sentence could disintegrate. To critics, this is an attempt by judicial elites to rewrite the narrative of December 3, transforming an armed attempt to overthrow democratic rule into a mere procedural irregularity.

The Democratic Party’s Indecision and the Threat of Impeachment

Despite possessing legislative dominance, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea finds itself paralyzed by internal division over how to respond. The debate centers on two competing options: launching a parliamentary investigation (gukjeong josa) into judicial misconduct, or drafting articles of impeachment against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

Frustration among progressive backbenchers is boiling over. Representative Lee Sung-yoon publicly admonished his own party leadership, observing that "the Democratic Party becomes timid and overly cautious whenever it stands before the courthouse doors." Some within the party fear an electoral backlash, while others worry that provoking the courts could imperil separate, ongoing legal proceedings involving political figures. However, hardliners argue that playing defense has already cost them valuable time. Lawmakers like Kim Byung-joo point out that the party has repeatedly missed opportunities to rein in Chief Justice Cho's court, from early controversies over judicial interference to contentious judicial appointments.

While the Rebuilding Korea Party has spent 15 weeks mobilizing street protests and demanding an unyielding response, the Democratic Party's leadership has oscillated between tentative rhetoric and inaction. But with Han's release looming, patience is wearing thin. The consensus among the party's legal committee members is hardening around a blunt ultimatum: if the Supreme Court frees the former Prime Minister on November 20, the National Assembly must immediately send Chief Justice Cho to face trial at the Constitutional Court.

External Pressures: Jim Jordan and the Economic Front

This domestic institutional warfare arrives at an acutely dangerous moment for President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, which is simultaneously confronting sharp geopolitical headwinds from Washington. The political landscape in the United States has shifted dramatically with the election of Representative Jim Jordan, an Ohio Republican and staunch ally of Donald Trump, as the House Republican Majority Leader.

Jordan has long signaled a confrontational stance toward Seoul. As Chairman of the House Judiciary Committee, Jordan spearheaded a scathing report titled Blocking Competition: Korea’s Discriminatory Assault on American Businesses. The document alleged that South Korean regulators unfairly target American digital platform companies—most notably the e-commerce giant Coupang—while steering market advantages toward domestic enterprises and Chinese joint ventures. Jordan's posture, combined with his past backing of steep 25 percent tariffs on South Korean manufactured goods, signals that Washington's conservative establishment is preparing to exert fierce economic leverage.

The broader volatility within the U.S. political sphere—highlighted by the razor-thin 211-to-204 House vote referring articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee—underscores an unpredictable international environment. If South Korea appears consumed by internal constitutional paralysis, with senior figures of an insurrection walking free while the legislature wars against the top court, Seoul’s diplomatic and economic bargaining power on the global stage will be severely diminished.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether South Korea can consolidate its post-martial-law democratic recovery or descend into a prolonged constitutional impasse. Key markers to monitor include:

  • The November 20 Ruling on Han Duck-soo: Whether the Supreme Court approves Han's release or drastically modifies the terms of his 15-year sentence will serve as the immediate trigger for legislative retaliation.
  • The Democratic Party’s Floor Decision on Cho Hee-dae: If Han is freed, watch whether the Democratic Party caucus unites behind a formal motion to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, or defaults once again to a non-binding parliamentary inquiry.
  • The Supreme Court’s Ruling on Recusals: Any movement on the five-month stall regarding Choi Sang-mok's recusal motion will indicate whether the top court intends to continue insulating defendants from Judge Lee Jin-kwan’s bench.
  • Trade and Regulatory Signaling from Washington: With Jim Jordan commanding House floor leadership, observe whether U.S. congressional committees initiate formal trade inquiries or tariff threats targeting South Korea’s digital economy and industrial exports.
