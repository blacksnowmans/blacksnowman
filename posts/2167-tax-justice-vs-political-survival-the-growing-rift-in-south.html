Tax Justice vs. Political Survival: The Growing Rift in South Korea’s Ruling Party Over Property Taxes

November 10, 2026

Imagine you are a middle-class homeowner in Mapo or Yongsan, two sought-after districts in Seoul. Over the last decade, you haven't moved, your salary has grown modestly, but the market value of your apartment has nearly doubled. Under South Korea’s unique tax system, you aren't just paying standard property tax; you are increasingly caught in the net of the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (CRET), known locally as Jongbuse. Originally designed as a "wealth tax" for the top 1% of property owners, Jongbuse has become a source of immense anxiety for hundreds of thousands of residents in the Seoul metropolitan area as housing prices soared.

Inside the halls of the National Assembly, lawmakers from the Democratic Party (DP), South Korea's ruling liberal party, are feeling that anxiety firsthand. With a general election on the horizon, these politicians are hearing a singular message from their constituents: "Fix the tax or lose our votes." This has triggered a high-stakes confrontation between the party’s leadership and the Blue House—the executive office of President Lee Jae-myung. While the party is pushing for a dramatic increase in tax deductions to soothe angry voters, the Lee administration is digging in its heels, citing the principles of tax justice and the need to claw back "unearned income."

The $1 Million Threshold: A Dilemma of Numbers and Principles

The current flashpoint centers on a proposal to raise the Jongbuse deduction for single-home owners to 1.4 billion KRW (approximately $1.05 million). Currently, the threshold stands at 1.1 billion KRW (about $825,000). While a $225,000 difference might seem technical, it represents a massive shift in how many people are hit by the tax. More controversially, the Democratic Party is suggesting this deduction should apply to all single-home owners regardless of whether they actually live in the property or just own it as an investment.

This "unified 1.4 billion KRW deduction" is a radical departure from the party’s traditional stance. For years, the liberal platform has emphasized that tax breaks should be reserved for those who actually reside in their homes, punishing those who own property purely for speculation. By removing the "actual residency" requirement, the party is essentially signaling a retreat from its reformist roots. Within the party, the move is seen as a necessary survival tactic. Lawmakers from the Seoul metropolitan area are warning that without a clear and substantial tax cut, the party faces a total wipeout in the upcoming elections.

However, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is reportedly cold toward the idea. For President Lee, whose political brand is built on "fairness" and the aggressive redistribution of wealth, backing down on property taxes feels like a betrayal of his core mandate. The Blue House has officially stated that the tax threshold was "not a topic of discussion" in recent meetings, a polite way of telling the party to stop pushing. The administration fears that weakening the tax now would send a green light to speculators, potentially reigniting the very housing bubbles they have worked to stabilize.

Survival Instincts in the Seoul Metropolitan Area

To understand why the Democratic Party is willing to fight its own president, one must look at the electoral map. South Korea's elections are won and lost in the "Sudo-gwon"—the massive metropolitan area comprising Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province, which houses half the country's population. In these districts, Jongbuse is no longer a "rich person’s tax"; it is a "middle-class headache."

A group of 16 influential Democratic Party lawmakers has recently formed a "Real Estate Pragmatism Group." Their mission is simple: pivot the party away from punitive regulations and toward market-friendly relief. These members argue that the party’s previous ideological rigidity on housing is what led to the public's frustration. They point to the anger of voters who feel they are being "fined" with taxes simply because the government failed to control housing prices in the first place.

"Principles are empty echoes if they ignore the public's will," says one faction within the party. This group is planning a major forum on November 11th to advocate for a total shift in real estate policy. They are not just asking for a tax cut; they are asking for a change in the party’s soul. They want to move from "punishing" homeowners to "stabilizing" their lives. If the party leadership fails to adopt this pragmatic line, many fear the internal rift could become an unbridgeable chasm, leaving the party fractured as they face a challenge from the People Power Party (PPP), the country's main conservative opposition.

The Ghost of the Past: Moving Away from the Yoon Era

Part of the Blue House's resistance is rooted in the recent history of Korean politics. The administration remains wary of repeating the mistakes of the previous period. During the time of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was eventually impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence—unregulated tax cuts for the wealthy were blamed for a massive "tax revenue hole" and deepening social inequality.

President Lee Jae-myung and his advisors view Jongbuse not just as a revenue source, but as a symbolic pillar of a fair society. They argue that the revenue from this tax is a vital source for regional balancing funds, helping to develop poorer provinces outside of Seoul. To them, the Democratic Party’s current proposal looks dangerously similar to the "rich-friendly" policies they once criticized Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol for.

Progressive civil society groups and media outlets like Hankyoreh and Kyunghyang Shinmun have also joined the fray, accusing the ruling party of "shaking the foundations of tax justice" for the sake of a few votes. They argue that if the Democratic Party abandons its commitment to taxing property wealth, it loses its identity as a party for the common person. The dilemma is stark: win the election by appealing to the Seoul middle class, or keep the party’s ideological purity and risk losing power entirely.

Market Confusion and the Crisis of Trust

The most immediate victim of this "Party-Blue House" discord is the real estate market itself. Markets loathe uncertainty, and right now, the signal from the government is a cacophony of contradictions. One day, a senior lawmaker promises a $1.05 million deduction; the next day, a Blue House official denies it. This "revision of the revision" approach has left potential buyers and sellers in a state of paralysis.

When policy fluctuates based on the daily political wind, the public stops trusting the government’s long-term plans. This often leads to a "wait-and-see" approach, where homeowners refuse to sell their properties in hopes of a better tax deal later, further squeezing the housing supply and driving prices up—the exact opposite of what the tax was intended to do. For a government that came to power promising to fix the "unfairness" of the housing market, this perception of flip-flopping is a serious blow to its credibility.

Furthermore, the fiscal reality is daunting. Experts warn that raising the deduction to 1.4 billion KRW would lead to a loss of several trillion won (billions of dollars) in tax revenue. In an era where the government is trying to expand social welfare programs, losing this revenue without a clear replacement plan is a significant risk. The Democratic Party has yet to explain how they will fill this gap, leading to accusations that they are engaging in "populist math."

What to Watch Next

The clock is ticking. The Democratic Party has set the fourth week of November as their "deadline" to pass the new tax legislation. This is because tax bills for the year are typically sent out in late November, and the party wants to ensure homeowners see the relief in their actual bills before the year ends. Here are the key developments to watch for:

  • The "Third Way" Compromise: Watch for a deal where the 1.4 billion KRW threshold is accepted, but with strings attached. This might include a "differential deduction" system where those who actually live in their homes get a larger break than those who don't. This would allow the Blue House to claim it is still protecting "tax justice" while giving the party the headline number they want.
  • The November 11th Forum: The meeting of the 16 "pragmatist" lawmakers will be a bellwether for the party’s internal balance of power. If they successfully sway the party leadership, the pressure on the Blue House will become almost unbearable.
  • Opposition Strategy: The People Power Party (PPP) is currently watching the ruling party's infighting with interest. They are likely to propose even more aggressive tax cuts to "out-bid" the Democratic Party, forcing the ruling party to decide how far they are willing to go toward the right.
  • Revenue Projections: Keep an eye on reports from the Ministry of Economy and Finance. If they release data showing a catastrophic drop in revenue from the proposed changes, it will give the Blue House the ammunition it needs to veto or stall the plan.

Ultimately, this is more than just a debate over property taxes. It is a test of the Lee Jae-myung administration’s ability to lead its own party and a preview of the ideological battle that will define the next general election. Whether the party chooses "principles" or "votes" will determine the trajectory of South Korea’s housing market for years to come.
