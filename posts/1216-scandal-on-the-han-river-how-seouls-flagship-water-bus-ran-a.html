Scandal on the Han River: How Seoul's Flagship Water Bus Ran Aground in Kickbacks and Cover-Up Claims

October 16, 2026

Under the blinding glare of television cameras inside the National Assembly's audit chamber, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon found himself cornered. Lawmakers thrust precise dates, internal municipal logbooks, and sworn testimonies in front of the high-profile conservative politician, demanding an explanation for what happened to a damning corruption dossier that had vanished inside Seoul City Hall. To each pointed question about an alleged million-dollar bribery scheme at the heart of his prized transit project, Mayor Oh and his top deputy offered variations of the exact same defense: they simply could not recall.

The controversy centers on the Han River Bus, an ambitious public ferry network designed to turn the wide waterway cutting through the capital into a bustling commuter artery. Launched with grand promises of easing Seoul’s notorious road congestion, the project has instead become a political lightning rod. What began as public skepticism over technical viability and vessel safety has metastasized into allegations of structural corruption: an illicit money trail involving a private boatbuilder, an intermediary broker, kickbacks allegedly pocketed by an executive at a municipal development corporation, and an aggressive effort by city leadership to sweep internal findings under the rug.

The $1.1 Million Paper Trail and the Municipal Housing Agency

From the moment Seoul announced the water bus project, industry observers and municipal council members questioned how contracts were handed out. The contract to manufacture the river vessels was awarded to a domestic builder named Gadok Heavy Industries. But beneath the routine public procurement filings, city auditors later stumbled upon a far murkier transaction.

According to Seoul Metropolitan Government internal investigative findings, a suspicious sum of roughly 1.5 billion won (about $1.1 million) flowed out of the project funds disbursed to Gadok Heavy Industries. Rather than paying for ship parts or engineering labor, this money traveled through an external subcontracting service provider called Seongwoo ENG, which allegedly functioned as an illicit broker. Most explosive of all, the city's internal inquiry determined there was a high probability that a senior official at the Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation (SH Corporation)—the municipal agency responsible for urban development and public housing—received a portion of those funds as a kickback.

In South Korea’s public works sector, where municipal corporations manage massive civic budgets, kickbacks funnelled through intermediary service firms represent a classic playbook for illicit enrichment. But this was not merely a case of an isolated bureaucrat taking a bribe. The internal discovery ignited panic at the very top of municipal government, setting off what critics argue was an orchestrated campaign to bury the truth before the boats ever took on their first paying passengers.

Missing Memos and a Race Against the Ferry Timetable

The timeline of how City Hall handled its own explosive discovery is at the center of the current political firestorm. On September 9, 2025, Park Jae-yong, then the head of Seoul’s municipal Audit Committee, delivered a closed-door, in-person briefing directly to Mayor Oh Se-hoon. Sitting alongside the mayor was Jeong Sang-hoon, then serving as the city’s First Administrative Vice Mayor. During that meeting, the watchdog chief laid out the irregularities in how Gadok Heavy Industries was selected, alongside the evidence pointing to broker commissions and illicit kickbacks.

The findings were severe enough that by approximately September 12, an official criminal investigation referral—a formal document requesting a police or prosecutorial probe—was drafted for submission. Under Article 7 of the Seoul Metropolitan Government’s official regulations governing public servant criminal referrals, all documentation concerning suspected job-related crimes must be archived and maintained, even if an agency head decides not to immediately refer the matter to law enforcement.

Yet that referral never reached the police. Instead, allegations surfaced that the draft and its supporting evidence were slated for destruction. On September 30, the head of the audit committee reportedly ordered staff to scrap all related files, including the formal investigation referral.

Why would City Hall take the perilous legal risk of destroying public records? The answer lies in the unforgiving calendar of city politics. Mayor Oh had staked substantial personal capital on launching the Han River Bus on September 18, 2025. When the newly launched vessels immediately suffered embarrassing mechanical breakdowns, service was hastily halted on September 29 for passenger-free test runs, with a firm promise to resume full operations on November 1. A full-blown criminal bribery investigation breaking open at that exact juncture would have dealt a devastating blow to the city administration. For City Hall leadership, keeping the transit launch on schedule appears to have taken precedence over pursuing criminal corruption.

'Selective Amnesia' Under the Parliamentary Spotlight

When the National Assembly convened its annual state audit, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea pounced. Representatives Park Ju-min and Kim Young-bae methodically walked Mayor Oh and Vice Mayor Jeong through the dates, demanding to know who gave the order to shelve the criminal probe.

The response from the city's top two administrators was a uniform retreat into memory loss. Questioned about what transpired during the September 9 closed-door briefing, Vice Mayor Jeong insisted he could not clearly recall the conversation, adding that he honestly had no memory of whether he ordered the investigation referral to be drafted. Mayor Oh struck the identical stance, declaring that he had no recollection of being briefed on the formal referral document around September 12.

Pushed harder during afternoon questioning, Mayor Oh shifted tactics. Rather than acknowledging a substantiated internal investigation, he dismissed the bribery allegations as nothing more than unverified gossip:

"This was not an established matter, but merely an incident based on rumors and unsubstantiated allegations."

It was a jarring defense. The "rumors" in question included documented financial flows of $1.1 million, internal audit interviews, and a drafted criminal complaint. Mayor Oh further claimed that the city had sought an audit from the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), South Korea’s premier national state auditor, assuming national investigators would handle it. But the BAI dismissed the city's audit request on the very day it was filed. Under normal administrative protocol, such an immediate dismissal requires the municipal government to mobilize its own inspectors or immediately notify prosecutors. Instead, Seoul City Hall did nothing for weeks, only conceding under parliamentary grilling that it would consider filing a complaint "sometime soon."

Shallow Waters, Rescues, and Semantic Gymnastics

As the legal maneuvering played out behind closed doors, real-world hazards were mounting on the river itself. Official municipal maritime records show that the Han River Bus fleet and related vessels repeatedly ran aground in shallow sections of the riverbed.

The safety record between late 2025 and 2026 proved alarming:

  • November 15, 2025: Vessel No. 102 of the Han River Bus fleet struck the shallow riverbed near the Jamsil dock, stranding the boat and forcing emergency responders to rescue 82 passengers.
  • March 28, 2026: The river cruise vessel EQ2 (Love Cruise) ran aground downstream of the Banpo Bridge in shallow water, triggering a massive emergency operation to evacuate all 359 passengers on board.

Rather than pausing operations to address dredging depths and navigational safety, City Hall turned its attention to public relations management. Mayor Oh’s office circulated an official directive to Seoul's autonomous district offices titled "Establishment of Han River Vessel Accident Manual and Strict Adherence to Terminology." The document explicitly instructed municipal staff and press officers never to use the standard Korean word for running aground or shipwrecking (jwacho) in official records or media releases. Instead, officials were ordered to substitute the obscure maritime technical term for touching bottom without hull rupture (jwaju).

The semantic dodge was transparent. To an ordinary citizen reading the morning news, a "grounding" evokes imagery of danger, mismanagement, and maritime catastrophe; the clinical, unfamiliar label was intended to soften public alarm. But altering bureaucratic vocabulary did nothing to change the underlying physical reality: heavily loaded passenger ferries were repeatedly getting stuck on mudbanks in one of the capital's busiest waterways.

What to Watch Next

The parliamentary clash has transformed the Han River Bus from a local transit experiment into a full-scale legal and political vulnerability for Mayor Oh Se-hoon. With the conservative People Power Party now in the parliamentary minority and facing a central government led by Democratic Party President Lee Jae-myung, Seoul City Hall cannot rely on political insulation from above.

Three key developments will determine where this scandal heads next:

  • Formal Police and Prosecutorial Probes: Now that the existence of the drafted criminal referral and the $1.1 million transaction via Seongwoo ENG has been entered into the public record, civic watchdog groups and opposition lawmakers are preparing formal criminal complaints alleging dereliction of duty and destruction of public records against senior city officials.
  • Forensic Audits of the Audit Committee: Scrutiny will focus heavily on whether digital files, emails, and physical memos detailing the September 2025 investigation were unlawfully purged, which carries severe criminal penalties under South Korea’s public records management laws.
  • Operational Viability of the Fleet: With shallow-water groundings already triggering mass evacuations, maritime safety regulators will face intense pressure to review whether the river's draft depths can safely support high-frequency passenger transit without costly, continuous dredging.

If the paper trail proves that Seoul City Hall prioritized a ribbon-cutting photo opportunity over safety and financial integrity, Mayor Oh's carefully cultivated image as a polished, competent administrator may find itself stranded in the very shallows that trapped his boats.
