The Great Divergence: Analyzing South Korea's "First-Generation" New Town Real Estate Market

November 10, 2026

If you were to look at the South Korean property market today, you would see a tale of two realities. In the "First-Generation New Towns"—the massive residential districts built on the outskirts of Seoul in the 1990s—the market has fractured. On one hand, you have regions like Sanbon and Jung-dong, where transaction volume is surging as buyers scramble for affordability. On the other, you have Bundang and Pyeongchon, where trading has slowed to a crawl, yet property prices remain stubbornly, almost aggressively, high.

This is not a simple case of "up or down." It is a manifestation of a market reacting to the conflicting signals of the current administration’s push for housing stability and supply expansion, pitted against tightening loan regulations. For those watching the market, it is vital to understand that the days of blanket trends are over; today, the market is defined by extreme polarization.

The Sanbon-Jung-dong Surge vs. The Bundang Price Wall

The most striking indicator in recent data is the explosion of transaction volume in Sanbon. Reports show a staggering 108% increase in trading activity compared to previous periods—nearly double. This stands in sharp contrast to Bundang, which saw a 41% decrease in transactions. This disparity confirms that as loan regulations tighten, real buyers are retreating to more affordable, "non-regulated" areas. It is an act of "survival buying" for the younger 20s and 30s demographic, who view these districts as their last realistic foothold in the housing market.

However, while Sanbon and Jung-dong are seeing the volume, the "prestige" districts of Bundang and Pyeongchon are holding the price lines. In Bundang, prices have jumped by about 35%—an overwhelming figure for a 1st-generation new town. Even with trading volume effectively halved, prices are rising because, quite simply, there are no sellers, but plenty of buyers waiting in the wings. This is a classic "blue-chip" market pattern where the value of infrastructure and proximity to jobs—long established in Bundang—prevents any price correction.

Meanwhile, Ilsan continues to languish, highlighting a deeper structural problem. While Bundang benefits from the massive job hub of Pangyo, Ilsan has struggled to shed its identity as a mere "bedroom community." This divergence serves as a bitter lesson in urban planning: without sustainable economic engines, proximity to Seoul is not enough to maintain long-term property value.

Administrative Innovation and the Speed of Redevelopment

One of the greatest historical bottlenecks in South Korean redevelopment has been "administrative drag." Traditionally, a project might have to clear hurdles across 21 different government departments, with each step offering new opportunities for delay. Recently, however, the Dongjak District Office has implemented a "one-stop" system, consolidating all permit consultations into a single department. This reflects the administrative efficiency prioritized by the current government under President Lee Jae-myung, aimed at accelerating housing supply.

Furthermore, the decision to transfer permit authority for 213 redevelopment and reconstruction sites in Seoul from the central government to local district offices is a watershed moment. By empowering local officials—who better understand the specific needs of their neighborhoods—the government is attempting to move past the rigid, one-size-fits-all zoning and height restrictions that defined the era of the previous administration, specifically the period under the now-imprisoned former leader, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The goal is to move from bureaucracy-heavy processes to a more nimble, local-centric model. However, the challenge for the Lee Jae-myung administration will be ensuring that this autonomy does not lead to reckless development or cozy ties between local officials and construction firms. Maintaining public interest while increasing speed will be the true test of this policy.

The Burden of Construction Costs and the "Reconstruction Windfall Tax"

The euphoria of new projects is often tempered by the harsh reality of costs. Samsung C&T’s selection as the contractor for the Mokdong Complex 13 project—a massive 2.3 trillion won (about $1.65 billion) deal—signals that the market for major reconstruction is heating up again. Yet, this comes with a heavy price tag. The "Reconstruction Excess Profit Recovery" tax—a levy designed to curb speculative gains—is hitting some projects with staggering bills. For instance, the Banpo Hyundai project was recently hit with a levy of about 220 million won (about $158,000) per member.

This tax has become a flashpoint. While intended to prevent "unearned income" for speculators, it is often viewed as a punitive tax on long-term, single-home resident owners. As projects across the country—from Seoul to Daejeon—grapple with these costs, the government faces a dilemma: if the tax is too high, projects collapse, and housing supply disappears. If it is too low, it invites the return of the unrestrained, speculative frenzy that characterized the failed housing policies during the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

Safety, Accountability, and What Lies Ahead

Not all sites are success stories. In Daegu, there are 77 redevelopment sites that have stalled before construction even began, leaving behind vacant lots and ghost-like structures. These sites represent a safety crisis and a failure of oversight. These are the "forgotten" corners of the real estate market, where the dream of new housing has turned into an urban blight that threatens public safety.

Looking ahead, the market will likely be defined by three key factors:

  • The "Selective" Market: Expect the gap between well-connected districts (Bundang) and transit-dependent, budget-friendly districts (Sanbon/Jung-dong) to persist. The era of "rising tides lifting all boats" is over.
  • Administrative Efficiency: Keep a close watch on whether other districts follow Dongjak’s lead. The speed of supply will depend entirely on whether the "one-stop" administrative model becomes the new standard across Seoul.
  • The Regulatory Tightrope: The Lee Jae-myung government must find a middle ground on the Reconstruction Excess Profit tax. How they balance the need for profit-taking prevention with the reality of crushing construction costs will determine whether the current wave of reconstruction projects succeeds or stalls.

For the average buyer, the advice remains the same: stop looking for the general "market trend." The market is now a series of micro-climates. Whether a project succeeds or fails will depend more on local administrative cooperation and the specific fiscal burden of the individual project than on the broader national economic outlook.
