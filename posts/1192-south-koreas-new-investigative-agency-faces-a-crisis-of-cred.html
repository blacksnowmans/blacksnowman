South Korea’s New Investigative Agency Faces a Crisis of Credibility Before Its First Case

October 16, 2026

Inside the hearing rooms of South Korea’s National Assembly, confirmation hearings rarely fail to produce drama. But the recent questioning of Kim Ji-yong, tapped to lead the country’s newly established Major Crimes Investigation Agency, felt less like an audition for a fearless reformer and more like an exercise in bureaucratic evasion. For hours, lawmakers probed Kim’s past role in one of the most politically charged prosecutions in recent memory, only to be met with shifting recollections, absent meeting minutes, and carefully choreographed non-answers. For an agency created to symbolize the ultimate break from South Korea’s legacy of politicized justice, the spectacle was an alarming start.

To understand what is at stake, one must understand the unique shadow the state prosecution service has cast over South Korean democracy. For seven decades, Korean prosecutors possessed an almost unprecedented combination of powers: the exclusive authority to indict, direct police investigations, and initiate their own criminal probes into political figures and conglomerates. Under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the long-sought institutional separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers culminated in the creation of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency. Designed to strip direct investigative authority from the state prosecution, the new body was supposed to usher in a neutral, transparent era. Instead, the fight over its inaugural director suggests the old habits of the prosecution apparatus are merely migrating under a new roof.

The Memory Hole: How Case Approvals Exposed Bureaucratic Complicity

The central confrontation during the hearing focused on Kim’s conduct during a bitter 2020 scandal involving prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong. At the time, Jeong was pursuing an investigation that touched high-ranking prosecutor Han Dong-hoon. During a physical search, an altercation occurred, leading to Jeong himself being indicted for physical assault during the execution of official duties—a charge widely criticized by legal observers as a retaliatory, politically driven strike designed to protect one of the prosecution’s own.

Before the parliamentary committee, Kim initially insisted that he had firmly opposed indicting Jeong. However, documents obtained by Democratic Party lawmaker Yoon Kun-young painted a starkly different reality. In his written review at the time, Kim had laid out legal arguments for pursuing charges under "occupational negligence causing injury." While some commentators noted that substituting negligence for intentional assault was technically less severe, it nevertheless provided the institutional rationale needed to put Jeong on trial.

Furthermore, records revealed that when the original junior prosecutor on the case hesitated to sign off on the indictment, the case was reassigned to a divisional chief. Such a reassignment required formal approval up the supervisory chain—an approval stamped directly by Kim. In the hierarchical world of Korean justice, signing off on a case reassignment is not an administrative formality; it represents official consent to the direction of an inquiry. Confronted with this paper trail, Kim retreated into classic bureaucratic semantics, shifting from flat denial to claiming a foggy recollection.

This tactic—known colloquially in Korean legal circles as acting like a "law eel" by slippery maneuvering through loopholes—points to a deeper institutional malady. Under South Korea's Public Records Management Act, key administrative conferences and high-level meetings involving vice-ministerial officials must maintain verbatim transcripts or audio recordings to guarantee public accountability. Yet Kim maintained that internal deliberations on the indictment left behind no formal dissenting opinions, no minutes, and no formal paper trail. By declining to create a written record of objection while privately letting an unjust prosecution proceed, Kim demonstrated the classic survival instinct of the prosecutorial bureaucracy: protect the organization, protect yourself, and leave no fingerprints.

A Reform Agency in Danger of Becoming a Figurehead Operation

The broader critique leveled against Kim’s nomination is that he lacks the fierce independence required to challenge the entrenched interests he is meant to police. Critics argue that he resembles a compliant manager rather than an independent leader—a figure capable of bending whichever way political power blows. The structural architecture of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency, which places it under the supervisory sphere of the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, was intended to balance power. However, placing a classic institutional insider at its helm risks reducing the director to a compliant placeholder while established factions pull strings behind the curtain.

The danger is that the bad habits forged during the presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ousted via impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence—could easily reconstitute themselves. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, the prosecution service became heavily factionalized, with internal rivalries and targeted probes destabilizing public trust. Simply moving investigative desks from the Supreme Prosecutors' Office to a new agency achieves nothing if the personnel driving it are steeped in the same culture of self-preservation and backroom maneuvering.

This institutional rot is exacerbated by what observers call the "complaint business" in Korean politics. When public officials or whistleblowers—such as reform-minded prosecutor Im Eun-jung—fall out of favor, opposing interest groups routinely file criminal complaints, which are then routed straight into specialized anti-corruption divisions. Trivial disputes and internal policy disagreements are weaponized into criminal investigations. If the head of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency cannot show the spine needed to reject manufactured cases and preserve records, the new body will inevitably be dragged into the same partisan trench warfare.

Phantom Cults and AI Witch Hunts: Self-Inflicted Wounds in the Majority

While the opposition People Power Party and civil society scrutinize the new investigative leadership, the ruling Democratic Party has found itself distracted by internal conspiracy theories. A recent controversy sparked by politician Kim Min-seok alleged that members of Shincheonji—a controversial religious sect that faced heavy public scrutiny during the COVID-19 pandemic—were covertly infiltrating the party ranks. The proposal to deploy "artificial intelligence screening" and full-scale audits of hundreds of thousands of party members was met with incredulity.

From a technical standpoint, filtering political party registries via automated keyword algorithms to identify religious affiliations is impossible; from a constitutional standpoint, attempting to weed out citizens based on private religious faith breaches fundamental democratic rights of political participation. Senior figures within the Democratic Party, including supreme council member Lee Sung-yoon and former supreme council member Jung Chung-rae, publicly pushed back, calling the initiative a dangerous and divisive campaign that damages the party's moral high ground.

The dispute carries an uncomfortable sense of deja vu. It closely mirrors factional tactics from the 2021 presidential primary, when the camp of rival contender Lee Nak-yon attempted to taint then-candidate Lee Jae-myung with cult associations. Reheating these factional purges during a pivotal phase of legal and institutional reform is an exercise in political self-sabotage. While ordinary citizens look to the ruling majority to steady the economy and complete judicial overhauls, the spectacle of lawmakers chasing phantom religious infiltrators only erodes public confidence.

What to Watch Next

The controversy surrounding the Major Crimes Investigation Agency’s leadership is far from settled. As the process moves forward, several concrete milestones will reveal whether meaningful reform is still viable:

  • The Legislative Confirmation Report: Watch whether Democratic Party lawmakers attach formal dissenting opinions to the confirmation report regarding Kim Ji-yong's contradictory statements on the 2020 indictment records, or if the administration proceeds with his appointment regardless.
  • Record-Keeping Protocols: Scrutinize the internal operating charter established by the new agency. If binding requirements for recorded transcripts and written dissents are not instituted from day one, it will signal that the agency intends to keep investigations shielded from public scrutiny.
  • Early Jurisdictional Clashes: Observe how the Major Crimes Investigation Agency interfaces with the existing prosecution service on high-profile corporate corruption and political cases. Whether it aggressively asserts its statutory jurisdiction or quietly defers to senior prosecutors will determine if it is an independent watchdog or a hollow facade.

Institutional reform cannot be accomplished merely by redesigning organizational charts or swapping office signs. If the leadership of South Korea's premier investigative body remains bound to the unwritten codes and secretive habits of the old prosecution service, the public promise of genuine judicial reform will remain unfulfilled.
