Beyond Foreign Sell-Offs: How Chips, Pricing Power, and Domestic Cash Rescued Korea’s Benchmark Market

November 02, 2026

When the opening bell rang on Monday morning in Yeouido, Seoul’s financial heart, the mood across trading desks was thick with dread. Following a rocky session on Wall Street and compounding macroeconomic uncertainties, the KOSPI—South Korea’s benchmark stock index—opened at 5,637 points. Retail investors, anxious after a weekend dominated by dire market forecasts, hovered over their sell buttons, bracing for a painful session.

Then, the script flipped entirely. Instead of collapsing under the weight of international liquidations, the KOSPI climbed relentlessly throughout the day, touching 5,900 points before closing at 5,859—a single-day surge of 128 points, or 2.24 percent. Investors who gave in to weekend panic were left watching a classic contrarian rally unfold. Heavyweights in electric electronics and automotive led the charge, while beaten-down cyclical sectors such as construction, shipbuilding, logistics, transport equipment, and machinery experienced sharp elasticity. Defensive plays like banking, insurance, and food and beverage lagged, confirming a classic rotation into oversold value. Meanwhile, the tech-heavy KOSDAQ index gained 9.98 points (1.38 percent) to close at 734, playing catch-up behind large-cap chipmakers.

Old market adages often prove their worth during times of peak emotional distress: buy when pessimism is suffocating, and exercise caution when optimism is unanimous. Monday's dramatic reversal served as a textbook reminder of how price bottoms are forged precisely when crowd terror reaches its peak.

The Mechanics Behind KOSPI's Defiant Rally

The most intriguing aspect of Monday's price action was not just the magnitude of the rally, but the underlying capital flows. For decades, an unwritten rule has governed Korean equities: when foreign investors buy, the market rises; when they sell, it falls. Yet on Monday, the market staged a massive 2%-plus rally while foreign funds maintained a resolute selling stance. Who drove the resurgence?

A closer look at domestic institutional books reveals that South Korea’s massive National Pension Service (NPS) played a minimal role. Instead, the heavy lifting came from domestic securities brokerages and private equity funds. Spotting valuations that had become ridiculously depressed after a short-term sell-off, these institutional players aggressively stepped in to buy the dip. In a market frequently buffeted by the mechanical rebalancing of global passive exchange-traded funds (ETFs), domestic institutions stepping up as a structural shock absorber is a notable development.

Furthermore, heavy foreign selling does not necessarily signal a collapse in corporate fundamentals. Global funds operate under strict mandates regarding single-stock concentration limits and MSCI benchmark weightings. When a key stock rallies rapidly or an asset class reshuffles, systematic passive strategies are legally obligated to trim holdings regardless of long-term earnings potential. Savvy domestic capital recognized that foreign outflows were driven by mechanical portfolio adjustments rather than structural flaws, sweeping up discounted shares at the bottom.

Meanwhile, the foreign exchange and bond markets painted a more complex picture. South Korea’s 10-year Treasury yield settled quietly at 4.03 percent. However, currency markets presented a stark divergence: while the Korean Won (KRW) remained stuck in a rut of persistent weakness against the US dollar, the Chinese Yuan (RMB) staged a dramatic surge, pushing the RMB/KRW exchange rate up to 202 Won per Yuan.

Charts of the Dollar/Yuan pairing show an unnatural, heavily suppressed sequence of red candles, fueling widespread suspicion that the People's Bank of China (PBOC) is aggressively intervening via daily fixing rates and capital controls. Analysts remain divided on Beijing’s true motive: whether the Chinese authorities are trying to cushion domestic purchasing power amid a sluggish home economy, or making a tactical gesture of currency appreciation to defuse trade tension with the US and Europe. Despite domestic demand woes, China racked up a record trade surplus exceeding $1.2 trillion last year. With international bodies like the IMF estimating the Yuan to be undervalued by 16 to 20 percent, Beijing appears to be prioritizing financial stability over pure export-dumping.

The Korean Won’s failure to track the Yuan’s strength highlights a painful vulnerability in the domestic economy: persistent capital flight as local retail traders—often dubbed "Seohak ants" for their aggressive buying of overseas assets—funnel money into foreign equities, exposing a lag in local economic stamina.

Why Semiconductor Fundamentals Are Sharper Than Wall Street Thinks

Fresh trade statistics from South Korea’s Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy (MOTIE) offer a concrete look at the nation’s economic backbone: semiconductors. South Korea delivered stellar overall trade numbers for October, breaching $110 billion in monthly exports for the second consecutive month and maintaining a trade surplus above $40 billion, putting a historic $1 trillion annual export target within reach. Automotive exports generated $6.5 billion, petroleum products brought in $6.9 billion, and petrochemicals added $4.0 billion.

Crucially, semiconductor exports hit $56.4 billion. While slightly below September’s record-breaking figure above $60 billion due to seasonal holiday scheduling, the underlying structural metrics tell a powerful story. NAND flash memory alone accounted for $5.9 billion of the total.

The defining takeaway from the October trade data lies in pricing rather than sheer volume. Semiconductor export volume actually declined by 3.1 percent year-over-year, yet total export value exploded because export unit prices surged by nearly 100 percent. In short, Korean fabrication plants shipped fewer physical chips, but collected nearly double the cash. This pricing power illustrates why investors examining tech giants like Samsung Electronics must look past raw inventory headcounts and focus instead on pricing leverage in high-value products like High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) and enterprise solid-state drives (eSSD).

Consider the disconnect surrounding Samsung Electronics. The tech powerhouse recently reported staggering quarterly performance, posting 133 trillion KRW (about $98 billion) in revenue and 57 trillion KRW (about $42 billion) in operating profit, alongside a massive 30 trillion KRW (about $22 billion) dividend allocation. Yet foreign investors dumped trillions of Won in Samsung shares, keeping the stock suppressed. Why?

Equity markets are forward-looking mechanisms; past earnings reports are old news. Global investors feared a "peak-out" in legacy commodity DRAM and NAND memory, questioning whether booming AI server demand could truly compensate for sluggish consumer purchases of smartphones and PCs. But October’s export data proves these fears were blown out of proportion. Rather than crashing, memory prices are staying elevated as manufacturers pivot capacity away from legacy chips toward HBM4 and enterprise storage, preserving strong supplier pricing power.

This thesis is supported by research from KB Securities analyst Lee Chang-min, who directly refutes claims of an "AI capital expenditure bubble." Lee argues that massive data infrastructure investments by global tech firms are not speculative mania, but an urgent operational scramble to keep pace with exploding global AI workloads. As a result, the gap between record chipmaker earnings and depressed stock valuations must eventually close. Lee projects a powerful multi-year upward cycle starting in earnest, naming Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix alongside key supply-chain beneficiaries like equipment leader Hanmi Semiconductor and foundry specialist DB HiTek.

Long-term equipment spending curves reinforce this view. Capital expenditure across the world’s top five semiconductor equipment makers—ASML, Applied Materials, Tokyo Electron, Lam Research, and KLA—froze during the severe 2023 downturn and showed only modest recovery through 2025. However, revenue forecasts for these equipment giants curve upward into 2026. Given the extreme engineering complexity of modern manufacturing nodes, actual physical chip output will remain tightly constrained until late 2027. Consequently, 2026 is shaping up to be the absolute peak of memory supply tightness and pricing power.

Big Tech's AI Divide and the Global Infrastructure Bottleneck

Beyond South Korea's borders, global corporate earnings reveal a widening divergence among tech giants investing in AI. In the third quarter, Meta reported a remarkable 26 percent year-over-year surge in digital ad revenue. By contrast, Google’s total ad revenue growth managed just 12 percent—marking the widest growth gap between the two rivals since 2021.

The reason for this divide comes down to how AI interacts with each company's core business. Meta successfully integrated its open-source Llama AI models into its ad targeting and content recommendation engines, driving conversion rates higher for advertisers. For Meta, AI serves as an operational engine that breaks its dependence on third-party mobile platform rules. For Google, generative AI presents a structural dilemma: as AI overviews directly answer user queries, users click fewer sponsored search links, creating a cannibalization effect on Google’s core search business. This forces Google to spend aggressively on AI infrastructure simply to defend its legacy moat.

Yet as Big Tech pours capital into computing hardware, physical infrastructure is hitting a wall. Across North America, the rapid proliferation of AI data centers is straining municipal power grids and consuming vast quantities of water for cooling, sparking intense community backlash over rising household utility bills. Ahead of upcoming regional elections, major candidates in more than half of US states have adopted platforms opposing or delaying data center construction.

Ironically, these regulatory and power bottlenecks create immense scarcity value for companies that have already secured real estate, power grid hookups, and supply chains. For South Korea’s tech sector, maintaining a steady expansion of data infrastructure capacity—around 20 gigawatts annually—will be critical to absorbing global hardware demand without suffering domestic grid bottlenecks.

The resilience of digital infrastructure was illustrated in another sector by the recent security breach at Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange. Following a high-profile exploit, Binance lifted withdrawal freezes and weathered an immediate $4.65 billion (roughly 6 trillion KRW) capital outflow in just four days without halting operations. Binance absorbed the shock by promising 100 percent compensation to affected users out of its corporate reserves while working with industry peers to freeze approximately $3 billion in stolen assets. The episode underscored how high-margin digital platforms possess massive internal liquidity that acts as an informal backstop during market volatility.

Geopolitical Shifts and Korea's Low-PBR Corporate Reckoning

In international macroeconomics, unexpected pragmatic deals are beginning to surface. Reports indicate that US representatives—including figures like Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff—have floated proposals to resume the transit of Russian natural gas to Europe using existing pipelines running through Ukraine.

The economic logic behind this proposal is straightforward: restoring pipeline natural gas (PNG) flows to energy-starved European industrial centers would sharply reduce natural gas prices, dampening European inflation and allowing central banks to proceed with aggressive interest rate cuts. While politically jarring given the ongoing geopolitical conflict, it highlights how harsh economic realities—specifically energy inflation—can force pragmatic recalibrations in global trade routes.

Back home, domestic market reform is taking a firmer stance. Starting this November, the Korea Exchange (KRX) initiated a formal public classification program targeting chronically undervalued companies. Listed firms in the bottom 25 percent of Price-to-Book Ratios (PBR) on the main KOSPI board, and the bottom 10 percent on the KOSDAQ, were officially flagged if they failed to submit corporate "Value-Up" action plans aimed at improving shareholder returns.

The resulting list of 151 non-compliant, low-PBR companies includes major industrial names: retail giants E-Mart (PBR 0.15) and Lotte Shopping (0.17), petrochemical anchor Lotte Chemical (0.18), steel manufacturer Hyundai Steel (0.19), insurer Hanwha Life (0.27), alongside regional brokerages like DB Financial Investment (0.33) and Hyundai Motor Securities (0.31). By publicly identifying companies trading well below their net liquidation value, market regulators under the current Lee Jae-myung administration are escalating pressure on corporate boards to address the persistent "Korea Discount" through dividends, share cancellations, and operational restructuring.

What to Watch Next

  • Semiconductor Price Realization: Monitor monthly export figures from MOTIE to confirm whether memory chip unit pricing continues its upward trajectory into early next year, validating chipmaker margin expansion despite flat volume growth.
  • Corporate Value-Up Compliance: Watch how the 151 flagged low-PBR companies respond to the Korea Exchange’s public designation. Moves by corporate boards to announce share buybacks or capital efficiency plans could trigger sharp revaluations across legacy retail, chemical, and financial sectors.
  • Foreign Exchange Divergence: Keep a close eye on the RMB/KRW pair and PBOC daily fixings. Persistent weakness in the Korean Won alongside a managed Yuan will test the input cost margins of domestic Korean manufacturers.
  • 2026 Equipment Order Lead Times: Track order books at major fab equipment suppliers (ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research). A rise in tool bookings will provide early confirmation of the anticipated 2026 structural supply deficit in memory.
