The Mayor’s Gavel and the Receding Hairline: Navigating South Korea’s Crisis of Identity and Power

October 26, 2026

In the quiet, high-end clinics of Gangnam and the echoing halls of the Seoul Metropolitan Government, two very different types of survival struggles are unfolding. For the average resident, the battle might be found in the mirror, counting the strands of hair lost to a "10-million hair loss era"—a staggering statistic in a country of 51 million. For the political elite, the struggle is for the survival of leadership in the capital. As the city waits for a high-stakes court ruling on Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, the anxiety of losing power mirrors the personal desperation of those losing their hair: both are struggles over self-esteem, legacy, and the fear of an uncertain future.

To live in modern South Korea is to exist in a state of hyper-competition where appearance is often treated as social capital and political stability is a rare commodity. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the focus has shifted toward social welfare and the protection of the vulnerable, yet the "shame" of hair loss remains a stubborn psychological barrier. Simultaneously, the legal fate of Mayor Oh Se-hoon threatens to create a massive power vacuum in the heart of the nation. Whether it is the redistribution of follicles or the redistribution of political authority, Koreans are currently obsessed with "management"—the art of preventing a total collapse before it’s too late.

The Legal Precipice of the Seoul Mayoralty

The atmosphere around Seocho-dong—Seoul’s central legal district—and Seoul City Hall has turned frigid as the sentencing date for Mayor Oh Se-hoon approaches. This is not merely a trial for an individual; it is a watershed moment for the leadership of South Korea’s most powerful municipality. Oh Se-hoon, a prominent figure in the People Power Party (PPP), the nation’s main conservative opposition, faces charges that could strip him of his office. Legal experts are skeptical about the possibility of an acquittal, noting that unless the judiciary "closes its eyes to the facts," a guilty verdict seems inevitable based on the current evidence.

The shadow of the past looms large over this case. Many recall the aggressive prosecution of former Prime Minister Han Myeong-sook, and there is a sense that unless the court makes a purely political calculation, the law will dictate a harsh outcome. Evidence suggests that during the last presidential election cycle, Oh was already setting up campaign offices and mobilizing supporters, actions that critics say reveal a hunger for power that bypassed legal boundaries. For the Mayor, the number "1 million" has become a terrifying wall; under South Korean law, if an elected official is fined more than 1 million won (about $720) for an election law violation, they are immediately stripped of their position.

If Oh is forced out, Seoul will be plunged into a by-election. Such an event would likely galvanize President Lee Jae-myung’s Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), strengthening the current administration's mandate to pursue reforms that have been stalled since the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The belief that "justice must prevail" is not just a partisan hope; it is a reflection of a public sentiment that is increasingly weary of elite exceptionalism. A by-election in Seoul is not just a local vote; it is a referendum on the direction of the entire country.

The Shadow Candidates and Internal Betrayals

As the possibility of a by-election grows, potential successors are already moving. Rep. Park Ju-min, a prominent lawmaker within the ruling Democratic Party known for his reformist stance and "lawyer for the people" image, has signaled his readiness. While he admits that many in his circle are cautious, his supporters see him as the right person to clean up what they describe as the "exhibitionist administration" of Oh Se-hoon, specifically criticizing projects like the "Hangul Bus" and other costly riverfront developments that critics say favor optics over the actual welfare of citizens.

Interestingly, the most dangerous threats to Oh Se-hoon may come from within his own camp. Rumors are swirling that segments of the People Power Party are secretly hoping for Oh’s downfall. The logic is Machiavellian: a by-election would allow the conservative party to reset its leadership and potentially find a fresh face to challenge the DPK in the next general election. Some insiders claim that "everyone in the PPP is praying for a by-election," viewing Oh’s potential exit as a necessary sacrifice for the party's long-term survival. This internal power struggle highlights a conservative movement still in disarray following the collapse of the previous regime under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, where personal ambition often outweighs party loyalty.

A Nation of 10 Million: The Science of the Receding Line

While the politicians fight for their seats, millions of Koreans are fighting for their hair. According to Dr. Han Sang-bo, a leading hair transplant specialist, hair loss in modern Korea is a "cold reality" dictated 80% to 90% by genetics. This means that for the vast majority of the "10-million hair loss population," the condition is a biological blueprint inherited from their parents rather than a result of poor hygiene or bad luck. Remarkably, the demographic is shifting; hair loss is no longer just a "man’s problem," as the number of women seeking treatment has surged to nearly half of all clinic visits.

Koreans often turn to folk remedies—shampoos, black beans, or scalp massages—hoping to defy their DNA. However, experts warn that this is like "building a sandcastle against a tsunami." The biological mechanism involves testosterone converting into DHT (dihydrotestosterone), which attacks the hair follicles. Without medical intervention, lifestyle changes do little to stop this hormonal onslaught. The acceptance of hair loss as a "manageable medical condition" rather than a shameful secret is a significant cultural shift, yet the psychological toll remains immense in a society that prizes youthful vigor.

Dietary habits are also playing a destructive role. Dr. Lee Sun-min, a family medicine specialist, points to extreme weight loss trends as a major culprit. When the body is deprived of nutrients, it prioritizes survival over aesthetics. The hair follicles are the first to be "laid off" by the body's energy management system. For many young Koreans, the price of a slim figure is a thinning scalp, leading to a tragic paradox where they lose their self-confidence in the pursuit of beauty. This nutritional deficiency prevents the synthesis of keratin, effectively putting hair production on strike.

Of Cats, Chemists, and the Myth of Black Beans

For those seeking a medical solution, the drug Minoxidil remains the gold standard. Originally developed for high blood pressure, its side effect of hair growth turned it into a multi-billion dollar industry. It works by improving peripheral blood flow to the follicles. However, its use comes with a warning that is particularly relevant in pet-loving Korea: Minoxidil is highly toxic to cats. A pet owner who applies the liquid to their scalp and then lets their cat lick their hair or sleep on the same pillow could inadvertently kill their pet. The drug causes heart failure and low blood pressure in felines, making it a "lethal poison" for the household’s furry companions.

Furthermore, the pharmaceutical market is flooded with supplements like beer yeast and biotin, but experts like pharmacist Jeong Jae-hun are quick to debunk the hype. While beer yeast is rich in amino acids, it only helps those who are severely protein-deficient. For the average Korean who eats a balanced diet, these supplements offer "zero benefit" for hair growth. Biotin deficiency is also extremely rare in modern society. Much of the "hair growth supplement" industry is driven by commercial marketing rather than clinical data. Investing in proven medications is far more effective than chasing the "black bean myth" that suggests eating dark-colored foods will magically darken and thicken one's hair.

The "Active Senior" trend has also reached the hair loss clinics. Men in their 70s and 80s, who are staying socially active and even dating, are now frequent customers. They are often prescribed combination pills that mix hair loss medication with ingredients found in erectile dysfunction drugs like Viagra. This "dual-track" treatment aims to restore both the hair on their heads and their masculine confidence. In an aging society, maintaining one's appearance is increasingly seen as a competitive necessity rather than vanity.

Redistributing the Wealth: The Mechanics of Hair Transplants

When medication fails, the final frontier is the hair transplant. Dr. Han Sang-bo describes the procedure not as a "creation of new hair," but as a "redistribution of assets." He likens it to taking wealth from a prosperous district and moving it to a struggling one. The "donor site" is almost always the back of the head, where hair is naturally resistant to DHT. The success of the transplant depends entirely on the quality and density of this "back-of-the-head capital."

The surgery itself has become surprisingly routine. Patients often spend the four-hour procedure chatting with the doctor, making phone calls, or even eating ramen provided by the clinic. In the competitive world of Korean medical tourism, "hospitality" like a hot bowl of noodles is part of the service. However, the one thing money cannot buy is someone else's hair. Due to immune rejection, transplants must be autologous—using your own tissue. This makes your own follicles your most precious physical asset.

The future, however, looks toward "hair farming." Scientists are currently working on technology that would allow them to extract just 100 hairs and culture them in a lab into 5,000 or 10,000 follicles. This "cloning" technology has been in development for 20 years and is reportedly close to a breakthrough. If successful, the "redistribution" model will be replaced by a "growth" model, where the supply of hair becomes infinite. This would effectively end hair loss as a social concern, much like how vaccines ended smallpox.

A Note on the Intersection of Power and Follicles: There is a poetic irony in the doctor's description of moving hair as "collecting taxes from the back to spend on the front." Just as Seoul’s political map may be redrawn by a court's decision, the individual’s physical map is redrawn by a surgeon's hand. In both cases, the goal is to maintain a facade of stability and strength in a world that is constantly eroding it.
