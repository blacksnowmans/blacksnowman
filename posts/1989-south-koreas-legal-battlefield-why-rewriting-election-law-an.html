South Korea’s Legal Battlefield: Why Rewriting Election Law and Targeting the Chief Justice Go Hand in Hand

November 05, 2026

In South Korea’s National Assembly, high-stakes political battles frequently spill out of committee hearing rooms and into the courts. But the latest confrontation reaches deeper into the architecture of the state itself. Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are simultaneously pushing to rewrite a controversial clause in the country’s Public Official Election Act and mounting an unprecedented drive to hold Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae accountable through a formal parliamentary investigation and possible impeachment.

To an outside observer, amending an election statute and threatening to impeach the head of the judiciary might look like separate, run-of-the-mill skirmishes between rival parties. Inside South Korea, however, they represent two fronts in a single, fundamental struggle: deciding where legitimate judicial oversight ends and dangerous overreach begins in a constitutional democracy.

The “Poison Clause” That Puts Political Memory on Trial

At the center of the legislative debate is a specific provision within South Korea’s Public Official Election Act: the criminal ban on “publishing false facts regarding actions.” Under current law, political candidates can face severe criminal penalties not merely for fraud or defamation, but if prosecutors decide that a subjective claim, an explanation of past conduct, or a memory shared on the campaign trail was factually inaccurate.

In South Korea’s fiercely competitive campaigns, this clause has turned the courts into an everyday political arbiter. Instead of voters assessing an opponent’s platform, integrity, or rhetoric at the ballot box, campaigns and state prosecutors routinely comb through transcripts, looking for a misplaced word or differing recollection to build an indictment. For reformers, this has created a chilling effect that strikes at the core of democratic debate.

The problem is structural. Because the boundary between subjective interpretation and willful falsehood is inherently blurry, the statute grants state prosecutors an extraordinary amount of discretionary power. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was removed from office following his impeachment and is currently incarcerated—prosecutors frequently faced accusations of selective, targeted indictments aimed at sidelining political rivals. When campaign statements become matters for the criminal code, the judiciary ceases to be a neutral forum and becomes the ultimate gatekeeper of electoral politics.

Reformers inside the Democratic Party argue that the true test of a political assertion belongs to the electorate, not a prosecutor’s indictment sheet. Striking the clause on "actions" from the law is framed not as a favor to politicians, but as an overdue step to normalize basic freedom of expression during elections and stop the relentless criminalization of political speech.

Beyond Collusion Claims: The Race Against Final Verdicts

Whenever amendments to the Public Official Election Act surface in the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee, South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, along with conservative media outlets, quickly sound the alarm. Their primary charge is that modifying the statute is a self-serving fix—a political maneuver designed to grant legal immunity to key ruling-party figures.

The mechanics of South Korean criminal law explain why the timing is so contentious. Under South Korean jurisprudence, if a penal provision is abolished while a case is still pending, the prosecution typically ends in an acquittal or dismissal of the indictment (known as myeon-so), because the underlying conduct is no longer a crime. Opponents argue this would disrupt ongoing legal proceedings, including trials connected to the aftermath of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure or past legal challenges surrounding President Lee Jae-myung.

Proponents of the bill reject this framing as bad-faith obstruction. They argue that when a statute has served as an instrument of judicial overreach, the National Assembly has an institutional duty to fix it, regardless of whose trials might be affected. The fact that pending cases would be dismissed under a corrected statute merely underscores that the original clause was defective to begin with.

Crucially, reform advocates—such as Democratic Party lawmaker Lee Yong-woo—insist that the National Assembly must act before courts deliver final, irrevocable verdicts. If the legislature waits until final rulings come down and then changes the statute, it will inevitably face harsher accusations of retroactively crafting law for specific individuals. Party leadership, including Democratic Party head Kim Min-seok, is being urged to treat this window as a golden hour: passing the amendment now directly cures the legislative flaw rather than attempting messy cleanup after the fact.

Judicial Arrogance and the Case Against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

While the National Assembly wrestles with the election code, tensions between the legislature and the Supreme Court of Korea have reached an unprecedented boiling point. Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae now faces calls for a National Assembly parliamentary inquiry and an impeachment motion, driven by accusations that the judiciary under his leadership has treated itself as an untouchable constitutional sanctuary.

Critics argue that the Supreme Court has abandoned the delicate system of checks and balances that binds the executive, legislative, and judicial branches. Invoking judicial independence, the court stands accused of operating as an unchecked authority that resists all democratic oversight while directly challenging legislative prerogatives and executive governance under the Lee Jae-myung administration.

The most explosive legal charge against Chief Justice Cho centers on the violation of procedural constitutionalism: allowing or directing trials to be conducted by individuals who lack legitimate judicial authority (심판권 없는 자). In constitutional law, a court's authority rests entirely on lawful jurisdiction and properly constituted panels. Entrusting or delegating judicial rulings to parties without legitimate jurisdictional mandate is not a routine bureaucratic mistake; it strikes at the bedrock of due process and renders the resulting verdicts constitutionally illegitimate.

For reform-minded lawmakers and legal observers, allowing such administrative shortcuts or jurisdictional overreaches to stand sets a dangerous precedent. If the head of the highest court fails to uphold the lawful boundaries of judicial authority, constitutional accountability—beginning with the National Assembly’s power of impeachment—becomes the only remedy left.

Why a Parliamentary Inquiry Is the Next Front Line

Historically, the South Korean judiciary has operated behind thick walls, handling internal scandals and allegations of misconduct through internal reviews that critics dismiss as self-protecting wrist-slaps. It has never faced an exhaustive, mandatory inquiry led by an outside constitutional body.

That dynamic is shifting. Progressive commentators and public platforms—from independent media voices like Kim Ou-joon’s Humility is Hard News Factory and anchor Lee Jae-seok to reporters like Min Dong-ki, Lee Hyun-hwa, and Cho Yoon-ju—have steadily built public momentum for breaking through the Supreme Court’s institutional insularity. Democratic Party figures argue that the National Assembly must deploy its full investigative powers to expose how irregular jurisdictional decisions were made inside Cho Hee-dae's court and determine who approved them.

Rather than viewing an impeachment push as a partisan attack, proponents frame it as a long-overdue constitutional check. The trauma of the Yoon Suk-yeol era—during which close alignments between senior prosecutors and judicial factions created a weaponized legal system—convinced many in the ruling coalition that systemic judicial democratization cannot be achieved through polite consensus alone.

What to Watch Next

As the political calendar advances, this two-track confrontation will test the durability of South Korea's institutional guardrails. Concrete milestones will determine how this battle plays out:

  • The Legislation and Judiciary Committee vote: Watch whether the Democratic Party uses its legislative strength to swiftly advance the Public Official Election Act amendment through committee, specifically removing the clause on false facts regarding candidates' actions before pending appeals conclude.
  • Opposition resistance: Track how the conservative People Power Party responds. Will it mount filibusters or legal challenges alleging that the reform is tailored to shield ruling-party figures?
  • The formal launch of a parliamentary inquiry: Monitor whether the ruling coalition introduces a formal motion for a National Assembly investigation into the Supreme Court’s jurisdictional irregularities, which would mark the first time the modern South Korean judiciary is subjected to a public legislative probe.
  • The threshold for impeachment: Keep an eye on whether Democratic Party leadership formally drafts an impeachment resolution against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, a step that would trigger a high-stakes review before the Constitutional Court and plunge the country into uncharted constitutional territory.

South Korea’s democracy has repeatedly reshaped its presidency and parliament through the ballot box and peaceful mass mobilization. The question now is whether the same democratic pressure can successfully rewrite the laws of campaign speech and bring the country’s most insulated institution—the judiciary—under constitutional accountability.
