South Korea’s New Property Tax Overhaul: Who Wins and Who Loses?

September 07, 2026

Picture sitting at your kitchen table with a stack of tax documents, watching the rules governing your largest asset shift overnight. That is the reality facing property owners in South Korea following the government's sweeping tax overhaul. Whether you own a home, rent, or hold multiple properties, the changes are rewriting the financial rules of everyday life in a country where real estate has long been the primary vehicle for household wealth.

To understand why these reforms are causing such intense anxiety—and triggering frantic moves across the housing market—we need to look past the political rhetoric and examine how South Korea uses the tax code to force a fundamental behavior change among its citizens.

The Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax: Winners and Loses

Relief for Primary Homeowners, Pressure on Luxury Properties

The most significant change in the new tax framework is the raising of the basic tax exemption for primary homeowners who actually live in their properties. The threshold jumps from 1.2 billion won (about $870,000) to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million). In practical terms, this means owners of homes valued on the market at roughly 2 billion won (about $1.4 million) are largely shielded from the country's heavy Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax, because once the official appraisal value is offset by the 1.4 billion won deduction, their taxable base drops to zero.

However, this relief does not extend across the board. For ultra-luxury homes with market values exceeding 3.3 billion won (about $2.4 million), the tax burden is actually increasing. Combined with a fixed fair-market value ratio of 70%, the tax rate for portions of the taxable base exceeding 600 million won climbs from 1% to 1.3%. The state is drawing a hard line: moderate homeowners get breathing room, but the truly wealthy face stricter enforcement.

The Trap for Non-Resident Single Homeowners

While primary residents breathed a sigh of relief, owners of single properties who do not live in them faced a turbulent ride. Initial government proposals threatened to slash the basic deduction for non-resident homes from 1.2 billion won down to 900 million won (about $650,000). The public backlash was immediate. Homeowners flooded public forums with appeals, arguing they weren't speculators—many had simply moved closer to aging parents or shifted locations for child-rearing, leaving their original homes vacant or rented out.

Faced with mounting pressure, the government backed down, restoring the deduction for non-resident single homes to 1.2 billion won. Yet, this was merely a return to the status quo, not an expansion of benefits. The underlying message from Seoul is unmistakable: if you own a home but do not live in it, prepare to pay, or move in. The policy bias heavily favors actual physical occupancy.

The Complex Mathematics Punishing Multi-Property Owners

For individuals holding multiple properties, the tax calculation is deliberately punitive. The formula subtracts the appraised value of a primary residence from the total portfolio value, divides it by the number of properties, and applies a complex weighting system. Exemptions range from 400 million won to 900 million won, but the outcome is clear: the more properties you own, the heavier the tax penalty.

Furthermore, owners of three or more properties face a higher fair-market value ratio of 80% rather than 70%. Combined with lower deductions, their tax liabilities compound exponentially. This creates a forced choice for investors: absorb the mounting tax bills or liquidate assets and flood the market with inventory.

Capital Gains and Long-Term Holding Deductions

The Two-Year Residency Rule Divides Owners

The overhaul did not stop at holding taxes; capital gains taxes underwent an equally dramatic restructuring. While the capital gains tax exemption for primary homeowners with properties valued under 1.2 billion won remains intact, the emphasis on the actual duration of residency has intensified sharply. A "holding period" now carries less weight than a "residency period."

For non-resident single homeowners, the requirement to fulfill a strict two-year residency period to qualify for tax exemptions has been finalized. The policy rationale is brutally transparent: if you want tax-free gains when selling, move into the house yourself. Those who fail to meet this requirement must choose between paying hundreds of thousands of dollars in taxes or evicting current tenants to move in—igniting a fresh wave of disruption in the rental market.

The Countdown Before Deductions Shrink

Adding urgency to the market, the government scheduled significant reductions to the long-term holding special deduction, scaling it back sharply by 2028 and 2029. Historically, a home that appreciated by 3 billion won could see up to 80% of those gains deducted, easing the tax blow. Under the upcoming rules, those deductions will be heavily capped.

This creates a clear deadline for wealthy owners and investors: sell before the deductions diminish. Consequently, many high-value and multi-property owners are treating 2027 and 2028 as the ultimate window to offload inventory before the tax penalties reach their maximum potential.

How the Market is Responding on the Ground

Polarization: Gangnam Cools While Outer Districts Surge

The policy shock registered immediately in transaction data. High-end apartments in Seoul’s affluent Gangnam district took an immediate hit, with some luxury units seeing asking prices drop by millions of dollars as leveraged owners rushed to offload properties they could no longer afford to hold.

Conversely, mid-to-lower-priced areas experienced a surge. Districts across Seoul and commuter cities in Gyeonggi Province saw sharp weekly price jumps as frantic buyers rushed into the market. This surge was driven not by speculators, but by exhausted renters pushed out of high-end areas, scraping together maximum allowable loans to secure modest housing near schools and transit lines.

The Disappearing Jeonse Rental Supply

The rental market, specifically South Korea's unique *jeonse* system—where tenants pay a large lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent—is facing a severe crisis. Rental supply has plummeted due to a drop in new construction starts combined with government policies stripping non-resident owners of their tax protections.

As landlords reclaim properties to satisfy residency requirements, tenants are left with fewer options. With jeonse prices climbing and inventory shrinking, desperate renters are being forced into the purchasing market, feeding a self-reinforcing cycle of rising housing demand.

Survival Strategies for a Volatile Market

Navigating this transformed landscape requires cold calculation rather than emotional reactions:

  • For Homebuyers and Renters: Avoid panic buying during sudden market surges. Evaluate personal cash flow strictly, and for renters, check lease expiration dates early—do not assume a landlord will automatically renew a contract when residency pressures are mounting.
  • For Primary Homeowners: The relief up to 1.4 billion won provides security against immediate holding taxes, but owners should plan ahead for future residency requirements and deduction rollbacks.
  • For Investors: The window to restructure portfolios is narrowing. Decisions must be made regarding whether to absorb rising tax burdens or liquidate before capital gains deductions shrink further.

Ultimately, South Korea's tax overhaul delivers a stark philosophical message: real estate is no longer viewed as a passive investment vehicle, but as a space meant strictly for physical habitation. For ordinary citizens caught in the crossfire, survival depends not on debating the fairness of government policy, but on reading the rules clearly and protecting one's financial footing.
