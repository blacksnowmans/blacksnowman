Why Foreign Money Is Fleeing Seoul—and Why South Korea’s Stock Crash Is Not What It Seems

November 16, 2026

By noon on November 16, 2026, South Korea’s trading screens looked less like a routine market correction and more like a liquidity freeze. The benchmark KOSPI index tumbled 2.63 percent intraday to 5,154 points, while the tech-heavy KOSDAQ plunged 3.63 percent to 674.0. With the South Korean won under pressure near 1,335 to the U.S. dollar, foreign investors unloaded more than 1.3 trillion won (about $974 million) on the main board and over 200 billion won ($150 million) on the KOSDAQ—marking a second straight session of trillion-won sell-offs.

The panic was compounded by an eerie silence on the buy side. During South Korea’s bull runs, daily trading value on the main exchange regularly hit 40 trillion to 50 trillion won ($30 billion to $37.5 billion). Recently, turnover has fallen below 20 trillion won ($15 billion), shrinking to just 16.7 trillion won ($12.5 billion) in the previous session—roughly a third of normal liquidity. In a market hollowed out to that degree, sustained foreign selling acts like an avalanche.

What rattled investors even more, according to a pointed market analysis circulating in Seoul, was the response from regulators following a high-level weekend policy meeting. Even as trillions of dollars in market value evaporated and fears of forced margin liquidations spread, the chairman of the Financial Services Commission (FSC)—South Korea’s top financial regulator—dismissed calls to activate the government’s Stock Market Stabilization Fund, describing it as a tool reserved only for “very temporary and exceptional circumstances.”

“It sounds to me like talking about farming when a war has broken out. Flaming arrows are flying in, and they’re asking how we’re going to plant seeds and farm right now.”

Yet beneath the surface of Seoul’s worst market anxiety in years lies a counterintuitive reality. According to quantitative market data cited in that Korean commentary, roughly 200 trillion won ($150 billion) of the 210 trillion won ($157 billion) foreigners have pulled from South Korean stocks this year was not a vote of no confidence in Korea Inc., but the result of an automated index rule—colliding head-on with a shockwave from Silicon Valley and decades-old corporate governance flaws at home.

An Optional Market Without a Safety Net

To understand why Seoul bleeds so quickly during global tremors, foreign observers first have to look at South Korea’s precarious place in institutional portfolios. For global pension funds and asset managers, U.S. tech giants such as Nvidia, Apple, and Microsoft are mandatory holdings; running a major equity fund without them is virtually impossible. South Korea enjoys no such privilege. Its weighting in the MSCI Emerging Markets Index has steadily shrunk, leaving it as an expendable tactical bet rather than a core allocation.

As the Korean market commentary bluntly puts it, foreign investors have no obligation to own Korean equities—it is merely one option among many. A past study by the state-run Korea Development Institute (KDI) underscores this vulnerability: when global macro shocks hit or liquidity tightens, foreign institutions do not carefully sort through balance sheets. They sell whichever large-cap markets are deepest and easiest to exit. Because South Korea’s investor registration system—in place since the market opened to foreigners in 1992—publishes daily foreign flow data in real time, Seoul effectively doubles as a transparent ATM for global hedge funds.

That structural fragility is precisely why South Korean market veterans are furious over the FSC’s reluctance to deploy—or even verbally commit—the Stock Market Stabilization Fund. Korea’s own financial history suggests that bold policy signaling matters far more than the cash actually spent:

  • 2003 credit card crisis: An emergency injection of 400 billion won (roughly $300 million at current exchange rates) across late February and March helped establish a firm market bottom.
  • 2008 global financial crisis: Deploying 515 billion won (about $386 million) sparked a 17 percent KOSPI rebound within a month.
  • 2020 pandemic crash: Merely announcing a 10 trillion won ($7.5 billion) stabilization fund triggered a 30 percent V-shaped recovery in a single month, without a single won being spent.
  • 2022 Legoland crisis: After Gangwon Province defaulted on asset-backed commercial paper guarantees and rattled sovereign-level bond credibility, announcing the reactivation of a 10 trillion won fund spurred a 5 percent rebound in a month and halted systemic contagion.

Critics argue that leaving the stabilization fund locked in a vault today simply signals to foreign short-sellers that regulators will not defend the downside—allowing downward pressure to persist even when the currency shows moments of strength.

The Nvidia Shock and Anthropic’s $90 Billion Liquidity Vacuum

Seoul’s domestic policy paralysis has coincided with a violent shake-up in the global artificial intelligence ecosystem that sits at the heart of South Korea’s export economy. The immediate external shock is an unprecedented governance crisis at Nvidia following the abrupt ouster—officially termed a resignation—of founder and CEO Jensen Huang. Within two days, an online petition launched by employees and shareholders demanding Huang’s return drew 70,000 to 80,000 signatures, including endorsements from Tesla’s Elon Musk and Park Hyeon-joo, chairman of South Korean financial powerhouse Mirae Asset.

Washington, however, appears to be moving in the opposite direction. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Fox News and other outlets that if Nvidia had accumulated internal problems, its next chief executive should be brought in from outside to lead a sweeping overhaul. With both Bessent and Huang slated to testify before the U.S. Senate Banking Committee on December 10, the turmoil has escalated beyond corporate governance into national security allegations over illegal AI chip smuggling to China. Even former White House AI czar David Sacks remarked on a podcast that “the more you dig, the more questions keep popping up instead of answers,” fueling speculation of U.S. intelligence involvement.

For South Korea’s semiconductor supply chain, the danger is structural. Analysts note that Nvidia has functioned less like a pure chipmaker and more like the AI industry’s central bank—taking equity stakes in cash-burning startups and backing massive cloud commitments for customers buying its GPUs. As of its latest quarterly report, Nvidia’s AI cloud payment guarantees for “neocloud” operators such as CoreWeave, Nebius, and IREN topped $36 billion. Elon Musk warned that while Huang managed Nvidia’s supply chain masterfully, many of those arrangements were “handshake deals” resting on personal trust rather than airtight formal contracts. Under a numbers-focused interim CEO, any cracks in that circular financing model threaten Korean chipmakers directly.

At the same time, Nasdaq is bracing for a record-shattering initial public offering from AI developer Anthropic. Priced at the top of its range, Anthropic would raise $90.4 billion—eclipsing SpaceX’s record—at a $1.4 trillion valuation that would immediately place it among the world’s ten largest companies. Institutional demand has topped six times the available shares as CEO Dario Amodei pushes ahead to fund massive capital expenditures. Yet because Anthropic will not enter the Nasdaq index for two to three weeks after listing, global funds are draining existing tech stocks to subscribe—creating a liquidity vacuum twice as severe as the SpaceX IPO.

Geopolitics offers little relief. Ahead of a trilateral summit involving Chinese President Xi Jinping, U.S. President Donald Trump, and Russian President Vladimir Putin around the November 18 APEC meetings, hopes for tariff or rare-earth concessions ahead of a January U.S.-China trade truce deadline have been dimmed by the Nvidia espionage controversy. Even when Elon Musk’s accompaniment lifted Chinese battery giant CATL by 1.7 percent intraday, South Korean electric-vehicle battery stocks surrendered early gains and slipped into the red—a stark illustration of how domestic industries are decoupling from global rallies. Meanwhile, bizarre domestic headlines—from the top scorer on Korea’s tax accountant exam confessing to cheating with a micro-camera and AI, sparking calls to delay the national college entrance exam, to Samsung Electronics and eyewear brand Gentle Monster instantly selling out AI glasses—have only reinforced the sense of a market drifting without direction.

Breaking the Chaebol Grip Through Hostile Takeovers

Beyond global tech turmoil, market reformers argue South Korea will remain vulnerable to capital flight until it dismantles the “Korea Discount”—the chronic undervaluation of Korean equities driven by family-controlled conglomerates, known as chaebol. Controlling families frequently dominate corporate empires with stakes of just 20 to 30 percent, spinning off prized subsidiaries at cheap prices, dual-listing core businesses, and leaving parent holding companies trading at 50 to 70 percent discounts to net asset value.

For years, a compromise bill requiring a “50 percent plus one share” mandatory tender offer during changes of corporate control has languished in the National Assembly. Market experts argue that only a 100 percent mandatory tender offer—forcing acquirers to buy out all remaining minority shareholders on equal terms—can end backroom deals by controlling families.

Crucially, the political framing around corporate control may be shifting. President Lee Jae-myung recently drew market attention by arguing at length that hostile mergers and acquisitions are not inherently evil. Reformers see that shift as vital for forcing accountability onto entrenched owners.

“Who is a hostile M&A hostile toward? It’s not hostile toward regular shareholders—it’s hostile toward the controlling shareholder.”

If hostile takeovers became a routine threat, controlling families would have to boost dividends and defend share prices to win over minority investors. Consider South Korea’s chip sector: buying SK Hynix outright on the open market is virtually impossible, but acquiring its holding company, SK Square, is far more plausible for global funds or activist coalitions. In the past, when activist fund KCGI and Bando E&C waged a control battle over Hanjin KAL, the parent of Korean Air, the stock surged from the 20,000–30,000 won range to 120,000 won and eventually 200,000 won. Even major entertainment players such as JYP Entertainment could face control challenges with roughly 500 billion won ($375 million) in capital. Opening that door, analysts argue, could re-rate holding companies across the board and unlock a path toward a 10,000-point KOSPI.

The 25% Rule Behind the Chip Sell-Off—and What to Watch Next

Before concluding that foreign capital has permanently abandoned South Korea, investors should look closely at where the money actually left. Of the 210 trillion won ($157 billion) foreigners have net sold this year, roughly 200 trillion won ($150 billion) came from just two stocks: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. According to quantitative analysis cited in the Korean commentary, those massive outflows were driven largely by mechanical rebalancing under MSCI’s 25 percent single-stock weight cap rule—forcing passive funds to trim oversized semiconductor positions—rather than a broad collapse in corporate fundamentals.

In fact, seven core quantitative valuation metrics for the KOSPI have reached historical extremes, including a forward price-to-book ratio of 0.94 and dividend yields of 3.3 to 3.4 percent, suggesting strong potential for a technical rebound once forced selling subsides.

Whether Seoul finds its footing in the weeks ahead hinges on four concrete developments:

  • Regulator resolve: Whether President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the FSC move beyond cautious rhetoric to deploy—or credibly pledge—a 10-trillion-won-scale stabilization backstop to deter short-sellers.
  • December 10 in Washington: How U.S. lawmakers, Scott Bessent, and Jensen Huang frame Nvidia’s leadership transition and AI export controls before the Senate Banking Committee.
  • Post-IPO liquidity normalization: Whether money flows back into Asian tech equities once Anthropic’s $90.4 billion listing clears its two-to-three-week Nasdaq index inclusion lag.
  • Governance legislation: Whether the National Assembly breaks its stalemate on mandatory tender-offer rules and passes genuine minority-shareholder protections rather than a watered-down compromise.

Until those pieces settle, South Korea’s stock market will remain caught between deeply discounted valuations and a policy vacuum that global funds are all too happy to exploit.
