A Mayor's Acquittal, a Cult Scandal, and Judicial Reform: South Korea's Ruling Party at a Crossroads

October 25, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, the mood among lawmakers from South Korea's ruling Democratic Party was palpably tense on October 24. As the governing party under President Lee Jae-myung, the Democrats carry the dual burden of keeping the gears of state turning while satisfying a restive political base that expects deep, structural change. On this single day, the ruling party found itself hit by a sudden judicial setback, locked in a bitter internal debate over alleged religious infiltration, and navigating fierce ideological warfare at the close of the annual parliamentary audit season.

For observers outside South Korea, understanding these developments requires seeing how legal battles, partisan memories, and everyday governance overlap in Seoul. In South Korea's hyper-polarized political landscape, court rulings do not simply conclude legal proceedings; they often become the fuel for constitutional confrontations. The events of late October show a ruling party attempting to balance aggressive reform rhetoric with the institutional restraint demanded of a party in power.

The Court Acquits Oh Se-hoon: Fury, Restraint, and the Battle over the Judiciary

The immediate spark for the day’s political turbulence was an appellate court ruling clearing Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, one of the most prominent figures in the conservative opposition, of election law violation charges. When the high court announced its verdict of not guilty, the Democratic Party leadership and its parliamentary spokespersons reacted with open outrage. In its official commentary, the party criticized the bench for prioritizing vague conjectures by the defense over objective evidence, urging state prosecutors to immediately lodge an appeal to the Supreme Court.

To the Democratic Party's grass-roots supporters, the ruling was seen as proof of a compromised judicial branch. Discontent spilled over into angry claims that the courts act as a "vending machine for acquittals" whenever elite conservative figures stand in the dock. The decision reignited long-simmering demands among progressive loyalists to overhaul the nation's courts and purge what they view as an entrenched, partisan judiciary.

That fury quickly translated into calls to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Yet, faced with a chorus demanding the chief justice's immediate removal, Democratic Party Leader Kim Min-seok chose a more measured course of action. Speaking to reporters, Kim signaled that the party would not rush into a destabilizing impeachment showdown without proper groundwork:

"We will proceed step by step, according to our original plan, including through a parliamentary investigation."

This calculated pause reflects significant political maneuvering. An immediate, unilateral impeachment push against the head of the judiciary would risk provoking severe public pushback and accusations of executive overreach, handing a tactical victory to the conservative opposition. By opting first for a formal parliamentary investigation into the court’s decision-making and perceived biases, the ruling party hopes to lay out its case before the broader public, building the procedural legitimacy needed before deciding whether to take the ultimate step of impeachment.

The Shincheonji Infiltration Allegation: An Internal Clean-up Turns Volatile

While managing the fallout from the courts, party leaders found themselves dealing with an uncomfortable controversy from within their own ranks: an internal probe into whether members of Shincheonji, a controversial fringe religious sect, had engaged in a coordinated effort to join the Democratic Party.

In South Korea, Shincheonji is widely viewed with deep public suspicion, having previously faced intense scrutiny over its clandestine recruiting methods and past entanglements in conservative politics. For a progressive party that prides itself on civic transparency, even the suggestion of ties to such a group is deeply toxic. As the party’s internal fact-finding committee reached the final stages of its investigation, deep disagreements erupted within the leadership over how and when to release the findings.

During Supreme Council discussions, Lawmaker Park Sun-won took a hardline stance, insisting on personally releasing the complete details without filtering. Park and other transparency advocates argued that any hesitation or ambiguity would make the party appear guilty of a cover-up, compromising its moral authority. On the other side of the debate, party moderates urged extreme caution, warning that an uncoordinated or incomplete disclosure could trigger a destructive internal witch hunt and fracture party unity at a sensitive legislative moment.

The main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, moved quickly to exploit the rift. Accusing the Democrats of concealing the truth, the People Power Party demanded an independent Special Counsel investigation. Democratic leaders dismissed the demand as pure political posturing, arguing that a regular internal party audit does not warrant a taxpayer-funded special prosecutor. Turning the offensive back on their rivals, Democratic spokespersons reminded the public of the well-documented, murky alliances between conservative circles and fringe religious organizations during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office—a record the ruling party insists remains ripe for proper public scrutiny.

A Polarized Assembly: Clashing over History while Passing Trillions in Aid

These disputes framed the final days of the National Assembly's annual audits, where standing committees turned into ideological battlegrounds between the governing Democrats and the opposition People Power Party.

Two legislative panels became the primary flashpoints:

  • The Legislation and Judiciary Committee: Democratic lawmakers pushed forward institutional safeguards, advancing a subcommittee bill that would explicitly bar future presidential pardons for the crimes of insurrection and treason. The legislation is designed as a direct structural guarantee to prevent pardons for constitutional violations such as those witnessed during the crisis that led to the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.
  • The Science, ICT, Broadcasting, and Communications Committee: Bipartisan conflict flared over the leadership of the state media regulator, the Korea Communications Commission. Lawmakers clashed sharply over a procedural motion to recuse its chair, Lee Jin-sook, amid ongoing accusations of biased regulatory actions and administrative paralysis.

Ideological tensions peaked further when conservative lawmakers hosted a forum inside the National Assembly Members' Hall celebrating former authoritarian ruler Park Chung-hee as a "revolutionist." Democratic lawmakers condemned the event as historical revisionism that glorified a military dictatorship and trampled upon South Korea's hard-won constitutional order.

Yet, behind the rhetorical firestorms, the ruling party sought to project that it had not lost sight of bread-and-butter governance. Negotiators secured a bipartisan agreement to pass a 26.2 trillion won (approximately $19.5 billion) supplementary budget bill through the plenary session. Designed to ease the burden of persistent inflation and an economic slowdown on working-class households, the budget serves as a practical counterweight to charges of excessive political warfare. Simultaneously, the party worked on regulatory relief clauses in the Mega-Region Special Zone Act and reviewed timelines for the August 13 housing supply initiatives to ensure construction in the Seoul metropolitan area proceeds on schedule.

Solidarity, Memory, and What to Watch Next

Beyond committee rooms and press conferences, the day was marked by appeals for unity in a volatile political climate. Senior figures gathered to mourn the passing of former Interior Minister Choi Hyung-woo, a giant of South Korea’s democratization struggle during the authoritarian era. At the memorial, veteran lawmaker Park Ji-won issued a stark warning against factional infighting, cautioning that internal division would reduce party members to "frogs in a boiling pot." Former President Moon Jae-in echoed that sentiment, releasing a message urging the party and the administration to maintain solid chemical unity.

The party leadership under Kim Min-seok also confirmed its plans to attend the public memorial for the Itaewon crowd crush disaster, reaffirming its pledge to enact legislation that centers public safety and honors the victims' families. Meanwhile, on the foreign policy front, Rep. Yoon Gun-young, ranking member of the parliamentary Intelligence Committee, briefed colleagues on regional intelligence regarding North Korean leader Kim Jong Un’s diplomatic movements in China, including high-level engagements with Chinese Premier Li Qiang, seeking diplomatic openings to stabilize the peninsula.

Looking ahead, the Democratic Party faces three concrete milestones that will reveal whether its strategy of measured escalation succeeds:

  • The parliamentary probe into the courts: Observers should watch the precise wording and scope of the parliamentary investigation request the ruling party submits to the floor. The level of detail will reveal whether party leaders intend to use the inquiry as an educational forum or as an active prelude to an impeachment vote against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.
  • The Shincheonji probe disclosure: How the party eventually publishes its internal findings will test party cohesion. A transparent, orderly release could close the book on opposition attacks, but any sign of suppression or redaction could spark fresh internal disputes.
  • Execution of the $19.5 billion budget: The swift passage and deployment of the 26.2 trillion won supplementary budget will determine whether the Lee Jae-myung administration can maintain public trust on the economy while fighting long-term battles over the judiciary and constitutional reform.
