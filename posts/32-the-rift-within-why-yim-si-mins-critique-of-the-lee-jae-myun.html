The Rift Within: Why Yim Si-min’s Critique of the Lee Jae-myung Administration Matters

September 24, 2026

For those watching South Korean politics, the current atmosphere is thick with a sense of déjà vu—and deep unease. There is a palpable tension within the ruling bloc, a sound of grinding gears that usually signals a major internal fracture. What makes this particular moment so startling is the emergence of a rumor that the administration is actively "cutting ties" with Rhyu Si-min, a man who has served as the intellectual compass and primary ideological defender of the liberal movement since the Roh Moo-hyun administration (2003–2008).

When a figure as foundational as Rhyu begins to launch what can only be described as a "bombardment" against the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, and the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea’s main liberal opposition-turned-governing-party—reportedly responds by marking him as a liability rather than an ally, it forces us to ask: What is really happening behind the closed doors of the Blue House (the South Korean presidential office)?

The Illusion of Policy Failure

President Lee’s approval ratings are currently in a steady decline. While it is easy to point toward tangible issues like inflation or housing prices as the culprits, astute observers—Rhyu Si-min among them—argue that the root of the problem is not just policy, but attitude. The administration’s default response to criticism has been defensive, shifting from acknowledging mistakes to insisting that everything is actually going well. This refusal to own up to shortcomings is viewed by many as the administration’s core weakness.

Rhyu has likened the government’s approach to wearing a pair of shoes that don't fit. "Prosecutorial reform," he argued, "is a shoe the President doesn't want to wear, but is forced to keep on because the public put it on him." By refusing to simply take the shoe off and shake out the pebbles causing discomfort, the administration is effectively allowing those stones to bruise its own feet. Meanwhile, prominent political commentator Kim Ou-soo has pointed out that the administration is losing its "core" base—not necessarily those who have turned against the party, but those who have crossed their arms in disappointment. In politics, an arm-crossed supporter is just one step away from turning their back entirely.

The Strategy of Isolation

Reports from credible political journalists suggest that the pro-Lee faction within the Democratic Party has moved past debating Rhyu to actively isolating him. The consensus among some key party insiders appears to be: "Rhyu Si-min is no longer someone we can bring into the fold; we are moving on without him."

This "isolation strategy" is a calculated gamble. The administration seems to have chosen to lean on other, more compliant political influencers—such as those on the popular YouTube channel Maebulshow or Kim Ou-soo’s news program—who frame their role as a "breakwater," protecting the President from criticism. Rhyu, conversely, has always viewed his role as a "traction engine," pushing the government toward reform through relentless, uncomfortable pressure. This ideological clash is no longer just a debate; it has become a full-scale purge of dissent.

The upcoming Democratic Party national convention in August is set to be the decisive battleground for this struggle. The party is essentially divided between those who prioritize "centrist expansion"—a pragmatic move to appeal to moderate and conservative voters—and those who believe the party’s primary duty is to safeguard the reformist principles that brought them to power. By labeling those who prioritize pragmatism as "opportunists," Rhyu has effectively drawn a line in the sand, forcing the administration to choose between its identity and its survival.

The Real Danger: The Sound of Silence

History suggests that for a South Korean administration, the greatest threat is not the loud jeers of the conservative People Power Party, but the sudden silence of its own supporters. When the people who once stood on the streets to chant for change stop clapping, it is a sign that the administration has lost its moral mandate. Supporters rarely flip to the other side overnight; they simply stop voting, stop talking about politics, and quietly drift away.

The administration’s current reliance on "centrist expansion" is, in many ways, an act of political self-harm. By sidelining reform tasks to appease a moderate middle that may never truly be satisfied, they risk hollowing out the very base that serves as their foundation. As we look at the historical trajectories of past presidents, the slide toward single-digit or low 20% approval ratings almost always begins with this alienation of the core base.

What to watch next:

  • The August Convention: Watch for whether the party leadership moves to solidify a "pro-Lee" monolith or allows for the continued coexistence of different reformist factions.
  • The "Silence" Metric: Monitor whether the administration changes its tone from defensive platitudes to substantive acknowledgment of policy failures following the Chuseok holiday period.
  • The Rhyu Factor: Whether Rhyu Si-min continues his public critiques or whether the "isolation" efforts successfully dampen his influence on the broader liberal movement.

Power is not truly tested when it is first won, but when it is forced to reconcile its actions with the people who granted that power in the first place. If the administration continues to view its most critical allies as enemies to be discarded, it may find that it has built its house on very thin ice.
