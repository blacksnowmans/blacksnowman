The Architecture of Ambition: Why South Korea’s Liberal Leaders Are Failing to Remake the State

October 10, 2026

If you have spent any time following South Korean political discourse recently, you have likely encountered a viral clip of Rhyu Si-min—a veteran intellectual, former minister, and one of the country’s most incisive political commentators—dissecting the nature of power. His latest analysis, which has been making the rounds on YouTube Shorts, does not just offer a critique of the current administration; it provides a stark diagnosis of why the South Korean progressive movement continues to stumble despite holding the levers of power.

For observers outside of Korea, the country’s politics often appear as a blur of aggressive partisanship and sudden scandals. But beneath the noise, a fundamental tension is brewing: the clash between political idealism and the stubborn structural reality of governance. To understand where South Korea is heading under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, one must first understand the different DNA of the liberal leaders who preceded him.

The Evolution of Liberal Leadership: From Moon to Lee

The transition from former President Moon Jae-in to current President Lee Jae-myung marks a radical departure in leadership style. Moon, who served from 2017 to 2022, was a man of the "camp." Heavily influenced by the painful political demise of his mentor, the late President Roh Moo-hyun, Moon spent his presidency wary of fracturing his political base. He sought stability, largely operating within established institutional frameworks and avoiding the kinds of high-risk gambles that could alienate his core supporters.

President Lee Jae-myung, however, is a different animal. As Rhyu Si-min aptly put it, "Lee Jae-myung’s ambition is on an entirely different scale compared to Moon Jae-in’s." Where Moon played defense, Lee plays offense. He is willing to cross ideological lines, recruit from the conservative camp, and pursue grand, high-stakes policy experiments. He is a "purpose-rational" leader, prioritizing tangible results over ideological purity.

Yet, this ambition comes at a cost. Recent polling data indicates that Lee’s approval rating has slipped to around 33%, with disapproval hitting 64%. Much of this frustration stems from a series of high-profile personnel controversies—specifically, the nominations of Justice Minister candidate Kim Seung-won and Gender Equality Minister candidate Ryu Ho-jeong. These picks have been marred by allegations of ethical lapses and conflicts of interest, turning what was supposed to be a revitalizing cabinet reshuffle into a political liability.

The Trap of Idealism and the Illusion of Quick Change

The central fallacy plaguing the current administration is the belief that political willpower alone can instantly reshape a nation. Rhyu Si-min warns against this, noting that while some things in society can be changed quickly, others take decades, and some—due to human nature or structural limits—are near impossible to alter. When leaders attempt to force progress without acknowledging these distinctions, the resulting backlash is often severe.

This is where the "human error" versus "system error" debate becomes crucial. Supporters often brush off administrative failures as simple mistakes by individuals. Rhyu argues that if a system is incapable of replacing the people responsible for those mistakes, it is no longer a human error—it is a system error. The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the ruling party, remains plagued by a culture where entrenched power brokers prioritize self-preservation over genuine accountability.

In many districts, DPK lawmakers operate like feudal lords, exerting undue influence over local nominations. This creates a chain of corruption where local officials serve the lawmaker rather than the constituents. As long as this structure remains intact, individual leadership changes—no matter how charismatic the president—will inevitably run into the same brick wall of institutional rot.

A Warning Sign for the Future

Perhaps the most alarming trend for the Lee administration is the alienation of the younger generation. In recent surveys, the disapproval rating among voters in their 20s and 30s has actually surpassed that of voters in their 70s. For a movement that prides itself on being the voice of the future, this is a catastrophic signal. Young Koreans are not necessarily abandoning progressive ideals, but they are turning away from a political system that they perceive as hypocritical, opaque, and increasingly defined by the same "old-school" cronyism it once campaigned against.

The DPK’s recent dip in support—falling below the 40% threshold in some polls—coincides with a tightening race against the main opposition, the People Power Party (PPP). While the PPP has its own baggage—most notably the imprisonment of former President Yoon Seok-youl for abuse of power during his tenure—the DPK cannot rely on the failures of the past to secure its future. The public, especially the centrist swing voters, is losing patience with a party that seems more interested in internal power dynamics than in fixing the systematic failures of the state.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea prepares for upcoming local elections, the "Lee Jae-myung experiment" is entering a decisive phase. Here is what to watch for:

  • Personnel Discipline: Will President Lee continue to shield controversial appointees, or will he demonstrate the courage to purge those who damage the administration’s credibility? A failure to do so will signal that "system error" has won.
  • Structural Reform: Look for any genuine attempt by the DPK to reform its nomination processes. If the party continues to let local lawmakers hoard power, they will face a grassroots revolt in the next election cycle.
  • The Youth Vote: Monitor whether the administration makes a concerted effort to address the disillusionment of voters under 40. If this demographic continues to sour on the government, the DPK’s long-term electoral prospects will be in serious jeopardy.

Politics, at its core, is not just about the leader at the top; it is about the machinery of government. If President Lee Jae-myung cannot reconcile his grand ambitions with the cold, hard requirements of institutional integrity, he risks becoming yet another chapter in a history of unrealized potential. The irony of the current situation is that the very ambition that brought him to power is now the factor most likely to complicate his legacy.
