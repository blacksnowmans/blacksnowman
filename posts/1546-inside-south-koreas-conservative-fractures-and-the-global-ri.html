Inside South Korea’s Conservative Fractures and the Global Rise of the Far-Right

October 27, 2026

When political parties begin fighting over geography months before a legislative race, it is rarely just about logistics. It is about survival, identity, and a profound anxiety over where the voters have gone. In South Korea, that anxiety is currently tearing through the conservative and opposition camps. As the country navigates the political landscape under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—with the former conservative leader impeached and imprisoned—South Korea's fragmented right is locked in a bitter internal struggle over how to reinvent itself.

This factional bickering is not happening in a vacuum. From the street corners of Seoul to the presidential rallies of France and the polarized electorates of Brazil and Israel, a familiar script is playing out. Mainstream conservative movements are being pulled toward radical fringes, entangled in conspiracy theories, and tested by leaders who refuse to compromise. Understanding this moment requires looking past the daily headlines of Korean politics and examining how a fractured conservative identity is colliding with a global surge in far-right populism.

The Reform Party's Tug-of-War Over Geography and Strategy

The Reform Party, a minor conservative splinter group attempting to carve out a distinct identity between the mainstream conservative opposition and the ruling Democratic Party, is currently locked in an internal turf war. The point of contention is a strategic proposal known as the "Gyeonggi South drafting theory"—an initiative pushed by party leadership that would pull prominent figures away from safe or symbolic regional seats and redeploy them to the densely populated, fiercely contested Gyeonggi Province surrounding Seoul.

Lawmaker Chun Ha-ram has flatly refused to budge. Chun has insisted that he has no intention of abandoning his constituency in Suncheon, a city in the country's southwest where he has built a base, arguing that breaking his promise to local voters would destroy the party's credibility. Lee Gi-in, a member of the party’s emergency steering committee, has publicly pushed back, demanding that Chun fall in line for the sake of a unified provincial campaign. The public clash has laid bare the deep tactical divides within the party's ranks.

This is not merely an argument over real estate; it is a symptom of a party struggling with stagnant approval ratings and competing visions of how to win. When practical strategists and high-profile lawmakers publicly pull in opposite directions, it exposes the lack of a coherent unifying ideology. Politics is always a negotiation between grand principles and cold political utility, but the Reform Party’s rough-edged public disputes risk alienating the very voters looking for a clean break from traditional partisan bickering.

Conservative Contortions Under the People Power Party

Meanwhile, the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea's main conservative party and the primary opposition in the National Assembly—is wrestling with its own leadership dilemmas. Jang Dong-hyrax, securing a notable tenure as one of the party’s longest-serving leaders in recent memory, has taken his leadership on the road, attempting to shore up the party's historical base. Jang recently led party leadership to a memorial ceremony marking the anniversary of the death of former authoritarian president Park Chung-hee, a move clearly designed to anchor the party in traditional conservative orthodoxy.

Yet Jang’s wide-ranging moves, including high-profile dinners with Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, have failed to quell internal dissent. Debates over whether to pursue a broader coalition with former party heavyweights like Han Dong-hoon and Lee Jun-seok have triggered fierce counter-arguments. While figures like former lawmaker Yoo Seung-min argue that all conservative factions must eventually unite to stand a chance in future legislative elections, others—such as Lee Jun-seok—reject the idea outright, declaring that they will not partner with what they view as political maneuvering and backroom engineering.

The problem with these constant discussions of grand conservative alliances is that they often amount to political math without a shared moral foundation. Merging disparate factions purely for electoral survival, without agreeing on core values, rarely convinces an electorate. Jang’s longevity at the head of the PPP risks looking less like a display of stable leadership and more like a holding pattern while the party avoids the harder work of genuine ideological renewal.

Street-Level Extremism and the Temptation of Conspiracy

Beyond the formal halls of the National Assembly, a darker strain of conservatism has metastasized on the streets and online. Kim Byeong-heon, an activist notorious for historical revisionism and rhetoric targeting comfort women, has found himself the target of a police investigation over allegations of illegal fundraising. For years, fringe groups operating at the edges of the political right have weaponized hate speech and distorted historical narratives, but law enforcement and civil society are slowly tightening the net.

These groups are not merely expressing unorthodox political opinions; their activities strike at the root of a pluralistic democracy by dehumanizing minorities and treating constitutional norms as negotiable. The revelation that these operations may be tied to financial exploitation—via questionable fundraising schemes—suggests that far-right agitation has become a lucrative enterprise for some of its purveyors.

Compounding this is the persistence of baseless election fraud conspiracies pushed by extremist YouTubers and fringe organizations. While mainstream opposition politicians sometimes focus on policy disputes with the Lee Jae-myung administration, they frequently tolerate or ignore the street-level extremists who scream about stolen elections. When mainstream politicians remain silent to court fringe votes, they effectively mainstream conspiratorial poison. A healthy conservative movement must draw a hard line against extremism rather than treating it as a noisy, useful asset.

A Global Echo Chamber: The Far-Right Playbook Abroad

South Korea’s struggles with the fringes of the right are part of a much larger, interconnected global phenomenon. Across Europe and the Americas, far-right movements are capitalizing on economic anxiety and social fragmentation to reshape the political terrain.

In France, Marine Le Pen has launched her latest presidential bid leaning heavily on the familiar pillars of "restoring law and order" and sealing borders against immigrants, turning societal turbulence into her primary vehicle for power. In Germany, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) continues to push radical agendas, including discussions around dismantling European integration. These movements wrap themselves in the language of democratic order, but their underlying philosophy relies on the scapegoating of minorities and an exclusionary, defensive nationalism.

The pattern repeats across the globe. In Brazil, supporters of the defeated right-wing populist Jair Bolsonaro have contested democratic election results, echoing similar playbook tactics seen in other fragile democracies. In Israel, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s political survival has increasingly relied on empowering hardline, far-right nationalist factions, accelerating national division. When billionaires and international observers alike begin publicly warning Israeli leadership to cut ties with extremists, it signals that the economic and social risks of far-right governance have crossed a tipping point.

These global parallels carry a stark warning for South Korea. Economic polarization and widening inequality act as incubators for populist anger. When mainstream political parties fail to address those structural pressures, the resulting vacuum is quickly filled by demagogues who promise simple, destructive answers.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves forward, the key indicators of where the conservative movement is heading will not be found in closed-door leadership meetings, but in how political elites respond to the fringes.

  • The Reform Party's Internal Discipline: Watch whether internal disagreements over candidate placement lead to actual defections or if the leadership can enforce party unity ahead of upcoming electoral contests.
  • The PPP’s Stance on the Fringe: Observe whether mainstream conservative leaders continue to tolerate or court street-level conspiracy theorists, or if they take definitive steps to isolate extremist voices.
  • Civil Society's Response to Hate Speech: Monitor the legal outcomes of investigations into illegal fundraising by radical groups, which will test the judiciary's willingness to draw legal boundaries around political harassment and historical distortion.

Democracy does not maintain itself by default. The political theater of factional infighting and fringe agitation serves as a constant reminder that when dialogue and compromise are replaced by absolute self-righteousness, the entire political system suffers. The question facing South Korea's opposition is whether it has the courage to choose substantive alternatives over cheap division—before the fringes dictate the terms of the debate entirely.
