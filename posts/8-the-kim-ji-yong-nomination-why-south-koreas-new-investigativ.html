The Kim Ji-yong Nomination: Why South Korea's New Investigative Agency is Trapped by the Prosecutor Cartel

September 24, 2026

South Korea’s political circles and legal community are currently consumed by a fierce battle over a single appointment. As the administration attempts to establish the architectural framework of a brand-new investigative agency—the tentatively dubbed Serious Crime Investigation Office (SCIO)—the choice for its inaugural chief has become a lightning rod. Supporter factions defend the nominee, Kim Ji-yong, by insisting he is "not a pro-Yoon prosecutor"—referring to loyalists of former President Yoon Suk-yeol. Critics, however, argue that his past actions expose a careerist institutionalist whose loyalties shift with the political winds. When foreign observers look past the surface-level political noise, this appointment lays bare the deep structural resilience of South Korea's entrenched prosecutor cartel.

The Careerist Playbook: Stamping Approvals and Denying Responsibility

Defenders of Kim Ji-yong often point out that he was never part of any core political faction during past administrations. Yet public officials must be judged by concrete actions and official paper trails rather than convenient labels. As opposition lawmaker Park Eun-jung has pointed out, Kim’s behavior during the controversial prosecution of Prosecutor Jung Jin-woo in the "severe abuse" case involving high-profile prosecutor Han Dong-hoon reveals a pattern of evasive compliance. At the time, prosecutors favored indicting the subject, but formal indictments required the explicit sign-off of the senior deputy chief prosecutor and the chief prosecutor of the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office—a position Kim held.

Despite this clear structural requirement, Kim later claimed he had opposed the indictment from the beginning. Yet records kept in the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office archives clearly show Kim’s official approval seal stamped on the trial card. If he genuinely opposed the move, refusing to stamp the document was the only way to demonstrate true accountability. Instead, signing off and later whispering among colleagues that he "actually disagreed" is a cowardly abdication of ethical responsibility.

This bureaucratic sleight of hand highlights an opportunistic ecosystem within the prosecution service. Rather than wielding the law with principled conviction, officials maintain plausible deniability by quietly stamping documents while keeping an eye on their own career preservation. Expecting someone who hid behind official seals while reading the political room to successfully lead a newly minted anti-corruption agency defies common sense. Prosecutors are supposed to speak through indictments and leadership through approvals; hiding behind the defense of "I am not pro-Yoon" while ignoring one's own paper trail is a hollow defense.

Rewriting History on High-Profile Investigations

Kim’s involvement in approving the indictment of Lee Kwang-cheol, a former presidential secretary for civil affairs, further exposes his true operating style. According to media reports from July 2021, Kim—who was then serving as the chief of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office Criminal Department—gave the final sign-off to indict Lee. Neither Kim nor his defenders have ever issued a formal correction or retraction regarding those reports. Why, then, do rumors persist that he secretly opposed it?

This behavior mirrors the prosecution's traditional internal culture of granting absolution and currying favor. At critical moments, officials sign documents to align with institutional priorities; when political winds shift, they quietly rebrand themselves as progressive reformers. An official’s historical record cannot be scrubbed by media gossip or peer testimonials. The documents and administrative titles point to a single conclusion: Kim is an individual who comfortably thrived within the protective walls of the prosecutorial establishment.

Figures who move with the prevailing winds cannot be trusted when a crisis hits. Even if Kim currently appears to march in step with the new administration's agenda, he will inevitably revert to his instincts as a institutional loyalist the moment organizational interests are threatened. Placing such a figure at the head of a new investigative agency risks neutering the historic task of separating investigative and prosecutorial powers, turning the new body into a mirror image of the turf wars it was designed to replace.

The Shadow of the Prosecutor Cartel and the Illusion of Reform

Journalist Bong Ji-wook has noted that major controversies involving Han Dong-hoon consistently intersect with the offices Kim has held throughout his career. Reviewing Kim's tenure as head of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office's Inspection Division 1 clarifies how the prosecution routinely buries inconvenient truths. A prime example is the 2015 sexual misconduct case involving Prosecutor Jin Dong-gyun at the Southern District Prosecutors' Office. Inspection concluded without disciplinary action, allowing Jin to resign quietly and take a high-paying executive job at a major conglomerate—a textbook case of prosecutors protecting their own. When the MeToo movement dragged the scandal back into the light three years later, those responsible for correcting the injustice remained silent.

More egregiously, the framing of Prosecutor Jung Jin-woo for alleged abuse during the infamous Channel A media-prosecution collusion scandal completely flipped the narrative to protect powerful figures. Then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk-yeol and his loyalists fought frantically to protect Han Dong-hoon’s smartphone during the investigation. The spectacle of officials wrestling over an iPhone to prevent a passcode entry from breaching search boundaries was a dark comedy of legal evasion. Yet the media reframed the incident as an instance of "prosecutorial assault," turning the investigative team trying to uncover the truth into the villains. The eventual dropping of the case after core forensic evidence was thwarted during Kim's tenure as head of the Criminal Department illustrates how this vast cartel protects its members.

Similarly, defenders point out that under the Moon Jae-up administration, Kim was promoted to chief prosecutor and ordered a reinvestigation into Choi Eun-soon, the mother-in-law of Yoon Suk-yeol. They cite this as proof that Kim is neither a pro-Yoon loyalist nor a political hack. Yet a closer look reveals that Supreme Prosecutors' Office reinvestigation orders are frequently used as toothless face-saving measures. They typically point out minor omissions in lower court rulings and instruct investigators to "clean it up," rarely resulting in actual indictments.

By November 2021, Choi's case was officially closed with a non-indictment decision—which was given final approval by Kim himself as the head of the Criminal Department. Ordering a reinvestigation with one hand and approving the final dismissal with the other, only to later pose as a victimized dissident targeted by powerful figures, is blatant deception. Treating such a figure as a neutral reformer is an insult to the documented history of prosecutorial overreach over the past decade.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this delicate transitional phase in its legal and political systems, several key developments will determine whether the new administration's reform agenda survives:

    The SCIO's Final Staffing Composition: Watch closely to see whether the newly formed Serious Crime Investigation Office is genuinely staffed by outside investigators and human rights experts, or if it quietly becomes a landing pad for veteran career prosecutors looking to salvage their jurisdictional turf. Legislative Oversight and Confirmation Hearings: Pay attention to how aggressively opposition lawmakers press Kim Ji-yong on his documentary record—specifically his approval stamps on past indictments and non-indictments—rather than letting him hide behind political labels. The Administration’s Internal Discipline: Observe whether the presidential office listens to civil society warnings about entrenched institutional networks, or if it continues to prioritize bureaucratic compliance over genuine judicial reform.

Reform is measured by concrete actions rather than rhetorical flourishes. If the leadership of an agency meant to symbolize prosecutorial reform is handed someone who cannot shed the habits of the old guard, South Korea risks repeating a cycle of institutional disappointment. Entrusting the future to opportunistic bureaucrats who serve only the cartel's survival is a gamble the country cannot afford.
