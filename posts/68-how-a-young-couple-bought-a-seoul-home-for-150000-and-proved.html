How a Young Couple Bought a Seoul Home for $150,000 and Proved the Apartment Obsession Wrong

September 02, 2026

Picture the scene: you are sitting in a cramped Seoul cafe, casually mentioning that you are looking to buy property in the capital for 200 million won (about $145,000). You would be met with immediate, pitying laughter. In a city where a cramped deposit on an aging suburban villa outside the city limits costs more than that—let alone the eye-watering sums required for key money on an apartment in Gangnam—such a budget sounds like a delusion.

Yet, a certain couple in Seoul recently did just that. They bypassed the gleaming model home showrooms and ignored the endless queues of prospective buyers desperate for brand-new high-rises. Instead, they hunted down a 40-year-old red-brick multifamily walk-up, snapped it up for 150 million won (about $110,000), and poured another 50 million won (about $36,000) into a total overhaul. For a grand total of roughly $145,000, they carved out a fortress of their own.

To real estate commentators and self-styled financial gurus on YouTube, the property checked every box of what to avoid. It featured traditional brick masonry and projecting bay windows—the classic hallmarks of aging, illiquid housing stock that experts routinely warn against. But while the crowd chased brand-name apartments, this couple looked past the stigma to ask a much harder question: what are we actually paying for when we buy a home?

Breaking the Illusion That Brand-Name Apartments Are the Only Answer

Modern South Korean apartments, for all their uniform luxury, often suffer from a subtle spatial oppression: the standard 2.2-meter to 2.3-meter ceiling. Built for construction efficiency and standardized HVAC routing, these low ceilings create a subtle, persistent claustrophobia. This couple tackled the problem radically. They ripped out the drop ceilings of their aging red-brick building to reveal an expansive, airy 3.8-meter vertical sweep.

Height, as they demonstrated, changes the physical mechanics of living. By stripping away extraneous light fixtures and recessed cans, they left behind only the raw bones of the architecture, turning the interior into a blank canvas. The superior vertical clearance of older buildings—built during eras with different structural engineering norms—offers a sense of volume that modern high-rises simply cannot replicate without paying astronomical sums for penthouse tiers.

They applied the same surgical logic to the rest of the layout. An awkward water tank space was converted into seamless custom storage; single-pane windows were reframed to create deep, sun-drenched window seats. This was not mere interior decoration; it was an act of architectural respect. Having previously endured the relentless urban roar from a tiny studio apartment next to a railway line, the couple used the renovation to dictate their own domestic tempo.

Challenging the Dogma of South Korea's Real Estate Obsession

In South Korea, the cultural pressure to buy into brand-name apartment complexes—often summarized by the bitter slang term eol-juk-sin ("freezing to death before giving up a new apartment")—is absolute. Brand recognition dictates social standing, and real estate is viewed almost entirely as a liquid financial instrument rather than a domestic sanctuary. Financial YouTubers routinely dismiss older walk-ups precisely because their resale liquidity lags behind master-planned mega-complexes.

But this obsession with liquidity carries a hidden cost: it forces buyers to trade their immediate quality of life for future speculative value. By treating a home purely as an asset to be flipped, owners willingly subject themselves to cookie-cutter floor plans and developer-driven aesthetics. The couple behind the 200-million-won renovation inverted this priority. They chose low liquidity in exchange for high autonomy, proving that a well-interpreted older structure can offer vastly more character and psychological breathing room than a standardized unit in a multi-thousand-unit complex.

The prevailing societal narrative insists that homes must be pristine, brand-new, and easily resold to be considered successful purchases. Anyone stepping outside those rigid parameters is often viewed as financially reckless. Yet, that very mindset traps millions in a loop of crushing mortgage payments for spaces they do not even love, all while waiting indefinitely for government housing lottery tickets.

Redefining the Home as a Sanctuary, Not a Balance Sheet

By turning a neglected veranda into a sunroom and installing automated blinds to track the shifting seasons from their living room, the couple achieved a kind of luxury that money alone cannot buy off the plan. The morning light hitting a custom-finished interior designed to match one's exact habits yields a deeper satisfaction than checking the daily price fluctuations of a brand-name complex on a smartphone app.

The 50 million won spent on renovations was not an expense; it was an investment in personal agency. In a society where housing has become heavily financialized, taking a hammer to a 40-year-old wall is a quiet act of rebellion. It shifts the fundamental definition of a home from an asset meant for public display back to a private shelter designed for human restoration.

What to Watch Next

As housing costs in major urban centers remain out of reach for a vast segment of the population, look for more creative micro-renovations to challenge the hegemony of the brand-name apartment. Pay attention to municipal regulatory changes regarding older building retrofits in Seoul's older neighborhoods, as well as the shifting cultural attitudes of younger generations who are increasingly prioritizing lifestyle over speculative asset accumulation. The real estate paradigm is shifting slowly, and the true pioneers may no longer be those who buy the most expensive apartments, but those who learn how to make old spaces truly theirs.
