Glass Frames and Disaster Alerts: Inside South Korea's Scramble to Keep AI Out of Its College Entrance Exam

November 18, 2026

Picture a quiet high school classroom in South Korea just minutes before the first bell of the morning. Thousands of teenagers sit stiffly at their desks, waiting to open a test booklet that effectively compresses twelve years of schooling into a single day. At the front of the room, proctors are not just checking admission slips or collecting backpacks. They are leaning in closely to inspect the temples of every student's eyeglasses and the faces of their wristwatches—a ritual they will repeat before every single test period until evening. Later, in the middle of the exam, the government will even send out an emergency disaster text alert, listening closely to see if any unsubmitted phone vibrates or chimes from a pocket.

For decades, anti-cheating enforcement on the Suneung—South Korea's College Scholastic Ability Test, held every year on the third Thursday of November—meant collecting mobile phones and electronic watches at the door. But those rules belonged to an era before cameras and wireless chips could be hidden inside ordinary-looking eyeglass frames. As wearable artificial intelligence hardware grows slimmer and harder to spot, the Lee Jae-myung administration and regional education offices have been forced into an extraordinary mobilization to defend the exam room using 20th-century human labor.

To grasp why the government is treating smart glasses like a national emergency, it helps to understand what the Suneung means in South Korea. Because university admissions rely heavily on this single-day national test, even a whisper that someone cheated with hidden technology can shatter public confidence in the entire education system. Yet the frantic nature of this year's crackdown has exposed an uncomfortable reality: South Korea's defense relies on thousands of extra human eyes, post-exam digital forensics, and last-minute procurement—and even its own officials admit this arms race cannot last.

An 8,400-Strong Human Shield and Every-Period Inspections

On November 17, following a cabinet meeting, Prime Minister Han Seong-sook delivered a public address underscoring the Lee Jae-myung government's urgency. Calling AI-assisted cheating a "social evil," she pledged to deploy an all-government emergency response team to stamp it out completely.

"I am well aware that test-takers and parents have tremendous anxiety over cheating that exploits AI." — Prime Minister Han Seong-sook

That anxiety has translated into an unprecedented deployment of manpower. Education authorities are adding 8,400 extra personnel to test sites nationwide—comprising 1,800 staff dedicated exclusively to smart-device detection and 6,600 hallway proctors. In Gyeonggi Province, the densely populated region surrounding Seoul that hosts more Suneung candidates than any other jurisdiction, the Gyeonggi Provincial Office of Education has entered emergency duty and deployed an additional 702 dedicated specialists of its own.

Having to station thousands of extra supervisors and trigger emergency phone alerts reveals a simple truth: the naked eye can barely tell modern smart glasses apart from regular frames. Rather than trusting a single sweep at the start of the day, Gyeonggi Superintendent of Education Ahn Min-seok announced that proctors will inspect glasses and watches before every single period, cutting off any attempt by students to swap in hidden hardware from their bags during breaks or lunch.

Under the Gyeonggi office's strict guidelines, authorities have left almost no gray area for test-takers:

  • Passive possession counts as a violation: Simply wearing a smart device during the exam is treated as cheating, even if the student never activates it.
  • No break-time loopholes: Using a smart device between test periods is penalized identically to using it during the exam itself.
  • Compliance is mandatory: Refusing a proctor's request to inspect eyeglasses or a wristwatch is automatically classified as cheating.

Even with such sweeping rules, officials drew one crucial boundary to prevent irreversible harm. A student wearing slightly thick-rimmed ordinary glasses or an unusual watch could easily trigger a proctor's suspicion. Superintendent Ahn made clear that proctors will not immediately halt a student's exam or expel them mid-test on suspicion alone. Instead, the default principle is to let the student finish the exam, conduct a thorough investigation afterward when suspicious behavior is clear, and provide legal support so that innocent students are not wrongly punished. It is a pragmatic compromise designed to keep the test room calm while protecting a teenager's year of preparation from a false alarm.

Big Tech Digital Forensics and the Limits of Deterrence

Because visual inspections can miss hidden hardware, the government has paired its physical dragnet with a technological pivot—one focused on catching cheaters after the fact rather than blocking AI in real time. In a public briefing, Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Science and ICT Bae Kyung-hoon announced that Seoul is partnering with global big tech companies to trace leaked exam questions.

Under this plan, if a student uses wearable smart glasses to photograph a test page and transmit it to an external server for AI-generated answers, authorities and tech firms will analyze image patterns and query logs afterward to identify the offender. Crucially, Bae acknowledged why the government could not simply ask AI providers to turn off answers during test hours. Tuning global AI models or building special filters restricted purely to South Korea's exam schedule and question formats is technically difficult. Given that hundreds of millions of people worldwide use these general-purpose platforms simultaneously, tracking digital footprints post-exam emerged as the only realistic alternative.

To reinforce this thin technical shield, lawmakers and policy advocates are pushing for harsher legal deterrence. Active discussions are underway to introduce legislation imposing a 10-year ban on retaking the Suneung for anyone caught cheating with smart glasses or similar advanced devices. For a South Korean student, losing a decade of eligibility effectively closes the door on domestic higher education. Still, as Kim Hyeong-yeon argued in the policy debate, administrative searches and dedicated proctors have inherent limits unless legal sanctions and technical blocks work together. Heavy penalties only deter misconduct if students believe they will actually get caught; if word spreads that smart glasses slip past proctors unnoticed, a 10-year ban risks becoming a paper tiger.

A July Warning, an October Order, and Political Pushback

Behind the sweeping announcements and tough rhetoric, however, sits an awkward paper trail that has drawn criticism even from within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. While the Suneung takes place on a fixed date every November, the government's technical preparations appear to have lagged months behind its own warnings.

Records surrounding procurement orders for "AI smart-glasses radio-wave detectors" that surfaced in October show that officials saw this problem coming far earlier. Back in July, the Ministry of Personnel Management, the Ministry of Employment and Labor, and an affiliated public human resources agency convened to discuss smart-glasses cheating. Even then, participants concluded that existing technical detection tools had significant limitations and gaps. Yet it was not until autumn—with the national college exam weeks away—that equipment orders went out and an emergency inter-ministerial response team was assembled.

That delay drew sharp criticism from Seongnam City Council member Lee Se-mi, a member of the ruling Democratic Party, who described the government's rollout as looking "too hastily thrown together" despite its acknowledged technical hurdles. She even noted skeptical chatter questioning whether authorities had only scrambled to this level because politicians' own children were among those sitting for the exam.

Other voices inside the ruling party argued that regardless of past delays, holding the line right now is non-negotiable. Veteran Democratic Party lawmaker Park Jie-won urged the administration to spare no visible effort—whether through metal detectors or thermal sensors—to show students that the state is actively protecting fair competition.

"Whether it takes metal detectors or thermal sensors, the government must do everything it can so that our children can trust the safety net of this society." — Rep. Park Jie-won, Democratic Party of Korea

Both critiques point to real stakes. Administrative delays deserve scrutiny so that next year's procurement does not repeat a last-minute rush. At the same time, in a society where the Suneung serves as a baseline guarantee of meritocratic fairness, visible physical safeguards remain essential just to keep public trust intact.

What to Watch Next: Beyond the Proctor Arms Race

Adding 8,400 proctors and leaning on post-exam Big Tech log analysis may get South Korea through this November's exam, but nobody in charge believes the model is sustainable. Smart glasses are already shrinking toward ordinary frame weights, and future iterations could take the form of contact lenses, microchips, or offline AI processors that never ping an external server. Deploying 10,000 proctors next year will only invite smaller hardware; jamming radio frequencies will only push devices offline.

Gyeonggi Superintendent Ahn Min-seok openly acknowledged these limits, stressing that technical countermeasures alone are not enough and calling for fundamental changes to both the Suneung format and university admissions. Rather than trying to lock every AI tool outside the classroom door forever, an increasingly vocal group of educators and policymakers argues that Korea must shift from testing memorization under strict bans to designing open-AI assessments—evaluating how students frame questions, filter out machine errors, and construct independent arguments through essays or project-based work.

Over the coming months, three concrete developments will show whether this year's crisis sparks real reform or merely another cycle of patches:

  • Post-exam investigations and false-positive disputes: Watch how many suspected cases emerge from Gyeonggi's post-test reviews and Big Tech query tracking, and whether the promise of legal support prevents disputes over wrongfully flagged students.
  • Movement on the 10-year ban bill: Watch whether lawmakers turn the proposed 10-year exam ban into formal legislation—and whether it is paired with earlier, standardized procurement of detection hardware before next summer.
  • Structural exam reform proposals: Watch whether the Lee Jae-myung government and regional education chiefs move beyond rhetoric to outline concrete pilot changes to Korea's multiple-choice college entrance system before next November arrives.

For now, proctors standing at classroom doors scrutinizing eyeglass hinges capture a country caught between two eras. South Korea has mobilized massive human resources to defend the integrity of an old exam—buying itself time, perhaps, to decide what merit and ability should look like in an age when answers are built into the glass.
