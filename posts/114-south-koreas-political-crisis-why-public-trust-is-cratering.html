South Korea’s Political Crisis: Why Public Trust is Cratering

September 29, 2026

The Chuseok harvest holiday—typically a time for families to gather and for politicians to gauge the mood of their constituents—has ended, but the air in Seoul feels heavy. For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the post-holiday return to work has been marked by a chilling reality: the President’s approval ratings are dipping below their psychological support levels, and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s main liberal party, is sliding right along with him.

Lawmakers returning from local markets across the country brought back stories that were far from celebratory. From frustrated merchants calling for a change in leadership to internal factional infighting, the political class seems increasingly detached from the economic pain of the people. Rather than focusing on livelihoods, the political arena has retreated into a self-destructive cycle of infighting and blame-shifting.

The Warning from the Markets: A Cost-of-Living Crisis

During the holiday, politicians visited traditional markets—the heart of Korea’s grassroots economy—from Yeongju to Jeonju. The message they heard was unanimous: "We are struggling to survive." While lawmakers often try to spin these visits as proof of their connection to the people, the reality on the ground is stark. As of 2026, the national consumer price index has risen by 3.1% year-on-year, a figure that fails to capture the agony of families facing a dramatic surge in grocery prices. For the average person, the cost of living has long since surpassed the limits of household budgets.

Constituents didn't offer grand visions for the state; they offered blunt warnings. Voters criticized the party’s constant internal bickering, urging the DPK to focus on supporting the Lee administration so it can actually get to work. Most notably, supporters warned against "arrogance," noting that any small gap in the administration’s armor could lead to a catastrophic political backlash. While politicians in Yeouido—Seoul’s political district—are busy running calculators, the public has already arrived at a much harsher verdict.

Recent polling reflects this exhaustion. A survey by Korea Gallup for KBS shows President Lee’s approval rating at 42%, with 51% disapproving. Other surveys using automated systems (ARS) show approval dropping into the mid-30s, crossing a critical threshold. The most alarming trend is that the ruling DPK’s own support is falling in tandem. The public no longer distinguishes between the President’s office and the ruling party; they view them as a single entity responsible for the current lack of competence in managing the nation’s crises.

Leadership Vacuum and the Danger of Internal Infighting

The DPK’s leadership, including Party Chairman Kim Min-seok, is facing a severe legitimacy crisis. With job performance ratings of just 29% approval versus 51% disapproval, the leadership is increasingly seen as a liability rather than a shield for the President.

Internal divisions have reached a fever pitch. While moderate voices like former National Assembly Speaker Woo Won-shik have called for a cessation of hostilities, the party remains fractured. The obsession with "loyalty competitions"—debating who is "truly pro-Lee"—has become a form of political self-harm. By labeling dissenting voices as "traitors" or "divisive elements," the party is shrinking its own base and alienating moderate voters.

President Lee Jae-myung’s recent social media activity has only added fuel to the fire. In a lengthy post, he called on supporters to act as "the eyes and ears" of the truth, fighting back against a "manipulated minority" in what he described as a "low-quality political battlefield." While his base took this as a rallying cry, many saw it as a risky move. By framing the landscape as a battle between "friends and foes," the President risks further deepening the divide rather than serving as the leader of all citizens.

The Clash Over Reform and the Judiciary

Two major issues have further polarized the current landscape:

  • The Role of Intellectual Critics: Commentator Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal figure, recently faced backlash from the party’s hardline base for criticizing the President. However, polls show that 58% of the public view Rhyu’s comments as "constructive criticism," rather than "unfounded undermining." This suggests that the public is desperate for honest advice, not just blind sycophancy.
  • The Judiciary Battle: There is a massive demand among DPK supporters for the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. In recent online polls, support for such a move reached 95%. Supporters view the current judicial structure as a roadblock to reform, particularly regarding high-profile cases involving First Lady Kim Keon-hee. While party strategists fear the legal and political fallout of a total judicial paralysis, the base is growing increasingly impatient with "cautious" approaches.

Foreign Policy and the "Korea Passing" Risk

While the DPK is preoccupied with internal squabbles, the administration is facing a major diplomatic headache. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy recently disclosed a sensitive intelligence matter regarding North Korean prisoners, a move that blindsided Seoul. The government had maintained a carefully neutral stance to avoid becoming entangled in the conflict and to protect its own delicate diplomatic standing. By dragging South Korea into its own political narrative, Ukraine has highlighted the danger of "Korea passing"—a situation where Seoul is sidelined in international negotiations.

The lack of a coherent response from the President's office is being blamed on the current "two-top vacuum," where both the Chief of Staff and the Policy Chief remain in limbo following failed personnel appointments. Without a functioning control tower, the administration is struggling to respond to both domestic economic pain and external diplomatic pressures.

What to Watch Next

The path forward for the Lee administration and the DPK is narrow. To recover, they must look beyond the "Yeouido bubble" and address three critical areas:

  • Personnel Reform: The administration must move quickly to fill the vacancies in the Blue House with competent, fresh faces and move away from the failed personnel vetting systems that led to this crisis.
  • From Conflict to Competence: The party must pivot from internal "loyalty wars" to tangible legislative achievements. The public does not care who is the most "pro-Lee"; they care about the price of groceries and the state of the economy.
  • Judicial Roadmap: Rather than just shouting for impeachment, the party needs a concrete, legal roadmap for judicial reform that addresses systemic issues like the concentration of power within the Court Administration Office.

The coming months will serve as a litmus test. If the government continues to prioritize silencing internal critics and blaming others, it will inevitably face a storm it cannot navigate. The message from the Chuseok markets was clear: the people are waiting for leadership, not more excuses.
