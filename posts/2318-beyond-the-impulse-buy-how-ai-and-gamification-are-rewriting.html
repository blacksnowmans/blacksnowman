Beyond the Impulse Buy: How AI and Gamification are Rewriting South Korea's Live Commerce Playbook

November 14, 2026

Picture sitting in your living room, scrolling through your phone as a livestream begins. But instead of a human host holding up a bottle of serum, the screen features a hyper-realistic, AI-generated hamster avatar with 710,000 followers, effortlessly chatting with viewers and clearing out its entire inventory in under ten minutes. At the same time, miles away in Seoul’s corporate boardrooms, major retailers are quietly dropping the frantic discount wars that defined the pandemic era, pivoting instead toward sophisticated entertainment, gamified travel booking, and relentless global expansion.

This is not a scene from a science fiction novel. It is the snapshot of South Korea's rapidly maturing e-commerce ecosystem. For years, the country's online retail market was a battlefield of sheer price aggression and overnight delivery races, spearheaded by giants like Coupang. But as recent industry developments reveal, the paradigm has shifted. Live commerce in Korea is no longer just about shouting deals into a camera; it has transformed into a high-stakes arena where artificial intelligence, deep influencer partnerships, and gamified user experiences dictate who wins and who gets left behind.

The AI Revolution: From Chatbots to Livestream Hosts and Global Translators

When Cafe24, a major South Korean e-commerce platform builder, hosted its "Expand 2027" strategy event, the message to the market was unequivocal: artificial intelligence is no longer a peripheral novelty or a helpful administrative assistant. It has become the core engine driving revenue. By 2027, the platform aims to equip independent merchants—many of whom suffer from chronic labor shortages—with automated solutions capable of handling everything from content generation to customer service without requiring deep technical expertise.

This push toward automation is bleeding directly into the consumer-facing side of live commerce. Take the phenomenal success of "Kim Hamzzi," an AI-generated creator boasting 710,000 followers created by Shinsegae Department Store, one of South Korea's premier luxury department store chains. When Kim Hamzzi hosted a pre-reservation stream, inventory sold out in a blistering ten minutes. The fact that an entirely artificial persona can command this level of consumer trust and drive immediate purchasing behavior signals a profound psychological shift in how shoppers interact with digital media.

Simultaneously, the physical barriers of broadcasting are crumbling through real-time AI translation. Technologies promoted by firms like Mobedu are shifting toward an "always-on" global model where AI handles multi-language interpretation on the fly. For sellers, this means the traditional reliance on a charismatic human host with silver-tongued sales pitches is taking a backseat. The new competitive edge belongs to those who can cleverly combine proprietary AI intellectual property with unique digital content, proving that technology is evolving not to erase human creativity, but to supercharge it.

Celebrity Power and Gamification: Keeping Viewers Hooked

If AI provides the backend muscle, compelling content provides the front-end gravity. Data released by Shinsegae Live Shopping, the home shopping subsidiary of the retail giant, offers hard numerical proof as to why the industry is obsessed with securing famous personalities. Beauty broadcasts featuring genuine celebrities pulled in viewer counts 23% higher than standard, host-led broadcasts. This gap highlights a vital truth about modern retail: consumers do not just want a sales pitch; they want the borrowed trust and specialized expertise that a credible public figure provides.

Brands are also realizing that viewers will stick around if you turn shopping into a game. 11st (Elevenia), a major South Korean e-commerce marketplace operated by SK Planet, recently introduced "PlayTrip," a travel booking feature infused with gamification elements. By stepping away from boring lists of flight times and hotel rooms and forcing users to actively participate and make choices like a game, the company saw its order conversion rate skyrocket to a staggering six times higher than conventional methods.

When shopping feels like a game rather than a chore, psychological purchase barriers dissolve. In high-involvement categories like travel, a sixfold increase in conversion proves that passive viewing is dying.

This integration of entertainment extends even to traditional food and beverage brands. Fast-food giant bhc collaborated with Shinsegae Live Shopping to introduce variety-show elements into its food broadcasts. By leaning heavily on visual and auditory ASMR elements—the ultimate "mukbang" style presentation—they successfully anchored viewers to their screens, treating the livestream as an entertainment event rather than a digital catalog.

The Battle for Profitability and the Global K-Beauty Export Wave

While the front-end dazzles with AI and games, the back-end reality of Korean e-commerce is experiencing a much-needed reality check. For years, massive marketing expenditures and logistics investments crippled balance sheets across the industry. Now, the narrative has pivoted firmly toward structural profitability.

Market watchers have welcomed news that Kurly, the pioneer of dawn-delivery grocery shopping in South Korea, has successfully transitioned past its initial loss-making phase into sustained margin expansion. Similarly, competitor Oasis Market reported a 143% surge in third-quarter operating profit, validating a disciplined approach to logistics efficiency. These successes serve as a warning to smaller players: chasing raw gross merchandise value (GMV) while bleeding cash is no longer a viable path forward.

However, margin pressures remain real. Brands like d'Alba Global are facing noticeable profit margin compression in the fourth quarter, illustrating that aggressive marketing can easily turn into a double-edged sword. To escape domestic margin squeezes, Korean brands are looking outward with unprecedented aggression.

TikTok Shop is aggressively expanding its global footprint, sparking fierce competition for talent in markets like Vietnam—one of the world's most vibrant live commerce battlegrounds. Meanwhile, Korean beauty brands continue their unstoppable march across Western markets. Skincare labels selected by Olive Young, South Korea's dominant health and beauty store chain, now account for a striking 17% of total skincare sales at Sephora in the United States. Propelled by global platforms and high-profile endorsements—such as actor Lee Jung-jae of Squid Game fame partnering with TikTok to push cosmetics into the US and Japanese markets—Korean consumer goods are proving that their ultimate destination is the global stage.

What to Watch Next

  • The Balance of AI and Authenticity: Monitor how consumer fatigue reacts to AI-generated avatars. Will novelty wear off, forcing brands to ground their virtual creators in deeper narrative arcs?
  • Profitability vs. Growth Metrics: As Kurly and Oasis Market hold the line on margins, watch to see which mid-tier e-commerce players stumble from over-expansion versus those that successfully adopt lean logistics.
  • Global Platform Battles: Keep an eye on how Korean cross-border sellers navigate the fierce competition between TikTok Shop and local e-commerce giants in Southeast Asian markets like Vietnam.
  • Gamified Commerce Adoption: Look for whether 11st’s sixfold conversion spike with PlayTrip inspires a wider wave of interactive, game-like shopping features across fashion and electronics categories.
