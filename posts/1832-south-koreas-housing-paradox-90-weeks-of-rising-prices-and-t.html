South Korea’s Housing Paradox: 90 Weeks of Rising Prices and the End of the "Housing Ladder"

November 02, 2026

For a young professional or a growing family in Seoul, the dream of homeownership feels less like a goal and more like a receding horizon. Imagine checking the real estate listings every Friday for nearly two years, only to find that prices have ticked upward every single time. This is not a hypothetical scenario; as of November 1, apartment prices in Seoul have officially risen for 90 consecutive weeks. It is a streak that defies cooling measures, high interest rates, and the government’s own rhetoric of "normalization."

The situation has reached a boiling point where the psychological exhaustion of the public is clashing with the political strategy of the Blue House. President Lee Jae-myung, representing the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), recently signaled that the government is "enduring" this period to ensure the long-term stabilization of the asset market. However, for those living in districts like Nowon or Seongbuk, where monthly rents are skyrocketing by double digits, "endurance" is a luxury they can no longer afford. The "housing ladder"—the traditional path of moving from monthly rent to a lump-sum deposit (Jeonse) and finally to homeownership—is effectively snapping in half.

The President’s "Endurance" vs. Public Anxiety

In a recent public message, President Lee Jae-myung shared an outlook suggesting a future decline in Seoul’s housing prices, accompanied by a note that the government is currently "holding its ground" to normalize the asset market. This framing suggests that the current high prices are a bubble that the administration is waiting to burst through supply-side pressure and regulatory discipline. For the Lee administration, "normalization" means decoupled growth—ensuring that housing becomes a utility rather than a speculative vehicle.

However, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has been quick to criticize this stance as being "out of touch." They argue that calling a 90-week price hike a "normalization process" is an insult to the millions of non-homeowners who are watching their purchasing power evaporate. The political stakes are high; housing was the decisive issue that led to the downfall of the previous administration and the subsequent imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol. President Lee is acutely aware that his political capital depends on cooling the Seoul market without causing a systemic financial collapse.

The market’s reaction to the President’s "endurance" message is polarized. While some analysts believe the government’s firm hand on lending will eventually force prices down, others point to the "balloon effect." As the government tightens regulations on high-end districts like Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa (often called the "Gangnam Trio"), demand simply spills over into the surrounding areas, driving up prices in the suburbs and northern Seoul.

The Death of Jeonse and the Rise of the "Wolse" Crisis

To understand why this matters to the average Korean, one must understand Jeonse. In this unique Korean system, a tenant provides the landlord with a massive lump-sum deposit—often 60% to 80% of the property’s value—instead of paying monthly rent. The landlord earns interest on that money, and the tenant gets the full amount back after two years. For decades, Jeonse was the primary way Koreans saved for a home. By not paying monthly rent, they could accumulate wealth.

That system is now in crisis. A shortage of Jeonse listings, fueled by high interest rates and fears of "Jeonse fraud" (where landlords cannot return the deposit), has forced tenants into Wolse, or traditional monthly rent. The result has been catastrophic for low-to-middle-income earners. In Nowon-gu, a northern district known for being a bastion of middle-class families, monthly rents have surged by 12.9% this year. Seongbuk-gu followed closely with an 11.8% increase.

In these districts, monthly rents for modest apartments are now reaching 3 million KRW (about $2,200). For a young worker earning the median salary, this can consume more than half of their take-home pay. This "Wolse-ification" of the market means that the money previously saved under the Jeonse system is now being handed over to landlords as an expense. When people cannot save, they cannot buy. The housing ladder is being replaced by a treadmill.

Greenbelts and the Battle for Supply

The Lee administration’s primary solution to this crisis is a massive increase in supply. The government recently announced plans to lift "Greenbelt" restrictions—protected land intended to prevent urban sprawl—to build 37,000 new homes in Gimpo and nearly 10,000 in Gwacheon. While the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) frames this as a necessary sacrifice for the greater good, the local response has been one of fierce resistance.

In Gimpo, residents are furious. Their argument is simple: you cannot build 37,000 homes without building the trains to move the people. The extension of Seoul Subway Line 5 to Gimpo has been stalled in bureaucratic limbo for years. Residents fear that the new housing will turn their city into a "bed-town" nightmare with even more soul-crushing commutes. Meanwhile, in Gwacheon, Mayor Shin Kye-yong has officially opposed the plan, arguing that the central government is infringing on local autonomy and urban planning.

There is also the issue of land compensation. Landowners in the Seoripul district and other newly designated sites are protesting what they call "forced expropriation" at below-market rates. These legal and social battles mean that while the government can announce 37,000 homes today, those homes likely won’t be ready for occupancy for another decade. For the person facing a rent hike next month, these "ghost homes" offer no relief.

The Debt Trap and the "Lottery" Mentality

Despite the government’s efforts to tighten the taps, household debt in South Korea is rising again. Major commercial banks have seen their loan balances increase by over 1 trillion KRW (about $725 million) in the last month alone. The "FOMO" (Fear Of Missing Out) effect is powerful; many believe that if they don’t buy now, they will be priced out forever.

However, even those who "win" the right to buy a new apartment—often through a highly competitive "subscription" (Cheongnyak) lottery system—are finding themselves in financial distress. Interest rates for "mid-payment" loans (loans taken during the construction phase) have climbed to around 5%. For a family that thought they had won the "lottery" of a new home, the monthly interest payments are becoming a crushing burden. This creates a "rich get richer" scenario where only those with significant cash reserves can comfortably navigate the market, while the middle class is squeezed between high rents and high debt.

Furthermore, the government is currently accepting appeals for Gongsi Jiga, or the officially assessed land price, until November 27. These assessments are critical because they determine property taxes, comprehensive real estate taxes, and even National Health Insurance premiums. With the market in such a volatile state, many property owners are bracing for a tax hit that doesn't necessarily reflect their actual "liquid" wealth, but rather the speculative value of the roof over their heads.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the final months of the year, the stability of the Lee administration’s "endurance" strategy will be tested by three key factors:

  • The Subway Line 5 Decision: If the government can fast-track infrastructure for Gimpo and other "New Towns," it may appease local resistance and make the new supply plans credible. Without it, the supply announcements will remain mere paper promises.
  • Rental Market Legislation: Watch for whether the DPK-controlled National Assembly introduces new subsidies or caps for monthly rents (Wolse) in the northern districts of Seoul. The 12% rent hikes are becoming a primary driver of public dissatisfaction.
  • Bank Lending Caps: The Financial Services Commission (FSC) is under pressure to further tighten lending. However, if they squeeze too hard, they risk a "credit crunch" for actual end-users who need to move for work or family reasons.

The South Korean housing market is no longer just about real estate; it is a battle over the country's social contract. If the government cannot find a way to repair the housing ladder, the 90-week streak of rising prices will be remembered not just as a statistical anomaly, but as the moment the Korean dream became unaffordable for a generation.
