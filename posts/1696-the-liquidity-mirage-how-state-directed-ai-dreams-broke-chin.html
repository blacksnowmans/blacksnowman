The Liquidity Mirage: How State-Directed AI Dreams Broke China’s Tech Rally

October 29, 2026

When the People’s Bank of China and Beijing’s top financial regulators rolled out an aggressive stimulus package in late 2024, mainland trading floors erupted in euphoria. The explicit message from authorities was unmistakable: the state was stepping in to use equity markets as an active instrument of economic revival. Around the same time, authorities formally closed their grueling three-year antitrust crusade against Alibaba, having extracted an 18.2 billion yuan (around $2.5 billion) penalty. To domestic retail traders and foreign funds hunting for a rebound, it looked as though the shackles had finally been removed. The consensus narrative hardened overnight: Beijing had changed its tune, and China’s artificial intelligence champions were about to challenge Silicon Valley on equal financial footing.

Yet that celebration has unraveled with shocking speed. Within months, benchmark equity gauges surrendered virtually all of their post-stimulus gains, tumbling back toward the depressed levels of mid-2025. The CSI 300, mainland China’s blue-chip index, plunged more than 20% from its recent peak into technical bear territory. For outside observers—particularly those familiar with South Korea’s own developmental past—the dynamic is painfully recognizable. It is the classic East Asian story of state-directed capitalism colliding with market reality: liquidity poured into an artificial boom cannot compensate for weak underlying corporate earnings, anemic consumer demand, and a financial apparatus designed to serve national policy goals rather than return capital to shareholders.

Trapped Domestic Capital and the "Paper" Benchmark Trap

To understand the violent swings in Chinese tech stocks, one has to examine the psychological trap gripping domestic retail investors. Throughout late 2024 and early 2025, global AI infrastructure trades were minting fortunes in New York and Seoul. While Nvidia powered Wall Street and memory giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix surged on the back of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) demand, ordinary Chinese retail investors were locked out by Beijing’s rigid cross-border capital controls. In mainland investing forums, a single question echoed endlessly: how could domestic capital buy into the semiconductor giants powering global AI?

Unable to access foreign exchanges, that desperate liquidity swirled inward, engineering bizarre, localized speculative bubbles. The most striking example occurred in optical transceivers. Companies such as Zhongji Innolight and Eoptolink saw their share prices multiply three- to four-fold simply because they supplied components to Western hyperscalers and Nvidia. While these firms possessed genuine manufacturing capabilities, the valuation spike was driven by an unhinged concentration of trapped mainland capital. As soon as the global semiconductor cycle took a breath, the bubble burst, sending these optical module darlings down nearly 60% from their record highs.

A similar gap between perception and reality plagued China's foundation model race. Alibaba had presented itself as the standard-bearer of Chinese AI, spending 2024 and early 2025 unveiling iterations of its Tongyi Qianwen (Qwen) family, culminating in the massive, 2.4-trillion-parameter Qwen 3.8-Max. Market cheerleaders pointed to glowing scores on developer benchmarks like PaperBench (academic paper reproduction) and IFBench (instruction following), claiming Alibaba’s models were on par with Anthropic’s Claude or OpenAI’s flagship systems.

Yet when tested against frontier reasoning evaluations, the facade cracked. On Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE)—a benchmark designed to test multi-domain, expert-level academic and reasoning limits—Alibaba’s model managed a meager 43.6 points, landing dead last among the world’s four top-tier flagship model families. The shortfall underscored a stubborn truth: while state-subsidized tech giants can build heavy models optimized for standardized benchmarks, physical limits on compute power—worsened by US advanced GPU export curbs—have severely constrained genuine foundational reasoning.

The Rise of Lean Startups and the Devastation of the STAR Market

Disillusionment with legacy tech platforms has prompted a quiet realignment. Developers and capital have increasingly migrated toward nimble, specialized startups like DeepSeek and Moonshot, as well as Hong Kong-listed pure-play AI ventures like MiniMax and ZetAI. Rather than pursuing brute-force parameter expansion, DeepSeek (with its V4 Flash model) and Moonshot (with Kimi K3) focused on extreme architectural efficiency and lightweight inference. By outperforming heavyweights on practical coding and domain-specific tasks with far less hardware, these upstarts proved that algorithmic agility could partially offset hardware starvation. Yet they offer no refuge for equity investors: DeepSeek remains unlisted, while Hong Kong-traded peers have been pummeled by the broader tech hardware sell-off and China’s macroeconomic freeze.

Nowhere has Beijing’s heavy-handed market management produced more collateral damage than on the Shanghai STAR Market (the Sci-Tech Innovation Board, or Kechuangban). Conceived in 2019 under President Xi Jinping, the board was established as China’s answer to Nasdaq, designed with relaxed listing thresholds so that early-stage, loss-making semiconductor and hard-tech innovators could raise capital to counter US sanctions—a mandate akin to South Korea’s KOSDAQ tech-exemption track.

Instead of nurturing young innovators, however, policymakers treated the STAR Market like an industrial dumping ground. In an effort to demonstrate self-reliance achievements, regulators pushed ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT)—China’s premier state-backed DRAM champion—onto the junior board rather than Shanghai’s main board. In financial terms, this was the equivalent of taking SK Hynix, South Korea’s $100-billion memory behemoth, and dropping it directly onto the fragile KOSDAQ.

The consequences were mathematically catastrophic:

  • Liquidity cannibalization: CXMT sucked in astronomical sums of capital, draining the lifeblood from surrounding fabless chip designers and enterprise software firms. The market simply lacked the depth to absorb a state champion of that magnitude.
  • Index collapse: Compounded by the high-profile listing of humanoid robotics maker Unitree (which drew an absurd 8,000-to-1 retail subscription ratio), the STAR 50 index collapsed by more than 40% from its summer high, erasing over $480 billion in equity value.
  • Post-IPO wipeout: The newly minted darlings fared no better. Within a month of its debut, CXMT shed 27% from its peak. Unitree dropped 50%, while domestic GPU contenders like MetaX and Moore Threads—which initially soared hundreds of percent on nationalistic fervor—saw their shares halved.

This dynamic mirrors the fate of foundry giant SMIC, which listed on the STAR Market in 2020 only to spend the next four years languishing near its lows. When state prestige dictates market structure, capital is destroyed rather than allocated.

Predatory Finance and the Absence of Shareholder Value

The overarching flaw of China’s equity market is not technological; it is institutional. Mainland exchanges do not function as arenas for corporate governance and shareholder return. They operate as state-directed capital extraction mechanisms, designed to socialize the enormous financial risks of achieving technological self-sufficiency.

This year alone, initial public offerings on mainland exchanges raised roughly $37 billion, marking a three-year high, with more than a third concentrated in tech firms. Whenever liquidity flickered back into the market, authorities opened the listing floodgates, unloading state-affiliated hardware ventures onto eager retail accounts. The state gets its research and fabrication facilities funded; corporate insiders offload risk; and everyday investors are left holding devalued paper.

A Historical Parallel: South Korea has weathered its own painful encounters with this playbook. The authoritarian military regimes of the late 20th century routinely used state-directed finance (gwanchi geumyung) to pick winners and force market outcomes, leaving behind bad debts and deep market distortions. More recently, during the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—Korean retail investors faced bitter frustration as financial authorities neglected structural market fairness and minority protections. Policies that allowed conglomerates to spin off prized business units into duplicative listings without compensating parent-company shareholders deepened the notorious "Korea Discount." China is executing that same extraction at an unprecedented, state-mandated scale.

This predatory structure is colliding with a frozen real economy. While Beijing has mobilized a 60 billion yuan ($8.3 billion) state fund to underwrite AI and robotics, domestic consumers hit by the prolonged property downturn have no disposable income to spend on AI-driven gadgets or enterprise software. Companies are locked in destructive domestic price wars, and export avenues remain blocked by US trade restrictions.

Foreign institutional capital has drawn its conclusions. International investors dumped approximately 90 billion yuan (around $12.5 billion) in mainland equities via Stock Connect links in the third quarter alone, wiping out over half of the net foreign inflows recorded earlier in the year. Even after its 40% plunge, the STAR 50 index trades at roughly 100 times earnings—down from a dizzying 250 times, but still more than triple the valuation of the US Nasdaq 100, which trades near 30 times with vastly superior cash flows.

What to Watch Next

As the artificial excitement of state-led stimulus clears, global investors tracking East Asian technology should watch three specific fault lines to gauge whether China’s AI sector can find an organic floor:

  • STAR Market IPO Pause: Watch whether the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) quietly halts or throttles mega-cap tech listings. Until authorities turn off the supply spigot that allowed CXMT and GPU makers to vacuum up market liquidity, small- and mid-cap tech valuations cannot stabilize.
  • Commercial Monetization Beyond the State: Monitor enterprise software revenues from Alibaba Cloud and listed software aggregators. If tech adoption is limited to state-owned enterprise (SOE) procurement contracts rather than private, margin-accretive corporate adoption, domestic AI revenue will remain an accounting illusion.
  • Cross-Border Flow Reversals: Pay attention to net monthly foreign institutional flows through the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Connect programs. Sustainable recoveries in East Asian tech require global institutional sponsorship; as long as foreign funds view Chinese tech as a high-multiple extraction trap, any domestic rally will remain short-lived.
