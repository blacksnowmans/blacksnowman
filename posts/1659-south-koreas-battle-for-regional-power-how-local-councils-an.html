South Korea’s Battle for Regional Power: How Local Councils and Mega-Projects Are Challenging Seoul’s Grip

October 29, 2026

On any given weekday, the polished marble hallways of South Korea’s National Assembly in western Seoul are filled with lawmakers, lobbyists, and national journalists. Lately, however, a different contingent has been crowding the elevators: provincial councilors, rural county chiefs, and regional committee leaders carrying thick dossiers of petitions. From coastal mudflats in the southwest to mountain valleys in the southeast, local politicians are descending on the capital with an urgent message: the provinces can no longer survive on crumbs handed down from the center.

South Korea is one of the most geographically unbalanced democracies in the developed world. More than half of its 51 million residents live crammed into the Greater Seoul metropolitan area, which occupies barely an eighth of the country's land. Outside this economic magnet, towns and small cities face the looming specter of "local extinction"—a demographic collapse driven by ultra-low birth rates and the steady drain of young people toward Seoul's corporate campuses. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, regional revitalization has become a flagship priority. Now, local legislative bodies are taking advantage of this political opening, demanding legal autonomy, resurrected megaprojects, and permanent funding to secure their own survival.

The Fight for Autonomy: Why Local Councils Want Their Own Law

To understand why provincial assemblies are currently marching on Seoul, one must look at how local democracy is structured in South Korea. While the country restored direct local elections in the 1990s after decades of military dictatorship, the devolution of power has remained fundamentally lopsided. Mayors and provincial governors command immense executive authority and sweeping budgets, but the local councils tasked with holding them accountable operate with their hands tied.

Under the existing Local Autonomy Act, council secretariats are largely dependent on the very regional executives they are supposed to monitor. Councils lack independent budgetary control and full personnel authority. Local lawmakers often refer to their mandate as "half-baked autonomy," arguing that without the power to hire their own independent policy researchers or draft their own operating budgets, they are reduced to rubber-stamping mayoral initiatives.

Now, a coordinated national campaign is underway to change that. Regional and municipal legislatures across the country—including the Incheon Metropolitan Council, the Gyeonggi Provincial Council, and the South Chungcheong Provincial Council—are demanding that the National Assembly pass a dedicated Local Council Act before the end of the year. Delegations from smaller districts, such as Incheon's Geomdan and northern border counties like Yeoncheon, have made direct lobbying trips to parliament.

The push has taken on strong partisan momentum. The governing Democratic Party of Korea, which holds the presidency and champions administrative decentralization, recently launched a nationwide council of metropolitan legislators to give provincial politicians a direct pipeline into central party decision-making. Lawmakers like Nam Jong-seop in Gyeonggi Province have shuttled back and forth between provincial capitals and the National Assembly, joining forces with delegations from the southwestern strongholds of Gwangju and South Jeolla Province. The goal is to establish local assemblies as a co-equal legislative branch capable of checking executive overreach and addressing local crises without waiting for a nod from Seoul.

Resurrecting Saemangeum: A 15-Year Fast-Forward in the Southwest

If legislative autonomy is the political battleground, large-scale infrastructure remains the economic lifeblood of the provinces. Nowhere is this clearer than at Saemangeum, an enormous reclamation project on the southwestern coast of North Jeolla Province.

Begun in 1991, Saemangeum involved constructing a 33.9-kilometer sea wall—the world’s longest—to turn vast tidal mudflats into commercial, industrial, and agricultural land. For over three decades, the project has served as both a symbol of regional hope and a byword for bureaucratic delays and broken political promises. The area suffered a devastating blow in August 2023, during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, when the botched World Scout Jamboree was held on the undeveloped site. Following the high-profile logistical fiasco, the central government slashed state budgets for Saemangeum's infrastructure, sparking bitter resentment across North Jeolla.

The Lee Jae-myung administration is now moving aggressively to reverse course. At a recent meeting of the Saemangeum Committee chaired by Prime Minister Han Seong-sook, the government unveiled an ambitious roadmap: slashing the projected completion timeline by 15 years, moving the final target from 2050 to 2035.

Crucially, the administration is abandoning the previous model, which relied on enticing reluctant private investors to build essential infrastructure on empty land. Instead, the government is shifting to a state-led, public development framework. Central ministries, led by Minister Park Hong-geun alongside regional lawmakers like Lee Won-taek, are pitching the reclaimed zone as a hub for next-generation industries, including physical AI, renewable energy, and carbon-neutral manufacturing. By front-loading national spending to build deep-water ports, railways, and airport links, the government hopes to remove the risk for high-tech manufacturing firms and create an economic anchor that can counterbalance the Seoul capital region.

Sejong’s Decisive Moment and the Scramble for State Funding

Saemangeum is not the only grand project on the operating table. Approximately 120 kilometers south of Seoul sits Sejong City, established two decades ago as an administrative center to decentralize government functions. While dozens of ministries have relocated there, Sejong remains an incomplete experiment: the Blue House, the National Assembly, and several key regulatory commissions have remained anchored in Seoul, forcing civil servants into endless highway commutes between the two cities.

That logjam could soon break. The National Assembly is scheduled to begin reviewing the long-delayed Special Act on the Administrative Capital in mid-November. The proposed legislation would provide the statutory foundation to turn Sejong into the nation's true de facto capital by transferring critical national institutions. The Lee administration and the ruling Democratic Party have pledged to prioritize the bill, though passing it will require navigating delicate negotiations with the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party.

Meanwhile, the debate over specific institutional moves remains contentious. Critics have pointed out that the central government has remained noncommittal on relocating the Nuclear Safety and Security Commission to Sejong, highlighting how difficult it is to pry powerful regulatory watchdogs away from Seoul's corridors of power.

For municipal leaders elsewhere, the focus is less on grand constitutional questions and more on immediate fiscal survival. Across South Korea, regional heads are engaged in a fierce war for national grant funding:

  • Suwon: Officials in this major industrial city south of Seoul are holding policy forums in the National Assembly to push a special law enabling the long-stalled relocation of a military airfield, aiming to unlock land for southern Gyeonggi Province's expanding tech belt.
  • Southeastern Alliances: In the Busan-Ulsan-South Gyeongsang region, conservative-leaning county councils in rural areas like Sancheong, Hamyang, Geochang, and Hapcheon have formed a rare united front to demand state feasibility approval for a regional Red Cross hospital, warning of a total collapse in emergency medical care.
  • Renewable Grid Integration: In Damyang, a rural county in the southwest, officials are pleading for national subsidies for community solar villages, unable to connect clean energy installations to an outdated regional electricity grid managed by state utilities.

The Dark Side of Local Politics: Petty Grudges and Gridlock

Yet for all the impassioned rhetoric about empowerment, the case for local autonomy is frequently undermined by the behavior of local politicians themselves. Even as county heads plead for hospitals and basic infrastructure, several local councils have ground to a dead halt due to bitter factional infighting over committee assignments and parliamentary perks.

In Daegu's Nam-gu district—a traditional conservative bastion—the People Power Party’s regional headquarters launched disciplinary proceedings against two of its own district councilors following a chaotic election for the council speakership. In Incheon's Yeonsu-gu district, business has stalled completely after councilors from the conservative opposition walked out over a dispute regarding who would chair the ethics committee. In Daejeon's Daedeok district, the frustration boiled over publicly when the district mayor openly berated party floor leaders, demanding to know why urgent welfare spending and local livelihood ordinances were being held hostage to internal squabbles over the speaker's chair.

"When council chambers freeze over internal prestige and party rivalries, it is the residents who pay the price through delayed budgets and abandoned public services."

National political distrust continues to cast a long shadow over local governance as well. Nearly five months after the legislative elections, partisans gathered at Seoul’s Olympic Park for a tense, public hand-count verification of unsealed ballot boxes, exchanging insults and accusations of electoral fraud. Despite the introduction of 68 different reform bills aimed at overhauling the National Election Commission and restoring public confidence, the National Assembly has left every single one unaddressed.

Against this backdrop of cynicism, occasional bipartisan breakthroughs and humanitarian legislation provide a different narrative. Lawmaker Joo Cheol-hyun recently introduced measures to expand state compensation and official rehabilitation for the surviving families of the Yeosu-Suncheon October 19 Incident, a tragic 1948 military uprising and civilian massacre that remains one of modern Korean history's deepest unresolved wounds. Simultaneously, lawmakers have advanced bills to require direct national funding for regional firefighting services—ensuring that local fire stations are not starved of modern gear simply because their municipal tax base is small.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether this flurry of regional advocacy translates into permanent institutional change or fades into familiar political theatre. Several key milestones will signal which way the wind is blowing:

  • The November 11 Committee Review: The parliamentary committee's handling of the Special Act on the Administrative Capital will reveal whether the Democratic Party and the People Power Party can reach a working consensus on upgrading Sejong City, or whether decentralization will once again be stalled by partisan gridlock.
  • The Year-End Budget Showdown: As the National Assembly debates the upcoming national budget, watch for the actual funding allocations granted to Saemangeum. The scale of upfront state expenditure for logistics infrastructure will show whether the Lee administration’s 2035 target is a serious developmental blueprint or an optimistic political forecast.
  • Floor Debates on the Local Council Act: With metropolitan and municipal councils warning of sustained collective protests, the Assembly's willingness to schedule and vote on independent personnel and budgetary powers for local chambers will test how serious Seoul’s political establishment really is about sharing power.

For decades, regional revitalization in South Korea has been an inspiring campaign slogan that consistently lost out to the economic and bureaucratic gravity of Seoul. As provincial assemblies demand institutional teeth and national mega-projects shift gears, the country is once again testing whether it can build a balanced republic—or whether the center will continue to hold all the cards.
