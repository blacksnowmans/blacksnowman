Why South Korea's Chip Giants Are Crashing Even as the AI Boom Accelerates

September 27, 2026

Open a retail brokerage account in South Korea right now, and you are unlikely to hear anything optimistic. Across trading apps and message boards, individual investors are singing a familiar, desperate refrain: "Just let my stock get back to my entry price, and I’m dumping every single share and walking away."

It is a textbook portrait of market-wide panic. Earlier this year, South Korea's benchmark Kospi index embarked on a dizzying sprint, surging from around 4,000 points toward 9,000. But that spectacular rally gave way to a brutal, month-long correction that has left retail investors—affectionately known locally as "ants"—nursing severe losses. In July alone, SK Hynix, the world's premier memory chipmaker and a dominant force in artificial intelligence infrastructure, saw its share price cut almost in half, plunging from 2.56 million won (about $1,800) down to roughly 1.32 million won ($940). Samsung Electronics, the country's heavyweight tech bellwether, slid from over 310,000 won ($220) to the low 200,000-won range (about $150). When the market's two apex predators stumble, the entire index inevitably follows.

The End of the Traditional Memory Cycle or the Arrival of the "Plateau"?

Historically, South Korea’s memory semiconductor industry has been defined by extreme volatility. For decades, DRAM chips moved in rigid three-to-four-year cycles of boom and bust. According to conventional timing models, next year—2027—was supposed to mark the ultimate cyclical peak, after which profits would inevitably tumble. Yet, industry insiders and analysts monitoring the current landscape argue that 2027 will not trigger a familiar crash, but rather mark a structural transition into an entirely new regime.

Speaking on the YouTube channel Knowledge Exchange Center, media commentator Sung Sang-hyun noted that the artificial intelligence industry remains in its absolute infancy. Given the immense capital pouring into AI infrastructure and the scalability of upcoming models, profits are unlikely to fall off a cliff. Instead, the market is entering a "plateau" phase, characterized by a sustained, high-level earnings plateau rather than a sharp cyclical correction.

This structural shift was echoed by Lee Seung-woo, head of research at Eugene Investment & Securities, who has tracked the semiconductor beat for 27 years. In a recent contribution to the Korea Development Institute (KDI), Lee pointed out that today's supply shortages bear little resemblance to past business cycles. During the PC and smartphone eras, memory chips were treated as basic cost variables. In the AI era, however, high-bandwidth memory has graduated to become the core infrastructure determining the performance of entire neural networks.

Because memory now functions as the cognitive engine of AI rather than mere passive storage, the old rules of oversupply and price collapse may no longer apply. Viewed through this lens, the recent violent sell-off is less a reflection of collapsing fundamentals and more a valuation breather following an overheated start to the year.

What the CEO of Phison Saw on a Whiteboard in Taiwan

To understand where the market is actually heading, it helps to look beyond domestic borders. Na Kwang-hyun, a journalist who recently visited Taiwan, sat down with the CEO of Phison Electronics, the world’s leading NAND controller design firm. When asked when the current memory super-cycle might finally burn out, the CEO’s blunt opening answer was, "I don't know."

However, drawing on executive intuition, he walked over to a whiteboard and sketched out a comparative index between 2026 and 2028. First, the global population of daily AI users is projected to double from 1 billion to 2 billion by 2028. Second, user interaction is rapidly evolving from text-based kilobytes to image- and video-heavy gigabytes and terabytes, causing memory requirements to multiply exponentially. Third, as AI use becomes a daily habit, frequency of interaction is skyrocketing.

Plug these three variables into any capacity model, and a clear picture emerges: token demand two years from now will far outstrip anything current production lines can possibly accommodate. Armed with this on-the-ground insight, the journalist returned home and promptly bought more SK Hynix shares during the absolute depths of the panic.

It raises an uncomfortable question: while industry insiders are quietly pressing the buy button during market panics, why are retail investors staring at their account balances in tears and dumping their holdings at a loss?

The Great Divergence Within the 2026 Chip Ecosystem

Beneath the broader market volatility lies a profound divergence in corporate fortunes. According to analyst Lee Hye-jin, data centers have solidified their position as the primary engine of semiconductor demand, growing at a robust 8% to 9% annually. Meanwhile, traditional consumer electronics markets like smartphones and PCs remain trapped in a sluggish 2-3% growth band.

NVIDIA has posted astronomical annual revenues approaching 308 trillion won (about $220 billion) on the back of insatiable data center demand, while SK Hynix—which commands the market for high-bandwidth memory (HBM)—has notched a staggering operating margin of 49%. Conversely, companies that failed to secure the advanced packaging technology required to produce HBM chips—which sell for multiples of standard DDR5 memory—have found themselves entirely sidelined amid a historic boom.

Layer on geopolitical friction, such as tightening US export controls against China and subsidies tied to domestic manufacturing investments, and the global supply chain is undergoing a permanent reconfiguration. The era when a company's stock price would rise simply because it manufactured generic memory chips has officially come to an end. Survival now depends entirely on whether a firm holds the high-end, ultra-expensive product lines required to build advanced AI systems.

The Tragedy of "Rescued" Retail Investors Running Away

Why are tech giants printing record profits while individual portfolios languish? The answer is straightforward: during the speculative expansion that pushed the Kospi toward 9,000 points earlier this year, stock prices outran corporate fundamentals. When regulatory authorities stepped in to rein in household credit and debt-fueled investing, market sentiment flipped overnight. The underlying fear—that any recovery in share price should be immediately met with profit-taking or loss-cutting before the market drops again—is now deeply ingrained. For leveraged investors trading on borrowed money rather than surplus cash, this level of volatility is a psychological nightmare.

Parallels to the Dot-Com Era and the Reality of US Fiscal Deficits

Wall Street adage holds that stock markets trade on the future. Have current equity valuations already fully priced in the long-term promises of the artificial intelligence boom?

Historical precedent offers a useful guide. During the PC adoption cycle of the late 1980s and early 1990s, massive capital expenditures were deployed long before productivity gains became visible. Similarly, during the massive telecommunications infrastructure build-out from 1995 to 1999, related equities experienced a parabolic surge. Yet, the period when economy-wide productivity growth actually accelerated above 3% coincided with the 1999–2003 window—ironically, the exact period when the dot-com bubble burst and stock prices plummeted.

Today’s hyperscalers—Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and others—are pouring trillions of dollars into data center construction. Their capital expenditure mirrors the fiber-optic infrastructure boom of the late 1990s. Capital is being deployed at a historic scale, even if everyday productivity gains are only just beginning to materialize. Declaring the AI industry "over" displays a fundamental misunderstanding of historical economic cycles.

The ultimate fuel driving asset prices, however, is liquidity. Just before the dot-com bubble burst in March 1999, the United States government was running a rare fiscal surplus. As the state sucked liquidity out of the private sector through taxes, the stock market balanced precariously on a combination of retail FOMO and leveraged debt. The moment those fiscal surpluses peaked, the market collapsed.

Today, Washington is moving in the exact opposite direction. According to long-term projections by the Congressional Budget Office, the US federal deficit is expected to reach -6.7% of GDP by 2036—nearly double the 50-year historical average. The US government is maintaining massive deficit spending during a non-crisis period for one clear reason: to wage a state-directed, planned-economy push to secure dominance in critical technology supply chains against China.

When a government runs massive fiscal deficits, that capital ultimately flows into private markets and corporate balance sheets. Traditional free-market capitalism has largely given way to a fragmented, state-directed global economy where government spending acts as the ultimate industrial lifeline. As long as this liquidity pipe remains open—and barring a severe credit crunch driven by sudden interest rate spikes—the fundamental thesis supporting the semiconductor industry remains intact.

Decoding Foreign Selling: Leaving South Korea vs. Trimming Leaders

When foreign institutional investors dump trillions of won worth of Korean equities and send the Kospi into a tailspin, domestic media outlets routinely warn of a "foreign capital exodus." A colder look at the underlying flow data, however, reveals a different reality.

Local database tracking shows that foreign net selling in the South Korean market during the first half of the year totaled roughly -89.2 trillion won (about $64 billion). Of that total, -50.4 trillion won was pulled from Samsung Electronics and -34.4 trillion won from SK Hynix. Combined, those two memory giants account for -84.8 trillion won of the outbound flow.

Strip away Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, and the total net selling by foreign investors across the rest of the South Korean market shrinks to just -4.4 trillion won. Foreign capital did not abandon South Korea as an investment destination; institutional funds were simply trimming their exposure to two mega-cap stocks that had experienced historic run-ups in valuation.

Even as foreign institutions unloaded shares in the two chip giants, steady net-buying was maintained in other sectors such as defense, shipbuilding, biotechnology, and power infrastructure. The underlying strength of the memory super-cycle remains intact, but the market is navigating a transitional phase where institutional and retail holders are locking in gains after a period of intense overheating.

Twin Speculative Markets: South Korea and Taiwan

South Korea and Taiwan share an industrial destiny, as well as a striking retail trading culture. At the end of March, retail investors accounted for 53.8% of total trading volume in Taiwan, while individual investors made up 55.2% of the Kospi over the past two years—vastly higher than the roughly 20% retail participation rates seen in the US or Japan. When Taiwanese equities hit record highs, margin debt surpassed 800 billion New Taiwan dollars (about $25 billion). At the exact same time, South Korea’s retail margin debt reached a record 38.6 trillion won (about $28 billion). In both markets, day trading accounts for well over 40% of total volume.

The Limits of Corporate Shielding and the Need for Governance Reform

The primary floor supporting South Korean equities during the recent correction has not been foreign buying, but aggressive share buyback programs by Samsung and SK Hynix. SK Hynix committed 40 trillion won ($29 billion) to purchase and retire 24.07 million shares, while Samsung introduced its own massive buyback initiative.

Yet, these corporate price-defense mechanisms are consuming capital faster than anticipated. At the current pace, buyback programs originally scheduled to run through November risk exhausting their allotted funds by mid-October. With overall market liquidity running dry, once these massive corporate safety nets disappear, the market will need to find a new source of buying support.

Ultimately, until domestic capital reserves regain momentum, the return of foreign institutional capital—driven by confidence in chip fundamentals—remains the only reliable catalyst for a sustained Kospi recovery. Upcoming signals from the US Federal Reserve and earnings guidance from US memory competitors like Micron Technology will likely dictate the direction of this fragile supply-and-demand balance.

For South Korean corporations, however, returning capital to shareholders requires moving beyond cosmetic gestures. The ultimate purpose of shareholder returns is corporate governance reform aimed at "re-rating" undervalued price-to-earnings multiples. For companies whose growth has matured, simply increasing cash dividends is an insufficient strategy.

True shareholder value creation rests on a straightforward mechanism: buying back shares and immediately retiring them. Reducing the total share count directly increases earnings per share (EPS). Over the past decade, Apple transformed its market valuation not merely through profit growth, but through continuous share reduction that drove explosive EPS growth and earned the trust of global investors.

If South Korea's tech leaders fully institutionalize this capital allocation model, it will mark a historic turning point—not just for individual corporate valuations, but for the structural discount that has long weighed on the broader South Korean market.

As retail investors stare at falling account balances and fight the urge to panic, the challenge is to look past short-term volatility and focus on broader industrial trends and liquidity cycles. The artificial intelligence build-out is far from over, and industrial fundamentals remain solid. The divergence in outcomes will belong to those who yield to panic and dump their assets at cost, versus those who understand historical cycles and hold fast to genuine underlying value.
