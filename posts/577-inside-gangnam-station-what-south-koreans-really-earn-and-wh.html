Inside Gangnam Station: What South Koreans Really Earn and Why They Are Exhausted

October 02, 2026

If you stand in the middle of Gangnam Station—Seoul's relentless commercial and financial heartbeat—it is easy to feel entirely invisible. Thousands of people rush past in tailored suits, oversized puffer jackets, and slick sneakers. They carry the distinct posture of people with places to be and deadlines to meet. But like most global capitals, Seoul is a city built on a strict, unspoken taboo: you never, under any circumstances, ask someone how much money is sitting in their bank account.

A recent street-interview video from a popular Korean YouTube channel broke that taboo wide open. Armed with a microphone and a simple premise—asking everyday pedestrians to reveal their job titles, years of experience, and exact take-home pay—the creator captured a startling cross-section of modern South Korean life. The participants ranged from major commercial bank employees and overworked junior lawyers to kindergarten teachers, tax law aspirants, and a 23-year-old arcade worker who has quietly saved about $75,000 (100 million won). 📊

To a foreign observer, these numbers offer a rare window past the glossy K-drama facade of Seoul into the actual math of living here. Behind every figure lies a distinct story of trade-offs, burnout, and the quiet pressure of surviving in one of the world's most hyper-competitive economies.

The Illusion of the Big Paycheck: Bankers and Lawyers

Few institutions in South Korea carry the dual aura of prestige and resentment quite like the commercial banking sector. A 31-year-old employee at Kookmin Bank, South Korea's largest commercial bank, stepped up to share the reality of his fifth year on the job. Rumors on anonymous worker apps like Blind often suggest that starting salaries at top banks clear 70 million to 80 million won (about $50,000 to $58,000), but he clarifies that such figures usually require aggressive loan-backed financing and maximum overtime.

In reality, his current annual salary sits around 90 million won (about $65,000)—just shy of the coveted 100-million-won mark, though promotion is technically on the horizon. That money does not come easy. To stay competitive, he has had to pass eight mandatory professional certifications while constantly studying for internal company exams. 📚

There is a public assumption that people working at the very center of the financial system must be financial wizards in their personal lives. When asked about his own wealth-building strategy, the banker offered a weary, self-deprecating laugh: "You know how they say the shoemaker's children go barefoot... it's not easy." While he isn't losing money, growing personal wealth turns out to be just as elusive for him as it is for anyone else.

That high-earning contradiction plays out against a tense backdrop. Major commercial banks in South Korea routinely post record net profits—often touching 3 trillion won (about $2.2 billion)—largely driven by the wide spread between deposit and lending rates. While everyday citizens groan under crushing household debt and high interest rates, bank unions frequently strike or demand massive performance bonuses. The public watches these clashes with mixed feelings, acutely aware that the high salaries of financial workers are propped up by an economy hooked on high-interest loans.

Further down the professional ladder, the junior legal sector presents its own version of a grueling trade-off. To a university student or recent graduate, becoming a lawyer in South Korea remains the ultimate ticket to upper-middle-class security. The persistent rumor is that starting salaries at large law firms automatically begin at 150 million won (about $110,000). ⚖️

An interview with a third-year lawyer shatters that myth. Working at a smaller firm, she confirms that starting pay rarely clears the 100-million-won mark immediately. Now, in her third year, she earns over 100 million won—but she earns every single won through sheer exhaustion. Handling painful domestic cases involving divorce, inheritance, assault, fraud, and drug offenses exposes her daily to the darkest corners of human behavior.

Her work week routinely stretches past 70 hours across six or seven days. In Korea, this is bluntly referred to as gyakbong—money earned by literally grinding down one's own physical body. When asked if she has sacrificed work-life balance, she smiles wryly: "Not completely abandoned... just slightly different. Completely different. Okay, slightly abandoned." Romance and personal hobbies take a permanent backseat to a crushing mountain of legal briefs and court dates.

Leaving the Track: Career Pivots and the Cost of Ambition

Step away from the high-stress professional classes, and the anxieties shift from sheer endurance to the search for personal meaning. Consider a former kindergarten teacher who walked away from a monthly salary in the low 3 million won range (about $2,200 to $2,500)—a relatively generous figure in an industry historically plagued by abysmal private-sector pay. Spurred by government pushes to close the wage gap between private and public childcare facilities, she had secured a solid job. 🧸

Yet, she handed in her resignation simply because she found the process of designing classrooms and developing educational content more genuinely fun. When asked if she feels anxious watching her university peers settle into stable corporate tracks, she just flashes a calm, reassuring smile. In a society obsessed with standardized milestones, her choice to trade a predictable paycheck for personal engagement is a rare, defiant act.

Meanwhile, in the creative sector, survival requires an entirely different playbook. A 6th-year advertising designer revealed that he is currently on his fourth company. In South Korea's notoriously underpaid design industry, where starting salaries can languish in the late 20-million-won range (around $20,000), loyalty to a single employer is a fast track to financial stagnation. 🎨

By strategically jumping ship whenever a larger agency offered room to grow, he has nearly doubled his salary in five years, landing in the mid-to-high 40-million-won range (about $35,000) plus bonuses. But senior designers in the same industry note that longevity requires keeping a painfully close finger on the pulse of younger generations. In advertising, the moment you stop understanding what teenagers are watching, your seniority stops protecting you.

The Grindstone: Students, Savers, and Industrial Shifts

For the current generation of university students, the mood is heavy with structural anxiety. A sophomore studying computer science at Gachon University—located just south of Seoul—has bucked conventional wisdom by dedicating himself entirely to the rigorous Certified Tax Accountant exam. Despite his parents telling him to pick something easier and more conventional, he dug in his heels. "If a guy is going to show some grit, he has to do it right," he says, displaying the stubborn determination of a young man who successfully quit smoking three years ago.

To stay sane, he maintains a monastic routine, cutting off casual contact with friends and limiting his social life to a single weekly date with his working girlfriend. When asked if he ever feels a sense of inferiority comparing his broke, studying self to her stable, salaried life, his response is remarkably grounded: "Nobody else thinks that way. Only you do yourself. Some people let feeling small cause problems, but that's just male grit." 🔥

An even more striking story of delayed gratification comes from a 23-year-old English literature major at Kookmin University. While many young people express despair over youth unemployment or chase speculative crypto trends, this student took a much simpler route: he goes to class in the morning, then works a nine-hour shift at an arcade five days a week, bringing home roughly 2.5 million won (about $1,800) a month. 💰

Watching his aging mother work grueling manual labor jobs left a permanent impression on him. Instead of complaining about the job market, he funneled his earnings into safe savings accounts while keeping only 10% in stocks. His bank account balance has now crossed the 100-million-won mark (about $75,000). It is a fortune built entirely out of exhausted weekends, missed parties, and the quiet dignity of manual labor.

On the corporate front, South Korea's job market is undergoing a quiet tectonic shift. The old obsession with landing software engineering roles at trendy IT firms in Pangyo—Korea's Silicon Valley—has cooled. Today, top engineering students are pivoting back to traditional manufacturing powerhouses. A senior studying computer science at Sungkyunkwan University has already locked down a job at Hyundai Mobis, an automotive parts giant, through a university-industry hiring track. He notes that his peers are increasingly bypassing pure tech startups in favor of the stability offered by semiconductors and heavy industry. 🚗

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates cooling economic growth, persistent household debt, and a rapidly aging population, street-level snapshots like these reveal a society caught between rigid traditional expectations and desperate personal reinvention. When watching the news out of Seoul in the coming months, keep an eye on a few critical indicators:

    Labor Union Showdowns: Watch how major commercial banks and financial institutions handle upcoming wage negotiations and restructuring plans amid high public scrutiny over interest rate profits. The Manufacturing Pivot: Track employment rates and recruitment quotas at traditional conglomerates (Samsung, Hyundai, SK) as engineering graduates increasingly abandon IT startups for heavy industry. Certification Fevers: Monitor application numbers for professional license exams (tax accountants, certified public accountants) as a barometer of how deeply young job seekers distrust the standard corporate hiring pipeline. The Real Estate and Savings Divide: Pay attention to how Gen Z savings rates and alternative investment patterns evolve as high interest rates make traditional cash accumulation more attractive than risky speculative plays.
