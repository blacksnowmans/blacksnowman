The Great Semiconductor Disconnect: Why Samsung and SK Hynix are Plummeting Despite Record DRAM Prices

October 31, 2026

On the morning of October 30, the trading floors in Seoul felt less like a financial hub and more like a blast zone. Despite the hum of activity within the high-tech semiconductor fabrication plants in Pyeongtaek and Icheon, the digital screens across the city’s brokerage houses were bathed in the deep blue of falling prices—the color that signifies a market rout in South Korea. On this single day, the KOSPI (the Korea Composite Stock Price Index, South Korea’s benchmark stock market index) tumbled 3.48%, driven by a massive exodus of capital.

Foreign investors, the traditional bellwethers of the Korean market, offloaded a staggering 1.6 trillion won (approximately $1.16 billion) in shares in a matter of hours. At the center of the storm were the nation’s two industrial pillars: Samsung Electronics, the world’s largest memory chipmaker, and SK Hynix, its primary rival and the current leader in high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Samsung fell by over 3%, while SK Hynix suffered a bruising collapse of more than 7%. For many observers, the sheer scale of the sell-off felt disconnected from reality, creating a massive rift between the record-high prices of physical memory chips and the psychological terror gripping the stock market.

The Alphabet Shock and the Crisis of Faith in AI

The immediate catalyst for this "Black Wednesday" did not originate in Seoul, but in Silicon Valley. The so-called "Alphabet Shock"—referring to the quarterly earnings report from Google’s parent company, Alphabet—sent ripples across the Pacific. While Alphabet’s numbers were not inherently catastrophic, they ignited a dormant fear among global investors: that the massive capital expenditure (CAPEX) being poured into Artificial Intelligence (AI) by Big Tech firms might not be generating returns fast enough to justify the cost.

For South Korea, which has essentially tethered its economic future to the AI hardware cycle, this doubt is existential. If companies like Google, Microsoft, and Meta begin to tap the brakes on their server investments, the demand for the high-end memory chips produced by Samsung and SK Hynix could theoretically dry up. This "AI bubble" narrative overshadowed even the most bullish news from the companies themselves. For instance, SK Hynix had recently announced it had already sold out its HBM production capacity through 2029 and secured long-term contracts with global storage firms like Seagate. In a rational market, such news would provide a floor for the stock; in the face of the Alphabet Shock, it was largely ignored.

The contagion was not limited to Korea. The Nikkei (Japan’s primary stock index) dropped by over 1,300 yen, and TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), the world’s leading foundry, saw its shares slide by 3.67%. In the United States, the pain was mirrored by Micron Technology and storage giants like Western Digital and Seagate. What we witnessed was a global "risk-off" maneuver—a collective decision by institutional funds to pull back from the AI semiconductor ecosystem simultaneously, regardless of individual company performance.

The Paradox of Record-High DRAM Prices

The most confusing aspect of this downturn is the "DRAM Paradox." While stock prices are cratering, the actual price of memory chips is soaring. In October, the fixed transaction price for PC DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory) rose nearly 6% month-on-month, hitting a record high of $27.50. This surge is driven by a simple, brutal reality of manufacturing: supply is being choked off.

Samsung and SK Hynix have shifted a significant portion of their production capacity away from "legacy" or "commodity" DRAM—the kind used in laptops and smartphones—to focus on HBM and high-capacity DDR5 chips used in AI servers. This pivot has created a vacuum in the general-purpose memory market. With fewer factories churning out standard PC memory, supply has plummeted, driving prices to heights that have begun to worry other sectors of the electronics industry. We are seeing a ripple effect where:

  • Canon, the Japanese camera giant, has issued warnings that camera prices may need to rise due to the high cost of memory.
  • Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI, has even commented on the memory market, expressing concern that the concentration of memory production for AI servers is creating a monopoly-like squeeze on other tech hardware.

However, the stock market is a forward-looking machine, and it is currently looking at a different set of numbers. While DRAM is booming, NAND Flash—the memory used for long-term data storage in SSDs and phones—has seen its price growth halt for the first time in 21 months. This stagnation in NAND suggests that while the AI server market is white-hot, the "real world" demand for consumer electronics like smartphones and budget laptops remains tepid. Investors fear that Samsung and SK Hynix are becoming too dependent on the high-margin DRAM/HBM segment, making them vulnerable if the AI investment cycle even slightly decelerates.

The Dragon in the Rearview Mirror: The Rise of CXMT

Beyond the immediate market jitters, a more structural threat is emerging from China. CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies), China’s champion in the DRAM space, recently reported a staggering 613% increase in third-quarter revenue compared to the previous year. With 269 billion yuan (roughly $37 billion) in cumulative revenue for the first three quarters of the year, CXMT has officially turned a profit and even announced a 2 billion yuan (about $280 million) share buyback program to signal its confidence.

Supported by massive subsidies from the Chinese government, CXMT is aggressively filling the void left by Korean and American firms. As Samsung and SK Hynix chase the high-value AI market, CXMT is flooding the legacy DRAM market with cheaper alternatives. This "pincer movement" is what keeps analysts awake at night: Western and Korean firms are vacating the low-end market to chase an AI dream that might be a bubble, while Chinese firms are rapidly building a foundation in the essential, high-volume commodity market. The 25% drop in Micron’s stock from its June peak is, in part, a reaction to this Chinese breakthrough in DRAM production, which traders fear will eventually lead to a global supply glut in legacy chips.

Navigating the Noise: The View from the Lee Jae-myung Administration

Inside Korea, the current Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party) face a delicate balancing act. The government is acutely aware that the "Korea Discount"—a term used to describe the chronic undervaluation of South Korean companies compared to global peers—is being exacerbated by these wild swings. Unlike the previous era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration was marked by instability, the current government is pushing for more robust support for the "K-Semiconductor Belt," viewing it not just as a business sector but as a matter of national security.

Critics of the current panic argue that the market is conflating two different things: the health of the PC market and the health of the AI infrastructure build-out. Intel, for example, saw its stock price crater by nearly 30% recently, but that was largely due to its failure to pivot away from the stagnant PC market and internal manufacturing failures. Samsung and SK Hynix, by contrast, are seeing their factories run at full capacity. Their "problem" isn't a lack of orders, but an inability to build factories fast enough to meet demand.

"The noise of the stock market often drowns out the signal of the factory floor," notes one local analyst. "When you see companies like SK Hynix selling out their stock five years in advance, a 7% drop in a single day is a symptom of trader anxiety, not business failure."

The consensus among the Lee Jae-myung government's economic advisors is that the AI cycle is not a temporary fad but a generational shift in computing architecture. While the "Big Tech" firms might slow their spending in 2028 or 2029 once the initial infrastructure is built, the immediate horizon for 2025 and 2026 remains remarkably clear. The current sell-off may simply be a painful but necessary correction that separates the firms with true technical moats—those producing HBM3E and high-end enterprise SSDs (eSSDs)—from those merely riding the wave.

What to Watch Next

The road ahead for Korean semiconductors depends on three concrete signals that investors and observers should monitor closely over the next quarter:

  • Nvidia's Supply Chain Guidance: As the primary customer for SK Hynix and potentially Samsung’s HBM3E chips, any shift in Nvidia’s production timelines will have a magnified effect on Korean share prices.
  • Chinese Legacy Expansion: Watch for whether CXMT begins to export its DRAM in significant volumes to third markets (outside China). This would signal a direct price war in the commodity memory space.
  • Government Incentives: The Lee Jae-myung administration is expected to announce further tax credits and infrastructure subsidies for "Mega-clusters" in Gyeonggi Province. The scale and speed of this support will be a key indicator of Korea's ability to maintain its lead over US and Chinese competitors.

In the end, the "Alphabet Shock" has provided a cold shower for a market that was perhaps too intoxicated by AI hype. But for the giants of Suwon and Icheon, the fundamentals of supply and demand remain in their favor. The machines are still running, the chips are still being sold at record prices, and the disconnect between the screen and the fab will eventually have to close.
