Street Politics and the Budget Battle: Why the People Power Party is Taking Its Fight to Busan

November 15, 2026

The rain at Busan Station on November 14 was more than just a seasonal inconvenience; it was the backdrop for a significant escalation in South Korean partisan warfare. Thousands of members of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, gathered in the square to launch a nationwide protest tour. Their target is a controversial new piece of legislation: the "Special Counsel Law on Fabricated Indictments."

In the high-stakes theater of Korean politics, names are everything. While the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) frames the bill as a necessary check on prosecutorial overreach, the opposition PPP has branded it the "Lee Jae-myung Prison Break Act." By bringing their grievances to the streets of Busan, the PPP is signaling that the battle for the country’s legal and political future has moved beyond the halls of the National Assembly and into the court of public opinion.

This move comes at a critical juncture. The National Assembly is currently in the middle of its annual budget session, the period where the government’s trillion-dollar spending plans for the coming year are scrutinized and finalized. By choosing this moment to launch a "street struggle," the PPP is making a calculated gamble that public anger over the President’s legal immunity will outweigh concerns about a stalled legislative process.

The "Nakdong River Line" and the Rhetoric of Resistance

Busan holds a deep symbolic resonance for South Korean conservatives. During the Korean War, the city served as the last bastion of the "Nakdong River Perimeter," the final line of defense against the North Korean advance. In the modern political imagination, Busan remains the gateway to the Yeongnam region—the conservative heartland. When a conservative leader takes to a podium at Busan Station, they are not just giving a speech; they are invoking a history of holding the line against existential threats.

Standing in the rain, Jang Dong-hyeok, the chairman of the People Power Party, used language that reflected this sense of siege. He called for the immediate resumption of various trials involving President Lee Jae-myung, arguing that the presidency should not be used as a "safe haven" from justice. His rhetoric was blunt, asserting that the current administration is dismantling the rule of law to protect a single individual.

The PPP claims that 150,000 people attended the rally. While such numbers are often disputed by police and rival parties, the visual of a crowded square under umbrellas sent a clear message to the Blue House (the presidential office and residence). The opposition is betting that by framing the "Special Counsel Law" as a tool for a "prison break," they can unify a base that has felt sidelined since the DPK took power. They are demanding that President Lee exercise his power of veto, arguing that the law is "100% unconstitutional."

A Clash of Realities: Fabricated Indictments vs. Judicial Accountability

To understand why this matters, one must look at the specific law being contested. The "Special Counsel Law on Fabricated Indictments" is a piece of legislation pushed by the ruling Democratic Party. Its stated goal is to investigate whether prosecutors have used "fabricated" evidence or coercive tactics in their long-running investigations into high-ranking officials, including President Lee Jae-myung himself.

In the eyes of the ruling DPK, the prosecution service has become a political weapon, and this law is the only way to ensure a fair trial for the President. They argue that the judicial system has been tainted by a "prosecutorial dictatorship" that seeks to undermine the democratically elected government. From this perspective, the PPP’s rally is not a defense of the law, but an attempt to protect a corrupt status quo.

The PPP sees it differently. Representative Jung Jeom-sik, a prominent voice within the party, described the bill as a "zombie special counsel." The opposition argues that by allowing the ruling party to have a heavy hand in selecting the special prosecutor, the law effectively allows the President to choose his own investigators. This, they argue, is the ultimate subversion of the separation of powers. By calling for the resumption of five specific trials currently facing the President, the PPP is trying to keep the focus on the President’s legal vulnerabilities rather than the alleged misconduct of the prosecutors.

The stakes are remarkably high. If the law stands, it could potentially derail ongoing prosecutions and lead to the dismissal of charges against the President. If the President vetoes it, he risks being accused of hiding from the truth. By taking the fight to the streets, the PPP is trying to make a veto politically necessary—or at least, to make the political cost of signing the bill as high as possible.

The Budget Session and the Abandonment of "Livelihood Politics"

While the rhetoric in Busan was fiery, the reaction in Seoul was one of sharp condemnation. The ruling Democratic Party was quick to point out that the PPP was holding rallies while the National Assembly’s most important work—the budget—remained unfinished. A DPK spokesperson accused the opposition of "abandoning the budget parliament" to indulge in "mockery of the President."

In South Korea, "livelihood politics" (minsaeng) is a powerful concept. It refers to the everyday economic concerns of the public—inflation, housing, and social welfare. By skipping committee meetings to shout slogans in the rain, the PPP leaves itself open to the charge that it cares more about partisan warfare than the actual needs of the people. The DPK is banking on this narrative, hoping that middle-of-the-road voters will see the Busan rally as an unnecessary distraction from the $500 billion (approx. 670 trillion won) budget currently under review.

The paralysis of the National Assembly is a recurring theme in Korean politics, but the current standoff feels particularly acute. When the main opposition party decides that the legislative process is no longer a viable venue for its grievances, the democratic machinery begins to grind to a halt. The DPK argues that the PPP is "taking the people’s livelihood hostage" for the sake of a political frame. The PPP counters that there can be no "livelihood" in a country where the President is above the law.

This "parallel play" of politics—where the two parties aren't even arguing about the same thing, but are instead shouting different narratives to different audiences—is creating a deep sense of political fatigue among the public. For the average resident of Busan or Seoul, the technicalities of the Special Counsel Law are often less important than the fact that their elected representatives seem unable to sit in the same room.

Internal Fractures and the Strategic Gamble

Despite the show of force in Busan, the rally also highlighted some uncomfortable truths for the People Power Party. Reports emerged that despite a "general mobilization order" from party leadership, several PPP lawmakers were noticeably absent from the event. This suggests that the party’s "hardline" strategy is not universally supported from within.

Some moderate members of the PPP are reportedly concerned that a prolonged "street struggle" will alienate centrist voters. They worry that by focusing so intensely on "stopping the prison break," the party is failing to offer a positive vision for the country. There is also the reality of internal power struggles. Jang Dong-hyeok’s leadership has been tested by factional infighting, and some see the nationwide tour as his attempt to consolidate power by forcing party members to show their loyalty in a public setting.

Furthermore, there is the risk of the "far-right" label. When a major political party moves out of the parliament and onto the streets, it often attracts more extreme elements of the political spectrum. Radical YouTubers and fringe activist groups were present in Busan, their rhetoric often far more aggressive than that of the official party leadership. If the PPP becomes too closely associated with these groups, it may find it impossible to win over the swing voters it needs for future elections.

The strategy for the coming weeks is clear: the PPP will move from Busan to Daegu on November 21, and then to Daejeon on November 28. This is a map of the conservative heartland moving toward the center of the country. Each stop is designed to build momentum, turning the "Special Counsel Law" into a national scandal. But with each stop, the party also risks deepening its reputation as a "protest party" rather than a "governing party in waiting."

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether the PPP’s street politics can actually shift the legislative needle. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Presidential Veto: All eyes are on President Lee Jae-myung. If he vetoes the Special Counsel Law, the battle returns to the National Assembly for a re-vote, which requires a two-thirds majority. The PPP will be looking for any signs of "defection" within the DPK ranks during a potential re-vote.
  • The Budget Deadline: The legal deadline for the budget is approaching in early December. If the PPP continues its rallies and the budget is delayed, the public backlash could be severe. Watch for whether the PPP offers a "grand bargain"—returning to the budget table in exchange for concessions on the Special Counsel Law.
  • The "Daegu Test": The next rally in Daegu on November 21 will be a crucial indicator. Daegu is even more conservative than Busan. If the turnout there is significantly higher—or lower—it will tell us much about the grassroots energy behind this movement.
  • Judicial Rulings: While the politicians argue, the courts are still moving. Any new developments in the actual trials involving President Lee or his associates could instantly change the political atmosphere, either vindicating the PPP’s "prison break" narrative or strengthening the DPK’s "fabricated indictment" claim.

South Korea’s democracy has always been a contact sport, but the shift from debating bills to shouting in rain-slicked squares suggests a widening chasm. As the People Power Party takes its grievance tour across the country, the question remains whether they are defending the constitution or simply deepening the divide.
