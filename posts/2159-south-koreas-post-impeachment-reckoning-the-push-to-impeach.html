South Korea’s Post-Impeachment Reckoning: The Push to Impeach the Chief Justice and Dismantle Political Prosecutions

November 10, 2026

Even with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol stripped of the presidency by impeachment and currently sitting inside a detention cell, the political tempest that convulsed South Korea shows no signs of calming. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and its energized grassroots base, the former prosecutor-turned-president’s downfall was only the first chapter of a much wider reckoning. The crosshairs of progressive politics have now turned squarely toward the country’s legal establishment: the conservative judiciary and the powerful state prosecution service.

In recent days, two parallel maneuvers have brought South Korea’s legal institutions to a boiling point. First, a formal parliamentary petition demanding the impeachment of Chief Justice Jo Hee-de has surged across progressive networks. Second, the National Assembly has pushed through a contentious Special Counsel bill designed to investigate alleged "fabricated indictments" orchestrated by prosecutors during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office. Together with President Lee Jae-myung’s appointment of an uncompromising reformist to lead the Ministry of Justice, South Korea is entering an unprecedented confrontation over who controls the nation's scales of justice.

A Citizens' Petition Puts the Chief Justice on Notice

The push against the judiciary is not beginning in the courtroom, but on the digital portal of the National Assembly. Under South Korean law, an e-petition that gathers 50,000 verified citizen signatures within thirty days cannot simply be ignored by politicians; it is formally treated as a legislative bill and referred directly to the relevant standing committee—in this case, the powerful Legislation and Judiciary Committee.

Spearheaded by eight grassroots organizations affiliated with the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the petition calls for the immediate introduction of an impeachment resolution against Chief Justice Jo Hee-de. To outside observers, seeking to remove the head of the highest court over allegations of ideological bias might appear to threaten the separation of powers. Within the progressive camp, however, the petition represents an exercise of constitutional checks and balances under Article 65 of the South Korean Constitution, which permits the impeachment of top judges who violate the law or their official duties.

The grievances against Chief Justice Jo reflect deep-seated grievances that date back years. Critics argue that under his leadership, the Supreme Court provided judicial cover to Mr. Yoon’s administration. Progressive organizers accuse the bench of applying a glaring double standard: offering procedural leniency and delayed hearings to members of Mr. Yoon’s inner circle while swiftly issuing search warrants and handing down punitive rulings against progressive figures. For these critics, the memory of the 2018 Yang Seung-tae "judicial interference" scandal—in which court administrative leadership colluded with the conservative Park Geun-hye administration—never truly dissipated. They argue that the judiciary has long shielded itself behind the lofty rhetoric of judicial independence while functioning as a quiet redoubt for conservative elites.

If the petition crosses the 50,000-signature mark, it will force the Legislation and Judiciary Committee to convene formal hearings. Such an outcome would drag the Chief Justice and the Supreme Court administration into public view, subjecting top judges to televised interrogation by lawmakers—an institutional showdown with few precedents in modern Korean history.

The Special Counsel on 'Fabricated Indictments' and the Budget Standoff

While the petition gathers momentum online, the legislative battleground has already erupted over the Special Counsel Act on Fabricated Indictments. The bill, which has passed the National Assembly, authorizes an independent investigation into allegations that politically motivated prosecutors coerced testimony and manufactured evidence to pursue opposition figures during Mr. Yoon’s tenure.

Despite the bill's passage, leadership within the Democratic Party of Korea faces sharp criticism from its own base for moving too slowly. Many party loyalists argue that the leadership missed its "golden hour" by failing to push the special probe through immediately after the party convention. That hesitation, critics argue, granted compromised prosecutors precious time to align their legal defenses and purge paper trails. The upcoming investigation will focus heavily on high-profile inquiries launched under the previous administration, including the controversial Ssangbangwool North Korean remittance probe and allegations of pressured testimonies during the sprawling Daejang-dong real estate development investigation.

The conservative People Power Party, now adjusting to its role as the primary opposition, has reacted furiously. Labeling the special counsel an act of political retribution aimed at dismantling legitimate law enforcement, the party has launched a boycott of the National Assembly’s annual state budget deliberations. In South Korean legislative politics, halting budget reviews is the standard weapon of last resort for a minority party seeking to paralyze the parliamentary schedule.

Yet the ruling majority appears unbothered by the blockade. Democratic Party lawmakers, including Representative Kim Young-hwan, have pointed out that the National Assembly Act provides clear legal avenues to bypass an opposition boycott. The budget can proceed either through the Special Committee on Budget and Accounts or via the Assembly Speaker’s statutory authority to table the fiscal bill directly (known as jikgwon sangjeong) ahead of the legal deadline. With the Lee Jae-myung administration eager to fund its domestic policy agenda amidst economic pressures, the ruling party seems fully prepared to pass the national budget unilaterally rather than negotiate concessions with an opposition it views as defending past administrative abuses.

The Roh Moo-hyun Legacy: Lee Yong-cheol's Perilous Mission

The tip of the spear in this institutional overhaul is President Lee Jae-myung’s nominee for Minister of Justice, Lee Yong-cheol. The nomination carries immense symbolic weight for the South Korean center-left. Lee is widely viewed as an ideological descendant of the late President Roh Moo-hyun, having worked alongside Roh since his underdog primary campaign in the early 2000s.

Roh’s tragic death in 2009, following intensive and humiliating interrogations by state prosecutors after leaving office, remains the founding trauma of modern South Korean progressivism. For many within the Democratic Party, the Moon Jae-in administration’s efforts to rein in the prosecution via investigative authority adjustments were too tentative, ultimately paving the way for a former top prosecutor to capture the presidency. By nominating an uncompromising figure like Lee Yong-cheol, President Lee is signaling an end to moderate compromise with the legal elite.

However, the nominee faces a hazardous confirmation process and an extraordinary operational dilemma: the question of whether to exercise the justice minister’s legal authority to drop charges (gongso chwiso) in ongoing trials that the ruling party considers fraudulent. Progressive lawmakers argue that allowing prosecutions built on falsified evidence or extortion to proceed through the courts makes the state complicit in prosecutorial misconduct. Conversely, the People Power Party and conservative legal organizations are already preparing to frame any withdrawal of indictments as outright obstruction of justice and an illegal abuse of authority.

Navigating that storm will determine the fate of Lee Yong-cheol’s tenure. To break through the institutional resistance of career prosecutors, the new minister will need to withstand blistering attacks during his parliamentary confirmation hearing while maintaining total discipline across the judicial bureaucracy.

What to Watch Next

The convergence of a judicial impeachment petition, an aggressive special counsel probe, and a transformative justice ministry appointment marks a critical juncture for South Korea's constitutional framework. Over the coming weeks, several concrete milestones will dictate the direction of this struggle:

  • The Petition Tally and Committee Action: Watch whether the citizen petition against Chief Justice Jo Hee-de reaches the 50,000-signature mark to force mandatory review by the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee, and whether committee leaders move to summon top judicial administrators for public hearings.
  • Lee Yong-cheol’s Confirmation Battle: Pay close attention to the nominee's testimony regarding the withdrawal of indictments (gongso chwiso). His specific language will reveal how aggressively the Justice Ministry plans to dismantle politically sensitive cases initiated under the previous administration.
  • The Deployment of the Special Counsel: Once the special counsel team is appointed, early search-and-seizure operations against current and former senior prosecutors tied to the Ssangbangwool and Daejang-dong probes will signal how deep the investigation intends to cut into the prosecution's hierarchy.
  • Unilateral Budget Passage: Monitor whether the National Assembly Speaker exercises ex officio tabling power to pass next year's state budget over the People Power Party’s boycott, cementing the ruling party’s resolve to govern without legislative compromise.

South Korea’s democracy has demonstrated a remarkable capacity to hold its executive leaders accountable, culminating in Mr. Yoon’s removal and incarceration. But reshaping the permanent machinery of the prosecution and the judiciary is an altogether different test. As the Lee Jae-myung administration and parliament press forward, the battle over Chief Justice Jo and the legacy of politicized indictments will determine what South Korean judicial independence actually looks like in the post-Yoon era.
