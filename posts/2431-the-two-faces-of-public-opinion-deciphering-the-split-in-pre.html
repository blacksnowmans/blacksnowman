The Two Faces of Public Opinion: Deciphering the Split in President Lee Jae-myung’s Latest Approval Ratings

November 17, 2026

Imagine you are a professional in Seoul, checking your phone during a crowded subway commute. You open a news portal and see two headlines side-by-side. The first claims that the Democratic Party of Korea, the nation’s ruling party led by President Lee Jae-myung, has seen its support soar to 47%. The second, published almost simultaneously, suggests the party’s support is crumbling, falling to 41.9% while the conservative opposition takes the lead. For a casual observer, it feels as though the pollsters are describing two entirely different countries.

This is the current state of political data in South Korea. Recent figures analyzed by Humility is Hard News Factory, a popular current affairs program hosted by influential commentator Kim Eo-jun, have highlighted a growing divergence in how the Korean public perceives the government. While one set of data shows a steady rise in support for the Lee Jae-myung administration, another reveals cracks in his core support base and a resurgence of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition.

To understand why these numbers matter, one must look beyond the percentages. These polls are not just measuring popularity; they are capturing a complex tug-of-war between high-profile economic events and a growing sense of frustration over the speed of government promises. At the heart of this divide is a fundamental difference in how Koreans answer their phones, and a massive industrial project involving the nation’s largest conglomerate, Samsung Electronics.

The Methodology War: Human Callers vs. Automated Robots

The discrepancy in the latest polling data—where the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) hit 47% in one survey but fell to 41.9% in another—is primarily a result of methodology. In South Korea, political sentiment is measured through two main channels: Telephone Interviews (often called CATI) and Automated Response Systems (ARS).

In the telephone interview polls, the DPK saw a significant 2.9 percentage point jump, reaching 47%. In these surveys, a human interviewer speaks directly to the respondent. Because a real person is on the line, these polls tend to capture a broader spectrum of the population, including moderate voters and those who are only casually interested in politics. They are generally considered more stable and reflective of the "quiet majority" who respond to the overall mood of the country rather than the outrage of the day.

Conversely, the ARS polls—where a recorded voice asks the questions and the respondent presses buttons on their keypad—told a different story. In this format, DPK support dropped by 1.5 percentage points. More strikingly, when looking at general party preference, the ARS results showed the opposition People Power Party at 41.7%, narrowly overtaking the DPK at 39.9%. This "reversal" within the margin of error is a psychological blow to the ruling party.

Why does this gap exist? ARS polls are notoriously dominated by "high-involvement" voters. These are people who are either deeply passionate about their party or deeply angry at the current state of affairs. They are the ones willing to stay on the line with a robot for five minutes. When the ARS numbers drop while CATI numbers rise, it suggests that while the general public is warming up to the President’s recent moves, his most vocal supporters or critics are experiencing a moment of intense friction. It is a sign that the political environment is in a state of volatile transition.

The Samsung Effect: Why Chungcheong is Turning Blue

The most geographic shift in the recent data occurred in the Chungcheong region, the traditional "swing" province of South Korean politics. In telephone interviews, President Lee Jae-myung and the DPK saw their approval ratings in this area climb by roughly 4 percentage points. To understand why, one must look at an event that took place last Thursday at the Samsung Electronics Onyang Campus.

President Lee Jae-myung traveled to the campus to attend the groundbreaking ceremony for the "Chungcheong Mega Project." This is a massive industrial initiative aimed at turning the region into a global hub for high-tech manufacturing, anchored by Samsung's investment. In South Korea, the involvement of Samsung—the country’s preeminent chaebol (family-run conglomerate)—is often seen as a guarantee of economic prosperity and job security.

"When the President personally picks up a shovel at a Samsung site, it sends a message that transcends partisan bickering. It tells the local residents that their economy is a top priority for the Blue House."

For the residents of Chungcheong, who often feel caught between the political gravity of Seoul and the traditional strongholds of the south, such a high-profile commitment to regional development is powerful. The 4% jump reflects a pragmatic response to tangible economic news. In a climate where inflation and global economic uncertainty are constant worries, the promise of a "Mega Project" provides a rare sense of optimism. However, this boost is "conditional trust." The history of Korean politics is littered with grand industrial promises that never moved past the groundbreaking stage. For now, the "Samsung Effect" is buoying the President, but the clock has started on the actual delivery of these jobs.

Cracks in the Stronghold: The Honam and Progressive Dilemma

While the President found success in the swing regions, the ARS data revealed a troubling counter-trend in his traditional strongholds. In the Honam region (the southwest) and among self-identified progressives, support showed signs of erosion. The most telling statistic was the split among progressive voters: while telephone interviews showed a 7-to-3 ratio of positive to negative views, the ARS polls showed a much tighter 6-to-4 ratio.

The analysis from News Factory suggests that these "cracks in the base" are driven by suspicion over project delays. Paradoxically, the same "Mega Projects" that are winning over voters in Chungcheong are causing anxiety in other areas. Voters in Honam and the progressive wing are beginning to ask why certain regional promises are moving slower than others. There is a growing fear that the government's grand designs are being prioritized based on political expediency rather than a balanced national strategy.

For a supporter of the DPK, seeing the opposition People Power Party overtake the ruling party in ARS polls (41.7% to 39.9%) is a wake-up call. It indicates that the opposition’s base is becoming highly energized and coordinated, while the President’s own "active" base is beginning to voice dissatisfaction. When the core supporters—those most likely to answer an ARS poll—start to waver, it usually precedes a broader decline unless the administration can address the root of the "delay suspicions."

The danger of the "groundbreaking" strategy is that it creates a benchmark. By celebrating the start of the Chungcheong project so publicly, the Lee Jae-myung administration has inadvertently highlighted every other project that hasn't started yet. In the eyes of a frustrated voter in Gwangju or Jeonju, a shovel in the ground in Onyang is a reminder of the empty lot in their own neighborhood.

What to Watch: From Ceremony to Execution

The divergence in these polls suggests that South Korea is entering a phase where "politics of the spectacle" is losing its effectiveness. While a visit to a Samsung factory can move the needle among moderates for a week, the high-involvement voters who drive the political narrative are demanding more granular details. The administration is currently caught in a tug-of-war between the excitement of a new announcement and the exhaustion of waiting for results.

Moving forward, there are three key indicators that will determine if the Democratic Party can consolidate its 47% peak or if it will slide toward the 39% floor seen in the ARS data:

  • The Budgeting Phase: Watch for the upcoming legislative sessions regarding the funding of these Mega Projects. Groundbreaking ceremonies are symbolic, but the passage of specific budget allocations will be the true test of the government's commitment. If the PPP manages to block or significantly delay funding, the DPK's regional gains could evaporate.
  • The "Honam Reconciliation": The President will likely need to make a significant gesture or policy announcement directed at the southwest to quiet the "delay suspicions." If the ARS numbers in Honam do not recover in the next month, it could signal a deeper internal crisis for the ruling party.
  • The Opposition's Momentum: The People Power Party's narrow lead in the ARS poll shows they have successfully consolidated their base. The question is whether they can translate this "angry voter" energy into the telephone interview polls. If the PPP’s numbers start rising in the CATI surveys, it means their message is breaking out of the conservative echo chamber and reaching the moderates.

In the end, the "two faces" of these polls represent a nation that is both hopeful and deeply skeptical. President Lee Jae-myung has proven he can win over the public with a vision of industrial strength, but he is now being judged by a public that keeps a very precise calendar. In Korean politics, a shovel is only a sign of progress until the dust settles; after that, it's just a tool, and the voters are waiting to see what—and how fast—is actually being built.
