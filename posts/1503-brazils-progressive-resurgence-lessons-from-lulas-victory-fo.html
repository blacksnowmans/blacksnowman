Brazil’s Progressive Resurgence: Lessons from Lula’s Victory for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 26, 2026

The air in Brazil during the final hours of the presidential runoff was thick with a tension that resonated far beyond South America. For many observers in South Korea, the sights of citizens scrambling to voting booths despite roadblocks and transport shutdowns felt hauntingly familiar. It was a battle for the soul of a nation, pitting the incumbent Jair Bolsonaro—often dubbed the "Trump of the Tropics"—against Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, the former president seeking a dramatic return from political exile. When the final tally confirmed Lula’s victory, it wasn't just a win for the Brazilian left; it was a signal to progressive movements worldwide, including the currently governing Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), that the tide of right-wing populism could be stemmed through unity and a relentless focus on the working class.

For a reader outside of the Korean peninsula, the intense interest in a South American election might seem surprising. However, under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea has increasingly viewed its own democratic health through a global lens. Having moved past the tumultuous years of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—whose administration was marked by what many critics termed a retreat from democratic norms before his eventual impeachment and imprisonment—Korean progressives see Lula’s victory as a mirror. It is a case study in how to consolidate power, how to resist the "Blue Wave" of global conservatism, and, most importantly, how to prevent the eventual collapse that often haunts progressive governments once they take office.

A Dramatic Turnaround Built on Grassroots Grit

The path to Lula’s victory was anything but smooth. During the first round of voting, many pollsters and analysts predicted a comfortable majority for Lula. The reality, however, was a chilling wake-up call: he led by only 3.5 percentage points, a margin of roughly 4.4 million votes. This narrow lead forced a runoff, creating a period of extreme psychological pressure for the progressive camp. It was a reminder that the influence of right-wing populism is often deeper and more resilient than surface-level polls suggest.

What followed was a total mobilization. Supporters took to social media, operated phone banks, and marched in the streets, fighting against a negative media frame and the entrenched interests of the right-wing establishment. This "trench warfare" of politics proved that power is rarely handed over based on popularity alone; it must be seized through organizational discipline. For the Korean reader, this evokes the intense mobilization seen during the "Candlelight Revolution" that eventually led to the downfall of the conservative establishment years ago. The lesson remains the same: when pushed to the brink, the survival of a progressive movement depends on its ability to convince its base that every single vote is a shield against the erosion of democracy.

The victory was also a triumph over blatant state interference. In Brazil, voting is mandatory for citizens aged 18 to 70, a system that resulted in a staggering 81% turnout. Yet, the Bolsonaro government allegedly attempted to suppress the vote in Lula’s strongholds—specifically lower-income areas—by reducing bus schedules and setting up police roadblocks on election day. This attempt to weaponize the machinery of the state to disenfranchise the poor was met with a swift legal and political counter-offensive. Progressive leaders filed injunctions and secured extensions for voting hours, proving that a robust legal defense and civil vigilance are the only ways to safeguard the ballot box when the incumbent refuses to play by the rules.

The Geography of Ideology: Seoul vs. Tokyo

Perhaps the most fascinating data point for those studying the influence of political environments is the voting behavior of the Brazilian diaspora. The results from Brazilians living in South Korea and Japan offer a stark, almost startling contrast that highlights how the "political soil" of a host country shapes the consciousness of its residents. In South Korea, 64.35% of the 230 Brazilians who voted chose Lula. In sharp contrast, in Japan, a staggering 80.08% of the 35,636 Brazilian voters cast their ballots for the far-right Bolsonaro.

Why such a massive disparity? Analysts point to the prevailing social and media atmospheres in both countries. South Korea, currently under the Lee Jae-myung administration, is characterized by a high level of democratic engagement and a public discourse that frequently emphasizes social justice and the checking of authoritarian impulses. Living within this "K-Democracy" environment—a term used to describe Korea’s recent history of successful mass protests and the legal accountability of high-ranking officials—seems to have fostered a preference for Lula’s reformist agenda among the Brazilian community in Seoul.

On the other hand, the Brazilian community in Japan lives within a much more conservative political framework. Japan has been dominated for decades by the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP), a conservative juggernaut. The social atmosphere there tends toward stability and traditionalism, and the information channels within the Brazilian-Japanese labor community are often more insulated. This suggests that the "breath" of a nation’s politics—whether it is the air of reform in Seoul or the air of conservatism in Tokyo—eventually infiltrates the political identity of even those who are just passing through. It is a sobering reminder that the media and social environment we maintain at home has consequences that cross borders.

Policy as the Ultimate Defense Against Populism

While unity and turnout won the election, Lula’s campaign understood that staying in power requires more than just winning an argument about democracy—it requires "policy efficacy." The source material highlights two specific promises that resonated with the Brazilian masses: the 15% increase in Bolsa Família (a world-renowned social welfare program that provides financial aid to poor Brazilian families) and the push for a five-day work week.

These aren't just slogans; they are direct interventions in the lives of the working class. For a progressive government to remain sustainable, it must move beyond grand discourses on "reform" and "justice" to provide tangible benefits that a citizen can feel in their wallet and their daily schedule. By promising to fill the pockets of the poor and reduce the grueling hours of the laborer, Lula provided a concrete reason for voters to reject the seductive but often empty rhetoric of far-right populism.

This provides a direct blueprint for the Democratic Party of Korea. In the wake of the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, which many Koreans remember as a time of democratic backsliding and economic stagnation, the Lee Jae-myung government faces the same challenge. It is not enough to simply be "not the opposition." The administration must deliver on the "K-welfare" state, ensuring that the legacy of the previous administration’s failures is replaced by a sense of immediate, felt improvement in the lives of ordinary citizens. If the people do not feel the "efficacy" of the government, the door remains open for the return of the far-right.

The Fragility of Progressive Power

The most profound takeaway from Lula’s return is his own sober reflection on the fragility of power. Even in the heat of victory, the question was raised: "Why do progressive governments collapse?" This is the "memento mori" of modern politics. Lula, who had seen his previous successors fall and had himself been imprisoned on charges that were later overturned, knows that the establishment does not simply go away after an election. The right-wing remains entrenched in the bureaucracy, the media, and the judiciary, waiting for the first sign of internal division or policy failure.

"Progressives must constantly innovate themselves. As Lula emphasized, the results of reform must be quickly translated into legislation and policy, and the promises made to the base must be kept with a decisiveness that can suppress the persistent reactionary forces of the establishment."

The current Korean government must take this warning to heart. The People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, continues to challenge the Lee Jae-myung administration at every turn. If the ruling progressives fall into the traps of internal factionalism or become perceived as a new "establishment" that has lost touch with the grassroots, the pendulum will inevitably swing back. The victory in Brazil was a hard-won reprieve from a global trend of "Trumpism," but it is a temporary one unless the government can prove that progressivism is not just a moral stance, but a superior way to run a country.

What to Watch Next

As Lula begins his third term and the Lee Jae-myung administration continues its mandate in Seoul, there are several key indicators of whether these progressive movements will truly take root or merely act as a brief interruption to a conservative era:

  • Legislative Speed: Watch how quickly the Brazilian Senate and the Korean National Assembly (where the DPK holds a significant presence) can turn welfare promises into binding law. In Brazil, the 15% increase in Bolsa Família and the five-day work week legislation are the primary tests.
  • Countering Disinformation: Observe the strategies both governments use to handle "fake news" and media polarization, which were the primary weapons of the Bolsonaro and Yoon eras.
  • The 2024 U.S. Elections: The "Blue Wave" of conservatism often takes its cues from Washington. The outcome of the upcoming U.S. elections will significantly impact the global momentum of the movements Lula and Lee represent.
  • Economic Efficacy: Ultimately, the global price of commodities and the management of inflation will determine if these governments can afford their welfare expansions without triggering a backlash from the middle class.

The dramatic choice made by the citizens of Brazil—to fight through roadblocks and propaganda to return a progressive leader to power—serves as a guidepost for Korea. It shows that while the threat of a "Trumpist" regression is global, the tools to stop it are local: unity, tangible welfare, and a relentless focus on the people’s livelihood.
