The Machiavellian Trap: Why Rhyu Si-min is Turning on President Lee Jae-myung

October 27, 2026

Imagine a small, brightly lit YouTube studio in Seoul, the air thick with the tension of a political earthquake. On one side of the table sits Park Si-young, a prominent political strategist and pollster whose channel, Park Si-young TV, has become a digital town square for South Korea’s liberal base. On the other sits Rhyu Si-min. To an outsider, Rhyu is an author and intellectual; to a Korean, he is the "sharpened blade" of the liberal establishment—a former Minister of Health and Welfare, a close confidant of the late President Roh Moo-hyun, and a man whose words can sway hundreds of thousands of voters.

Rhyu’s appearance on a live broadcast scheduled for Wednesday, October 28, is not a routine book promotion. It is a calculated intervention. For months, the Lee Jae-myung administration has faced quiet grumbling from its own base over its governing style. Now, Rhyu is putting those frustrations into words that sting: he is calling out President Lee for practicing "Machiavellian politics." In the world of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's current ruling party, this is a declaration of intellectual war. Rhyu is signaling that the current leadership has abandoned the moral and procedural values of previous liberal administrations—those of Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—in favor of a cynical, power-first approach.

The Intellectual and the President: A Growing Rift

To understand why this matters, one must understand the unique position Rhyu Si-min holds. He is not a politician currently seeking office, but he acts as the ideological guardian of the "pro-Roh" and "pro-Moon" factions. These are the traditionalists of the liberal movement who prioritize institutional reform and democratic procedure. When Rhyu speaks, the "4050 generation"—voters in their 40s and 50s who form the backbone of the DPK’s support—listens.

The core of Rhyu’s critique is that President Lee Jae-myung is outsourcing the "dirty work" of governance to his subordinates while keeping only the popular, populist tasks for himself. "The President is handling problems Machiavellianly," Rhyu stated bluntly. "He makes the subordinates take the heat for unpopular decisions, while he only steps out for things that will gain him popularity." This style of leadership, Rhyu argues, is a relic of the Middle Ages that cannot survive in a modern democracy where social media and real-time information make it impossible to hide the hand behind the curtain.

This is a direct hit on the President's leadership. It suggests that the current administration is more interested in theatrical popularity than in the hard, often "un-popular" work of structural reform. For the liberal base, which spent years fighting for transparent governance, seeing their own president accused of hiding behind shields and "villain-outsourcing" is a bitter pill to swallow. It raises the question: if the President will not take responsibility for the difficult tasks, who is actually running the country?

The Ghost of the Central Investigation Unit

The most explosive part of Rhyu’s criticism involves the very institution that has defined Korean political warfare for decades: the prosecution. For years, the liberal movement has sought to strip the prosecution of its dual powers to both investigate and indict, a combination they argue leads to political "target hunting."

However, under the Lee Jae-myung government, Rhyu and other critics argue that prosecution reform has not only stalled but is being reversed. There are growing fears that the administration is quietly allowing the "revival of the Central Investigation Unit" of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office. This unit was historically the most feared arm of the state, capable of crushing political enemies through "cabinet investigations"—the practice of keeping files on influential figures and opening them only when it is politically advantageous.

Rhyu points to the SillaJen case as a prime example of this dark art. SillaJen, once a star biotech firm on the KOSDAQ (Korea’s tech-heavy stock exchange), saw its valuation soar to nearly 8 trillion won (about $6 billion) before its lead cancer drug failed clinical trials. During the time when Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (who was later impeached and is currently in prison) served as Prosecutor General, the prosecution was accused of using the SillaJen investigation to find "dirt" on Rhyu Si-min and other pro-Roh figures. They allegedly pressured Lee Cheol, the head of Value Invest Korea (VIK), to testify that he had given money to Rhyu, even threatening his family’s safety to get the confession.

The fact that such "political hunting" tactics seem to be persisting, or even evolving, under a Democratic president is what has Rhyu and his allies so alarmed. They argue that the current government is not trying to reform the prosecution, but rather to tame it for its own use. "The subjects of reform are not resisting," one commentator noted in the source material. "If the prosecutors are happy, it means reform has failed."

Destruction or Expansion? The Internal War for the Democratic Party

Beyond policy, there is a deeper, more personal struggle for the soul of the Democratic Party. Rhyu uses a metaphor of "reconstruction" versus "extension." The traditional liberal base wanted to "extend" the house—to build on the foundations of the Moon Jae-in and Roh Moo-hyun eras, expanding the party's reach while keeping its core values. Instead, they argue, President Lee is attempting a "total reconstruction."

This "reconstruction" involves clearing out the old guard to make way for the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction. Rhyu and others have highlighted a trend of internal attacks on figures like former President Moon Jae-in, lawmaker Choo Mi-ae, and Rhyu himself. These figures are often lumped together under derogatory labels by the President's more aggressive supporters. Rhyu alleges that the President has personally interfered in party nominations, such as the Seoul mayoral primary and the Gyeonggi gubernatorial race, to ensure his own people are in power.

This internal purge has created a "hate politics" within the party. By allowing or encouraging his "fandom" to attack senior party members, the President is accused of breaking the historical continuity of the liberal movement. Rhyu’s warning is clear: a party that destroys its own history and betrays its veterans to build a personality cult around a single leader is a party headed for a crash. The "reconstruction" isn't just replacing windows; it's tearing down the load-bearing walls of the party’s 70-year history.

The "Show of Mercy" and the Reality of Power

A recent incident perfectly illustrates the Machiavellian style Rhyu is criticizing. A conservative activist group filed a defamation complaint against Rhyu for comments he made about the President on a broadcast. Legal experts pointed out that the complaint was baseless—subjective criticism of a political leader is not a crime in a democracy. However, the police proceeded with the investigation anyway.

Then, the Blue House (the President's office) stepped in. The President’s staff announced that Lee Jae-myung "did not wish for Rhyu to be punished," effectively ending the case. To the casual observer, this looked like an act of presidential mercy. To Rhyu and his supporters, it was a "mercy show."

"Saying he doesn't want punishment doesn't sound like tolerance; it makes it sound like there was something to punish in the first place," critics argued.

By "forgiving" Rhyu for a crime that didn't exist, the administration successfully framed Rhyu as a wrongdoer who was only saved by the President’s benevolence. It was a classic Machiavellian move: using the state’s investigative machinery to create a threat, and then stepping in as the "generous leader" to neutralize it, all while diminishing the critic's moral standing. This kind of political maneuvering is what is driving the wedge between the intellectual wing of the party and the President’s office.

What to Watch Next

As this conflict breaks into the open, several key indicators will determine whether Rhyu’s warning sparks a real shift or if the President’s "reconstruction" continues unabated.

  • Polling Trends in Honam and the 4050s: Watch the ARS (Automated Response System) polls. These often reflect the views of highly engaged political partisans. If support in the Honam region (the liberal heartland) and among voters in their 40s and 50s continues to drop, it means Rhyu’s message is resonating. A drop below 35% in these core demographics would be a "code red" for the DPK.
  • The "Supplementary Investigation" Legislation: Keep an eye on the Criminal Procedure Act amendments. If the DPK moves to allow the prosecution to keep "supplementary investigation powers," it will be a clear sign that the administration has chosen to maintain the prosecution's teeth rather than follow through on the promised "complete separation of investigation and indictment."
  • The Rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party: As dissatisfaction with the DPK’s leadership grows, look at the polling for the Rebuilding Korea Party (led by Cho Kuk). If liberal voters feel the DPK has lost its way, they may migrate to this alternative, creating a fractured liberal front that could jeopardize the next election.
  • Internal Party Discipline: Watch how the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction reacts to Rhyu’s broadcast. If they double down on the "hate politics" and continue to target party elders, the risk of a formal split in the party becomes much more real.

Rhyu Si-min’s appearance on Park Si-young TV is a high-stakes gamble. He is betting that by speaking the "cold truth" now, he can prevent the liberal movement from drifting into a cynical authoritarianism. Whether President Lee Jae-myung listens, or simply treats Rhyu as another obstacle to be "reconstructed," will define the future of South Korean politics for the next decade.
