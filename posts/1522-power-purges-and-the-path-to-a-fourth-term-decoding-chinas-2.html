Power, Purges, and the Path to a Fourth Term: Decoding China’s 20th 5th Plenum

October 27, 2026

Inside the Great Hall of the People in Beijing, the air is thick with a tension that belies the carefully choreographed smiles shown on state television. The 5th Plenary Session (often called the 5th Plenum) of the 20th Central Committee of the Communist Party of China (CCP) has officially opened, but it is not the usual gathering of the country’s elite. This meeting, held roughly a year before the next major Party Congress, is meant to be a checkpoint for national policy. Instead, it has become a theater for the most significant consolidation of power in decades. As Xi Jinping prepares the groundwork for an unprecedented fourth term, the traditional machinery of the Chinese state is being dismantled in favor of something much older and far more volatile: absolute, one-man rule.

For observers in Seoul and across the globe, the stakes could not be higher. China is South Korea’s largest trading partner and the primary patron of North Korea. When the internal gears of the CCP shift, the tremors are felt across the Yellow Sea. This particular plenum is signaling a shift away from pragmatic economic management toward ideological purity and internal "cleansing." By understanding the empty seats in the hall and the rhetoric coming from the podium, we can begin to see the future of East Asian stability—and the immense challenge facing the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea's current leader from the liberal Democratic Party.

The Ritual of Power and the Empty Chairs

To understand why this meeting matters, one must first understand what the Central Committee is. It is essentially the "board of directors" for the Chinese state, consisting of about 205 full members and 171 alternate members who represent the top tier of the military, provincial governments, and ministries. Under normal circumstances, a "Plenum"—a full meeting of this group—is where five-year plans are debated and major economic reforms are announced. However, the 20th 5th Plenum has been defined by who is not there.

Reports indicate that a staggering 85 high-ranking officials were absent from this session. In the context of the CCP, such a high number of absences among the elite is unheard of and points to a single, brutal reality: a massive, ongoing purge. These are not merely bureaucrats who called in sick; they are the victims of a relentless anti-corruption campaign that has increasingly focused on political loyalty rather than financial impropriety. The "blade is turned inward," as Party rhetoric often describes it, cutting away any remnants of opposition or even mild skepticism toward Xi’s leadership.

This purge is the necessary prelude to Xi Jinping’s bid for a fourth term. By removing 85 members of the elite—many from the military and the powerful Rocket Force—Xi is ensuring that there is no institutional weight left to balance his authority. The 5th Plenum, which historically served as a space for the Party to course-correct, has been transformed into a loyalty test. For the remaining members, the message is clear: survival depends on absolute alignment with the "core" leader.

Ideology Over Markets: The Death of Pragmatism

Global investors and market analysts had been hoping that this plenum would finally deliver a massive stimulus package to rescue China’s ailing property sector and jumpstart consumer spending. Instead, they were met with a lecture on "strict party management" and ideological discipline. This pivot is perhaps the most concerning takeaway for the international community. Xi Jinping is signaling that he is willing to accept slower economic growth—and the social friction that comes with it—in exchange for total control.

The source material highlights that the primary agenda was not the economy, but "comprehensive and strict Party governance." In Xi’s worldview, China is entering a period of "great struggles" against a hostile West. To survive these "darker storms," he believes the Party must be purged of all ideological weakness. This is a departure from the era of Deng Xiaoping, the leader who opened China's economy in the late 1970s, whose philosophy was famously "it doesn't matter if a cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice." Under Xi, it seems the color of the cat—its ideological purity—is now more important than its ability to catch the "mouse" of economic prosperity.

The decision to prioritize internal control over economic relief is a classic crisis-management tactic. When a regime can no longer justify its rule through rising living standards, it must resort to tightening the screws of loyalty and fear.

This shift has direct consequences for the global market. If the CCP is no longer focused on market-driven growth, the predictability that businesses rely on disappears. Policies can change overnight based on the ideological whims of a single leader, making China a "black box" for foreign capital. For South Korea, which remains deeply integrated with Chinese supply chains, this unpredictability is a primary economic risk that the Lee Jae-myung government must now navigate with extreme caution.

The Ghost of Mao and the "Lingxiu" System

For decades, China operated under a "collective leadership" model. The idea was to prevent the return of a figure like Mao Zedong, whose unchecked power led to the tragedies of the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. There were term limits, age requirements, and a general consensus among the top seven or nine men in the Politburo Standing Committee (the CCP's inner sanctum). This system has been methodically dismantled.

The 5th Plenum marks the formalization of what many are calling the "Lingxiu" (Supreme Leader) system. This title carries a weight that the standard "General Secretary" does not; it implies a spiritual and historical mandate to lead the nation. By positioning himself as the "Lingxiu," Xi is moving beyond being a mere politician to becoming a historical figure on par with Mao. This isn't just about a fourth term; it’s about a lifetime mandate.

The danger of this model is the "dictator’s trap." When a leader surrounds himself with "yes-men" who are too terrified to report bad news, the quality of decision-making collapses. We see this in the military purges, where the leadership of the People's Liberation Army (PLA)—specifically the units responsible for China’s nuclear and missile capabilities—has been gutted. Xi is prioritizing personal loyalty over professional competence in the military, a move that could lead to dangerous miscalculations in the South China Sea or the Taiwan Strait. If the "Great Helmsman" is the only one allowed to steer, the ship of state becomes dangerously vulnerable to his personal blind spots.

Seoul’s Strategic Pivot: Pragmatism in a New Era

As China retreats into ideological isolation, South Korea finds itself in a precarious position. The current administration of President Lee Jae-myung is tasked with cleaning up the diplomatic wreckage left behind by his predecessor. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached and is currently in prison, followed an ideological foreign policy that often alienated Beijing without securing concrete gains from other partners. His tenure was marked by a rigid adherence to a "values-based" diplomacy that many critics, including the current ruling Democratic Party, argue ignored the cold realities of South Korea’s geopolitical neighborhood.

President Lee Jae-myung has signaled a return to "pragmatic diplomacy." This does not mean a submissive relationship with China, but rather a sophisticated, multi-vector approach. Seoul must maintain its vital security alliance with the United States while preventing a total breakdown in relations with a newly assertive and ideological China. The 5th Plenum’s outcome suggests that Beijing will be less willing to compromise on regional issues, viewing any South Korean alignment with the West as a direct threat to the CCP’s internal security.

The Lee administration’s challenge is to diversify South Korea's trade dependencies—reducing the "China risk"—while keeping diplomatic channels open to prevent accidental conflict. As Xi tightens his grip on the military, the risk of a "nationalist distraction"—a manufactured crisis abroad to justify domestic control—increases. South Korea must be ready for a China that is more prone to using economic coercion as a political tool, as seen in past disputes over missile defense systems.

What to Watch Next

The conclusion of the 5th Plenum is not the end of this story; it is the beginning of a high-stakes countdown to the next National Party Congress. Here are the concrete developments to monitor:

  • Personnel Appointments: Watch for the names of the "rising stars" who fill the 85 vacancies in the Central Committee. If they are all direct associates of Xi from his time in Zhejiang or Fujian, it confirms the total death of factional balance within the Party.
  • Military Readiness: Following the purge of the Rocket Force, keep a close eye on the PLA’s exercises near Taiwan. A leader who has just "cleansed" his military often feels the need to test its loyalty and effectiveness through provocations.
  • Economic "Self-Reliance": Look for policies emphasizing "Dual Circulation"—a strategy to make China less dependent on foreign technology and markets. This will be the clearest sign of how much economic pain Xi is willing to endure for the sake of security.
  • Seoul’s Diplomatic Tempo: Watch how President Lee Jae-myung’s Blue House (the presidential office) handles upcoming high-level summits. A move toward a "middle power" diplomacy, where Korea acts as a bridge rather than a frontline, would be a significant departure from the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era.

China is turning inward, closing its doors to the pragmatism that once fueled its rise. For the rest of the world, and especially for its neighbors in Korea, the challenge is no longer just about trade—it is about managing the risks of a giant that is becoming more powerful, more opaque, and more afraid of its own shadow.
