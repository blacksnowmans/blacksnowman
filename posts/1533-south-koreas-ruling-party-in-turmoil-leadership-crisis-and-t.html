South Korea's Ruling Party in Turmoil: Leadership Crisis and the Push to Oust Kim Min-seok

October 27, 2026

Inside the pressurized bubble of Seoul's political district, a quiet panic is taking hold of South Korea's ruling party. Just weeks into a new administration, the political conversation has pivoted sharply away from legislative agendas and toward an acute internal crisis: whispers of an impending purge at the top of the Democratic Party of Korea, the center-left party currently in power. At the center of the storm is Kim Min-seok, the party's interim leader, whose grip on power appears to be slipping by the day. When news broke of a private meeting between President Lee Jae-myung and Woo Won-shik, a prominent party veteran and former National Assembly speaker, it acted as a flashing red light for the political establishment. The message was unmistakable: the current leadership is failing, and the executive branch is growing impatient.

For observers outside Korea, watching a ruling party fracture so early in an administration can seem counterintuitive. President Lee secured the presidency on a mandate of decisive reform and sweeping governance overhaul after the tumultuous downfall of former conservative leader Yoon Seok-youl, who is now stripped of his title and imprisoned. Yet, rather than enjoying a traditional honeymoon period, the administration finds its momentum choked by internal divisions, stalling approval ratings, and a ruling party apparatus that appears fundamentally incapable of executing the legislative vision demanded by its base.

President Lee Jae-myung's SOS Signal and the Woo Won-shik Meeting

When President Lee summoned Woo Won-shik for a closed-door sit-down, it was treated by political insiders as far more than a routine courtesy call. In the intricate ecosystem of Korean party politics, meetings of this weight are signals of structural realignment. Analysts across the political spectrum view the encounter as a clear warning shot aimed at Kim Min-seok's leadership, signaling that the Blue House is preparing for an early exit strategy to replace the current party brass.

Kim's leadership was initially tasked with a straightforward mandate: maintain stability and manage the party machinery smoothly during the transition into the Lee administration. Instead, it has morphed into what critics inside and outside the party are openly labeling a failure of political intelligence. At a time when President Lee desperately needs a unified, disciplined legislative partner to push through critical reforms, the party leadership has instead amplified internal friction. Dissatisfaction with Kim has ballooned from quiet grumbling over sliding approval ratings into an existential panic that the party is actively squandering its governing momentum.

The outreach to Woo Won-shik suggests that the president is looking past the current leadership to orchestrate a controlled exit or a forced transition. This goes beyond mere musical chairs among political elites. It is a reflection of deep-seated anxiety within the Democratic Party over how to claw back public trust before the current paralysis hardens into permanent political damage.

Unresolved Scandals and the Demand for Accountability

Compounding the strategic missteps of the leadership are severe ethical clouds hanging over the party's top office. Internal pressure is mounting over unaddressed controversies, notably allegations surrounding a secretive three-way political alignment linked to the controversial Shincheonji religious movement. According to recent investigative reporting by the Hankook Ilbo, a major South Korean daily, a consensus has formed among rank-and-file lawmakers that the party can no longer sweep these allegations under the rug.

Frustration has boiled over in private and public caucus meetings. Senior multi-term lawmakers have begun openly demanding accountability, arguing that the party cannot simply pretend the controversy does not exist. The demand from within the party's own ranks is blunt: release the internal investigation findings transparently, hold responsible parties accountable, and cooperate with any necessary legal inquiries. Mid-tier lawmakers have echoed these warnings, pressing the leadership to make a hard choice rather than hiding behind procedural defenses.

Yet, the response from the leadership has been a frustrating exercise in institutional stonewalling. Officials close to Kim Min-seok have retreated into procedural formalism, insisting that they are merely following party rules and doing their jobs. To critics, citing party bylaws is nothing more than a defensive reflex designed to maintain legal control while ignoring the political reality. In a political culture that prizes swift, decisive action, hiding behind technicalities has only widened the chasm between the leadership and the public.

The Cost of Hesitation: Stalled Reforms and Sinking Polls

The roots of this crisis run deeper than one leader's missteps; they lie in a collective hesitation to seize the moment when the administration held its strongest hand. Political commentators analyzing the current downturn point out a bitter irony: during the height of their political leverage, the ruling party delayed difficult structural reforms out of a nervous fear of political backlash.

This dynamic has played out vividly in debates over the judiciary. Pundits have criticized the party's reluctance to aggressively pursue the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, noting that while leadership feared political blowback, the actual blowback has arrived precisely because they failed to deliver on deep-seated reform. As critics have bluntly noted, the fierce counter-offensive from the conservative-leaning judicial establishment did not materialize out of thin air—it was invited by the ruling camp's own complacency.

This cautious paralysis stands in stark contrast to President Lee's early governance style, which initially won praise for its radical transparency and direct communication. But as public approval ratings have slipped, that same directness has curdled in the public eye. What was once seen as refreshing openness is now increasingly viewed by voters as stubbornness and administrative tone-deafness. The party's leadership operates under the comforting illusion that their reformist credentials remain intact, while everyday voters experience only administrative gridlock and political bickering.

What to Watch Next

As the political landscape shifts toward a potential leadership showdown, several concrete indicators will determine whether the Democratic Party can right the ship or spiral into deeper fragmentation:

    Internal Caucus Rebellions: Watch for formal demands from multi-term and junior lawmakers calling an emergency general meeting of the party to challenge Kim Min-seok directly. If open letters or collective statements multiply, Kim's position will become untenable. Blue House Signals: Pay close attention to further meetings or public nods from President Lee toward alternative leadership figures like Woo Won-shik or other heavyweights, which would confirm that an orderly replacement process is underway. Handling of Investigative Pressures: Monitor how the party leadership addresses the Shincheonji-related allegations. Continued stonewalling will likely trigger deeper defections, while an abrupt pivot toward transparency could signal a desperate bid by Kim to save his job. Public Opinion Metrics: Keep an eye on weekly tracking polls, particularly regional sentiment shifts outside the Seoul metropolitan area, which will dictate how quickly nervous lawmakers abandon the current leadership ahead of upcoming electoral cycles.

The clock is ticking for the current ruling party leadership. If they fail to read the room and misjudge the depth of public and internal discontent, the transition from a powerful reform administration to an isolated, fractured regime may happen much faster than anyone anticipated.
