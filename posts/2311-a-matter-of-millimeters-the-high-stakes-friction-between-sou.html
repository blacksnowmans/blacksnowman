A Matter of Millimeters: The High-Stakes Friction Between South Korea’s Ruling Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party

November 14, 2026

Imagine a marriage proposal where one partner announces the wedding date to the local newspaper before even asking for the other’s hand. In the corridors of the National Assembly in Seoul, this is precisely the atmosphere surrounding the rumored merger between the Democratic Party of Korea, the nation's liberal ruling party, and the Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller but influential progressive group led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. While the Democratic Party—the political home of President Lee Jae-myung—has been signaling that a grand unification is imminent to bolster the administration’s legislative power, the response from the other side has been anything but romantic. Cho Kuk himself recently declared that merger discussions have progressed "not even by a single millimeter," sparking a "truth game" that highlights deep-seated distrust and a fierce battle for political autonomy.

To an outside observer, a merger might seem like a natural evolution. Both parties sit on the liberal-progressive side of the aisle and generally support the policy agenda of the Lee Jae-myung government. However, the current friction is about more than just seat counts in the legislature. It is a clash between a "big tent" ruling party trying to consolidate its grip on power and a smaller, more ideologically "sharp" party that fears being swallowed whole. As the Lee Jae-myung administration seeks to maintain its governing momentum, this internal rift in the progressive camp has become a focal point of South Korean domestic politics, revealing that the path to a "unified front" is blocked by heavy emotional baggage and strategic disagreements.

The Ruling Party’s Push for a "Unified Front"

The Democratic Party of Korea (DP) is currently operating from a position of significant responsibility. As the ruling party supporting President Lee Jae-myung, its primary goal is to ensure that the administration’s core policies—ranging from economic reforms to social welfare expansions—pass through the National Assembly with minimal friction. To do this, the DP needs a stable and disciplined majority. High-ranking members, such as Representative Lee Hae-shik, have recently begun floating the idea that a merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP) is not just a possibility, but a necessity for "national stability."

The logic from the DP’s leadership is straightforward: why remain as two separate entities when you share the same goals? By merging, the DP could simplify the legislative process and present a truly unified face to the public. However, the way the DP has handled this outreach has been criticized as "media play." By leaking news of potential leadership meetings to the press before they were finalized, the DP leadership attempted to create a sense of inevitability. This "top-down" pressure tactic is often used in Korean politics to force a smaller partner to the negotiating table, but in this case, it appears to have backfired spectacularly.

For the DP, the RKP represents a vital but sometimes unpredictable ally. The RKP’s supporters are often more ideologically driven and aggressive in their pursuit of judicial and social reforms—traits that helped them gain significant traction during the last general election. By absorbing the RKP, the DP hopes to capture that energy while bringing it under the centralized control of the ruling party's leadership. Yet, by treating the RKP as a subordinate branch rather than an equal partner, the DP has touched a nerve, turning a strategic discussion into a matter of pride and survival for Cho Kuk and his followers.

Cho Kuk’s "One Millimeter" Defiance and the Demand for an Apology

The response from the Rebuilding Korea Party has been chillingly cold. Cho Kuk, the party’s leader, has framed the DP’s merger talk as a deceptive performance—what Koreans call "show-tong" (a play on words meaning "show-style communication"). His insistence that "not even 1mm" of progress has been made is a deliberate attempt to shut down the DP’s narrative. For the RKP, their political identity is built on being "sharper" and "faster" than the mainstream Democratic Party. They see themselves as the vanguard of the progressive movement, and they fear that a merger would dilute their message into the DP’s more cautious, broad-based platform.

Beyond strategy, there is a deep sense of personal and institutional grievance. Cho Kuk has explicitly stated that no dialogue can begin until the Democratic Party offers a formal apology for past slights. Specifically, he is referring to the "secret pact" rumors that circulated within DP circles. These rumors suggested that Cho Kuk had made a backroom deal with high-ranking DP officials, such as Representative Jung Chung-rae, to trade his party’s independence for future political favors or a path to the presidency. Cho views these rumors not just as a lie, but as a coordinated attempt to undermine his party's legitimacy and treat them as a mere faction of the DP.

"A vision without dialogue is not a proposal; it is a lack of basic courtesy," Cho Kuk remarked, targeting the DP leadership.

This demand for an apology is a high-stakes political maneuver. By making a formal apology a prerequisite for talks, Cho Kuk is asserting the RKP's status as a sovereign political entity. He is signaling to his base—and to President Lee Jae-myung’s office—that the RKP will not be bullied or absorbed quietly. For Cho, the "millimeter" isn't just a unit of distance; it represents the vast gap in trust that the DP must bridge before any real cooperation can resume.

A Base Divided: The Polls and the "One Team" Dilemma

The tension between the two parties is mirrored in the demographics of their supporters. A recent Gallup poll of approximately 1,000 South Koreans revealed a striking split: among those who identify as supporters of the progressive camp, 44% are in favor of a merger, while 43% are opposed. This nearly identical split shows that the "truth game" being played by the leadership is causing genuine confusion and debate among the rank-and-file voters who put President Lee Jae-myung in office.

Those in favor of the merger argue from a position of pragmatism. They believe in the "One Team" philosophy—the idea that any division within the progressive camp only serves the interest of the opposition, the People Power Party (PPP). From their perspective, the primary goal should be protecting the Lee Jae-myung administration from political attacks and ensuring that the government’s mandate is carried out. They see the RKP’s insistence on independence as a form of "political ego" that risks fragmenting the coalition at a time when unity is paramount.

On the other hand, the 43% who oppose the merger value diversity and "sharpness." Many of these voters chose the RKP specifically because they felt the DP was too slow or too moderate on issues like judicial reform and the prosecution of past wrongdoings. They worry that if the RKP is absorbed, the "whip" that keeps the DP moving forward will be lost. They prefer a "strategic partnership" where the two parties coordinate on major votes but maintain separate identities. This split suggests that even if the leadership were to agree on a merger tomorrow, they would face a significant challenge in convincing nearly half of their base that it was the right move.

The Stakes for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

Why does this internal squabble matter to someone outside of Korea? It matters because the stability of the Lee Jae-myung administration depends on a functional relationship between these two groups. Unlike the previous era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, which was defined by extreme polarization and eventual impeachment, the current government is trying to prove it can govern effectively and move the country forward. If the ruling party and its closest ally are at each other's throats over "media play" and "secret pacts," the legislative engine of the country could grind to a halt.

The Democratic Party’s rush to merge may be born out of a fear of the RKP’s rising influence. If the RKP continues to act as a "sharper" alternative, it could eventually peel away enough voters to challenge the DP’s dominance within the liberal bloc. Conversely, the RKP’s refusal to merge could be seen as a gamble on Cho Kuk's own political future. By staying independent, he keeps his options open for a future presidential run, whereas merging would likely require him to defer to President Lee Jae-myung’s established hierarchy.

Ultimately, the current standoff is a lesson in the importance of political "etiquette" and trust-building. In a system like South Korea’s, where political loyalty is highly valued, the DP’s attempt to force a merger through the media was a tactical error that ignored the pride of its partner. For the Lee Jae-myung government to maintain its national momentum, the DP leadership will likely need to move beyond "show-tong" and offer the genuine, face-to-face respect that Cho Kuk is demanding.

What to Watch Next

The next few months will be critical for the future of the South Korean progressive coalition. Here are the key indicators to watch:

  • The Formal Apology: Watch to see if the DP leadership, particularly figures like Representative Jung Chung-rae or the party's Supreme Council members, issues a statement addressing the "secret pact" rumors. A formal apology would be the first real sign that the "1mm" gap is starting to close.
  • The Leadership Meeting: Despite the denials, rumors of a meeting between Cho Kuk and DP's Kim Min-seok persist. If and when this meeting happens, the official readout will tell us whether they are discussing a total merger or merely a "cooperation agreement" for the upcoming legislative session.
  • Public Sentiment Shifts: If the 44/43 split in the polls shifts significantly in one direction, it will give one side the mandate to either force the merger or walk away for good. If opposition to the merger grows among RKP supporters, Cho Kuk will have even more reason to maintain a hard line.
  • Legislative Coordination: Pay attention to how the two parties vote on high-profile bills in the National Assembly. If they begin to diverge on key government-backed legislation, it will be a sign that the rift is moving from a personal dispute to a policy-based schism.

In the world of South Korean politics, millimeters can feel like miles. Whether the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party can find a way to walk that distance together will determine the strength of the ruling coalition for the remainder of President Lee Jae-myung’s term.
