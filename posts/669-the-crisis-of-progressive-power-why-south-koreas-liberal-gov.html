The Crisis of Progressive Power: Why South Korea's Liberal Government is Losing its Base

October 04, 2026

As the autumn chill sets in following the Chuseok harvest festival, political commentators across South Korea are sounding alarms that would have seemed unthinkable just months ago. On popular political commentary broadcasts, analysts who once championed the current liberal administration are speaking with heavy hearts. What was supposed to be a season of bounty has instead soured into a period of deep introspection for the nation's progressives. The core of their anxiety is sharp and unsparing: if an election were held today, the ruling liberal bloc faces a crushing defeat in Seoul and the greater metropolitan area, where nearly half the country lives.

For outside observers watching South Korean politics from afar, the country often appears as a high-tech democracy ping-ponging dramatically between conservative and progressive leadership. But the current moment is not merely a routine swing of the electoral pendulum. It is a profound institutional crisis born of broken promises, economic stagnation for the working class, and a growing disconnect between a powerful political party and the grassroots citizens who put them in office. To understand why South Korea's current administration is hemorrhaging support, one must look past the glittering facade of Asia's fourth-largest economy and examine the quiet fractures opening up underneath.

The Erosion of Progressive Ideals and the Return of the Right

When the administration of President Lee Jae-myung took power, it did so with a sweeping mandate for reform. The rhetoric was grand—promising an egalitarian society where prosperity would be shared by all. Yet, as recent months have shown, the reality on the ground has fallen woefully short of these lofty ambitions. A presidential apology and subsequent press conference ahead of the Chuseok holidays had raised hopes that the executive branch was finally listening to its critics. Instead, subsequent personnel appointments and policy rollouts left many feeling that the administration was merely buying time rather than changing course.

Critics within the progressive movement argue that the government has diluted its core reform agenda out of fear of alienating moderate voters. High-profile intellectuals, such as writer Yu Si-min, have publicly criticized the watering-down of judicial and prosecutorial reforms, accusing the leadership of misleading the public about the true scope of intended changes. Meanwhile, the People Power Party—South Korea's main conservative opposition, which many expected to remain politically sidelined following past scandals—has found new life. Rather than soul-searching or disbanding, conservative figures have grown increasingly bold, enabled largely by the perceived ineptitude and missteps of the ruling party.

This dynamic has created a bitter irony for liberal voters. They see a ruling camp that is paralyzed by internal divisions and lacking a coherent legislative strategy, unable to effectively counter conservative rhetoric in the National Assembly. The leadership tandem of President Lee Jae-myung and party leader Kim Min-seok is facing historic lows in public approval ratings precisely because they have failed to present bold, structural alternatives to the country's deep-seated inequalities.

Economic Disparity: The Semiconductor Boom and the Forgotten Worker

While political spats dominate television screens, the deeper crisis for most ordinary South Koreans is economic. Government employment data paints a stark picture of a two-tiered labor market. While South Korea’s crown jewel—the semiconductor industry dominated by giants like Samsung and SK Hynix—continues to generate immense wealth, the rest of the economy is stagnating.

For workers outside the high-tech sector, particularly in small and medium-sized enterprises, real wages have flatlined or even decreased over the past year. Statistics from the Ministry of Employment and Labor show that after peaking briefly, workers' real wages have experienced consecutive periods of contraction. In a society where consumer inflation has driven up the cost of living in dense urban centers like Seoul, a stagnant paycheck means a steadily declining standard of living.

Yet, critics argue that the government's response has been superficial. Rather than instituting structural reforms to social safety nets or addressing the structural gap in wealth distribution, the administration has often relied on temporary, one-off measures—such as holiday shopping subsidies rolled out ahead of major festivals. Commentators have likened these policies to feudal handouts rather than the systematic redistribution required in a modern advanced economy. When working-class voters feel that a progressive government is behaving no differently than its conservative predecessors in managing economic inequality, the sense of betrayal is palpable.

Institutional Friction and the Battle Within the State

Compounding these economic woes is open institutional friction between the executive branch and key state apparatuses, most notably the prosecution and the justice system. As political support for the administration slips, traditional power centers within the bureaucracy have begun to push back against government oversight.

Recent internal leaks and public statements from senior legal officials—such as Kim Sung-dong, head of the Inspection Division at the Prosecution Service—have challenged the legality of government-led investigative bodies targeting the judiciary. These public acts of insubordination highlight a government losing its grip on the administrative machinery. When an administration's political capital is high, state agencies tend to fall in line; when it weakens, institutional resistance multiplies. The current standoff suggests that the executive branch lacks both the legislative dominance and the moral authority to push through its desired structural overhauls.

Furthermore, the administration's defensive posture regarding historical and constitutional controversies—such as how it handles far-right revisionism concerning democratic milestones like the May 18 Gwangju Uprising—has alienated its core ideological base. When conservative figures openly challenge historical consensus with apparent impunity, the ruling party's sluggish and legalistic responses are interpreted by its supporters as weakness or, worse, indifference.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward upcoming legislative milestones and future local elections, several concrete indicators will determine whether the current administration can arrest its decline or whether it is heading toward a historic defeat:

    National Assembly Audits: Keep an eye on how aggressively the ruling party uses the current parliamentary audit period to push concrete legislative proposals rather than engaging in rhetorical skirmishes with the opposition. Real Wage and Employment Trends: Watch upcoming releases from the Ministry of Employment and Labor to see if the government introduces genuine structural measures to address the wage gap between semiconductor conglomerates and small-business workers. Internal Party Realignment: Monitor whether grassroots pressure forces a leadership shakeup within the Democratic Party to distance itself from the current stagnation before upcoming electoral cycles.

For an administration that came to power promising a fundamental transformation of South Korean society, the margin for error is rapidly vanishing. If the ruling camp continues to substitute symbolic gestures for structural reform, the ultimate price will be paid not just by politicians at the ballot box, but by the everyday workers whose livelihoods hang in the balance.
