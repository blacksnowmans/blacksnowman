The Forgotten Guardians: Inside Korea’s Quiet Revolution in Fish Conservation and Care

September 08, 2026

There are many strange jobs in this world, but if you saw a man holding a stethoscope to a sick fish or wielding a surgical scalpel to save a creature no larger than your thumb, you might think you were witnessing a hallucination. Yet, this is the emerging reality in South Korea, where the "pet fish" market has moved from the back corners of hobbyist basements into the professional spotlight. In a country where the goldfish bowl has long been a staple of the living room, people are increasingly willing to pay for specialized medical care for their aquatic companions—creatures you cannot cuddle, stroke, or hold.

According to data from the Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs, fish are now the third most popular pet in South Korea, trailing only dogs and cats. As this industry grows into a sector worth hundreds of billions of won (hundreds of millions of dollars), it has been built on the backs of a dedicated, somewhat eccentric group of experts who have spent their lives peering through magnifying glasses at the water’s edge.

The Pioneers of Aquatic Science

The landscape of Korean fish research is defined by a few key figures, most notably Lee Wan-ok, chairman of the Korea Freshwater Fish Conservation Association, and Hwang Seon-do, a prominent marine ecologist. Their work—and the work of Choi Sang-ho, who runs Seoul's only dedicated hospital for ornamental fish—is a testament to a life spent in the shadows of "big science."

Lee Wan-ok has dedicated 45 years to documenting Korea’s inland water ecosystems. His career began in an era when field research was a grueling, solitary pursuit. He has discovered and named several new species, including the "Buan-jonggae," a small loach found in Buan, North Jeolla Province. While the academic world demands rigorous, dry naming conventions, Lee’s work serves a deeper purpose: identifying and documenting species before they vanish due to habitat destruction.

The tragedy of many of these indigenous species is that they are disappearing before they are even widely known. Lee and his colleagues argue that the key to conservation isn't just locking ecosystems away from humans, but fostering public appreciation. When a fish becomes a cherished pet, it gains a constituency—people who care if its habitat remains clean and its population stable.

When Life Doesn’t Go According to Plan

The path to these careers is rarely paved with grand, childhood ambitions. Take Choi Sang-ho, the founder of Korea’s first ornamental fish hospital. His journey through academia was defined by irony: he entered the Department of Oceanography not out of a burning passion for the sea, but because his college entrance exam scores pointed him there. He navigated a career path through theology, business management, and finally, aquatic life science—driven more by the necessity of survival and an eventual return to his deep-seated instinctual love for fish.

Choi’s story is a sobering look at how "success" is often just a byproduct of persistence in the face of mundane choices. His discovery of the "Yellow Sea Rockfish" (Sebastes koreanus)—a species he once proudly claimed as a Korean specialty—only for Chinese researchers to prove its existence in Shandong, serves as a humbler reminder that nature does not care about national borders or the academic ego of scientists. It is a lesson in the fluidity of the natural world, and in the resilience required to study it.

The Birth of the "Fish Doctor"

Perhaps the most radical development in this field is the rise of the ornamental fish clinic. For decades, South Korea’s aquatic research was strictly utilitarian, focused on maximizing the yield of food fish like mackerel or rockfish for the culinary market. The transition to treating "ornamental" fish represents a shift in how Koreans view non-mammalian life. When a fish is given a name and brought into a home, it ceases to be a commodity and becomes a family member.

The scarcity of this service is stark: in a country with a pet population of 15 million, there are only three or four qualified experts in the entire nation capable of providing medical care to ornamental fish. Choi’s clinic, the only one of its kind in the Seoul metropolitan area, is a response to this massive void. As Choi often notes, the act of watching fish swim in a quiet tank is a profound stress-reliever for urbanites living in high-pressure environments. The fish, in turn, provide a silent, steady companionship that requires nothing more than a well-maintained environment.

However, this industry is not without its dark side. The thoughtless release of pets into the wild or the barbaric practice of decorating animals for religious festivals are constant reminders that human "affection" can often lead to ecological disaster. The fish doctor’s role is as much about cleaning up the mess caused by human ignorance as it is about treating the animals themselves.

What Lies Ahead

For those looking to understand the future of Korea’s animal welfare and environmental policy, watch how the government and the public interact with these small, silent populations. The recent publication of a "Manual for Diseases of Freshwater Ornamental Organisms" by the National Institute of Fisheries Science is a vital, long-overdue step toward a standardized, preventive approach to care.

The real shift, however, is cultural. It is found in the people who are beginning to realize that the value of an ecosystem is not measured by its commercial utility, but by the diversity of the lives it supports. As we continue to urbanize, the "quiet revolution" of the fish hospital serves as a litmus test for our empathy. We are moving toward a society where the health of a tiny, nameless loach matters just as much as the health of a dog or a cat.

True progress in Korea will not come from massive, top-down infrastructure projects, but from the quiet, persistent work of those like Lee, Hwang, and Choi. They have spent decades looking through microscopes and into murky riverbeds, proving that even in a world obsessed with big, loud, and expensive solutions, the most meaningful work is often found in the smallest of places.
