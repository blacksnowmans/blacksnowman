South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Fracturing, And the Left Is Eating Its Own

October 31, 2026

Walk through the quiet neighborhoods of Seoul right now, and you can sense a peculiar kind of political exhaustion hanging in the air. South Korea has barely had time to catch its breath after the tumultuous fall of the conservative administration and the snap election that brought President Lee Jae-myung to power. Yet, inside the grand offices of the liberal establishment, the celebration is a distant memory. Instead of consolidating power and driving a sweeping reform agenda, the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal party—is tearing itself apart from the inside out.

For outside observers watching South Korea's dynamic politics, the spectacle can be dizzying. How does a progressive government, backed by an absolute majority and a wave of popular mandate, suddenly find its approval ratings in freefall, its core supporters jumping ship, and its leadership openly mocked by its own heavyweights? The answer lies in a toxic mix of internal factionalism, strategic blunders, and a high-stakes constitutional chicken game with the country's top judiciary. Understanding this crisis means looking past the daily headlines and examining how South Korea's progressive camp is grappling with the heavy, often paralyzing responsibility of actually governing.

Leadership Chaos: When the Ruling Party Turns on Itself

The trouble centers around the party leadership under Kim Min-seok, a senior figure whose control over the Democratic Party is looking increasingly shaky. Whispers of replacing the current leadership with an emergency committee—an institutional admission of failure in South Korean party politics—are growing louder by the day. The immediate spark has been a series of bitter internal feuds over seemingly disparate issues: an investigation into a secretive religious sect's alleged mass infiltration of party ranks, and a polarizing push to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

While the country faces pressing economic hurdles and deep-seated structural reforms, lawmakers have spent precious political capital on internecine bickering. This has pushed rank-and-file members to the edge of exasperation. Veteran politicians have started breaking ranks to sound the alarm. Woo Won-shik, a senior lawmaker and former National Assembly speaker, took to social media with a blunt public rebuke: “Just how long are we going to keep this up? Is this really more important than the lives of our citizens right now?” He warned that if the party squanders the hard-won opportunity handed to them by the public through internal bickering, voters will never forgive them.

These warnings are far from mere emotional outbursts. They reflect a growing panic that the administration is burning through its honeymoon period. Leaked private messages from party insiders have only poured fuel on the fire, with one prominent lawmaker reportedly dismissing the party leadership as "stupidly dictatorial" for its failure to manage internal dissent. When a ruling party begins talking openly about dissolving its own leadership structure just months into a term, it signals a profound failure of strategy and communication.

The Poll Collapse and the Exodus to the Rebuilding Korea Party

The political cost of this internal chaos is showing up clearly in the data. Recent surveys from pollsters like Realmeter and EveryResearch show the Democratic Party’s approval rating plunging—dropping by as much as 7 to 10 percentage points in a matter of weeks, allowing the conservative People Power Party to pull even or edge ahead.

Most alarmingly for the progressives, the bleeding is happening among their most loyal demographics. The party has shed double-digit support among progressive voters in their forties, while support among voters in their twenties and thirties has been slashed in half. Discontent over recent bureaucratic appointments, clumsy security management, and lingering anxieties in the Seoul metropolitan housing market have all contributed. But the deeper issue is a familiar progressive malady: when poll numbers are high, reforms are delayed out of fear of a conservative backlash; when poll numbers drop, the party becomes too paralyzed to act.

Crucially, disillusioned voters are not simply staying home—they are migrating. Youm Si-min, a prominent liberal intellectual and commentator, recently offered a sharp analysis of this shift. He noted that the drop in Democratic support and the concurrent rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, hawkish progressive satellite party—are two sides of the same coin. Voters unhappy with the Democratic Party's internal squabbles are using the Rebuilding Korea Party as a vehicle to send a sharp warning shot to the administration. Over 90% of those supporting the splinter party express dissatisfaction with Lee Jae-myung’s current governance. Far from a splintering of the progressive camp’s ideological base, it is a disciplined cry of anger from a betrayed base.

The Shadow of the Past and the Myth of the "Demonization Frame"

To understand the current anxiety, one must look at the historical trajectory of South Korean presidential politics. Progressive icons from Roh Moo-hyun to Moon Jae-in often struggled with tight parliamentary margins or hostile legislative majorities. Lee Jae-myung swept into office with 56.0% of the vote—an impressive mandate born largely out of the immense public backlash against the disastrous martial law declaration and subsequent impeachment of former President Yoon Seok-yeol, who is currently imprisoned.

Yet, maintaining that mandate has proven brutal. While Lee himself noted in a recent interview with The Economist that the risk of conservative media and prosecutors continuing their "demonization" of his administration remains high, blaming hostile outside forces no longer covers for internal policy missteps. Initial approval ratings that soared after his inauguration have suffered a "golden cross," with negative evaluations overtaking positive ones as cabinet controversies and economic realities set in.

The lesson from past progressive governments is stark. If the ruling camp retreats into a siege mentality, viewing every piece of criticism as an enemy plot, they risk alienating the moderate middle. The administration must use tangible policy wins—not just defensive rhetoric—to prove that the conservative framing of incompetence is entirely hollow.

Judicial Brinkmanship: The Clash with the Supreme Court

Nowhere is the administration's aggressive reform drive causing more friction than in its direct collision with the judiciary. The government's core push for judicial reform has crashed headfirst into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. When the presidential office rejected a formal judicial recommendation submitted by a senior judge, Cho fought back by refusing to reappoint key justices, sparking a fierce institutional standoff over whether executive appointment power supersedes judicial independence.

This battle is not merely academic. It serves as the backdrop for several high-stakes legal time bombs hanging over South Korean politics, ranging from high-profile political rulings to potential reviews of past decisions involving key political figures. Hardliners within the Democratic Party have floated the unprecedented option of drafting an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice.

However, forcing an impeachment through the National Assembly carries immense political risk. With Cho’s term winding down, initiating a lengthy constitutional battle risks painting the ruling party as overreaching bullies attempting to subjugate the courts. A more measured legislative approach to institutional reform, rather than an emotional war of attrition, is desperately needed if the progressives want to avoid looking like they are dismantling the rule of law to achieve their goals.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this turbulent political chapter, a few concrete markers will determine whether the ruling camp can right the ship or spiral into deeper irrelevance:

    Party Leadership Reshuffling: Watch whether internal pressure forces Kim Min-seok’s leadership to step aside for an emergency preparedness committee, or if they manage a fragile truce during upcoming plenary sessions. Voter Sentiment in Key Demographics: Keep a close eye on weekly tracking polls to see if the outflow of voters in their 30s and 40s stabilizes, or if support continues to hemorrhage toward the Rebuilding Korea Party. The Judicial Standoff: Monitor the National Assembly's legislative calendar regarding judicial reform bills. Whether the party pursues a reckless impeachment push against the Chief Justice or opts for quieter, systematic legal revisions will tell us if cooler heads are prevailing. Economic and Housing Measures: The ultimate test for the Lee Jae-myung administration remains the real economy. Look for tangible policy announcements addressing rental market instability and cost-of-living pressures in the greater Seoul area.

The progressive camp holds the legislative power and the executive office, but power alone does not guarantee survival in South Korea's unforgiving political arena. If the ruling party continues to prioritize internal score-settling over competent governance, it risks turning a temporary mid-term slump into a historic missed opportunity.
