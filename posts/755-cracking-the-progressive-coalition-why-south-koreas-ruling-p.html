Cracking the Progressive Coalition: Why South Korea's Ruling Party is Facing a Crisis of Confidence

September 14, 2026

For many watching South Korean politics unfold, the atmosphere in Seoul has grown distinctly heavy. Just months into the term of President Lee Jae-myung, public opinion polls are flashing warning signs that few expected this early. Instead of enjoying a traditional post-election honeymoon phase, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's primary liberal political force, is watching its approval ratings slide in tandem with the president's. Recent polling places the president's positive marks at 38% against 51% disapproval, driven by growing public frustration over controversial government appointments and policy missteps. Rather than rallying around the flag, the progressive camp is fracturing from within. The Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller progressive ally that helped bring down the previous conservative administration, has launched a blistering public offensive against the ruling party leadership. The friction raises a fundamental question for South Korea’s left-leaning coalition: Have the architects of the current government lost sight of the reformist zeal that brought them to power?

The Progressive Fracture: Inside the Clash Between the Democratic Party and Its Allies

The immediate catalyst for the public feud was a sharp rebuke delivered during a Rebuilding Korea Party leadership meeting, where lawmakers openly targeted Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party. The critique was blunt: while ordinary citizens and a united opposition coalition bled on the streets to unseat the previous conservative government, the current ruling leadership has fallen into the naive trap of believing that appointing conservative figures will somehow broaden their political base. This arithmetic of adding conservative faces to court the political center, critics argue, has come at the direct expense of alienating the progressive grassroots.

Behind this political friction lies a growing sense of disillusionment among voters who genuinely hoped for systemic change. Lawmakers from the Rebuilding Korea Party warned that public sentiment is shifting from active support to cold silence. The fear gripping the progressive base is not merely a drop in approval numbers, but a creeping suspicion that the Democratic Party is beginning to compromise with the very vested interests it vowed to dismantle. When party leadership attempts to brush off plunging polls as mere political noise rather than confronting structural policy failures, it only deepens the frost among core supporters.

Nowhere is this disconnect more visible than in the long-delayed arena of prosecutorial reform. The Rebuilding Korea Party has accused the ruling mainstream of dragging its feet, waiting nearly a year to push forward with legislative overhauls while engaging in internal turf wars. Government proposals aimed at restructuring investigative authorities have faced widespread ridicule from legal experts and activists alike as toothless compromises that merely change the nameplates on bureaucratic office doors while leaving the underlying prosecutorial apparatus largely untouched.

Dismantling Prosecutorial Privilege: The Battle Over Bureaucratic Power

To understand why prosecutorial reform is such an explosive issue in South Korea, foreign observers must look past technical legal jargon and recognize the unique status prosecutors have historically held in the country's political ecosystem. Unlike judges, who belong to an independent judiciary and enjoy constitutional protections, prosecutors in South Korea are technically administrative civil servants working under the executive branch. Yet, for decades, they have operated with a degree of structural privilege, special salary protections, and disciplinary frameworks that set them apart from all other civil service sectors, mimicking the independence of the judiciary without its constitutional checks.

Reformers argue that reining in this immense power requires stripping away the special statutory protections that shield prosecutors from ordinary bureaucratic oversight. Previous legislative attempts to narrow prosecutorial investigative powers from six major crime categories down to two—corruption and economic crimes—were repeatedly blunted by the Ministry of Justice through strategic revisions to enforcement decrees. When the government recently floated plans to establish a new prosecution service while keeping personnel caps intact, critics saw it as a bureaucratic sleight of hand designed to preserve the institution's footprint.

The Rebuilding Korea Party has pushed back with structural proposals, including a strict legal cap limiting the number of prosecutors to 2,000, ensuring the organization cannot quietly expand its reach through executive loopholes. For the progressive base, this is not a policy detail to be negotiated away; it is the ultimate test of whether the government is willing to dismantle the entrenched power cartels that have long influenced South Korean governance.

The Foreign Policy Trap: Foreign Pressure and the Debate Over Overseas Deployments

As domestic political tensions simmer, the administration faces an equally perilous test in foreign policy and national security. Whispers and preliminary reviews regarding potential military deployments linked to escalating Middle Eastern conflicts—specifically concerning the Strait of Hormuz—have alarmed the progressive wing. Critics point out that even in the United States, lawmakers have pushed resolutions questioning the wisdom of prolonged entanglements in volatile overseas conflicts. For Seoul to consider dispatching South Korean troops into potentially unstable theaters simply to appease Washington's strategic demands, critics argue, risks a return to submissive diplomacy.

The debate has laid bare a deep ideological fault line within the ruling camp. While some defense planners urge caution to avoid friction with the traditional security alliance, progressive lawmakers insist that national sovereignty and the lives of young conscripts cannot be treated as diplomatic currency. Pointing to international examples—such as Canada, where leaders standing firm against heavy trade and diplomatic pressures saw their public approval surge—reformers argue that the South Korean public expects a more confident foreign policy that puts national interest first.

Any formal motion concerning overseas deployments would require parliamentary consent under the South Korean constitution, setting up a potential legislative showdown. If the administration attempts to push deployment plans forward under pressure from foreign allies, it risks provoking a fierce backlash from the very progressive coalition that delivered its mandate.

What to Watch Next

The political trajectory of the Lee Jae-myung administration now hinges on how it navigates this multi-front crisis. Observers should monitor three critical developments in the coming weeks:

  • Cabinet and Personnel Reshuffles: Watch for whether the administration responds to sinking approval ratings by overhauling key administrative and legal personnel linked to controversial policy rollouts.
  • Prosecutorial Reform Legislation: Pay close attention to the National Assembly debates on the final structure and personnel limits of the new prosecution service, which will signal whether true institutional reform is taking place.
  • Security and Foreign Policy Signals: Track official statements regarding overseas deployments and alliance management, which will reveal whether the government intends to hold firm to an independent foreign policy or yield to external security pressures.

The current slump in popularity is a clear signal that South Korea's voters expect the ruling coalition to deliver on its foundational promises rather than settling into comfortable compromises with established bureaucracies. How the government responds will determine whether it can recapture its reformist momentum or watch its political capital erode away.
