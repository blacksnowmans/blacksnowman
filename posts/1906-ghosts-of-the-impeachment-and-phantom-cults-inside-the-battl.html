Ghosts of the Impeachment and Phantom Cults: Inside the Battle Fracturing South Korea's Ruling Party

November 03, 2026

Following the tumultuous impeachment and removal of former president Yoon Suk-yeol after his illegal December 3 martial law declaration, South Korea’s political landscape was expected to enter a period of consolidation under President Lee Jae-myung. Instead, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) finds itself entangled in a bizarre, self-inflicted internal crisis. Rather than capitalizing on its governing mandate, party leadership has turned inward, launching witch hunts over unsubstantiated allegations of cult infiltration and sparking fierce factional warfare.

At the center of the recent political storm—highlighted in widespread political commentary such as the popular commentary broadcast Hong Sa-hoon Show—is a narrative that reads more like political fiction than strategic governance: claims that Shincheonji, a controversial fringe religious sect, secretly orchestrated a voting bloc inside the ruling party’s internal primaries. Yet an examination of the numbers and the party’s internal dynamics reveals that this narrative is less about genuine security and far more about internal purges, failed electoral strategies, and a desperate struggle to control the party's future.

The Failed "Big Tent" Strategy and President Lee’s Stagnant Mandate

For years, progressive strategists inside the Democratic Party championed a policy known as the "structural reconstruction theory." The underlying logic was simple: to build an unbeatable majority, the party needed to move to the center, embracing moderate and conservative figures while pushing controversial core promises—such as sweeping prosecutorial and media reforms—to the periphery. Proponents argued that this broad-tent approach was the only way to secure lasting power in a polarized nation.

The empirical results of this strategy, however, tell a very different story. In his first presidential run, Lee Jae-myung secured 47.83% of the vote. In the subsequent election—held under extraordinary circumstances following the crisis and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—President Lee won with 49.42%. For a party expecting a landslide mandate following an unprecedented constitutional emergency, a mere 1.5 percentage point increase was a sobering reality check.

As prominent political commentators like Yu Si-min and analyst Park Si-young have observed, the fixation on courting conservative voters blinded the party leadership to the erosion of its own base. By sidelining structural reforms to appease swing voters, the DPK alienated its most committed supporters without winning over steadfast moderates. Instead of creating a stable coalition, the party left its core voters disillusioned, causing overall approval ratings to stagnate.

Key Takeaway: Strategic expansion fails when it sacrifices party identity. By prioritizing moderate optics over fundamental reform, the Democratic Party weakened its enthusiastic base while failing to build lasting trust among fence-sitters.

A Shadowed Judiciary: Post-Impeachment Rulings and Institutional Distrust

The internal factionalism plaguing the ruling party is further amplified by lingering institutional battles with South Korea’s conservative establishment, particularly within the judiciary. Despite the political shifts in Seoul, deep skepticism remains among progressive voters regarding the court system's impartiality.

Public anger has been repeatedly reignited by controversial judicial decisions. Among them were the total acquittals granted to eight military officers involved in a clandestine gathering at a Lotteria fast-food restaurant during the December 3 martial law episode, as well as favorable rulings toward conservative figures like Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon. These decisions have fueled allegations that entrenched legal cartels continue to protect conservative interests regardless of executive branch changes.

This frustration is not confined to the public; it has breached the walls of the judiciary itself. In a notable internal court board post, an anonymous judge publicly likened Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de to "Benjamin the donkey" from George Orwell’s Animal Farm—a cynical figure who remains silently passive while injustice unfolds around him. The call within judicial ranks to break the silence reflects a growing crisis of legitimacy within the legal system, where judges themselves fear the courts have become political shields rather than impartial arbiters of justice.

The Shincheonji Frame: How an Unsubstantiated Conspiracy Fractured the Base

Amid these external pressures, DPK leadership under Party Leader Kim Min-seok turned its attention to a far more destructive internal narrative: the alleged infiltration of party primaries by Shincheonji, a controversial religious group with an estimated 350,000 members nationwide out of South Korea’s 50 million citizens.

Party leadership set up a dedicated Task Force (TF) to investigate claims of a "three-party secret alliance" operating within party ranks. However, simple mathematics exposes the absurdity of the conspiracy frame:

  • Total DPK Dues-Paying Members: Approximately 1.5 million.
  • Estimated Shincheonji Overlap: Statistically, no more than 3,000 to 4,000 eligible voting members.
  • Electoral Impact: Out of approximately 700,000 active primary voters, this demographic represents under 0.5% of the total vote.

Despite these negligible figures, party leadership amplified the controversy. An internal investigation led by Representative Park Sun-won ended with only vague assertions that "a few things seemed slightly unusual"—a tacit admission that no actionable evidence existed. Veteran party figures, including Representative Park Jie-won and former party leader Jung Chung-rae, openly criticized the leadership, calling on Kim Min-seok to offer a clear apology for manufacturing internal panic.

Rather than dropping the issue, leadership floated plans to submit formal investigation requests to law enforcement. Critics argue this maneuver was merely a stalling tactic designed to keep an umbrella of suspicion over internal rivals. By labeling ordinary party members as subversives or cult sympathizers, the leadership effectively weaponized an unsubstantiated frame to purge non-mainstream factions and protect its own standing.

Stalled Mergers and Factional Self-Preservation

The destructive nature of this internal politicking is most evident in the stalled integration between the ruling Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive partner party led by Cho Kuk and currently headed by Shin Jang-sik.

While President Lee Jae-myung had previously signaled support for merging the two progressive parties to solidify a legislative majority, DPK leadership actively hindered progress. A glaring example of this friction occurred when Representative Kang Deuk-gu publicly claimed on Telegram to be verifying the President’s intent regarding integration, only to abruptly delete the messages when called out for misrepresenting internal dynamics.

The resistance to a merger stems directly from leadership self-preservation:

  • Supreme Council Control: If Cho Kuk’s party merges into the DPK, newly incoming leadership figures would disrupt the current majority held by Kim Min-seok’s faction within the party’s Supreme Council.
  • Candidate Nominations: A unified party structure would force current incumbents to share candidate nomination power for upcoming local and general elections.

Recognizing that they were being used as leverage in DPK internal battles, Rebuilding Korea Party Leader Shin Jang-sik declared that his party had no intention of accepting a merger designed merely as a political distraction. By playing short-sighted tactical games, the DPK leadership damaged trust with a vital legislative ally and alienated party members who favor broad progressive unity.

What to Watch Next

The internal strife within South Korea's ruling party highlights a dangerous drift from policy-driven governance to factional survival tactics. As the political calendar moves forward, several key indicators will reveal whether the Democratic Party can repair its internal rifts or face further destabilization:

  • Leadership Accountability: Watch whether DPK Leader Kim Min-seok delivers a direct, formal apology to party members over the Shincheonji Task Force findings, or if leadership continues to pursue police investigation requests to keep internal rivals on the defensive.
  • Rebuilding Korea Party Dynamics: Monitor whether official merger talks are permanently shelved, or if President Lee Jae-myung directly intervenes to overrule party leadership and revive joint legislative initiatives with Cho Kuk's party.
  • Judicial Reform Battles: Pay attention to mounting internal dissent within the judiciary regarding Chief Justice Cho Hee-de, particularly how the ruling party approaches legislative oversight of pending high-profile trials tied to the martial law crisis.
  • Presidential Approval Trends: Track whether President Lee shifts his strategy back toward core prosecutorial and economic reforms to re-engage disillusioned base voters after months of stagnant polling numbers.
