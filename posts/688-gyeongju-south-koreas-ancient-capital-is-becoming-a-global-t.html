Gyeongju: South Korea’s Ancient Capital is Becoming a Global Tourism Powerhouse

September 11, 2026

Walking through the streets of Seoul or Busan these days, it is impossible to ignore the sheer volume of international travelers. But if you look closely at the data and the changing landscape of the peninsula, you will find that the epicenter of this tourism boom has shifted far beyond the neon-lit skyscrapers of the metropolitan giants. The spotlight is now firmly fixed on Gyeongju, the "thousand-year capital" of the ancient Silla Kingdom.

For many Koreans, Gyeongju was a mandatory school trip destination—a place associated with dusty historical sites and static textbooks. However, a recent visit to the city revealed that this is no longer a sleepy regional town. Gyeongju has undergone a total systemic transformation, evolving from a fleeting stopover into a world-class destination that keeps travelers staying for days.

The APEC Effect and the Shift to "Stay-cation" Tourism

The turning point for Gyeongju arrived when it was selected to host the 2025 Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit. This high-profile event has acted as a catalyst, pulling the city onto the global map. By the second quarter of 2026, foreign visitors to the surrounding Gyeongsangbuk-do province had surged by 26.4% compared to the previous year. Even more impressive, the number of international passengers using the KTX (South Korea’s high-speed rail network) to reach Gyeongju hit 1.69 million in the first quarter of this year—a staggering 46.5% jump from the 1.15 million recorded in the same period last year.

The city's ambitious goal of attracting 1 million foreign tourists in 2025 no longer feels like a government slogan; it is a tangible reality. Gyeongju has positioned itself as the "Kyoto of Korea," utilizing its unmatched cultural heritage and a savvy, rapidly expanding infrastructure to bridge the gap between historical reverence and modern travel convenience.

However, this rapid growth brings risks. As the city maneuvers to accommodate this influx, it must avoid the trap of "overtourism," where the local character is diluted by commercialization and residents are pushed out by rising costs. Sustainable growth will require moving beyond simple headcounts to a model that integrates the local community into the tourism economy.

Must-See Sites: From Historic Temples to "Pyramids"

The transformation is evident at Bulguksa, one of Korea’s most famous Buddhist temples. Despite inclement weather, the parking lot was packed with tour buses, and the air was filled with a chorus of different languages. Global platforms like Klook and GetYourGuide now host thousands of reviews for Gyeongju tour products, signaling that the city has entered the "must-visit" itinerary for international travelers.

Yet, the site also highlights a critical infrastructure gap: the lack of multilingual support. While the architecture is breathtaking, the average visitor struggles to grasp the deep historical context without an expensive private guide. As Gyeongju reaches for the 2-million-tourist mark, upgrading its digital and physical information systems—beyond simple plaques—is essential to ensuring that visitors leave with understanding, not just photos.

Perhaps the most fascinating shift in perception can be found at the Daereungwon Ancient Tombs. To locals, these are sacred Silla-era royal burial mounds. To international tourists, they are "Korean pyramids" or, more playfully, "Teletubby hills." These massive, grass-covered mounds located in the heart of the city are now viral photo spots. The fact that this 1,500-year-old site is open to the public without fences, acting as a functional urban park, provides a level of accessibility that makes it a unique cultural asset on the global stage.

The Rise of "Hwangridan-gil" and Localized Experiences

If you want to understand why travelers are staying longer, look no further than Hwangridan-gil. Data shows this area has the longest average dwell time of any tourist spot in the city (roughly 81 minutes). The secret is the harmony of "K-Street" food and strict urban planning. Local regulations mandate that buildings maintain a traditional Hanok (traditional Korean house) aesthetic, ensuring that the entire district feels like a walk back in time.

Culinary staples like the "Gyeongju 10-won bread"—a cheese-filled pastry shaped like a historical coin—have become global sensations, appearing on social media feeds from Europe to Southeast Asia. There is a sense of "authentic novelty" here: visitors are eager to try local treats like the Gyeongju Bread or sticky barley cakes, realizing that the most "global" experience in Korea is often the most deeply rooted local one.

Nighttime Magic: The New Core of Gyeongju’s Economy

Gyeongju’s most potent weapon in extending visitor stays is its nighttime landscape. The Woljeonggyo Bridge, a stunning reconstruction of a Silla-era structure, and Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond offer an "ancient night" experience that modern metropolitan skyscrapers cannot replicate. When darkness falls, these sites become glowing, reflective stages that pull thousands of tourists out of their hotels.

The city has also mastered the use of media facade technology at the Cheomseongdae Observatory, where light shows project the history of the structure directly onto its stones. This is the definition of "stay-type" tourism: moving the focus from a quick daytime visit to a full evening experience that involves dining, local culture, and nocturnal exploration.

What to Watch Next

  • Sustainable Infrastructure: Keep an eye on how the city balances the surge in visitors with the preservation of residential quality. The success of the APEC summit will be the ultimate test of Gyeongju’s capacity.
  • Multilingual Accessibility: Watch for investments in AI-driven or app-based translation services, which are critical for the city to move from a "viewing" destination to an "understanding" destination.
  • Community Governance: The long-term viability of Gyeongju’s boom depends on whether the profits from this tourism influx are successfully reinvested into the local community to prevent the "overtourism" backlash seen in other global cities.

Gyeongju is no longer just a chapter in a history book. It is a living, breathing, and remarkably resilient city that has found the perfect formula: blending the depth of a thousand years of history with the accessibility and vibrancy that modern international travelers crave. It is a destination that proves, quite effectively, that the deeper you go into the past, the more relevant you become in the future.
