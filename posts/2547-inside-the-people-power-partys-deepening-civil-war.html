Inside the People Power Party’s Deepening Civil War

November 20, 2026

On November 19, a leadership meeting inside South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), came to an abrupt halt. Lawmaker Bae Hyun-jin had just raised an explosive allegation that past disciplinary proceedings against her were deliberately fabricated. Rather than offering an explanation, party leader Jang Dong-hyuk shut the meeting down on the spot, according to broadcaster JTBC. Hours earlier, at a separate gathering of PPP lawmakers, non-mainstream members had already gone on the offensive, accusing party leaders of conducting "insurrection witch-hunts" against their own colleagues.

According to a November 19 political briefing by Korean commentary outlet Black Snowman, as well as reports from major South Korean networks, these clashes are no longer routine policy disputes. They represent a fight over who controls the conservative bloc—and whether the current leadership retains any moral authority. For international observers, the turmoil highlights how fractured South Korea’s political landscape remains after Yoon Suk-yeol’s removal from office through impeachment and subsequent imprisonment. Now relegated to the opposition while President Lee Jae-myung’s Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) governs, the PPP is struggling to act as a coherent counterweight, split by courtroom reversals, allegations of internal manipulation, and sliding public trust.

A Court Acquittal Upends the Party’s Internal Purge

At the center of the November 19 confrontation was a court verdict that overturned the party’s internal balance of power. Lawmaker Choo Kyung-ho, a veteran conservative figure, had faced allegations of involvement in insurrection, alongside disputes over whether changing a voting venue had effectively blocked lawmakers from casting ballots. PPP leaders had used those suspicions to pressure Choo as part of what internal critics described as a personnel purge. When a lower court acquitted Choo in his first trial, the leadership’s rationale collapsed.

At the November 19 assembly of PPP lawmakers, critics seized on the ruling to condemn what they saw as the leadership’s rush to label a colleague a traitor. Yet party leader Jang Dong-hyuk and floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik stayed silent when pressed for an apology. Some conservative media outlets argued that the verdict should be revisited on appeal, contending that the venue change did hinder vote participation. Black Snowman, however, described those arguments as weak defenses for a leadership that had pushed an ally toward political ruin.

The backlash quickly centered on the so-called "pro-Han" faction—lawmakers aligned with rival conservative figure Han Dong-hoon—which Yonhap News TV reported has become the primary target of conservative anger after Choo’s acquittal. Commentators on programs like Park Young-hwan’s Current Affairs No. 1 echoed suspicions that party leaders used "internal insurrection framing" to eliminate rivals, including senior figures who had stood by the party during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office. As regional broadcaster KBC Gwangju noted, critics accuse the leadership of "shaking off the hand of a comrade on the brink." For the party’s non-mainstream wing, Choo’s acquittal has become a powerful weapon against Jang’s authority.

"Fabricated Discipline" and Claims of Silencing Dissent

If Choo’s acquittal undercut the leadership’s political footing, lawmaker Bae Hyun-jin’s accusation struck at its ethical standing. Bae alleged that earlier disciplinary steps taken against her were not the result of a clerical mistake or misapplied rules, but an intentional "fabrication" designed to target her.

Jang’s decision to terminate the meeting rather than promise a review drew swift media scrutiny. Both the daily newspaper Hankyoreh and JTBC highlighted "the Jang Dong-hyuk who cut off the meeting," presenting the episode as proof of a closed decision-making circle where inconvenient voices are shut out. On JTBC’s Behind News, commentators mocked Jang with the satirical nickname "fabrication-nyangi," turning online slang Jang had previously used to needle the government back against him.

Black Snowman argued in its briefing that if a political party alters its disciplinary machinery to remove internal critics, it abandons democratic norms. Despite the seriousness of Bae’s claim—which, if substantiated, could carry criminal liability—the PPP has not formed a fact-finding committee. Whether Jang’s silence reflects caution or political vulnerability, walking out on a colleague’s accusation has deepened concerns that internal checks and balances inside the party have broken down.

Sliding Poll Numbers and the Shadow of Han Dong-hoon

The infighting is already visible in public polling. According to a Realmeter survey conducted in the third week of November, positive approval for Jang Dong-hyuk’s job performance fell 1.6 percentage points from the previous week to 32.9%. That drop occurred even as 63.4% of respondents rated President Lee Jae-myung’s livelihood and economic policies negatively. As Black Snowman noted, an opposition leadership failing to gain ground despite widespread dissatisfaction with the sitting government points to a deeper structural problem. Online outlet BreakNews similarly warned that Jang’s reliance on hardline confrontation to boost party ratings risks backfiring.

Adding to these polling troubles is an unresolved standoff over whether to allow Han Dong-hoon back into the party. In an interview with news agency Newsis, veteran strategist and former interim party chair Kim Jong-in offered a pointed explanation:

"The reason Jang Dong-hyuk opposes Han Dong-hoon’s reinstatement is fear."

Kim argued that Jang is blocking a major rival from re-entering the fold because he doubts he could win a head-to-head race at a future party convention. Other figures across the conservative sphere—including floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik and lawmakers Ahn Cheol-soo and Lee Jun-seok—have adopted varied or cautious stances ahead of potential leadership contests. Critics cited in the briefing argue that barring competitors under the banner of "reform" merely protects entrenched interests while alienating voters who want a broader conservative coalition.

Hardline Attacks Abroad, Fracturing Chapters at Home

Constrained by turmoil inside the party, Jang’s leadership has sharpened its attacks on President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. On November 19, PPP officials slammed the government’s handling of the Suneung—South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam—calling it an impending "Suneung disaster" and raising the prospect of dismissing the education minister. Yet JTBC and political analysts pointed out a contrast: while lawmaker Lee Ki-in shared a small personal story about lending a watch on exam day, party leaders leaned into mockery, using nicknames like "President Suneung-nyangi," without offering concrete policy alternatives for students and parents.

In the legislature, broadcaster YTN captured Jang’s uncompromising rhetoric against the ruling DPK. He denounced the Democratic Patriots Act as "hereditary privileges for former student activists" and rejected what he called a "retaliatory impeachment" of the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. Critics in the briefing noted an irony: demanding judicial independence from the ruling party while resisting the implications of Choo Kyung-ho’s acquittal or refusing to investigate Bae Hyun-jin’s allegations at home.

Meanwhile, central leadership friction is spreading to regional branches. According to local outlet Taehee, the PPP’s Cheongju Sangdang chapter in North Chungcheong Province is locked in severe internal conflict. Observers warn that when central leaders fail to unite members behind a shared vision, local chapters—essential to grassroots election campaigns—splinter into rival factions focused solely on survival.

What to Watch Next

With Jang’s approval rating hovering just above 30 percent and lawmakers openly challenging his legitimacy, the People Power Party faces several immediate tests:

  • The Response to Bae Hyun-jin: Whether Jang Dong-hyuk breaks his silence to launch an independent inquiry into the "fabricated discipline" claims, or continues to shut down debate.
  • Fallout from Choo Kyung-ho’s Verdict: Whether prosecutors appeal Choo’s first-trial acquittal, and how effectively non-mainstream lawmakers use the ruling to demand apologies or leadership changes.
  • The 30% Polling Threshold: Whether Jang’s positive rating slides below 32.9%, which commentators warn could accelerate demands to replace the leadership.
  • The Han Dong-hoon Question: Whether pressure from figures like Kim Jong-in forces the party to reopen its doors to Han Dong-hoon before the next party convention.

Until the PPP demonstrates that it can handle dissent fairly inside its own ranks, its aggressive campaigns against President Lee Jae-myung’s government are unlikely to deliver the credibility a major opposition party needs to recover.
