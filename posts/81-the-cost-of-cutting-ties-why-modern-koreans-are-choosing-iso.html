The Cost of "Cutting Ties": Why Modern Koreans Are Choosing Isolation

September 03, 2026

In South Korea today, it is becoming increasingly common to hear the phrase, "I’ve cleaned up my relationships." It is a casual, almost routine process: silently leaving a group chat, unfollowing someone on social media, or even deleting a phone number entirely. In Korean, this is known as sonjeol (손절)—a term originally borrowed from stock trading, meaning to sell a stock at a loss to prevent further damage. When applied to people, it has become a cultural phenomenon, viewed by many as a "cool" and rational way to protect one’s mental space.

But behind this seemingly pragmatic behavior lies a more fragile reality: a generation of isolated individuals who are bolting their doors to avoid the risk of being hurt. We have begun treating human connections like Lego blocks—detachable and replaceable at will. It raises a fundamental question: In our pursuit of self-protection, have we forgotten how to live in a world where we actually need one another?

The Invisible Lines We Draw to Protect Ourselves

Modern social life has turned into a constant internal calculation. We ask ourselves: "What does this person offer me?" or "Is this relationship worth the emotional energy?" Paradoxically, the obsession with sonjeol stems from a profound exhaustion with the complexity of human ties. Many believe that living in solitary peace is more "economically rational" than enduring the inevitable friction of social interaction. However, this is a dangerous illusion. By walling ourselves off to "protect" our peace, we are effectively imprisoning ourselves behind a wall of our own making.

This behavior is often a defense mechanism against the fear of rejection, which is one of the most primal traumas a human can experience. By cutting ties first, we attempt to seize control and ensure we are never the ones left behind. Yet, this is not true independence; it is avoidant isolation. The moment we start treating people as tools or relationships as contracts, we lose the very thing that makes us human: the joy of genuine, messy, and rewarding connection.

The Lesson: Relationships are not about how someone treats you, but about the lens through which you choose to view them. Constantly cutting ties is an act of avoidance, not a solution. We are meant to share the weight of life, not curate a circle of people who only serve our immediate interests.

The Art of Connection: Why Praise Beats Criticism

A toxic habit in modern communication is the "backhanded compliment" or the impulse to point out a flaw to assert superiority. Whether it’s saying, "You’re great, but that one thing is really a problem," or making a "joke" at someone else’s expense, these small slights erode the foundation of trust. We are often obsessed with critiquing others, when we should be focused on uncovering their "gem-like" strengths.

Praise, when used sincerely, is the most powerful lubricant for a relationship. It is not about hollow flattery; it is about acknowledging the value of the other person’s existence. A simple phrase like, "I appreciate your hard work today; I feel stronger knowing you're on my team," can lighten the heaviest of burdens. Good relationships begin when we help the other person feel more confident in their own skin. When you stop trying to make yourself shine and instead pull someone else to the center of the stage, you often find that you shine brighter yourself.

Managing Boundaries: Family and Money

In Korea, the traditional "mother-in-law and daughter-in-law" conflict (often referred to as gobu-galdeung) is frequently cited as a major social stressor. At its core, the issue is not about the people, but the absence of clear boundaries. As the old, vertical, hierarchical structures of Korean society crumble, we are in a transition phase where we have yet to agree on new rules for engagement. The assumption that one must sacrifice or suffer simply because "they are family" no longer holds up in a modern context. Healthy, long-lasting relationships today require explicit, respectful agreements on personal space and communication frequency.

Similarly, we must change our relationship with money. While financial security is vital, allowing money to become the sole purpose of life turns people into monsters. For those approaching their 60s and beyond, the anxiety over "children risk" or retirement planning often leads to a tragic loss of health and familial bonds. Money is a tool—it should never be the master. A truly mature adult sets a firm financial standard that allows them to enjoy life without being crippled by the pressure of social obligations like weddings or funerals. It is far more profitable to invest in memories with the people you love than to worry about how much you are spending to keep up appearances.

What to Watch Next

The trend of "cutting ties" is a symptom of a society that has lost its tolerance for emotional friction. As we watch this culture evolve, keep an eye on these indicators:

  • The Shift Toward "Flexible Boundaries": Watch for a move away from total isolation (cutting ties) toward "maintaining healthy distance." True maturity isn't about deleting people; it’s about knowing how to engage with them without losing your sense of self.
  • The "Value-Add" Mentality: Pay attention to the shift in how people define success. Are we moving toward a society where we measure "happiness" by the quality of our influence on others, rather than the efficiency of our social networks?
  • The Redefinition of "Adulting": Look for more public discourse—often led by figures like Professor Lee Ho-sun—that challenges the "disposable relationship" mindset, encouraging people to build stronger, more resilient emotional containers instead of running away.

Ultimately, we don’t need to lock our doors to keep our hearts safe. Sometimes, all it takes is placing a single, inviting chair outside that door. By giving others the space to exist alongside us, without the expectation of them being perfect, we might just find that the world—and our place in it—feels a lot less lonely.
