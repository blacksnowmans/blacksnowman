The Art of Aging: What South Korea's Veteran Psychologist Can Teach Us About Growing Older

September 03, 2026

Most of us prefer not to think about the passage of time until we catch a sudden, unflattering reflection in the mirror and wonder if our best years are already behind us. In a rapidly aging society like South Korea—where demographic shifts outpace almost anywhere else in the developed world—aging is rarely discussed as a personal journey of growth. Instead, it is treated as an impending crisis. Yet, after sitting across from tens of thousands of people in distress over a 27-year career, South Korean psychological counselor and professor Lee Ho-sun has arrived at a remarkably grounded conclusion: we do not fear growing old simply because our bodies change, but because we were never actually taught how to do it.

Lee's insights offer a refreshing antidote to superficial self-help. Rather than offering platitudes about keeping a positive mindset, her framework addresses the raw, unvarnished mechanics of aging—covering everything from physical maintenance and intellectual curiosity to navigating long-term marriages and the mental trap of social comparison. For international observers watching East Asia grapple with hyper-aging populations, Lee's advice cuts past cultural specificities to strike at a universal truth: getting older requires strategy, discipline, and a complete overhaul of how we view our remaining years.

Building the Hardware: Why Physical and Mental Maintenance Must Come First

When people begin to worry about aging, they almost always default to emotional self-reflection or spiritual cultivation. They try to find inner peace while ignoring the physical vessel carrying them through the day. Lee argues that this is a fatal mistake. If your physical foundation collapses, even the most profound philosophy begins to feel trivial and hollow. Too many people make the mistake of trying to update their psychological software while completely neglecting their physical hardware.

The first step toward a resilient older age is not grand philosophical meditation; it is basic strength training. This is not about chasing aesthetic perfection or bulky physiques; it is about building core stability. Physical strength directly underpins mental stamina. Pair this physical discipline with what Lee calls a "renaissance of the mind"—a deliberate commitment to curiosity. Learning in this context does not mean chasing academic degrees or career milestones. It means maintaining the intellectual flexibility to understand a changing world and viewing today through a slightly broader lens than yesterday. Without this input, the brain quickly stagnates.

Opening the Door to Intergenerational Friction and Connection

Staying locked inside the home during later life is a form of self-imposed solitary confinement. To stay vibrant, one must step outside and bear witness to how the world is changing—which often means deliberately mixing with younger generations. Too often, older adults view the energy of youth through a lens of resentment or jealousy rather than recognizing it as vital nourishment.

This philosophy is encapsulated in a practical Korean mnemonic known as "Mak-Gong-Na-Man": block disease by protecting your core muscles (Mak), study the world to keep your mind sharp (Gong), step out into society to witness change firsthand (Na), and mix with younger generations to absorb their energy (Man). Waiting for younger people to reach out is a losing strategy. Cultivating the boldness to bridge generational gaps is essential for keeping one's later years vital and engaged.

Reframing Long-Term Relationships and the Weight of Expectations

Relationships, particularly long-term marriages, often bear the heaviest strain as people age. Counseling rooms are perpetually filled with couples contemplating divorce, wondering if they should simply cut ties. However, Lee notes that unless a relationship involves severe pathology, addiction, or abuse, the healthiest solution is often establishing proper boundaries rather than pulling the plug. Living in each other's pockets and constantly inflicting minor wounds leaves relationships exhausted. Just as our bodies need care, our social connections require a form of dietary discipline—respecting individual space and learning to endure with a measure of grace.

Equally critical is mastering the language of consideration. Couples who have spent decades together often stop saying the things that matter most. A simple morning compliment or a quiet acknowledgment of exhaustion at the end of a workday acts as the structural reinforcement holding a relationship together. Lee points out a sobering reality: without deliberate effort, aging does not automatically make people mellow or generous. Unchecked habits of bitterness and deprivation only grow sharper over time. Happiness in a relationship is not won by luck; it is forged by consciously choosing to appreciate what is already in front of you.

Escaping the Trap of Comparison and Embracing Urgency

The root of much unhappiness lies in the eternal habit of comparison. Contented people look at what is currently in their hands, while unhappy people constantly gaze into other people's yards, wondering why they lack what others possess. Maintaining a habit of measuring one's worth against external benchmarks well into later life means wasting the time one has left.

Lee highlights the perspective of South Korean actor Kwon Sang-woo, who remarked that approaching 50 felt like acting with the desperate urgency of someone standing on the edge of a cliff. Getting older should not make a person dull or complacent; rather, it should imbue every remaining moment with a profound sense of purpose. According to Lee's decades of observation, roughly 90 percent of human happiness depends entirely on one's perspective, while external circumstances account for only 10 percent. Adjusting your mindset is vastly more efficient than trying to force the world to change around you.

What to Watch Next

  • Policy and Demographic Shifts: Watch how local governments and community centers in aging societies implement intergenerational programs that move beyond passive senior centers into active, shared spaces.
  • Corporate Longevity Initiatives: Pay attention to how the workforce adapts to extended retirement ages, particularly regarding re-skilling programs designed to keep older minds engaged with modern technology.
  • Personal Health Trends: Look for a growing clinical emphasis on "healthspan" over mere lifespan, focusing specifically on functional core strength and sarcopenia (muscle loss) prevention as core public health pillars.
