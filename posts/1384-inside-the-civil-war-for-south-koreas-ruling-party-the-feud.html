Inside the Civil War for South Korea’s Ruling Party: The Feud Between Lee Jae-myung’s Loyalists and the Old Guard

October 22, 2026

Behind the polished rhetoric of unity echoed in the halls of the National Assembly, South Korea’s ruling party is tearing itself apart from the inside. The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), which swept President Lee Jae-myung into the country's highest office, is now gripped by an intense factional struggle. It is not merely a clash over committee seats or regional influence; it is a calculated bid by the president’s hardline loyalists—often dubbed the "New Pro-Lee" faction—to purge the party of its historical roots, remake its electoral base, and establish unchallenged hegemony over the progressive establishment.

Yet an ambitious political timetable engineered to push out the party’s traditional factions has ground to an abrupt halt. Instead of walking away into political irrelevance, the party's entrenched grassroots members are digging in, backed by some of the country’s most influential progressive commentators. The result is an increasingly volatile standoff that has left the presidential office visibly unsettled and exposed the deep fractures running through South Korea’s governing coalition.

The Battle for the Party's DNA: Pragmatism Versus Historic Identity

To understand why this internal feud is so bitter, one must understand the distinct lineages that make up modern South Korean progressivism. For decades, the Democratic Party drew its moral authority and institutional identity from the legacies of late progressive icons, most notably former presidents Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun, alongside former president Moon Jae-in. The party faithful—frequently referred to as the "pro-Roh" and "pro-Moon" factions—form the bedrock of the party’s dues-paying membership. They view the party not simply as an electoral vehicle, but as the guardian of South Korea’s democratization struggle.

President Lee Jae-myung, by contrast, entered national politics as an outsider. Rising to prominence as the reformist mayor of Seongnam and later governor of Gyeonggi Province, Lee built his brand on unapologetic administrative delivery, populist welfare policies, and sharp-elbowed pragmatism. Lee himself famously admitted during his tenure as party leader that he possessed "no Democratic Party DNA," noting that early in his career he had even considered entering politics through the conservative Grand National Party, the predecessor to today's main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP).

While that brand of flexibility propelled Lee to the presidency, it created a fundamental cultural rift with the party's historic core. For Lee’s closest circle, the entrenched pro-Moon and pro-Roh factions represent an obsolete, moralistic establishment holding back the administration’s legislative agenda. Strategists linked to the New Pro-Lee camp have openly declared that their movement feels "no debt of gratitude" toward figures like Roh, Moon, or former justice minister Cho Kuk. At seminars hosted within parliament by aligned lawmakers like Lee Eon-joo, speakers have gone so far as to openly demean the records of past progressive administrations, signaling a deliberate campaign to dismantle the party’s traditional heritage from within.

The Defection Trap and How a Commentator Foiled It

The strategic blueprint formulated by Lee’s loyalists was ambitious: force an exodus of internal dissidents. By using partisan YouTube channels to demonize traditionalist leaders and creating an inhospitable climate within party committees, the pro-Lee faction intended to provoke the pro-Moon loyalists and non-mainstream lawmakers into staging a mass defection.

The ultimate objective was a sweeping restructuring of the political map. Once the recalcitrant old guard left, party strategists believed they could brand the defectors as fringe extremists, consolidate full control under Lee loyalists like newly minted party leader Kim Min-seok, and expand toward the center by peeling off moderate conservatives from the People Power Party and smaller groups like the Reform Party. The end goal was a permanent, structurally unshakeable legislative majority loyal strictly to Lee Jae-myung.

However, the strategy collapsed at the party's recent national convention because of a single, well-timed intervention. Rhyu Si-min—a widely respected progressive author, former cabinet minister under Roh Moo-hyun, and a towering intellectual figure among the base—publicly dismantled the defection narrative. Reviewing the convention results, Rhyu issued a direct appeal to the rank-and-file: do not abandon the party in anger. Instead, he urged them to launch an aggressive campaign to recruit "three million dues-paying members" and hold the party from within. Senior party heavyweight Jung Cheong-rae quickly reinforced this stance, warning that walking away would only leave loyalists isolated and surrender the party's historic banner to newcomers.

The call resonated. Instead of walking into the political wilderness, the party’s traditional base held their ground. Under the Democratic Party's "one member, one vote" governance framework, an organized and defiant membership bloc represents an immovable obstacle. The plan to push the old guard out had failed, instantly throwing the presidential office's political calendar into disarray.

Recycling Old Controversies: The SillaJen Smear Campaign

The frustration over this stalemate soon manifested in unconventional attacks. In an effort to counter Rhyu Si-min’s influence, online amplifiers aligned with the New Pro-Lee camp began resurrecting the ghost of the SillaJen scandal—a corporate fraud case from years past.

SillaJen, a biotechnology firm, saw its stock price collapse in 2019 following the failure of clinical trials for its experimental cancer therapy, Pexa-Vec, wiping out the savings of thousands of retail investors. At the time, conservative critics seized on the fact that an investment firm named Value Invest Korea (VIK) had been an early shareholder, and that Rhyu had delivered a two-hour lecture organized by VIK in 2015. Opponents attempted to spin this brief, formal appearance into an allegation that high-ranking progressives were complicit in financial manipulation.

The supreme irony is that this attack relies on a narrative thoroughly dismantled years ago. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term in office, prosecutors launched an aggressive investigation into the affair, deploying financial investigation teams to scrutinize bank records associated with Rhyu and his foundation. That investigation ended in complete exoneration, finding no financial wrongdoing or illicit connections whatsoever. Furthermore, public corporate filings confirmed that VIK had completely divested its SillaJen holdings back in late 2015, years before the clinical trial failures.

For long-time party members, watching elements of their own ruling party revive disproven talking points once pushed by conservative opponents felt like an astonishing betrayal. It laid bare the lengths to which the internal struggle had escalated: rather than debating policy or vision, factional surrogates were adopting the tactics of former political adversaries to neutralize inconvenient allies.

The Peril of 'Smart Dictatorship' and What to Watch Next

This internal friction has prompted growing alarm among moderate observers over the erosion of healthy debate within South Korea's governing party. Lawmaker Kim Young-hwan publicly compared the current party climate to a "totalitarian prison," pointing to what critics describe as an emerging "smart dictatorship." Rather than relying on overt coercion, this dynamic operates through coordinated social media pressure, sudden character assassinations—such as rumors linking veteran figures like Jung Cheong-rae to religious cults—and deliberate blackouts against dissenting views.

The administration’s growing anxiety has become difficult to conceal. President Lee himself has engaged in erratic social media activity on X (formerly Twitter), amplifying partisan posts and taking thinly veiled swipes at internal critics as his long-term restructuring plans stalled. Yet the party's traditional base remains unmoved, insisting that while administrations are temporary, the identity of the Democratic Party belongs to the millions of citizens who built it over decades.

As this power struggle unfolds, international observers should monitor three concrete developments:

  • The Membership Drive: Whether the progressive base successfully expands its dues-paying ranks toward the three-million mark. A massive influx of traditionalist members will make it virtually impossible for the pro-Lee faction to purge dissenting district candidates ahead of future elections.
  • Legislative Discipline: How effectively the administration can pass contentious domestic reform bills. If non-mainstream Democratic Party lawmakers feel their political survival is under direct threat from their own leadership, parliamentary discipline will crumble, complicating the government's agenda.
  • Realignment Maneuvers: Any formal attempts by the pro-Lee camp to recruit lawmakers from the conservative People Power Party or centrist splinter groups. If the leadership cannot dislodge its internal critics, it may still attempt an external realignment to bypass the traditional faction entirely.

What is happening within the Democratic Party is a profound contest over the ideological soul of South Korea's center-left. Whether President Lee Jae-myung can consolidate absolute control or finds himself checked by the party's stubborn past will determine how—and how long—his administration effectively governs.
