The Death of the Traditional Gatekeeper: Why YouTube Is Dominating Korean Media

September 24, 2026

For decades, the evening news on South Korea’s major terrestrial networks—MBC, KBS, and SBS—was the undisputed final word on the day's events. To appear on the 9:00 PM news was to be validated; to be ignored by them was to be invisible. But a seismic shift has occurred in the South Korean media landscape, and it is best summarized by the latest “2026 Media Credibility Survey” conducted by the current affairs weekly SisaIN and Gallup Korea. The results are nothing short of a shock: the crown of most trusted news program has been snatched by a YouTube channel.

Kim Ou-joon’s Humility is Hard: News Factory, a digital-only current affairs program, has officially surpassed traditional titans like MBC’s Newsdesk and KBS’s News9. To understand why this matters, one must look past the raw numbers. We are witnessing a fundamental "generational shift in the battlefield of perception." The era where high-power broadcast transmitters dictated public discourse is being replaced by an era of participatory digital journalism.

The Collapse of the Traditional Media Monopoly

The success of News Factory (6.3% trust rating) over traditional powerhouses (MBC at 5.9% and KBS at 4.9%) signals that the barrier between "official" journalism and "alternative" digital content has effectively vanished. Since its launch in early 2023, the channel has realized its host’s stated goal: becoming a "terrestrial broadcaster without a transmitter."

This shift isn't just about viewership; it’s about the erosion of institutional authority. While traditional broadcasters struggle under strict regulatory frameworks and perceived biases, News Factory has thrived by acting as a hub for "participatory journalism." When viewers feel that public broadcasters have lost their edge—or their neutrality—they are no longer waiting for a course correction. They are simply migrating to platforms that offer a more direct, albeit partisan, connection to the news. The survey also reveals a sobering reality: a massive 64.2% of the public either has no trusted journalist or refuses to answer, indicating a profound cynicism toward the media industry as a whole. In this void, personal branding and platform-based "brand value" have eclipsed the traditional prestige of a reporter’s credentials.

Accountability and the Shadow of Insurrection

While the media landscape fractures, the South Korean judiciary is facing its own crisis of legitimacy. The ongoing trials of military commanders involved in the recent insurrection (a period of severe political upheaval) serve as a painful reminder that the scars of the past remain unhealed. The prosecution’s demand for 30-year sentences for figures like former Capital Defense Commander Lee Jin-woo and former Defense Counterintelligence Commander Yeo In-hyung underscores the gravity of their assault on the democratic order.

However, the courtroom has provided a stark study in human accountability. Former Special Warfare Commander Kwak Jong-keun stands apart as the only defendant to admit his crimes and offer a sincere apology. His case has become a focal point of public discourse, with victim advocacy groups even filing petitions for leniency on his behalf—a rare gesture in such a polarizing case. It highlights a recurring theme in Korean social discourse: the desire for acknowledgment of wrongdoing over mere punishment.

This sense of justice is further complicated by administrative scandal. Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is currently embroiled in an "expense padding" controversy, with allegations that he misappropriated approximately $185,000 (260 million won) in government funds through fraudulent expense reports. For many, the sight of a judicial branch failing to manage its own modest budgets while presiding over historic national crimes is a source of intense indignation. The public is asking: if the judiciary cannot hold itself to a standard of basic integrity, how can it be trusted to deliver the final verdict on the nation’s survival?

The Political Tightrope: President Lee’s Struggles

Amidst these tensions, President Lee Jae-myung (the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea, the main liberal opposition) has been attempting to recalibrate his administration. His recent press conference, themed around the mantra that "the people are always right," marked an effort to pivot away from a confrontational governance style. Following the conference, his approval ratings saw a modest rebound to 34.8%.

However, the administration remains plagued by structural failures. The abrupt resignation of Kim Seung-won, a nominee for Minister of Justice, following the surfacing of sexual harassment allegations, was a significant blow to the administration’s vetting system. Furthermore, while the President aims to reorient the country's foreign policy during this "Diplomatic Super Week"—marked by major UN summits—critics argue that the government remains too reactive, acting as if they are defending on a soccer pitch rather than setting an agenda on the international stage.

The President’s recent social media gaffes—specifically the retweeting of disparaging comments—have only highlighted the fragility of his position. In a nation where political tribalism is deeply entrenched, the public is looking for a mediator, not a partisan warrior.

What to Watch Next

As we head into the final quarter of the year, three things are critical to watch:

  • The Media War: Will traditional networks attempt to replicate the "personality-driven" model of YouTube, or will they continue to lose influence to digital creators who prioritize interpretive clarity over institutional neutrality?
  • Judicial Integrity: How the Supreme Court handles the internal fallout from the expense scandal will determine whether the public views the upcoming insurrection verdicts as legitimate justice or systemic cover-up.
  • Governance Effectiveness: President Lee’s administration is at a crossroads. The rebound in his approval rating is a fragile opening. Whether he can turn "reflection" into tangible policy successes—particularly in judicial reform and diplomatic agency—will determine if he can retain the support of the moderate swing voters who are currently disappointed by the speed and nature of his reforms.

South Korea is in a state of high-stakes transition. The old guard of broadcasting and traditional governance is failing, and the new digital-first, high-intensity political culture is still searching for stability. For the citizens watching from the sidelines, the hope is that these changes lead to something more than just a different brand of political theater.
