Small Hands, Big Impact: How South Korea’s Daycare Bazaars Are Rebuilding Local Solidarity

November 12, 2026

Imagine a small gymnasium or a converted apartment playground filled with the high-pitched chatter of four-year-olds. They are clutching "vouchers" or small change, eyeing a slightly used Pororo toy or a pre-loved denim jacket. Beside them, parents and teachers navigate rows of tables laden with household goods, books, and handmade crafts. This is an "Anabada" bazaar, a quintessentially Korean event that has evolved from a national survival strategy into a cornerstone of early childhood education. While the individual transactions involve mere pocket change, the collective result is a massive infusion of grassroots charity into South Korea's local welfare systems.

On November 11, a day usually dominated by the commercial frenzy of "Pepero Day"—a Valentine’s-like holiday where billions of chocolate-covered cookie sticks are sold—a different kind of transaction took place across the country. Dozens of eorinijip (South Korea’s ubiquitous daycare centers and preschools) reported donating the proceeds of their autumn bazaars to help the vulnerable. From the industrial hubs of Chungcheong Province to the aging rural villages of Jeolla, these events have become a vital "circular economy" of kindness, teaching children the value of money, the necessity of environmental stewardship, and the weight of social responsibility before they even enter primary school.

For an outside observer, these donations of $700 or $1,500 might seem like small gestures. However, in the context of South Korea’s rapidly aging society and the increasing isolation of local communities, these daycare centers are functioning as the last remaining "hubs" of social cohesion. They are where young families meet, where local government services intersect with the private lives of citizens, and where the next generation of Koreans first learns what it means to be part of a "we" rather than just an "I."

The 'Anabada' Legacy: From Economic Crisis to Classroom

To understand why these bazaars matter, one must understand the term Anabada. It is an acronym for a slogan that emerged during the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis: A-kkyeo sseugo (Save), Na-nuo sseugo (Share), Ba-kkyeo sseugo (Exchange), and Da-si sseugo (Reuse). During the "IMF Era," as Koreans still call it, the movement was a desperate measure to reduce imports and conserve resources. Today, it has been rebranded as a pedagogical tool.

In a modern South Korean daycare center, an Anabada event is a multi-week curriculum. Children are asked to identify a toy or a piece of clothing they no longer use but that is still in good condition. They learn the "value" of that item, participate in setting a price, and finally, engage in the act of selling it to a peer or a neighbor. This process serves three distinct purposes in the Korean educational context:

  • Economic Literacy: In a society that is becoming increasingly cashless, children get to see the physical exchange of currency and the concept of "proceeds."
  • Environmental Stewardship: With South Korea’s strict recycling laws and growing concern over plastic waste, the "reuse" aspect of Anabada aligns with the national goal of carbon neutrality.
  • Social Solidarity: By designating the proceeds for the "vulnerable"—the elderly living alone, children with cancer, or low-income neighbors—the daycare centers instill a sense of civic duty.

The recent wave of donations shows how this curriculum translates into real-world support. In Asan, a city known for its manufacturing plants, the Arumdeuri Daycare Center donated 2.2 million KRW (about $1,580) to the city government. Meanwhile, the Daol Daycare Center in the same city contributed 1 million KRW (about $720). These are not corporate grants; they are the accumulation of hundreds of transactions involving items sold for $1 or $2 each. The sheer volume of participation required to reach these totals is a testament to the community engagement these centers command.

National, Public, and Private: The Infrastructure of Giving

The landscape of South Korean childcare is divided into several categories, and the recent charity drive highlights how different types of institutions are stepping up. To a foreign reader, these distinctions are crucial for understanding who is driving social change in Korea. The primary players in this movement are National and Public Daycare Centers (Guk-gong-rip), which are government-funded and highly sought after for their transparency and standardized programs.

For instance, the Grand Hills Daycare Center in Incheon’s Bupyeong District, a National/Public facility, recently made headlines for its donation to the local district office. Because these centers are overseen by the state, their charity work often serves as a "best practice" model that private centers then emulate. There are also Public-type Daycare Centers (Gong-gong-hyeong)—privately owned facilities that receive government subsidies in exchange for meeting strict quality and transparency benchmarks. In Suwon, an association of these Public-type centers pooled their resources to donate 1 million KRW ($720) collectively.

This organized, institutional approach to charity is significant. In many Western countries, daycare is seen as a private service for working parents. In South Korea, the eorinijip is increasingly viewed as a public asset. When a daycare center in Gimpo, such as the municipal Gamjeong Hessen center, raises 1.51 million KRW (about $1,080), it is not just the staff and children involved; it is the entire neighborhood. These events often involve local welfare agencies, such as the Happy Kium Support Group (a community-based social safety net committee), ensuring that the money doesn't just go into a general fund but reaches specific, high-need individuals in the immediate vicinity.

Bridging the Gap in Korea’s 'Shrinking' Regions

Perhaps the most poignant aspect of this charity movement is its impact on South Korea’s rural and provincial areas. While Seoul and the surrounding Gyeonggi Province struggle with overcrowding, many provincial towns are facing "regional extinction" due to the ultra-low birth rate and the exodus of young people to the capital. In these areas, a daycare center is more than a school; it is often the only place where you can still hear the sound of children playing.

In Hwasun and Jinan, two smaller regions in the southwest, local daycare centers like Jaendung-ee and Japo-Wonkwang recently donated their bazaar proceeds (amounting to roughly $800 and undisclosed smaller amounts, respectively) to support their elderly neighbors. This creates a powerful symbolic bridge. South Korea is currently grappling with a "generational divide," where the youth and the elderly often live in different worlds with different political and social outlooks. When a toddler’s donated toy helps buy a heater or a meal for an 80-year-old neighbor, it reinforces a "commonwealth" mentality that is increasingly rare in the modern, hyper-competitive Korean society.

"The scale of the donation is less important than the educational journey. The children learn that their favorite doll can become a bowl of rice for a neighbor. That realization is the foundation of a healthy citizen."

Furthermore, some centers are choosing to target specific medical needs. The Busan City Hall Daycare Center, for example, directed its bazaar earnings toward pediatric cancer treatment. By choosing a cause that directly affects other children, teachers can facilitate a deeper level of empathy. Children are taught that some of their peers are fighting illnesses and that their "hard work" at the bazaar has a direct, life-saving application. This elevates the event from a simple "market day" to a lesson in human rights and the value of life.

Challenges to Sustainability and the Road Ahead

Despite the heartening news, the sustainability of these community bazaars faces significant headwinds. The most obvious is the demographic crisis. As the number of children in South Korea continues to plummet, daycare centers are closing at an alarming rate. When a center closes, the community loses its primary venue for these grassroots solidarity events. There is a risk that the "circulatory system" of local charity will dry up as the "small hands" that power it disappear.

There is also the "fatigue factor" for educators. Preparing for an Anabada bazaar is labor-intensive. Teachers must sort through hundreds of donated items, clean them, price them, and manage the logistics of the event—all while maintaining their primary role as caregivers. For these events to remain meaningful rather than becoming a "photo-op" for local politicians or an annual burden for staff, there needs to be greater institutional support. This could include:

  • Digital Integration: Moving some aspects of the "exchange" to local neighborhood apps (like the popular Danggeun Market) while keeping the physical bazaar as a community festival.
  • Standardized Welfare Matching: Creating clearer pipelines between daycare centers and local NGOs to ensure transparency and show children the "results" of their giving through age-appropriate reports.
  • Parental Involvement: Shifting the burden of organization from teachers to parent-teacher associations to foster a true "village" atmosphere.

What should observers watch for next? The key indicator will be how these local charity efforts are integrated into the government's broader "childcare integration" (yu-bo-tong-hap) plan. As South Korea moves toward merging its daycare and kindergarten systems under the Ministry of Education, there is a debate over whether the "social welfare" and "community hub" functions of the eorinijip will be preserved or if they will be replaced by a more rigid, academic focus. If the latter happens, Korea may lose one of its most effective tools for building a compassionate society from the ground up. For now, however, the "small hands" of Korea’s toddlers continue to do the heavy lifting of keeping local solidarity alive.
