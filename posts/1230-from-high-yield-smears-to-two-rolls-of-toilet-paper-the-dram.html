From High-Yield Smears to Two Rolls of Toilet Paper: The Dramatic Legal Fall of Kim Se-ui

October 16, 2026

Inside a solitary cell at the Seoul Detention Center, one of South Korea's most influential and aggressive internet figures is counting his supplies. In letters sent to supporters outside, Kim Se-ui describes rationing four cartons of milk and two remaining rolls of toilet paper, lamenting that a bout of body aches and an upset stomach has left him pleading for relief without access to store-bought remedies. For a commentator who once spent years broadcasting scathing, deeply personal exposés from wealthy Gangnam studios to hundreds of thousands of live viewers, the letters read like an astonishing reversal of fortune.

Kim, the head of the hardline conservative YouTube outlet Garo Sero Institute, is currently behind bars awaiting trial on multiple criminal charges. Yet his greatest crisis at this moment is neither ideological nor purely penal: it is an aggressive, mathematically calculated campaign of civil asset seizures that has reduced his daily prison spending allowance to just 6,600 won—roughly $5 a day. As the courts shut down his attempts to turn his prosecution into a partisan culture war, Kim’s predicament offers an unprecedented case study in the rise, monetization, and vulnerabilities of South Korea’s notorious "cyber wrecker" industry.

From Political Hitman to Detained Defendant

For several years, Garo Sero Institute operated as the vanguard of a lucrative digital genre in South Korea known as the "cyber wrecker" (cyber rekka)—channels that quickly tow online rumors and scandalous claims into viral, revenue-generating live streams. Kim Se-ui, a former television broadcast reporter, built the outlet into a formidable tabloid force that targeted both liberal political figures and top-tier entertainment celebrities.

That momentum finally came to an abrupt halt following a relentless string of broadcasts aimed at popular actor Kim Soo-hyun. Kim Se-ui alleged that the actor had engaged in an inappropriate relationship with the late actress Kim Sae-ron dating back to when she was a minor, further claiming that pressure over debt repayment had driven her to suicide. The claims provoked widespread outrage, prompting criminal complaints that triggered a sweeping investigation by prosecutors.

When the Supreme Prosecutors’ Office forensic science team examined the digital files, the findings dismantled Kim Se-ui's defense. Investigators uncovered clear evidence that audio recordings had been arbitrarily spliced, edited, and distorted to manufacture allegations without even basic fact-checking. Kim was indicted while in detention on charges that include defamation under the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection, as well as violations of both the Stalking Punishment Act and the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Punishment of Sexual Crimes.

The pattern was familiar. Kim had previously faced indictment for claiming the son of Cho Kuk—head of the Innovation Policy Research Institute for the minor liberal Rebuilding Korea Party—was a school bully, and faced additional stalking and intimidation charges relating to the popular eating-show (mukbang) YouTuber Tzuyang. Confronted with the evidence at a pre-trial preparatory hearing before the Seoul Central District Court, Kim’s defense team insisted on total innocence, claiming he had no knowledge that the materials were false and demanding immediate release on bail to prepare his defense.

In a bold procedural gambit, Kim applied for a citizen participation trial—South Korea's limited version of a jury trial. The strategy was unmistakable: reframe an invasive celebrity smear campaign into a grand ideological struggle against an alleged "monolithic leftist establishment," hoping to sway a sympathetic jury. Both the prosecution and Kim Soo-hyun's legal counsel pushed back forcefully, arguing that public disclosure of graphic private material and sexual offenses in a jury setting would inflict irreversible secondary trauma on the victim. Presiding Judge Lee Hyun-kyung agreed, dismissing the jury request and refusing to allow the courtroom to be converted into a political theater.

The $5-a-Day Commissary Trap: How a Target Fought Back

While the criminal trial moves forward, Kim's most immediate distress stems from the civil courts. In South Korea, inmates rely on an internal commissary account, known as yeongchigeum, to purchase basic personal necessities beyond standard prison rations—such items as bottled water, extra hygiene supplies, over-the-counter medication, and snacks.

Under ordinary circumstances, a wealthy inmate's supporters or family can deposit funds into this account to make life behind bars tolerable. However, Kim found his commissary funds subjected to provisional attachment by an adversary he had crossed during his broadcasting career: entrepreneur Eun Hyun-jang, widely known in South Korea through his YouTube persona "God of Business."

Eun initiated an unyielding, costly legal campaign to pursue Kim’s assets. When Kim filed an objection requesting an exemption of up to 1 million won (about $750) per month to cover living expenses in detention, Eun fought the petition across three separate hearings, spending roughly 19.6 million won (about $15,000) in legal fees simply to block Kim's commissary cash. The court ultimately sided with the creditor, ruling that Kim could only access the statutory minimum cost of living: 200,000 won (about $150) per month. That translates to roughly 6,600 won—barely $5—each day.

"I cannot buy bottled water, toilet paper, toothpaste, or toothbrushes. With aches, chills, and stomach trouble morning and night, I cannot even buy cold or stomach medicine. If this continues, I will die," Kim wrote in his published prison letter, complaining bitterly about an uncooperative cellmate and the evaporation of his comforts.

The irony has not been lost on the South Korean public. For years, Garo Sero Institute routinely mocked public figures who wept or appealed for sympathy, branding any display of vulnerability as manipulative. Now, the spectacle of a previously well-funded provocateur complaining about having only two rolls of toilet paper left has gone viral, with mainstream network YouTube clips covering his commissary crisis racking up over 4.6 million views.

Eun’s civil offensive extends far beyond prison rations. The courts have issued attachment orders on 20,000 shares of Garo Sero Institute owned by Kim, threatening his control over the corporate entity itself. Meanwhile, Kim's high-end residential apartment in Seoul’s affluent Apgujeong neighborhood faces auction proceedings initiated by another creditor, lawyer Kim So-yeon, over an unpaid debt of 120 million won (about $90,000). Preparing for the long haul, Eun revealed that he has earmarked a litigation budget of 5 billion won (roughly $3.7 million)—having already expended about 2 billion won on advertising refunds and legal counsel—specifically to launch an avalanche of civil damages lawsuits the moment Kim completes his detention or secures release.

The Structural Flaw in Korean Defamation Law

While online audiences have reacted with open fascination to Kim’s financial asphyxiation, the saga highlights an uncomfortable reality about South Korea's judicial framework: holding internet agitators accountable currently requires immense personal wealth.

Under South Korea's current tort system, civil defamation compensation remains notoriously meager. Even when prominent victims prevail after years of litigation, courts rarely award more than 10 million to 20 million won ($7,500 to $15,000)—as seen in recent successful suits brought by Kim Hee-young, chair of the T&C Foundation, against malicious creators. By contrast, a single sensationalist livestream targeting a celebrity or politician can generate tens of thousands of dollars in live donations ("superchats") and ad revenue overnight.

Without Eun Hyun-jang's unusual willingness to burn tens of millions of won in pursuit of uncollectable legal costs, a standard victim would never have had the financial endurance to execute these commissary freezes and share attachments. Ordinary citizens targeted by cyber wreckers possess virtually no realistic means of making their abusers pay in real time. This glaring asymmetry has intensified calls across the legal community for structural reform, specifically:

  • The introduction of full punitive damages for intentional, profit-driven digital defamation.
  • Mandatory civil and criminal asset-forfeiture mechanisms that strip online agitators of all broadcasting revenue generated through unlawful material.
  • Streamlined enforcement procedures so that victims of modest means can execute damage awards without exhausting their life savings on specialized civil litigation.

The Parallel Playbook of Digital Deflection

Kim Se-ui's attempt to wrap personal torts in partisan armor mirrors a broader trend across South Korea's online ecosystem, where individuals facing legal censure attempt to buy social cover by appealing to the far-right.

A notable parallel occurred recently when American singer Steve Yoo (known in Korea as Yoo Seung-jun)—who has been barred from the country for over two decades after renouncing his Korean citizenship to evade mandatory military conscription—won a third administrative lawsuit challenging the South Korean consulate's refusal to issue him an entry visa. Despite the court's narrow ruling addressing administrative procedural discretion rather than vindicating his past draft evasion, Yoo immediately sought to mobilize online sympathy, weeping about his age and children while loudly inserting himself into Korean domestic politics.

Yoo lashed out at prominent figures like former Justice Ministers Choo Mi-ae and Cho Kuk, demanded the deportation of former President Moon Jae-in's son, and aggressively promoted right-wing political documentaries such as The Birth of Korea 2. In both Yoo's rhetoric and Kim Se-ui’s demand for a citizen jury, the playbook is identical: when facing societal and legal consequences for specific personal actions, pivot toward polarized culture-war grievances to recruit a passionate base of online defenders.

What to Watch Next

The proceedings around Kim Se-ui and Garo Sero Institute now move into a decisive phase:

  • The Bail Decision: The Seoul Central District Court’s Criminal Agreement Division 26 is expected to rule on Kim's bail petition shortly. Given the formal rejection of the jury trial and prosecutors' warnings regarding the risk of witness intimidation and privacy destruction, the legal threshold for release remains exceptionally high.
  • Forensic Scrutiny in the Criminal Trial: As trial hearings commence, the prosecution will introduce the detailed scientific evidence showing how recorded materials were altered to invent claims about actor Kim Soo-hyun. The verdict will set a critical precedent for how stringently Korean courts punish deliberate audio manipulation by online content creators.
  • Corporate Control of Garo Sero: With 20,000 of Kim's shares frozen and former associates maneuvering for ownership, the corporate shell of Garo Sero Institute faces potential restructuring or liquidation, testing whether the brand can survive its founder's insolvency.
  • The 5-Billion-Won Legal Campaign: Should Kim be released upon the expiration of his detention period in December, Eun Hyun-jang’s multi-billion-won civil litigation machine will begin testing the legal limits of post-release financial pursuit.

For South Korea's digital media sphere, the spectacle of a once-untouchable media personality subsisting on $5 a day marks a watershed moment. It serves notice that while the statutory legal framework may still lag behind digital harassment, weaponized civil execution can dismantle an internet empire from the inside out.
