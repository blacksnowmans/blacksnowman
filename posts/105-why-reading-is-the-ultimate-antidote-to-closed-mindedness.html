Why Reading is the Ultimate Antidote to Closed-Mindedness

September 03, 2026

Scroll through any South Korean online community or sit down at a family dinner, and you will likely hear the same complaint: "People just don't listen to each other anymore." It is easy to write this off as a simple clash of personalities, a byproduct of modern stress. But scratch beneath the surface, and a more unsettling pattern emerges. Why are we so fiercely protective of our own echo chambers, yet utterly incapable of stepping into someone else's world?

Drawing on insights from South Korean reading education expert Choi Seung-pil, a stark reality comes into focus. The further we distance ourselves from books, the harder and more barbed-wire-like the boundaries of our thinking become. True reading is not merely a mechanism for accumulating trivia or boosting productivity; it is an act of ego-renewal designed to tear down the mental walls we construct around ourselves. In a society grappling with deepening polarization, examining why reading—or the lack of it—shapes how we treat others offers a vital perspective on modern civic life.

Dismantling the Barbed Wire of Rigid Thinking

When you find yourself in a conversation thinking, "Why on earth don't they understand this?", it is a sign that your cognitive framework has calcified. A society devoid of reading is much like stagnant water. When the boundaries of your worldview are drawn too rigidly, every opposing opinion that crosses that line is perceived as a direct attack or a threat.

Left unchecked, this rigidity breeds hostility rather than dialogue. To those who believe their perspective is the center of the universe, an alternative narrative is nothing more than annoying background noise. Books introduce vital fractures into these closed systems. By immersing ourselves in the lives of people we will never meet, text gives us a healthy dose of doubt. It whispers a radical idea: "My thinking might actually be wrong." That humility is the first gift of deep reading.

Moreover, the modern obsession with efficiency often sabotages this process. We look for quick summaries and bullet points, starving our brains of deep engagement. Reading literature reshapes our emotional textures, while the humanities help us comprehend the broader structures of society. Skipping this difficult intellectual labor while expecting personal growth is an empty promise.

The Mechanics of Mental Weight Training

Speed-reading and volume are poor metrics for intellectual growth. In fact, a truly productive reading session is often peppered with pauses. When you encounter an unfamiliar term, look it up. When a sentence baffles you, stop and ponder why the author constructed it that way. This practice of "slow reading" may feel frustrating at first, but those moments of friction are precisely where cognitive muscle is built.

Repetitive reading serves a similar purpose. Reading a book just once often leaves it prone to mental evaporation. Revisiting a text on the same theme helps layer your perspectives, while rereading classic literature allows you to unearth historical contexts hidden beneath the surface. Through this kind of layered reading, text stops being external data and becomes a part of your intellectual framework.

Active engagement techniques, such as handwriting excerpts or structuring core arguments into your own words, act as profound cognitive training. Wrestling with an author’s choice of words and tracing the logic between paragraphs forces you to build an internal architecture of thought, keeping you from becoming a passive consumer of algorithmic feeds.

True Critical Thinking Requires Empathy First

In contemporary public discourse, "critical thinking" is frequently misinterpreted as an excuse to tear down opposing views indiscriminately. Genuine critical reading, however, begins with total immersion in the author’s universe. You must fully understand the fortress of logic an author has built before you can spot its structural weaknesses or propose an alternative view.

Without adopting the mindset of "What would I do in their shoes?", we risk becoming passive remote-controlled units swayed by whichever loud opinion crosses our screens. Cultivating the flexibility to step away from our own preconceived notions and walk directly into an author’s arguments is the hallmark of a mature reader.

Navigating a Complex Information Flood

The modern world presents a ceaseless deluge of complex information. In this environment, maintaining a sense of personal direction requires a robust internal philosophy—something that cannot be cultivated through brief social media snippets.

Books do not hand out neat, pre-packaged answers to life's dilemmas. Instead, they grant us the capacity to ask better questions. For anyone trying to navigate the turbulent waters of contemporary society, a disciplined reading habit serves as both a compass and a sturdy vessel. The goal is not to memorize every plot point, but to observe how your own mind shifts and expands in the process.

If we want to move past knee-jerk hostility and rebuild our capacity for mutual understanding, we have to start by opening the right doors. The next time you pick up a book, let it be one that challenges your certainties—and watch how quickly the walls around you begin to fall.
