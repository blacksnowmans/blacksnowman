The Lens of Fear: What a Solo Filmmaker’s Journey Teaches Us About Anxiety, Control, and Connection

September 11, 2026

It starts with a simple click on a video, but it often ends in a tangle of uneasy comments. When a Korean audio channel recently featured an interview with Park Jung-mi, director of the indie documentary film The Person in a Blanket, the comment section lit up with familiar anxieties. Viewers warned that a woman traveling alone was playing with fire, calling it a reckless stunt and expressing relief that she made it back in one piece. They added stern warnings: if anyone else tried to copy this, they would surely meet a grim fate.

For anyone who has ever watched a solo female travel or hiking vlogger, this chorus of worry is entirely predictable. In a world saturated with rolling news about crime and local dangers, the protective panic is understandable. But it completely misses the point of the film. The Person in a Blanket is not an instruction manual for high-risk adventuring, nor is it a call to court danger. Instead, it is an unflinching look into the mechanics of everyday dread—an exploration of how fear colors our perception of the world long after the actual threat has passed.

The Distorting Lens of Fear and Our Mind's Melancholy Inertia

Why do bad experiences leave such a disproportionate mark on the human psyche? A single failure convinces us that the next attempt is doomed; a single terrifying moment casts a long shadow over every similar situation we encounter. It is a strange quirk of human psychology that ten wonderful days can be entirely erased from memory by a single hostile encounter. This emotional math is precisely what hits home when watching Park’s documentary.

Park recounts how her travels were filled with unexpected kindness. Penniless and carrying little more than a backpack, she was repeatedly taken in by strangers who offered shelter, shared meals, and gave her rides. She felt a profound sense of connection to a world that seemed eager to protect her. But that worldview shattered when she faced a genuinely threatening confrontation in Iran. A single moment of real danger completely eclipsed weeks of warmth and hospitality.

Human beings are not wired to remember the thousand angels who helped us; our brains are evolutionarily tuned to obsess over the one demon who threatened us. This is a survival mechanism, a hardwired bias toward detecting threats. Yet, when unchecked, it distorts reality entirely. At the same time, our attempts to cope with this vulnerability often morph into an obsession with control. The more we plan, hoard information, and build safeguards to achieve absolute safety, the more terrifying the uncontrolled unknown becomes. Absolute certainty does not exist, and chasing it only amplifies our anxiety.

The Endless Screening Room Inside Our Heads

For Park, the most exhausting part of her ordeal in Iran wasn't the event itself, but what happened afterward. Long after she had returned to safety, a perpetual screening room opened inside her mind. The actual danger had passed, but the tragedy played on an endless loop: *What if I had died? What if it happens again tonight?*

Ghosts of the past and phantoms of the future constantly hijack the present moment. When we sit alone in a quiet room and let our imaginations run wild, we suffer a hundred imagined deaths before we ever face a real threat. As spiritual teachers often note, our actual physical reality is almost always safe in the immediate present; it is only our minds that sprint ahead into catastrophe. A painful event in reality might last all of five seconds, but we torture ourselves by hitting the replay button thousands of times.

This does not mean we should retreat into naive optimism or pretend the world is free of danger. Park’s journey teaches a more nuanced middle path: people are capable of both immense cruelty and profound kindness. The world holds both peril and warmth. The wisdom lies in refusing to let either extreme blind us to the other.

Stepping Through the Shadow: Facing the Phantoms

When Park first attempted "skip-diving"—rescuing discarded food from grocery store bins as part of her journey—she was paralyzed by what strangers might think. Sitting alone in her room, her mind conjured every possible judgment. Yet the moment she actually stepped onto the street with a friend, those imagined judgments evaporated. She realized a liberating truth: people are generally too wrapped up in their own lives to care very much about what you are doing.

Psychologists call this exposure therapy, but it is also a fundamental lesson in living. Fear grows in the dark room of avoidance and shrinks the moment you walk straight through it. The goal of personal growth is not to eradicate fear entirely—fear is a biological given. The goal is to learn how to carry fear alongside you while you live your life anyway.

More importantly, stepping out of our defensive shell allows us to see the quiet support systems that are already in place. After her traumatic experience in Iran, Park temporarily forgot the friends who cooked for her and the family that sheltered her until a warm embrace in a quiet courtyard brought her back to reality. We have to intentionally train our focus to notice the small mercies that kept us safe throughout the day, rather than letting our defense mechanisms block out the very people trying to help us.

What to watch next as you navigate your own relationship with anxiety and control:

  • Notice the mental replay: The next time an old embarrassment or worry loops in your mind, ask yourself if the danger is actually happening in the room with you right now.
  • Test your assumptions: Pick one small, safe thing you have been avoiding out of awkwardness or fear, and take a tiny step toward it to see what actually happens.
  • Balance your gaze: Actively look for one unexpected act of kindness or comfort you received today, consciously balancing out the brain's natural bias toward bad news.

We do not need to pretend the world is a utopia to find peace within it. By lowering our defensive walls just enough to let reality back in, we trade the exhausting illusion of total control for the quiet, breathing room of the present moment.
