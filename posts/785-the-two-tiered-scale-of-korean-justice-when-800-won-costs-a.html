The Two-Tiered Scale of Korean Justice: When 800 Won Costs a Career While the Judiciary Perks It Up

October 07, 2026

Imagine standing before a court of law, your livelihood stripped away, your reputation in ruins, all because of an alleged miscalculation totaling less than the price of a cheap cup of coffee. Now imagine walking down the halls of that same judicial system as a high-ranking official, collecting unvouched cash allowances every month while deliberately leaving constitutional benches vacant for months on end. For ordinary people outside of South Korea, the country's legal system is often viewed through the polished lens of high-tech infrastructure and rapid economic development. But inside the legislative auditing rooms, a much darker, deeply unequal reality is coming to light—one where the scales of justice tilt mercilessly depending on who is standing before them.

The recent parliamentary inspection targeting South Korea's Supreme Court and Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae laid bare the glaring double standards plaguing the nation's judiciary. While lower-court judges throw the book at working-class citizens for petty, survival-driven mistakes, the country's legal elite has cultivated an environment of institutional negligence and opaque financial self-dealing. To understand why this matters so deeply to the future of Korean democracy, one must examine how the law has been weaponized against the vulnerable while serving as a comfortable shield for the powerful.

The Cruel Precision of the Law Against the Working Class

During the parliamentary audit, Democratic Party lawmaker Seo Young-kyo brought up a haunting piece of judicial history that continues to stain South Korea's legal record. Years ago, Oh Seok-joon—then a senior judge at the Seoul Administrative Court and later a Supreme Court justice nominee—presided over what became known as the "800-won bus driver dismissal case." A public transit worker was fired by his company after allegedly pocketing about 800 won (roughly $0.60) from bus fare collections to buy coffee. While local labor boards ruled the dismissal unjust given the microscopic sum involved, Judge Oh sided with the employer.

The human cost was devastating. As former lawmaker Lee Tan-hee pointed out, the driver, who was desperately trying to support a family of five through manual labor, became socially branded. He spent the next decade unable to secure steady employment, eventually reduced to picking up trash to survive. This chilling case captures a broader, systemic bias: the legal establishment displays an infinite capacity for leniency toward the indiscretions of institutional insiders while showing absolute coldness toward society's most vulnerable. Even Oh later expressed regret during his confirmation hearings, admitting a heavy heart over the destruction of an innocent life, but the damage was irreversible.

This ruthless application of the law stands in stark contrast to how authorities treat white-collar or institutional misconduct. Under the disgraced former administration of Yoon Seok-yeol—who was ultimately impeached and is currently imprisoned—the catchphrases of fairness and common sense became hollow marketing terms. A working citizen faces swift financial ruin over pennies, yet prosecutors caught receiving hundreds of thousands of won in entertainment treats walk away with normalized impunity. When the judiciary itself acts as a rotting rope that discriminates based on social standing, the public is left wondering if the law was ever meant to protect them.

Another absurdity surfaced with the infamous "Choco Pie and Custard case" from a logistics center. Workers who ate factory snacks worth a mere 1,060 won ($0.80) intended as breakroom refreshments were actually slapped with criminal fines by the courts. Although aggressive questioning during parliamentary audits eventually led to a retrial and an acquittal, the underlying message is chilling. The judiciary will mobilize its entire apparatus over a stray piece of candy consumed by a hungry laborer, yet it turns a blind eye to the bloated, unaccountable budgets enjoyed by its own top brass.

Vacant Benches and the Abandonment of Constitutional Duty

The rot inside South Korea's judicial leadership extends far beyond harsh rulings on petty offenses; it reaches into the deliberate paralysis of the country's highest court. The Supreme Court faces an unprecedented crisis of long-term vacancies, leaving critical constitutional responsibilities unfulfilled. A Chief Justice has the fundamental duty to close these gaps and alleviate the immense suffering caused by severe trial delays. Yet, Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has reportedly responded with systemic inaction and obstruction.

Legal experts and witnesses, including lawyer Kim Kyung-ho who testified as a reference witness, have sharply criticized this foot-dragging as a textbook case of professional negligence. The pattern mirrors the controversies seen when previous acting prime ministers under Yoon Seok-yeol's regime refused or delayed the constitutionally mandated formation of key state organs. Under South Korea's constitutional separation of powers, independent agencies share a mutual obligation to cooperate. Refusing or delaying appointments without a legitimate, reasonable justification is a clear abuse of power and a dereliction of duty.

The timeline reveals a calculated strategy of delay. Following recommendations made in January, the appointment process stalled for more than 200 days. Even after recommendations were submitted later in the year, the court introduced the unprecedented move of rejecting and tossing back nominations, repeating a cycle of obstruction. By turning judicial appointments into tools of political maneuvering, the nation's highest court has actively undermined the rule of law it is sworn to uphold.

The Shadowy World of Unvouched Judicial Allowances

Perhaps the most explosive revelation from the audit concerned the opaque financial allowances funneled to senior judicial officials. The discovery of monthly "career-grade" cash stipends—roughly 2 million won ($1,500) for Supreme Court justices and 3 million won ($2,200) for the Minister of Court Administration—exposed a slush-fund structure eerily reminiscent of past political scandals. Critics quickly drew parallels to the notorious National Intelligence Service special activities fund scandals of previous conservative administrations, where millions of won in public money were handed out regularly in cash without a shred of documentary evidence or clear legal authorization.

When pressed during the audit, the Chief Justice attempted to sidestep accountability, claiming he did not disburse those specific funds, while the Court Administration Minister tossed responsibility back, stating the Chief Justice had to sign off on them. The disconnect is staggering. While working-class citizens are dragged into courtrooms and penalized over a 1,060-won snack, high-ranking judges were allegedly slicing up a secret corporate wallet every month without proper paperwork.

Comparing this behavior to recent Supreme Court precedents on state fund embezzlement—which stress that public funds must be strictly proven to serve the state rather than individuals—makes the judicial leadership's actions look less like an administrative oversight and more like criminal malfeasance. The public expects the judiciary to be the ultimate guardian of fiscal and legal integrity. Instead, taxpayers discover that state coffers have been treated like pocket change for the judicial elite.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this era of political and institutional reset under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the fallout from this parliamentary audit will be a crucial test for the nation's democratic checks and balances. The revelations have shattered whatever lingering illusions remained about the self-correcting nature of the courts.

Here are the key developments to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • Formal Investigations into Judicial Allowances: Watch whether civic groups or investigative bodies push for criminal probes into the unvouched "career-grade" cash stipends distributed within the Court Administration.
  • Resolution of Supreme Court Vacancies: Observe if the political and judicial friction over delayed appointments subsides, or if the leadership continues its stalling tactics, which could trigger further legislative retaliation.
  • Systemic Judicial Reform Bills: Keep an eye on the National Assembly for newly proposed legislation aimed at stripping away discretionary slush funds and increasing transparency across all levels of the South Korean judiciary.

When the law is applied with merciless cruelty to the poor while shielding the powerful behind unvouched cash envelopes, the entire concept of justice collapses into a hollow ritual. Clearing out these deeply entrenched privileges is no longer just a political talking point—it is a matter of survival for Korean democracy.
