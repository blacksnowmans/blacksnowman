How a Controversial Media Startup is Reinventing South Korea's Winter Fashion

September 29, 2026

As the autumn wind gives way to a sharper winter chill, office workers across South Korea face a familiar seasonal dread: peering into the wardrobe, failing to remember what kept them warm last year, and sighing at the prospect of buying a new wardrobe. In a retail market dominated by fast-fashion giants churning out disposable trends, the calculus of whether to open one's wallet is a recurring modern dilemma.

Yet, an unexpected player has waded into the apparel market to pitch a solution. Kim Eo-jun's Humble Factory (Kyeomson Gongjang), a prominent anti-establishment YouTube broadcasting network and media startup, recently used its airwaves to unveil an extensive new line of winter wear, knitwear, and bags. Rather than a standard sponsored infomercial, the broadcast featured studio fittings of items that took a year and a half to develop. For a channel better known for political commentary and fierce partisan debates, the sudden pivot to lifestyle and fashion merchandising highlights a fascinating trend in South Korea’s creator economy: media platforms expanding into physical consumer goods designed for their loyal base.

Engineering the Commute: The 18-Month Quest for a Better Scarf

Every winter commuter knows the frustration of wrapping a scarf: bunching up around the neck while cold drafts still manage to sneak down the collar. Recognizing this minor daily misery, Humble Factory’s new winter accessories line introduces a remarkably simple intervention — a built-in slot.

Rather than requiring complex knots, the scarf features a dedicated aperture that one end slips through, instantly securing the fit without bulk. Developed in collaboration with brand ArcNine, the accessories lineup — which includes five distinct scarf-and-glove sets — was the result of a grueling prototyping process. According to ArcNine representative Kim Eun-woo, who appeared via phone during the broadcast, designers discarded more than nine out of every ten samples before finalizing the collection.

The accompanying gloves are woven from a merino wool and cashmere blend, engineered with extended wrist cuffs designed to slide neatly underneath the sleeves of a dress shirt or suit jacket. By prioritizing structural utility over fleeting runway trends, the creators targeted the very specific friction points of the morning subway rush. For office workers with zero patience for elaborate winter styling, these thoughtful details transform a basic accessory into a reliable cold-weather shield.

The Evolution of Knitwear: Blurring the Lines Between Sweaters and Outerwear

Knitwear occupies an awkward middle ground in professional dress codes: it needs to insulate against building drafts while remaining breathable enough for overheated offices. The new knit jackets and zip-ups featured on the broadcast tackle this dual mandate by treating knit material with the weight and structure of traditional outerwear.

Responding to feedback from viewers asking for "a jacket made of knit," the designers utilized a tight, dense weave that holds its shape without sagging. The collection includes four distinct styles for men and four for women, featuring two-way zippers that prevent the fabric from bunching up when the wearer sits down — an essential feature for long hours at a desk or behind the wheel.

Attention to hidden detail defined the presentation. Female hosts modeled long knit zip-ups that featured contrasting inner linings and finished pocket interiors, ensuring that the garments retained an air of understated luxury even when draped over the back of a chair. By refusing to cut corners on invisible finishes, the creators offered a pointed critique of mass-market fast fashion, where outward appearances often mask shoddy internal construction.

Reviving the 19th-Century Doctor’s Bag for the Laptop Era

No winter outfit is complete without luggage, and the lineup's most ambitious entry draws inspiration from an unlikely historical artifact: the 19th-century medical doctor's bag.

Featuring a hinged, wide-opening frame that mirrors vintage medical satchels, the new "Doctor’s Bag" series is scaled up to swallow modern essentials, including a 17-inch laptop. For modern professionals carrying heavy digital arsenals, the design merges nostalgic, old-world gravitas with heavy-duty capacity.

The collection also addresses one of leather's greatest drawbacks: weight. Recognizing that heavy bags quickly get abandoned in favor of backpacks, the women’s versions utilize a lightweight nylon-leather hybrid and come in bright palettes like pink and beige. Alongside the structured doctor bags, the team introduced casual cross-body styles inspired by safari gear and medical relief missions, injecting a dose of playful utility into the daily commute.

What to Watch Next

The success of Humble Factory’s apparel experiment points to a broader shift in how digital media brands monetize their audiences in South Korea, moving beyond ad revenue and donations into direct-to-consumer e-commerce. As these winter items ship to consumers, several key questions remain:

  • Fulfillment and Scale: Whether indie production partners like ArcNine and design firm Design Owal can scale manufacturing to meet sudden surges in demand without compromising the rigorous quality control highlighted in the broadcast.
  • Brand Longevity: Whether merchandise tied to a politically aligned media personality can sustain long-term retail appeal outside of core supporters.
  • Seasonal Expansion: Whether this successful winter line will pave the way for a permanent, four-season lifestyle brand rather than a one-off seasonal drop.

For now, the project stands as a curious intersection of media influence and practical design — proving that even in a polarized political landscape, everyone is united by the desire to stay warm on the way to work.
