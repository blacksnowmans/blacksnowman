Trouble in the Progressive Stronghold: Inside President Lee Jae-myung’s Collapsing Approval Ratings

October 14, 2026

When President Lee Jae-myung assumed office following the historic impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, his supporters anticipated a swift era of democratic renewal, institutional reform, and economic stabilization. Instead, South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) finds itself confronting a full-blown political crisis. Fresh public opinion data from research firm EveryResearch has sent shockwaves through the political establishment: President Lee’s national approval rating has crashed to 35.6%, while his negative evaluation has surged to 61.8%.

For international observers following East Asian politics, this sharp decline represents a dramatic shift in South Korea’s governing dynamics. The phenomenon known locally as a "dead cross"—where negative public sentiment comfortably outpaces positive approval—normally plagues administrations toward the end of their five-year terms. Experiencing it so early indicates deep-seated structural friction between the administration's policies and the daily realities of South Korean voters.

Even more startling is the spillover effect on party preferences. The main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP)—still reeling from the legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who remains in prison—has overtaken the ruling DPK for two consecutive weeks, polling at 34.6% against the DPK’s 31.0%. How did an administration that entered office with a commanding reform mandate lose ground so quickly? The answer lies in a volatile mix of out-of-touch rural land enforcement, legal scandals that have fatigued moderate voters, persistent urban housing inflation, and an increasingly insular management style at the top of government.

The Breakdown: Disillusionment Spans Generations and Regions

A closer examination of the EveryResearch survey reveals that public dissatisfaction is no longer confined to traditional conservative strongholds. Instead, it spans virtually every demographic and geographic segment of South Korean society.

Among young voters aged 18 to 29, approval for President Lee stands at just 30.4%, compared to a staggering 66.5% negative rating. This sharp pushback highlights deep economic anxiety among young adults facing job scarcity, high living expenses, and unmanageable housing costs. Meanwhile, core progressive demographics are also showing severe strain. Voters in their 40s—historically the bedrock of the DPK—yielded an approval rating of barely 42.1% against 57.0% disapproval. In the 50s demographic, negative ratings nearly doubled positive ones, sitting at 65.8% to 32.9%.

Geographically, the map paints an equally difficult picture for the administration:

  • Seoul & Metropolitan Area: In the capital, positive evaluation plummeted to 33.7% against 65.9% negative. The surrounding Incheon and Gyeonggi Province region recorded 37.0% positive and 60.8% negative.
  • Central Swing Region (Chungcheong): In Daejeon, Sejong, and the surrounding Chungcheong provinces—a key battleground region—approval cratered to 27.3%, with disapproval hitting 67.0%.
  • The Progressive Bedrock (Honam): Most alarming for the ruling party is the erosion in the southwestern Jeolla region (Honam), the traditional heartland of South Korean progressivism. Approval in Gwangju dropped to 49.2% and to 47.0% in South Jeolla Province—breaching the critical 50% psychological floor that progressive presidents historically depend on to maintain governing leverage.

When an administration loses its majority support in Honam while simultaneously collapsing in central swing regions, it signals that core voters feel neglected while swing voters feel alienated.

The Policy Missteps: How a Farmland Audit Shocked the Countryside

To understand why rural and regional voters have turned so sharply against the administration, one must look at a well-intentioned policy that turned into an administrative disaster: a massive nationwide audit of agricultural land.

Under South Korea’s constitutional principle of Gyeongja-yujeon ("the tiller should own the land"), agricultural property is legally restricted to active farmers to prevent speculative real estate buying. The Lee administration launched a sweeping audit of 1.36 million hectares of farmland acquired since the passage of the 1996 Farmland Act. The nationwide inspection flagged approximately 284,000 land parcels—roughly 27% of the total—for potential violations such as unauthorized leasing or leaving fields idle.

The government threatened to levy heavy annual non-compliance fines amounting to 25% of either the land's official appraised value or public land price. However, this bureaucratic hammer ignored the stark demographic reality of modern South Korean agriculture. Rural provinces are severely aging, with acute labor shortages making active farming nearly impossible for elderly landowners. Furthermore, population decline in rural districts means many aging villagers cannot find buyers even if they want to sell their land.

The heavy-handed enforcement sparked widespread outrage across agrarian communities. Lawmakers within the ruling DPK, such as Representative Hwang Jeong-a and Representative Ieon Joo, publicly criticized the Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs, pointing out that treating elderly rural citizens like real estate speculators fails to recognize reality. Opposition leaders quickly capitalized on the blunder, with PPP Chairman Jang Dong-hyeok calling the audit a misguided crusade that punished vulnerable villagers instead of catching real speculators.

Though the DPK leadership belatedly moved to soften the penalties and pivot toward guidance rather than immediate fines, the emotional damage was done. For many rural residents in regions like South Jeolla, the audit created a deep sense of betrayal.

Legal Fatigue, Housing Inflation, and the Rise of Third Forces

While rural missteps eroded the administration’s base in the provinces, urban middle-class and moderate voters in the greater Seoul region have grown fatigued by ongoing legal disputes and economic pressure.

A separate poll by Jo Won C&I highlighted that controversy surrounding the cancellation of indictments in high-profile political cases was cited as the single largest reason (24.3%) for the slump in presidential approval. For moderate swing voters who expected higher ethical standards and procedural fairness from a progressive government, these legal squabbles have induced severe moral weariness. Coupled with rising rent prices and housing supply instability in the capital, suburban families feel that the government is overly focused on political maneuvering while ordinary citizens struggle with daily living costs.

This widespread frustration has created a political opening—not just for the conservative PPP, but for smaller progressive alternatives. The Cho Kuk Innovation Party (also known as the Rebuilding Korea Party), led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, has seen its national support rise sharply to 9.0% in the EveryResearch survey.

Crucially, Cho’s party captured 11.0% support among youth voters aged 18 to 29—surpassing smaller reformist factions and securing double-digit support among young adults. This surge indicates that disillusioned progressive voters and politically uncommitted youth are actively seeking an alternative voice to voice their dissatisfaction with the DPK’s legislative inertia.

Insular Governance and the Shadow of an Early Lame-Duck Phase

Underlying these policy issues is a growing concern over President Lee’s personnel decisions and governance style. The early months of the administration have been marred by cabinet confirmation stumbles, with two out of six initial ministerial nominees stepping down due to vetting failures. Key institutional positions across law enforcement and administrative agencies remain unfilled or bogged down in political disputes.

The recent resignation of Presidential Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik reflected the administrative strain. Yet, reports that National Tax Service Commissioner Lim Gwang-hyun is a leading contender to replace him have drawn groans from within party ranks, where critics worry the administration is recycling bureaucratic loyalists rather than bringing in bold, independent voices.

This closed-loop decision-making style has sparked hushed talk of an early "lame-duck" phenomenon—an unusual status for an administration mid-mandate. Media outlets and pollsters have already begun tracking preference numbers for future presidential contenders within the progressive camp. Figures like Gyeonggi Governor Kim Dong-yeon (14.0%), former party chief Song Young-gil (12.1%), Cho Kuk (12.9%), and senior lawmaker Jeong Cheong-rae (12.9%) are increasingly viewed by base voters as potential standard-bearers, signaling that public focus is already shifting away from the sitting president.

What to Watch Next

President Lee Jae-myung’s administration stands at a critical juncture. A drop below 35% approval risks stripping the ruling party of its momentum to pass key economic reforms. To reverse this trajectory, political analysts are closely monitoring three immediate developments:

  • Farmland Enforcement Policy Adjustments: Watch whether the Ministry of Agriculture formally waives punitive fees for elderly rural landowners and shifts toward legislative amendments that accommodate tenant farming in aging provinces.
  • The Cabinet & Presidential Staff Reshuffle: The selection of the next Chief of Staff and key agency heads will test whether President Lee is willing to broaden his appointments beyond loyalists to restore public confidence in cabinet competence.
  • Re-engaging the Honam Base: Watch how the DPK responds to the rising popularity of the Cho Kuk Innovation Party in the Jeolla region, particularly regarding long-promised local economic initiatives like semiconductor investments.

In South Korean politics, public sentiment can swing rapidly. However, rebuilding broken trust requires more than public relations fixes—it demands a clear acknowledgement of real-world policy mistakes and an open approach to governance.
