When Generals Play Politics: The Hollow Defenses and First Verdicts of South Korea's December 3 Martial Law

October 02, 2026

Picture the scene inside the Seoul Central District Court: a man who once commanded some of the most sensitive, powerful apparatuses in the South Korean military now stands before a judge, arguing that an attempted constitutional overthrow was simply a misunderstanding. Yeo In-hyung, the former head of the Defense Security Command—South Korea’s military counterintelligence agency—listened as the court handed down an 18-year prison sentence. Yet, rather than reckoning with the gravity of turning the nation’s armed forces against its own legislature, his defense relied on semantic gymnastics, blaming subordinates, and claiming that trying to detain lawmakers was just routine police work.

For outside observers trying to understand South Korea’s democratic resilience, the aftermath of the short-lived December 3 emergency martial law declared by former President Yoon Suk Yeol offers a chilling study in institutional capture. The trials of the military top brass are not merely legal proceedings; they are an autopsy of how quickly a modern democracy can tilt toward autocracy when high-ranking officers view loyalty to a leader as superior to their oath to the constitution. As the first wave of verdicts rolls in, the question gripping the country is whether these sentences truly match the crime of subverting the state.

The First-Instance Verdicts: Lighter Sentences and Diverging Accountability

The Seoul Central District Court's rulings sent a clear, if contested, message. Alongside Yeo In-hyung’s 18-year sentence, the court handed down significant prison terms to other key figures: Lee Jin-woo, former commander of the Capital Defense Command, received 15 years; Park An-su, former Army Chief of Staff, got 10 years; Kwak Jong-keun, former Special Warfare Commander, received 9 years; and Moon Sang-ho, former head of the Defense Intelligence Command, was sentenced to 12 years. These numbers fell short of the special prosecutor’s harsher demands—which included 30-year terms for Yeo and Lee—sparking a fierce debate in legal and civic circles over whether the judiciary is standing firm or offering a slap on the wrist to mutinous officers.

What distinguished these sentences was not just the military titles held by the accused, but how they behaved when the scheme collapsed. Kwak Jong-keun received a relatively lighter sentence of nine years primarily because he cooperated with investigators early on, submitting self-confession statements and acknowledging realities. In stark contrast, commanders like Yeo In-hyung doubled down on evasive maneuvers. The public, however, views these distinctions through a sharper lens: regardless of who confessed or who obfuscated, a group of generals treated the republic's highest institutions as personal chattel, deploying elite troops to paralyze parliament. No amount of legal posturing can sanitize that betrayal.

Passing the Buck: The Defense Security Command and the Myth of a 'Misunderstanding'

Perhaps the most surreal aspect of Yeo In-hyung’s trial was his insistence that the mobilization of his command to target and detain members of parliament was nothing more than an administrative mix-up. In court, Yeo claimed he never issued orders to arrest lawmakers, arguing instead that he viewed the chaotic deployments merely as individual investigations into "martial law violators." When confronted with evidence that he asked the National Police Agency for politicians' locations, he argued he expected the process to take days—a defense that sits somewhere between absurd and insulting.

Testimony from subordinates paints a vastly different picture. Kim Dae-woo, head of the Defense Security Command's investigation unit, testified that Yeo gave direct orders regarding detention facilities, including checking whether a secure bunker at the Capital Defense Command could be used to hold prisoners. The plot involved pre-coordinating actions using personal and school networks, leveraging intelligence assets for domestic political surveillance, and trying to muscle local police stations into forming a joint investigation headquarters. To dismiss these calculated, midnight troop movements as a communication glitch is an insult to common sense.

The Panic Before Dawn: Watching the Clock and Invoking God

As the martial law decree began to unravel in the dead of night, the panic among the military conspirators became palpable. Testimony revealed that around 12:30 AM, as television broadcasts showed members of parliament streaming into the National Assembly to vote down the decree, commanders realized their window was slamming shut. Yeo argued in court that he told his units to proceed with caution precisely because he knew parliament would soon vote to lift martial law, meaning any hasty arrests would lose their legal backing.

This defense acts as a self-indictment. It demonstrates that the commanders were not executing a legitimate national security operation, but rather racing against the clock to lock down the legislature before civilian authority could legally nullify their orders. When their power grab failed, they scrambled to rebrand their inaction as "prudence." In his final statements, Yeo even quoted scripture—proverbs about God guiding a man’s steps—attempting to cloak his unconstitutional actions in divine inevitability. For a public that watched soldiers attempt to scale the walls of parliament, such theatrical piety only deepens the insult.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves past these initial rulings, several critical developments will shape the final chapter of this constitutional crisis:

  • The Appeal Proceedings: Both the special prosecutors and the convicted generals have signaled appeals. Watch whether appellate courts toughen sentences closer to the prosecution's original demands or entertain the defendants' claims of operational confusion.
  • Broadening Investigations: Beyond the direct military commanders, investigators continue to probe the political wire-pullers who greenlit the operation, examining the chain of command extending upward from the Ministry of National Defense.
  • Institutional Reforms: Debates are intensifying within the National Assembly over overhauling the military counterintelligence and intelligence structures to ensure these secretive bodies can never again be weaponized for domestic politics.
  • Public Vigilance: Civil society groups remain mobilized, monitoring courtrooms to ensure that accountability reaches every corner of the conspiracy, cementing a zero-tolerance precedent for military intervention in politics.
