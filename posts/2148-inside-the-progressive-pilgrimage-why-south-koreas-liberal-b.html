Inside the Progressive Pilgrimage: Why South Korea’s Liberal Base Is Looking to the Past

November 09, 2026

On a recent Saturday, fourteen chartered buses carrying nearly 1,000 citizens wound their way through South Korea’s southern countryside. Participants paid their own way and gave up their weekend to join "Walking the Path of Democracy Together," a daylong pilgrimage to the spiritual landmarks of South Korea’s liberal movement. For many watching from home—including supporters unable to travel in person due to family health struggles—the live video streams broadcast by independent media outlets provided a profound sense of comfort during a troubling political moment.

The journey was more than a nostalgic memorial tour. As citizens laid flowers at the grave of former Prime Minister Lee Hae-chan, visited the cliffside resting place of former President Roh Moo-hyun in Bongha Village, and cheered former President Moon Jae-in at his rural bookstore in Pyeongsan, the event exposed deep emotional currents running through South Korea’s ruling liberal camp. At a time when South Korea is governed by President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), this grassroots pilgrimage highlighted both the enduring hunger for principled leadership and the growing friction between the party's historical identity and its current leadership.

The Sacred Sites of Korean Liberalism: From Lee Hae-chan’s Grave to Roh Moo-hyun’s Bongha

The pilgrimage began at the grave of the late Lee Hae-chan, a legendary figure in South Korean politics who served as Prime Minister under President Roh Moo-hyun and later as the chairman of the Democratic Party of Korea. Aerial drone footage captured endless lines of citizens queuing in solemn silence to lay flowers and offer condolences to Lee’s widow. For many in attendance, the moment was bittersweet. Lee Hae-chan was widely remembered as a firm political strategist who provided a clear compass during party crises. Standing before his grave, many participants expressed frustration with the current state of the ruling party, lamenting that if Lee were still alive, he would not have allowed the Democratic Party to lose its direction or display such political weakness.

From the grave site, the caravan traveled to Bongha Village in Gimhae, South Gyeongsang Province. Bongha is the birthplace and final resting place of Roh Moo-hyun, South Korea’s 16th president (2003–2008), who tragically died by jumping from Owl Rock behind his home in 2009 amid an aggressive prosecutor-led investigation. Bongha is not an easy place to visit; it is a remote, quiet farming community poorly served by public transit, requiring multiple train transfers and long walks along rural roads. Yet thousands of citizens make the journey every year to honor what progressives call Roh’s "foolish spirit"—his refusal to compromise with entrenched political interests or abusive prosecutorial power.

At the floral tribute table below Owl Rock, prominent commentators, journalists such as Lee Myeong-suk, actor Lee Geon-woo, and former lawmaker Cho Eung-cheon joined citizens in bowing their heads. While some online critics questioned the presence of certain political figures at Bongha, participants noted the hypocrisy of armchair commentators. Those who criticize visitors from afar often do so to mask their own guilt for never having made the difficult trek themselves. Visiting Bongha remains a physical act of political commitment, reminding Korea's liberal base of the painful costs paid in the fight for democratic consolidation.

Warm Solace in Pyeongsan: Moon Jae-in and the Contrast of Power

The emotional peak of the tour occurred in Pyeongsan Village, located in Yangsan, South Gyeongsang Province, where former President Moon Jae-in (who served from 2017 to 2022) resides. Since retiring from office, Moon has lived as a private citizen, operating a small community bookstore called the Pyeongsan Bookshelf.

As the crowd gathered in the bookstore courtyard under the coordination of Lee Jeong-won, head of the progressive media group Sisa Tapa, Moon stepped out to greet his visitors. Wearing simple clothes and smiling broadly, the former president was met with deafening applause and chants of "We love you, Moon Jae-in." For many attendees, seeing Moon living peacefully in his rural retreat provided a stark, therapeutic contrast to the chaotic political battles taking place in Seoul.

This dignified retirement stands in dramatic contrast to the fate of Yoon Suk-yeol. Following his impeachment by the National Assembly and formal removal from office, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was convicted and is currently serving a prison sentence. For the progressives gathered in Pyeongsan, Moon Jae-in's enduring popularity demonstrated that true presidential dignity does not come from wielding the coercive power of the state, but from earning the lasting affection of the people.

A Shadow Over the Pilgrimage: President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Reaction

Despite the uplifting atmosphere among the crowds in Bongha and Pyeongsan, the event was shadowed by political tension surrounding South Korea's current leader, President Lee Jae-myung. As the pilgrimage was underway, President Lee posted a message on his X (formerly Twitter) account that struck many observers as surprisingly defensive.

Rather than embracing the event as a celebration of his party’s historical legacy, President Lee’s social media reaction appeared to reveal annoyance at the outpouring of public affection directed toward former President Moon Jae-in. Online commentators and participants noted that the President seemed visibly "triggered" by the enthusiastic crowd gathered at Moon’s bookstore.

For many progressives who supported the Democratic Party’s return to power under President Lee Jae-myung, this petty social media reaction was deeply disappointing. Citizens expect a sitting president to display the broad-minded leadership of predecessors like Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—leaders who unified the party rather than engaging in passive-aggressive squabbles on social networks over minor slights. The contrast between Moon’s relaxed warmth in Pyeongsan and President Lee’s insecure posturing on X left a bitter taste for many party faithful.

Grassroots Media, Business Ties, and Strategic Solidarity

The success of the "Walking the Path of Democracy Together" event highlighted the vital role played by independent, crowdfunded media platforms in South Korea’s progressive ecosystem. Organized primarily through Sisa Tapa and its sister platform Sisa Geongeon, the pilgrimage demonstrated how modern political movements rely on alternative media networks to bypass mainstream corporate news outlets.

Building effective political solidarity requires more than emotional speeches; it demands financial and organizational sustainability. Platforms like Sisa Tapa maintain their independence through a combination of transparent citizen sponsorships—via domestic bank accounts like KB Bank and international services like PayPal—and commercial e-commerce partnerships. Cooperative e-commerce initiatives, such as joint product sales linking food platforms like Yori Ilga with alternative media channels, alongside corporate sponsors like Deep & Dutch, E-Flex, and I'm Good, provide the economic engine that keeps independent progressive voices afloat.

"True solidarity means reaching out before you need help yourself—paying your debts in advance so that when the political storms hit, you stand together on solid ground."

This philosophy of mutual support is essential in an era dominated by intense legal and political warfare. From complex legal investigations involving figures like former Seongnam Development Corporation official Yoo Dong-gyu to cabinet appointments like former Justice Minister nominee Lee Yong-cheol, the South Korean political landscape remains fraught with controversy. By strengthening independent media channels and commercial networks, progressive citizens ensure they have the tools to defend their values regardless of which way the political winds blow.

What to Watch Next

The weekend pilgrimage to Lee Hae-chan’s grave, Bongha Village, and Pyeongsan Village offered a powerful window into the state of South Korea’s liberal movement. As the country moves forward under the governing Democratic Party of Korea, several key dynamics bear close watching:

  • Factional Alignment Within the Ruling Party: Watch how President Lee Jae-myung manages relations with the party's traditional pro-Moon and pro-Roh wings. Continued insecurity or friction toward former President Moon Jae-in could alienate core party loyalists.
  • Grassroots Media Influence: Monitor the growth of independent platforms like Sisa Tapa. As traditional broadcast media faces public skepticism, these crowdfunded networks are becoming key power brokers in mobilizing voters and shaping intra-party debates.
  • Progressive Policy Reform Agenda: Track whether President Lee’s administration can channel the moral energy of the liberal base into concrete governance—specifically regarding long-promised judicial reforms, media restructuring, and economic relief—rather than getting bogged down in social media controversies.

The road from Bongha to Pyeongsan is more than a geographic route; it is the emotional backbone of South Korean liberalism. As long as citizens are willing to board buses and walk those rural roads, they will continue to hold their political leaders—past and present—accountable to the standards of democracy.
