The Battle for Democratic Legitimacy: Inside the Ruling Party’s Strategy to Reshape the Political Landscape

October 31, 2026

The air inside the National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul’s political heart, was thick with more than just the usual partisan bickering on October 30. It was the final day of the National Audit—an annual month-long marathon where lawmakers grill government agencies and public officials. But this was no ordinary wrap-up. For the Democratic Party, the current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, it was a day to assert dominance over a narrative that has become increasingly volatile. As cameras flashed and lawmakers traded barbs, the underlying struggle wasn’t just about policy; it was about who gets to define the truth in a post-martial law Korea.

To understand why the events of this day matter, one must look at the scars still healing on the Korean body politic. Since the downfall of the previous administration and the imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol following his failed attempt at martial law on December 3, the political landscape has been in a state of constant re-evaluation. The Democratic Party (DP) is now attempting to cement its authority not just through governance, but by dismantling what it calls "conspiracy-driven politics" and reforming the institutions that failed to stop the constitutional crisis of the past year.

Defunding the Conspiracy: The Push for Election Reform

The morning began with a sharp offensive from Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the Democratic Party’s Supreme Council. His target was the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, which has recently doubled down on claims of election fraud. For an international observer, the sight of an opposition party questioning the integrity of a highly digitized and historically reliable voting system might seem familiar, but in Korea, these claims are being used to mobilize a specific, hard-right base that feels alienated by the current administration.

Kim’s argument was straightforward: the PPP is using "fake news" to shake the foundations of democracy. He urged the opposition to stop following the scripts of far-right YouTube channels and instead sit down at the legislative table to discuss actual institutional reforms. The DP is proposing a major overhaul of the National Election Commission (NEC), the independent constitutional body that manages elections. The goal is to increase transparency to the point where even the most dedicated conspiracy theorist has no room to maneuver.

The stakes here are high. When a major political party begins to question the validity of the vote, the "social cost" mentioned by Kim Min-seok becomes a reality. It leads to a breakdown in civil discourse and a distrust of the government’s mandate. By framing the issue as an urgent need for "NEC Reform," the Democratic Party is attempting to pivot from a defensive stance—denying the fraud—to an offensive one—fixing the system so that such claims can never be credibly made again. However, the PPP remains lukewarm toward these constructive talks, preferring to keep the fire of suspicion burning on social media platforms.

The 'Immoral Three' and the Decay of Parliamentary Dignity

As the audit reached its climax, the rhetoric shifted from institutional reform to personal conduct. Rep. Han Byeong-do of the Democratic Party leveled a blistering critique against three figures he dubbed the "Immoral Three Siblings": Lee Jin-sook, the chairperson of the Korea Communications Commission (KCC); Kim Tae-gyu, the acting vice-chair of the KCC; and Joo Jin-woo, a high-profile lawmaker from the PPP. The KCC is the powerful agency that regulates broadcasting and communications in Korea, a role that has become a lightning rod for controversy as the government seeks to "normalize" the media landscape.

The DP’s anger stems from what they describe as a total lack of respect for the National Assembly. During the audits, these officials were accused of using inflammatory language and obstructive tactics designed not to answer questions, but to create "shorts"—viral, vertical video clips—for social media. This "YouTube-ification" of politics is a growing concern in Seoul. When a public official prioritizes a ten-second viral moment over a four-hour policy debate, the function of the National Audit as a check on executive power is neutralized.

Rep. Joo Jin-woo, in particular, was singled out for his transition from a perceived "elite" figure during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time to what Han called a "prince of far-right cyber wreckers." This term, "cyber wrecker," is a Korean colloquialism for YouTubers who profit from scandal and controversy. The accusation is that the opposition is no longer interested in governing or even traditional opposition; they are performing for an algorithm. The Democratic Party is now demanding formal disciplinary action, arguing that if these outbursts go unpunished, the dignity of the parliament will be permanently diminished.

The Shadow of Shincheonji and Party Integrity

While battling the opposition, the Democratic Party is also dealing with internal pressures. One of the most sensitive topics in Korean politics is the influence of religious groups, specifically Shincheonji, a controversial organization often labeled a cult by mainstream Christian groups. Rumors have persisted that Shincheonji members attempted to mass-enroll in the Democratic Party to influence internal primary elections.

On October 30, Rep. Lee Seong-yun and other party members pushed for the immediate release of an internal investigation into these allegations. For a foreign reader, the significance of Shincheonji cannot be overstated; the group was at the center of Korea’s first major COVID-19 outbreak and has been frequently accused of trying to infiltrate political circles to gain protection or influence.

The DP leadership has promised to report the findings of this investigation at a general meeting of lawmakers early next week. The party is in a delicate position. If they hide the results, they face a "bulletproof" framing from the opposition—the idea that they are protecting their own interests. If they reveal a deep infiltration, it could damage the party’s image. However, the prevailing sentiment within the party is that a "frontal breakthrough"—total transparency—is the only way to maintain the moral high ground against a PPP that they claim ignores its own scandals.

Investigating the Chief Justice: The Martial Law Connection

Perhaps the most legally significant development is the Democratic Party’s move to launch a National Assembly investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The focus is laser-targeted: his actions on the night of December 3, during the brief and illegal declaration of martial law by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The DP wants to know if the head of the judiciary fulfilled his constitutional duty to protect the rule of law that night. Did the Supreme Court communicate with the martial law command? Was there any degree of tacit cooperation or bystander apathy? The PPP has slammed this move as "political violence" and an attack on judicial independence, particularly as the courts are still handling cases related to the current administration’s figures.

"The investigation is not about interfering with court rulings; it is about the accountability of the head of a branch of government during an insurrection," a DP spokesperson clarified.

This is a pivotal moment for the Lee Jae-myung government. By investigating the Chief Justice, the DP is signaling that no one is above the "Truth of December 3." For the ruling party, the martial law attempt wasn't just a political failure by Mr. Yoon; it was a systemic failure of the Korean state. Proving who stood for the constitution and who sat on the sidelines is essential to their narrative of "cleansing" the government of the old guard's influence.

What to Watch Next

As the National Audit closes and the political calendar moves toward November, several key triggers will determine the stability of the Korean government:

  • The Shincheonji Report: Expected in the first week of November, this report will either put the rumors to rest or ignite a new internal conflict within the Democratic Party.
  • The Legislative Push for NEC Reform: Watch for whether the DP uses its majority to push through election reforms without the PPP’s consent, a move that would further polarize the electorate.
  • Police Investigation of Rep. Park Sun-won: A case involving the alleged disclosure of a whistleblower's identity by this DP lawmaker has been transferred to the police. The outcome will be used by the opposition to test the "fairness" of the current administration’s law enforcement.
  • The Judicial Inquiry: The formal commencement of the investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae will likely lead to a massive showdown between the legislative and judicial branches, a rare and high-stakes conflict in Korean democracy.

The events of October 30 show a ruling party that is no longer content with merely reacting to opposition attacks. By weaving together institutional reform, disciplinary demands, and historical accountability for the December 3 incident, the Democratic Party is attempting to build a new political consensus—one where the "old ways" of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s era are not just replaced, but thoroughly investigated and reformed out of existence.
