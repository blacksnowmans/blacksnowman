The Geopolitical Chessboard: Korea's Future in a Multipolar World

October 05, 2026

As we look at the shifting tides of international relations, it is difficult to shake the feeling that the pieces on a giant global chessboard are scrambling to find their footing. With the upcoming APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) summit on the horizon, the world is holding its breath to see how the three titans—the United States, China, and Russia—will redraw the map. For South Korea, this is no longer just a matter of diplomatic rhetoric; it has become a fundamental question of survival.

The APEC Summit and the Shift Toward Multipolarity

There is growing speculation about a potential three-way dialogue between the U.S., China, and Russia. Historically, the idea of these three powers sitting at the same table has been unthinkable since the end of World War II. For decades, the U.S. operated under the assumption that it was the sole superpower capable of solving global issues unilaterally. However, the current geopolitical landscape—and the realities of the Trump era—make it clear that Washington can no longer manage global affairs in isolation.

If such a summit occurs, it will mark the U.S. officially acknowledging China and Russia as peers. While some might question what the U.S. stands to gain, the reality is that the U.S. has little choice. Managing crises in Iran, the ongoing war in Ukraine, and rising tensions on the Korean Peninsula requires cooperation with Beijing and Moscow. This summit would serve as a symbolic milestone, signaling a shift from a period of unbridled conflict to a pragmatic, albeit fragile, era of managed relations.

This transition toward a multipolar world leaves South Korea at a precarious intersection. If our diplomacy remains stuck in a bygone era, we risk being left on the sidelines, much like the European nations or Saudi Arabia, which have found their traditional security guarantees fading as U.S. foreign policy shifts.

The Erosion of American Influence

The cracks in U.S. hegemony are becoming visible across the globe. In South America, the recent political volatility—epitomized by the electoral battles in Brazil—shows the limitations of the "Shield of the Americas," a conservative military alliance effectively backed by Washington. Designed under the guise of fighting drug cartels, the alliance has largely functioned as a political bloc to exclude left-leaning governments, and its influence is waning.

The situation in Saudi Arabia is even more telling. Long considered an indispensable American partner, Riyadh found itself vulnerable to Houthi insurgent attacks, only to be met with American hesitation regarding air support. When a nation is forced to look elsewhere for its survival—such as Saudi Arabia’s deployment of Chinese-made ballistic missiles—it sends a clear message: the old security architecture is broken. Riyadh, like many others, is deciding to "find its own way."

If South Korea’s security establishment continues to tether itself blindly to a rigid, U.S.-only diplomatic strategy, it is playing a dangerous game. As traditional allies like Canada explore new partnerships to reduce their dependence on Washington, South Korea must ask: what are we doing to fill the gaps in our own security?

North Korea and the "Golden Opportunity"

Despite the current climate, there is a tangible opening for dialogue on the Korean Peninsula. South Korea’s National Intelligence Service (NIS) has estimated a 50% chance of North Korea-U.S. talks. Given the inherently conservative nature of intelligence agencies, which typically downplay risks and probabilities, this is a remarkably high assessment.

For North Korea, the current U.S. political landscape presents a unique window. The current environment offers a lower barrier to entry for negotiations compared to potential future administrations, and the pressure regarding denuclearization and sanctions is relatively fluid. When the U.S. Pacific Command describes recent North Korean missile tests as "not threatening," it is a clear signal that Washington is attempting to set the stage for a thaw. This is a golden opportunity to push for a formal end-of-war declaration and a peace treaty.

Yet, our own foreign policy team remains passive. While the rest of the world is busy navigating an "America-less" security landscape, we seem paralyzed by hesitation. We must stop viewing peace on the Korean Peninsula as a peripheral issue and start treating it as a core component of our national survival.

The Challenge of Japanese Rearmament

We must also address the elephant in the room: Japan’s steady march toward remilitarization. Recent rhetoric from Japanese officials regarding nuclear-powered submarines, combined with the country's latent capability to produce nuclear weapons on short notice, is not accidental. Japan is preparing to fill the security vacuum left by a U.S. pivot away from traditional commitments.

The silence from our own government on this issue is deeply troubling. If we cannot critique Japan’s path toward nuclearization, we lose the moral and strategic standing to advocate for our own advanced defense capabilities, such as nuclear-powered submarines. Japan’s rearmament is a direct threat to our security, and failing to respond firmly is an act of diplomatic incompetence. Furthermore, the transfer of Wartime Operational Control (OPCON) back to South Korea can no longer be delayed; assuming the U.S. will always be there to save us is a dangerous fantasy.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward the APEC summit and beyond, there are three key areas that will define our future:

  • Independence in Security: Watch whether South Korea begins to secure independent security cards. If we remain a dependent variable in the U.S. strategy rather than a partner, we will be the first to be sacrificed when the global order changes.
  • Dialogue with the North: Observe if the government moves beyond passive reactions to actively facilitate a framework for denuclearization and peace, capitalizing on the current "window" before political winds in Washington shift.
  • Regional Diplomacy: Look for signs that Korea is diversifying its diplomatic ties. The "Valdai Club" and similar forums underscore that the center of gravity is shifting toward Asia. If we remain trapped in old ideological frameworks, we will be left behind by the very region we inhabit.

The era of begging for security under the American umbrella is coming to an end. It is time for South Korea to design its own century-long vision, secure its own borders, and conduct a foreign policy that prioritizes our survival above all else. The history of our nation is being written right now, and we cannot afford to be mere spectators.
