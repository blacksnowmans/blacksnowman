The $45 Billion Arms Race: How the "Physical AI" Pivot is Redrawing Korea’s Tech Map

October 21, 2026

Imagine a corporate budget so vast it rivals the annual defense spending of a mid-sized nation. In the rapidly escalating theater of artificial intelligence, these "astronomical" figures are no longer hypothetical. They are the new baseline. Recent reports indicating a potential $45 billion (approximately 60 trillion KRW) infrastructure deal between Anthropic and the UK-based computing firm Nscale have sent shockwaves through global markets, signaling that the AI battle has moved beyond clever algorithms and into the realm of brute-force physical infrastructure.

For the uninitiated, Anthropic is the San Francisco-based AI safety and research company often viewed as the primary rival to OpenAI, the creator of ChatGPT. Nscale, meanwhile, is a rising power in the specialized cloud computing sector. This gargantuan $45 billion negotiation underscores a cold reality: in the current AI era, the size of your Large Language Model (LLM) matters less than the sheer volume of computing power you can command. But as the industry pours trillions into servers and chips, the narrative is shifting. We are moving away from the "hallucinations" of chatbots toward what Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang calls "Physical AI"—the integration of intelligence into robots, factories, and the tangible world.

In South Korea, a nation whose economy is built on the "physical" foundations of manufacturing and semiconductors, this pivot is more than a tech trend; it is a matter of national survival. With the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's current ruling party—moving to dismantle the regulatory stagnation of the previous era, Korea is positioning itself as a central hub for this next hardware-centric wave of AI.

Infrastructure as the Ultimate Moat

The scale of the Anthropic-Nscale negotiations highlights a deepening divide in the tech world. When a single company seeks to secure $45 billion in computing power, it effectively builds a "moat" that few startups can ever hope to cross. This isn't just about training better models; it's about securing the "digital oil" of the 21st century before the supply runs dry. For Anthropic, this massive investment is a strategic play to close the gap with OpenAI, which itself is reportedly seeking a $30 billion (about 40 trillion KRW) funding round from heavyweights like SoftBank and MGX, potentially valuing the company at a staggering $1.4 trillion (1,860 trillion KRW).

The financial rewards for those who bet early on this infrastructure are already manifesting in historic proportions. Eric Schmidt, the former CEO of Google, currently holds a 1% stake in Anthropic. Analysts estimate his "jackpot" on this single investment to be worth roughly $20 billion (about 27 trillion KRW). This highlights a fundamental shift: the real winners of the first AI wave aren't necessarily the ones selling the software, but the individuals and entities that owned the infrastructure and the early equity.

However, this concentration of capital raises a chilling question for the broader market: is this a sustainable boom or a bubble in the making? While the investments are "astronomical," the path to profitability remains murky. If the returns on these tens of billions in server costs don't materialize soon, the "cold reality" of the market could trigger a correction that reverberates through global financial systems. For now, the "Big Tech" players are operating on the assumption that being second is far more expensive than overspending to be first.

The Rise of Physical AI and the Nvidia Effect

If the first wave of AI was about generating text and images on a screen, the "next wave" is about moving limbs and driving wheels. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang recently declared that "Physical AI" is the next frontier. This refers to AI that can perceive, reason, and act in the physical world—think humanoid robots on factory floors, autonomous logistics in warehouses, and self-driving vehicles that truly understand their environment.

This shift plays directly into South Korea’s traditional strengths. While Korea has struggled to produce a global-scale LLM to rival GPT-4, its manufacturing prowess is world-class. This is where "Physical AI" becomes a transformative opportunity for Korean firms. For instance, MDS Tech, a Korean embedded solutions provider, recently secured a $3 million (4 billion KRW) contract to supply Nvidia Jetson packages—specialized AI modules for edge computing—for a humanoid home robot project. Similarly, Bigwave Robotics, a Korean startup, saw its stock price surge by 11% following the launch of "Hyper Foundry," an integrated development platform for Physical AI.

The transition is also visible in the corporate strategies of Korea’s legacy giants. KT (formerly Korea Telecom), one of the nation’s largest telecommunications providers, recently hosted its "K-PATH 2026" event. There, the company detailed its "AI Transformation" (AX) roadmap, collaborating with 56 startups to apply AI directly to manufacturing and network infrastructure. These aren't just software updates; they are fundamental rewrites of how physical work is performed. When AI begins to replace or augment physical labor at scale, the nation that builds the hardware becomes the gatekeeper of the global economy.

The Shadow War: Espionage and National Security

As AI becomes the most valuable commodity on earth, it has inevitably become a target for state-sponsored espionage. A recent case involving OpenAI has sent a "shiver" through the industry. A former employee of Saudi Arabian nationality was arrested for allegedly planting wiretapping devices in OpenAI’s conference rooms. A U.S. court recently denied bail for the individual, citing strong evidence of support from the Saudi royal family and military, and a high risk of flight.

This incident proves that AI technology is no longer just a commercial asset; it is a core component of national security. The "wiretapping scandal" at OpenAI serves as a grim warning to Big Tech companies that their internal security measures may be woefully inadequate against the resources of a sovereign state. It is a "spy war" that mimics the Cold War era, but with GPU clusters and weights instead of nuclear blueprints.

The geopolitical tension is further complicated by the ongoing technological blockade between the U.S. and China. Despite heavy U.S. sanctions on advanced chips, Chinese firms are finding workarounds. DeepSeek, a Chinese AI startup, is reportedly collaborating directly with Huawei to design AI chips, aiming to reduce dependence on Nvidia. In response, the U.S. government has moved to ban DeepSeek AI from government agencies. For export-oriented economies like South Korea, this "technological bloc" era is a minefield. Navigating the space between U.S. security demands and the reality of Chinese technical advancement requires a level of diplomatic and strategic "finesse" that Korea is currently struggling to refine.

Korea’s Regulatory Pivot under President Lee Jae-myung

Domestically, the political climate regarding technology has shifted dramatically. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, there is a concerted effort to clear the "regulatory hurdles" that many industry leaders felt were left unaddressed during the previous years. On September 23, the government is scheduled to convene the Regulatory Rationalization Committee to overhaul rules governing semiconductors, robotics, and AI.

The consensus in the Korean tech community is that the era of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi (the former president currently in prison following his impeachment) was characterized by a lack of direction and "stagnant" industrial policy. During the time of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi, many Korean startups complained that while billions were spent on "public data" projects, the actual data was often unusable for real-world training. The Lee Jae-myung government is now attempting to pivot toward a more "results-oriented" approach, focusing on deregulation that allows AI to be integrated into the manufacturing sector—the heart of the Korean economy.

We are also seeing the democratization of AI within Korea. In Taean-gun, a rural coastal county, elderly residents are being trained to use ChatGPT to become "short-form" video creators. Universities like Yeungnam Yihee and local districts like Dobong-gu are launching public lectures on AI. This is the "cultural infrastructure" required for a technological revolution to take hold. When the general population, not just the elite engineers, begins to use AI as a tool for daily life and labor, the industrial transformation becomes irreversible.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the final quarter of the year, several key indicators will determine whether this massive capital injection into AI infrastructure will bear fruit or lead to a "market winter."

  • The Anthropic-Nscale Finalization: Watch for the official signing of the $45 billion contract. If it goes through, it will set a new global benchmark for "sovereign-level" infrastructure spending by a private firm.
  • The September 23 Regulatory Meeting: In Korea, the specific outcomes of President Lee Jae-myung’s Regulatory Rationalization Committee will be crucial. Specifically, look for changes in "Physical AI" safety standards and autonomous robot liability laws, which currently hinder mass deployment.
  • The "Turnbench" Rankings: Keep an eye on Neosapience, a Korean voice-AI startup that recently ranked 3rd globally in the "Turnbench" benchmark, beating out Google and OpenAI in specific categories. This proves that Korean firms can still compete on "technical depth" even if they cannot match the "capital breadth" of U.S. titans.
  • OpenAI’s Security Audit: Following the Saudi spy incident, OpenAI and other major labs will likely undergo massive internal security overhauls. This may slow down the pace of open research as companies "bolt the doors" to protect their intellectual property.

The AI landscape is no longer just a playground for software developers; it is a high-stakes battlefield of capital, hardware, and geopolitics. For the global reader, Korea is the place to watch—not necessarily for the next chatbot, but for the first generation of AI that actually walks, works, and builds in the real world.
