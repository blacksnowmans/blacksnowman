The Yong Hye-in Resignation: A Look Inside the Fractured Power Dynamics of South Korea’s Ruling Party

September 13, 2026

The recent resignation of Yong Hye-in, the nominee for Minister of Gender Equality and Family, has sent shockwaves through the South Korean political landscape. On the surface, it was a routine personnel failure—a candidate stepping down following public outcry. But for those watching the internal mechanics of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal party, this event acts as a flashpoint, illuminating a deep, festering rift between the party’s current power brokers and the reformist wing that helped usher President Lee Jae-myung into office.

To understand why this resignation matters, one must look past the headlines and into the "invisible hand" currently guiding the DPK—a group often referred to as the Chin-Seok, or the pro-Lee Seok-gi faction (a shorthand for the hardline core loyal to the party leadership’s current status quo). This crisis is not just about one nominee; it is about who truly holds the reins of the Lee Jae-myung administration.

The "Chin-Seok" Shadow over the Administration

For two weeks, the political atmosphere in Seoul was suffocating. While President Lee Jae-myung publicly stated he would wait for the parliamentary confirmation hearing process, the DPK’s leadership was working behind the scenes to force Yong’s exit. The pressure became unbearable after polling from firms like Gallup Korea showed that over 60% of the public—particularly younger voters—deemed her unfit for the role, largely due to controversies surrounding her plan to hold a ministerial post while simultaneously serving as a proportional representative legislator.

The symbolic breaking point came when Kim Tae-seon, a top aide to the DPK leader, publicly admitted to pressuring Yong to step down. This move effectively bypassed the President’s own Cabinet selection process. The message was clear: the party leadership, not the President, holds the final say on who is "acceptable" in the current political climate. By forcing this resignation, the Chin-Seok faction signaled that they are willing to sideline even the President’s own picks if they threaten the party’s broader approval ratings.

This reveals a disturbing trend: a vertical power structure where the ruling party dictates the President’s policy and personnel choices. Recent polling from MBC/Korea Research shows that 55% of South Koreans view the relationship between the President’s office and the ruling party as "hierarchically vertical" rather than a collaborative partnership. The Yong incident was likely the first signal that this structure will be used to "tame" any faction within the liberal camp that strays from the leadership’s preferred path.

The Politics of Creating "New Enemies"

Why would a dominant political faction, already in control of the party machinery, risk internal division by purging its own? The answer lies in the peculiar, often self-destructive, nature of South Korean progressive politics. There is a long-standing, cynical adage in the movement: "Conservatives perish through corruption, but liberals perish through division." The idea is that constant internal friction is a necessary byproduct of innovation.

However, the current behavior of the Chin-Seok faction goes beyond healthy debate. It is a strategic effort to consolidate power by labeling reformists—such as high-profile figures like Rhee Cheol-woo or influential commentators like Rhyu Si-min—as "enemies" or "anti-Lee" elements. By creating an internal antagonist, the leadership fuels their base on platforms like YouTube, where outrage generates engagement and keeps supporters mobilized.

The tragedy is that this energy is no longer directed at external rivals or the deep-seated "cartels" of the prosecution and media. Instead, it is aimed at the very reformers who push for the radical changes the administration promised during the election. By casting dissent as "betrayal," the leadership is effectively hollowing out the party’s ideological core, leaving behind a group focused more on maintaining its own status than on actual policy change.

The Dilemma of President Lee Jae-myung

President Lee recently shared a post on social media expressing his belief that "reform is harder than revolution." He argued that while revolutionary zeal is passionate, it often leads to catastrophic overreach, and that true reform must be incremental, procedural, and designed to minimize social pain and resistance.

While this sentiment sounds pragmatic, it has left many of his core supporters deeply disappointed. To those who voted for Lee to dismantle the systemic corruption within the prosecution and the judiciary, this "cautious approach" looks suspiciously like an excuse to maintain the status quo. If a surgeon opens up a patient to remove a tumor and then decides to "just apply disinfectant" because the patient is screaming in pain, is that really surgery? By framing the demands of his own reformist base as "radical agitation," the President risks alienating the very people who carried him to the Blue House.

What to Watch Next

As the dust settles on the Yong Hye-in resignation, the path forward for the Lee Jae-myung administration remains precarious. Here is what to watch for in the coming months:

  • The "Purge" of Reformers: Watch for further marginalization of independent-minded party members in upcoming internal elections. If the leadership continues to label dissenting voices as "anti-Lee," the party’s intellectual base will continue to erode.
  • Policy Stagnation: With the party focused on internal control, look for whether substantive reforms (especially regarding the prosecution and media laws) move forward or continue to be delayed in favor of "procedural" compromises.
  • Youth Sentiment: The youth demographic was instrumental in the current administration’s rise. If the gap between the party’s actions and the public's desire for "fairness" continues to widen, expect significant shifts in polling that could threaten the party's legislative majority.

Ultimately, the administration faces a binary choice: continue to play the game of internal scapegoating and incrementalism, or return to the "tool of the people" promise that defined their initial appeal. Without a pivot toward the latter, the government risks losing the mandate that voters so clearly granted them, leaving behind a legacy of squandered potential.
