The Trojan Horse of Prosecution Reform: Why Korea’s New Legal Plan Faces Backlash

September 08, 2026

For years, the South Korean public has pushed for a structural separation between the power to investigate and the power to prosecute. The goal was simple: to curb the unchecked influence of the Prosecution Service, a powerful institution that has long held both authorities, leading to accusations of selective justice and political bias. Recently, legislation was finally passed to decouple these functions. Yet, the Ministry of Justice’s new organizational proposal for the upcoming "Public Prosecution Office" feels like a slap in the face—a bureaucratic sleight of hand that threatens to undo years of hard-fought progress.

The proposal isn't just a technical document; it is a battle for the soul of the country's legal system. By burying the old structure under new names, critics argue that the government is essentially attempting to resurrect the "prosecutorial cartel" in everything but name. If the aim of reform was to dismantle a monolith, the current plan seems designed to preserve it.

The Illusion of Reform: 7,000 Investigators as a Trojan Horse

At first glance, the Ministry of Justice claims to be slimming down. They propose an organizational cap of 8,412 positions for the new Public Prosecution Office, representing a 21.4% reduction from the status quo. However, once you look past the headlines, the math tells a different story. If you subtract the 1,761 staff members being transferred to the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (a newly established body meant to handle high-level probes), the actual reduction in personnel is a mere 238 people.

Crucially, roughly 7,000 investigative officers—the core human machinery of the old prosecution—are slated to remain within the new Public Prosecution Office. If the law mandates a separation of investigation and prosecution, why is the investigative staff staying put? Critics view this as a "Trojan horse": an empty shell of reform that leaves the Prosecution’s original, massive investigative footprint intact, ready to revert to its old ways the moment political winds shift.

Adding to the controversy is the audacity of the staffing request. The Ministry is actually asking to increase the number of prosecutors by 281. In a system where prosecutors should focus solely on litigation and trial support, logic would dictate a leaner, more efficient office. Instead, this expansion suggests an intent to use the remaining investigative staff to keep a thumb on the scale, reviewing case files from other agencies to maintain a leash on investigations they are no longer legally meant to lead.

The "Judicial Control Unit": Power Under the Guise of Oversight

Perhaps the most baffling element of the proposal is the plan to establish a "Judicial Control Unit" within every regional prosecution office. Its stated purpose: to review "non-indictment" cases. In legal theory, judicial control is a check exercised by the courts to ensure the legality of investigations. However, this proposal creates a scenario where the very body that has been accused of past abuses—and which itself requires oversight—is now effectively positioning itself as the overseer of other law enforcement agencies.

For a group that has long been criticized for "selective investigations" and protecting its own, creating a department to monitor the results of other agencies is seen as an act of extreme hubris. It is, in the eyes of critics, an attempt to bypass the spirit of the law through an administrative order, maintaining control over the entire criminal justice process from a new, self-appointed vantage point.

The Core Contradiction: While the legislature has legally separated investigation from prosecution, the Ministry of Justice is using "enforcement decrees" to tie the two agencies back together with invisible chains. This bypasses the legislative intent and attempts to rule the judicial system through administrative power.

The "Two Roofs, One Family" Dilemma

The danger is compounded by the appointment of personnel. The public concern is that the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency will become an extension of the prosecution, creating a "two roofs, one family" arrangement. This fear has only deepened with the nomination of lawyer Kim Ji-young as the first head of the new agency. Kim has a track record of vocal opposition to the dismantling of the prosecution's investigative powers and was a signatory to statements protesting previous efforts to reform the office.

With an establishment figure at the helm of the "independent" investigative body, the risk of collusion between the Public Prosecution Office and the new agency is high. It threatens to turn the entire reform movement into a cosmetic change, where the same network of people continues to exert influence over the criminal justice system, insulated from meaningful democratic accountability.

What Comes Next?

This is not just a disagreement over office logistics; it is a fundamental debate about the role of the state in law enforcement. International standards in democratic nations generally favor a clear separation of powers to prevent the concentration of authority. Korea’s move to separate these functions is, by global standards, a process of "normalization," not a regression.

What to watch:

  • Legislative Deadlines: The public notice period for this proposal ends on the 9th. Watch for whether the Ministry extends this period under pressure from civic groups and the National Assembly.
  • Personnel Appointments: The confirmation process for the new agency’s leadership will be a major litmus test. Any move to install long-time prosecutors in these roles will likely trigger massive public pushback.
  • Legal Challenges: Expect civil society groups to challenge the legality of these organizational decrees in court, citing the Constitutional Court’s recent rulings that have affirmed the legislature's right to curtail prosecutorial power.

Reform is never a single event; it is a constant, exhausting tug-of-war. If the current proposal proceeds as written, it will likely be viewed as a cynical attempt to prioritize institutional survival over the public’s mandate for a fairer, more transparent legal system. Whether the Ministry of Justice chooses to retreat or double down will define the landscape of Korean justice for years to come.
