Inside the People Power Party’s Double Crisis: A Special Counsel Boycott and a Revolt From Within

November 19, 2026

On November 18, the sharpest political conflict in South Korea was not unfolding between rival parties on the floor of the National Assembly, but inside a single party meeting room. Lawmakers from the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, gathered for a general assembly meant to project resolve against President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party. Instead, according to the Black Snowman political briefing and South Korean media reports covering the day’s events, the party found itself fighting a two-front battle: boycotting a major special counsel bill outside while trying to contain a direct revolt against its own leadership within.

On the surface, the assembly produced a unified headline. Party leaders announced that the PPP would formally refuse to recommend a candidate for what has been dubbed the "Fabricated Indictment" Special Counsel Act, vowing zero cooperation with what they called an unconstitutional law. Yet almost simultaneously, dissident lawmakers inside the party went public with demands for PPP leader Jang Dong-hyuk to resign immediately over allegations of manipulated internal disciplinary proceedings.

For international observers trying to make sense of South Korean politics, the November 18 clash illustrates a deeper dilemma facing the country’s conservative bloc. As the PPP escalates street protests to block the ruling party’s legislative agenda, its own internal power struggle is threatening to undercut its credibility as an effective check on the administration.

Refusing the Special Counsel and Taking the Fight to the Streets

To understand the external battle, it helps to understand how special counsels operate in South Korean politics. When major political controversies arise and the opposition or ruling bloc questions the impartiality of regular prosecutors, lawmakers can pass a special counsel act to appoint an independent investigator. In this case, the PPP’s leadership drew a hard line against the "Fabricated Indictment" Special Counsel Act, arguing that the law itself violates constitutional order.

Before and after the November 18 party assembly, PPP Floor Leader Jung Jeom-sik repeatedly took to the microphone to declare that the party would boycott the recommendation process entirely. Jung framed the bill as an extension of what conservatives derisively label the "Lee Jae-myung Prison Break Act," calling it a "constitutional disgrace." According to Jung, the Lee Jae-myung administration is neglecting pressing public livelihood issues and education concerns in order to keep the opposition under political pressure.

The PPP leadership did not stop at verbal condemnation. As noted in the Black Snowman briefing, party leaders moved into outdoor protest mode, launching a protest march to signal total resistance to the special counsel law. At the same time, the party traded sharp blows with the ruling bloc across several policy fronts:

  • College entrance exam rules: After the government and the ruling Democratic Party announced plans to pursue a 10-year ban on retaking the Suneung—South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam—for anyone caught cheating, the PPP dismissed the proposal as a superficial "band-aid fix" and questioned whether it would actually work.
  • Corporate acquisition legislation: Tension also flared over a mandatory tender offer bill—legislation governing corporate takeovers that had already cleared the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee—with the PPP pushing back against a proposal to raise the mandatory purchase threshold to 75 percent.

Yet as the Black Snowman briefing points out, the PPP’s all-consuming focus on blocking the special counsel bill had an unintended side effect. With party leaders pouring their energy into boycotts and street demonstrations, substantive policy debates and livelihood legislation were pushed to the sidelines, leaving the National Assembly once again staring at legislative gridlock.

Resignation Calls and the "Fabricated Discipline" Scandal

While the PPP leadership tried to keep public attention fixed on the ruling party’s special counsel push, the political atmosphere inside the party was rapidly deteriorating. According to media coverage summarized in the briefing, PPP lawmaker Cho Kyung-tae publicly demanded that party leader Jang Dong-hyuk step down immediately—a striking challenge to a sitting party chief during a high-stakes legislative standoff.

At the heart of the internal uprising is a controversy over "fabricated disciplinary actions." A rival intra-party bloc known as the pro-Han faction pressured Jang to personally clarify allegations that the party’s internal disciplinary system had lost its fairness. Critics within the party have raised suspicions that behind-the-scenes personnel and disciplinary decisions were manipulated, and they have placed Jang at the center of that scrutiny.

South Korean media reports cited in the briefing identify a figure referred to as Professor Choi—described as a close confidant of Jang Dong-hyuk—as the focal point of the fairness dispute. Accusations and explanations surrounding Choi’s role and the legitimacy of the disciplinary process have continued to circulate inside and outside the party, turning what might otherwise look like an administrative dispute into a full-scale battle for control over the PPP’s future direction.

Lawmaker Bae Hyun-jin sharpened the attack by linking the leadership’s external rhetoric directly to its internal troubles. Bae argued that Jang should clean up misconduct inside his own party before speaking loudly about cracking down on cheating in the Suneung. The implication was clear: a party leader embroiled in questions of internal fairness cannot credibly lecture the government on ethics.

Jang Dong-hyuk pushed back aggressively against his internal critics, characterizing the calls for his resignation as an attack by an internal "insurrection-framing faction." He also attempted to pivot back to attacking the president, raising questions about whether President Lee Jae-myung had passed the bar exam. However, as the Black Snowman analysis observes, challenging the president’s legal credentials did little to calm the revolt inside his own ranks, where demands for transparency over the party’s disciplinary process remained unanswered.

When Factional Warfare Drowns Out the Opposition

In a functioning democracy, a strong opposition party serves a vital purpose: scrutinizing government policy, exposing administrative missteps, and presenting voters with realistic alternatives. What stood out on November 18, according to both the Black Snowman briefing and South Korean newspaper editorials, was how thoroughly the PPP’s internal conflict overshadowed its external message.

"Floor Leader Jung Jeom-sik emphasized that media scrutiny and evaluation should serve as an opportunity for politics to reflect on itself. Ironically, the news drawing attention inside and outside the party is not policy or public livelihood, but an internal power struggle."

That irony sits at the core of the PPP’s political dilemma. Even when the party raised legitimate questions about whether a 10-year exam ban is the right deterrent for cheating, or whether a 75 percent mandatory tender offer rule makes sense for capital markets, those arguments were buried beneath headlines about resignation demands and factional infighting.

From the perspective of the briefing’s commentary, aggressive parliamentary tactics and street protests carry weight only when backed by party unity and public trust. When a party appears preoccupied with covering up internal fractures, voters may come to view its hardline opposition against the government not as principled oversight, but as a defensive maneuver to distract from its own chaos. South Korean media editorials cited in the report have similarly criticized the opposition for prioritizing internal power battles while economic and livelihood pressures weigh on ordinary citizens.

For the Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party, a divided opposition presents an obvious tactical opening. Even as the ruling bloc faces its own challenges in steering bills through the legislature, a conservative opposition locked in an internal leadership crisis struggles to mount a coherent counteroffensive or build broad public sympathy for its street protests.

What to Watch Next

The events of November 18 leave several unresolved pressure points that will shape South Korean politics in the coming weeks. Rather than settling the conflict, the PPP’s general assembly exposed just how fragile the balance of power inside the conservative opposition has become. Three concrete developments will indicate where the party—and the broader standoff in the National Assembly—heads next:

  • Jang Dong-hyuk’s response to the disciplinary allegations: Watch whether Jang offers a transparent accounting of the accusations surrounding his confidant, Professor Choi, and the party’s disciplinary process, or whether continued stonewalling prompts Cho Kyung-tae and the pro-Han faction to escalate their campaign for his removal.
  • The trajectory of street protests and parliamentary gridlock: Monitor how long the PPP sustains its outdoor march and total boycott of the "Fabricated Indictment" Special Counsel Act, and whether that confrontation freezes unrelated bipartisan work in the National Assembly.
  • A return to policy substance—or deeper paralysis: Pay close attention to whether conservative lawmakers can re-engage constructively on specific legislative disputes, such as the mandatory tender offer bill and Suneung security reforms, or whether internal factional warfare continues to crowd out policy debate altogether.

Until the People Power Party resolves the trust deficit inside its own walls, its leaders will find it difficult to convince South Korean voters that they are ready to hold the government accountable—let alone offer a credible alternative for the country’s future.
