Inside South Korea’s Judicial Crisis: Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s Reversal and the Deepening Battle Over the Courts

October 26, 2026

When Senior Judge Koo Hoe-keun of the Seoul High Court handed down an appellate acquittal for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, the ruling sent shockwaves through South Korean political and legal circles. For months, allegations surrounding unpaid polling fees, shadowy political brokers, and indirect cash transfers had hung over Seoul City Hall like a dark cloud. A court of first instance had previously convicted the central operative in the scheme, treating off-the-books polling transactions as a severe breach of campaign finance laws. Yet in a single afternoon, the appellate court reversed that momentum, ruling that the complex financial transfers and non-public survey data constituted an unpunishable "private transaction."

To an international audience, the case might look like a localized campaign finance squabble. But in South Korea, where the line between judiciary independence and political maneuvering is currently being redrawn, Mayor Oh’s acquittal has become a flashpoint. It highlights growing friction between the judiciary under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, while exposing deep public skepticism toward municipal governance in the nation's capital.

The Myeong Tae-kyun Scandal: How Indirect Polling Cash Became a 'Private Transaction'

To understand why this verdict ignited such furious debate, one must look at the mechanics of South Korean municipal elections and the influential role of political polling. Oh Se-hoon, a prominent member of the People Power Party—South Korea’s main conservative opposition party—returned to office as Seoul Mayor following a high-stakes campaign. However, accusations soon surfaced that his campaign had benefited from covert, non-public opinion polling conducted by Myeong Tae-kyun, a political broker who has emerged at the center of several political finance controversies.

Prosecutors alleged that money from Oh’s campaign ecosystem was funneled through an intermediary, Kim Han-jeong, before reaching Myeong. This arrangement allegedly allowed the campaign to receive custom polling data while bypassing South Korea's strict Political Funds Act. The trial court viewed the setup as an intentional circumvention of the law, convicting Myeong and detaining him in court.

The Seoul High Court’s Criminal Division 7 turned that logic on its head. Senior Judge Koo—who had previously granted Myeong a controversial bail release on unverified medical grounds prior to the appeal—ruled that because the financial transfers occurred between individuals before they had formed close personal ties, the exchange was merely a personal commercial transaction rather than an illegal campaign contribution. Applying the principle of giving the benefit of the doubt to the accused, the court concluded there was insufficient direct evidence that Mayor Oh himself instructed or acknowledged the payments, despite existing text messages, phone logs, and financial records.

Legal critics argue that this reasoning creates a glaring loophole. By shielding high-profile politicians from liability whenever campaign funds are routed through third-party intermediaries, the ruling risks dismantling core protections designed to keep illicit money out of South Korean elections.

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and Accusations of 'Judicial Omnipotence'

The controversy surrounding Mayor Oh’s acquittal comes at a time of severe institutional strain between the judicial branch and the executive branch. Under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the Supreme Court has faced fierce criticism from legal analysts and ruling party lawmakers who accuse the judiciary of double standards and procedural obstruction.

Central to this conflict are claims of "judicial omnipotence"—a perception that senior judges are asserting authority over democratic processes. Tensions escalated when Chief Justice Cho rejected executive requests regarding Supreme Court justice nominations, a move viewed by supporters of President Lee Jae-myung as an overreach into constitutional prerogatives. Simultaneously, high-profile cases involving figures from the conservative establishment have seen significant delays or lenient outcomes.

A prime example is the ongoing special counsel case involving former Deputy Prime Minister Choi Sang-mok. In February, Choi’s defense team filed a motion to disqualify the presiding judge, Judge Lee Jin-kang. After lower courts rejected the challenge, the re-appeal was submitted to the Supreme Court on May 22. More than five months later, the high court has left the motion unresolved, effectively freezing the trial.

This deliberate pace stands in sharp contrast to other rulings where conservative ex-officials received substantial reductions in sentencing—such as former Justice Minister Park Sung-jae, whose initial sentence was reduced by 16 years on appeal. Observers also point to procedural maneuvers surrounding pending cases involving President Lee Jae-myung, where attempts to assign judges previously involved in conservative acquittals have fueled suspicions of political bias within the court system.

Local Backlash: The $100 Million Hangang Bus Controversy

While Mayor Oh Se-hoon secured a legal reprieve in court, his political standing in Seoul remains on shaky ground. Public dissatisfaction in the capital has centered on one of his flagship municipal initiatives: the "Hangang Bus" project, a high-cost network of passenger ferries along the Han River pitched as both public transit and a tourist feature.

The project has drawn intense scrutiny over inflated feasibility studies, safety risks, and allegations of improper rebate schemes between city officials and private vessel builders. Critics argue that Mayor Oh rushed the project to create a visible legacy for future presidential ambitions, risking public funds on an unnecessary venture.

Public anger over the project is reflected in recent polling data. In recent surveys, 74.2% of telephone-interview respondents (and 56.8% in automated ARS polling) stated that law enforcement agencies must launch an immediate criminal investigation into the Hangang Bus rebate allegations. For a mayor who just escaped campaign finance charges, having nearly three-quarters of surveyed citizens demand a prosecutor-led probe into his main policy project underscores a severe deficit in public trust.

Geopolitical Ripples: Kim Jong-un’s Surprise Trip to Dandong

As South Korea contends with domestic judicial and political turmoil, regional security dynamics on the Korean Peninsula continue to evolve. Over the weekend, North Korean leader Kim Jong-un made an unexpected, single-day trip across the border to Dandong, China, visiting a memorial dedicated to Chinese military participation in the Korean War.

Rather than holding a summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping, Kim met briefly with Wang Huning, the fourth-ranked official in the Chinese Communist Party, before returning to Pyongyang the same day. The quick, unannounced trip broke with traditional multi-day diplomatic protocol. Analysts view the visit as a calculated move by Pyongyang to signal internal stability and reinforce its alliance with Beijing ahead of shifting diplomatic calculations in Washington, particularly regarding a potential second term for Donald Trump.

However, South Korea's capacity to drive regional diplomatic initiatives remains constrained by internal friction. The Lee Jae-myung administration continues to navigate the aftermath of the previous government under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose presidency ended in impeachment and imprisonment, leaving behind a polarized political landscape and an opposition eager to challenge the current government's authority.

What to Watch Next

The converging legal and political disputes in Seoul point to several critical milestones in the coming months:

  • Prosecution Re-Appeal to the Supreme Court: Prosecutors are expected to challenge Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s acquittal, forcing Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s bench to definitively rule on whether third-party transfers count as private transactions under campaign finance law.
  • Legislative Oversight and Judicial Impeachment Motions: National Assembly lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea may pursue formal impeachment motions against senior judges accused of intentionally stalling high-profile trials.
  • Investigations into the Hangang Bus Project: With public demand for an inquiry reaching 74.2%, regulatory bodies and law enforcement face mounting pressure to audit contracts and financial flows behind Seoul's Han River ferry initiative.
  • Movement on Stalled High-Court Trials: Watch whether the Supreme Court resolves long-pending recusal motions, which will determine when delayed special counsel prosecutions can officially resume.
