Beyond the Red Scare: Why the 'New Lee Jae-myung' Coalition Matters More than Radical Factions

November 03, 2026

Whenever a major scandal or a shift in the political winds hits South Korea, the public's attention is often hijacked by the most provocative labels. Currently, the term "Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance"—a name that carries the weight of Cold War-era tension and radical activism—has resurfaced at the center of a fierce political debate. For the casual observer outside Korea, this might seem like a niche internal squabble. However, it represents a fundamental tug-of-war over the identity of the current administration and the future of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's ruling party.

To understand why this name is causing such a stir, one must look past the sensationalism. If critics and the opposition remain fixated on a few specific individuals or ghosts of the past, they risk missing the much larger, more complex structure of power currently governing South Korea. The real story isn't just about a small faction of activists from the 1980s; it is about the "New Lee Jae-myung" coalition—a diverse and powerful alliance that has reshaped the Korean political landscape under President Lee Jae-myung.

The Complex Roots of the 'New Lee Jae-myung' Coalition

In recent political discourse, the term "New Lee Jae-myung" has become a shorthand for the broad power structure supporting the President. This is not a monolith or a single, shadowy organization. Instead, it is a hybrid coalition formed over decades, merging various groups: the "Seongnam faction" (loyalists from the President's time as mayor), established members of the Democratic Party, the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance, and a passionate base of online supporters known as the "Sons of Lee Jae-myung."

This coalition did not appear overnight. It was forged in the trenches of local politics, specifically in Seongnam, a satellite city of Seoul. When President Lee was the Mayor of Seongnam, he orchestrated a pivotal political alliance during the 2010 local elections. At the time, he joined forces with Kim Mi-hee, a candidate from the now-defunct Unified Progressive Party (UPP). This was more than a mere tactical move to consolidate votes; it served as a bridge that allowed many activists from the radical progressive camp to enter mainstream local government administration. This history is the root of the current controversy surrounding the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance.

The Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance itself is a faction within the South Korean progressive movement that traces its origins back to the radical student activism of the late 20th century. Historically, they have been associated with National Liberation (NL) ideologies, which often emphasized anti-imperialism and a complicated stance toward North Korea. For years, conservative critics have used this association to paint the President as being under the influence of "pro-North" radicals.

However, equating the President directly with this faction is a factual oversimplification. While key aides such as Jeong Jin-sang (former senior secretary for political affairs) and Kim Yong have trajectories that intersect with these activist circles, President Lee Jae-myung’s own political identity is more rooted in administrative pragmatism. The relationship is less about shared ideology and more about a strategic, long-term political alliance. By focusing only on the "radical" label, one misses how this group evolved from outsiders to the very heart of the Blue House (the presidential office) and the ruling party.

The Trap of Red-Baiting and the Opposition's Strategic Failure

The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition party, has frequently seized upon these links. Whenever the Lee Jae-myung administration faces a crisis, the PPP summons the "ghosts" of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance or the defunct UPP. Their strategy is clear: frame the current government as an extension of radical, anti-state forces. This is a classic form of "red-baiting," a tactic used for decades in Korean politics to alienate moderate voters by stoking fears of communism or North Korean influence.

But there is a danger in this approach for the opposition. By focusing so heavily on a single, fringe-sounding group, the PPP often falls into a trap of its own making. When the criticism becomes overly focused on the past activism of peripheral figures, the "main event"—the actual performance and policy decisions of the current government—fades into the background. Instead of holding the administration accountable for economic policies or housing issues, the political debate devolves into a messy, ideological shouting match that many younger and moderate voters find exhausting and irrelevant.

"Stop worrying so much about the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance. It’s not the most important thing," some seasoned political analysts argue. This perspective suggests that by chasing after a specific faction, the opposition allows the broader "New Lee Jae-myung" power structure to escape scrutiny.

When the arrows of criticism are aimed only at the "radicals" or specific aides like Jeong Jin-sang, the central figures of power can simply step back and watch the supporting cast take the hits. This allows the administration to maintain its momentum while the opposition looks stuck in the past. For the PPP, the "red-baiting" frame might satisfy their core conservative base, but it rarely deals a substantial blow to the administration’s actual grip on power. In fact, it often gives the ruling party a reason to consolidate their ranks against what they perceive as an "unfair ideological attack."

Why the Entire 'New Lee Jae-myung' Structure Must Be Analyzed

To win a political battle, one must strike the center of the opponent's strategy, not just the supply lines. Fixating on a small faction like the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance is a strategic error if the goal is to provide a genuine political alternative. The real target for critics should be the entirety of the "New Lee Jae-myung" coalition and the way it manages power. This includes examining the perceived hypocrisy of an administration that campaigned on "fairness" but is often accused of insular hiring practices and "back-door" appointments.

The current struggle within the Democratic Party highlights this tension. There is a visible rift between the "Pro-Lee" (Chun-myeong) faction and the "Non-Lee" (Bi-myeong) faction. Figures like Kim Min-seok, a prominent party leader, are often at the center of these internal storms. When the core leadership of the party is challenged, it creates a vacuum that can lead to significant shifts in the political map. For example, some speculate about the return of older figures like former party leader Song Young-gil to head an emergency committee if the current leadership falters. These movements show that the party is currently resting on a fragile internal balance.

The criticism that matters is not about where a person was in 1990, but how they are exercising power in 2024. Critics argue that the "New Lee Jae-myung" era has seen a narrowing of the political circle. Instead of a broad "big tent" approach, the administration is often accused of prioritizing loyalty over expertise, filling key positions with those who have been with the President since his days in Seongnam or Gyeonggi Province. This "insider-only" culture is what truly threatens the long-term health of Korean democracy, regardless of the specific ideological roots of the individuals involved.

  • The Seongnam Connection: Loyalists who have followed Lee from his early mayoral days.
  • The Party Loyalists: Established politicians who moved to Lee's side to maintain power.
  • The Grassroots Base: Highly active online supporters who put immense pressure on "Non-Lee" lawmakers.

If the opposition focuses on the "incompetence" or "hypocrisy" of this entire structure—rather than just the "radicalism" of one part of it—they might find more resonance with the public. To reconstruct a viable opposition, they must prove that the current ruling coalition is a "league of their own" that has lost touch with the needs of the average citizen.

Personnel Appointments: The Mirror of Real Change

In any administration, the clearest indicator of intent is who they hire. In South Korea, "personnel is policy." When a government claims to be pursuing reform and national unity, the public looks at the list of appointments to see if that claim holds water. So far, the Lee Jae-myung administration has faced heavy criticism for what many see as "revolving door" appointments, where the same group of close associates moves from one high-ranking position to another.

The true "litmus test" for change is whether the administration is willing to appoint "Non-Lee" (Bi-myeong) figures to significant roles. To date, however, the trend has been to favor those with proven loyalty. Even when "neutral" figures are brought in, they are often perceived as being there for optics rather than actual influence. This has led to accusations that the administration is building an "exclusive fortress" rather than a representative government.

For the Democratic Party to truly rebuild its image and move beyond the current factional infighting, it must undergo a period of painful self-reflection. Relying on the mistakes of the previous administration—specifically the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached and is currently imprisoned—is no longer enough to maintain public support. While the memory of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's failures remains a powerful tool for the ruling party, it cannot be a permanent shield against their own shortcomings.

True innovation does not come from throwing stones at the opposition or hiding behind old labels. It comes from the courage to cut out the "rotten parts" of one's own power structure. If the Democratic Party remains a hostage to its most extreme factions or its most insular loyalists, it will eventually lose the trust of the very people it claims to represent. The "New Lee Jae-myung" coalition is currently the most powerful force in Korean politics, but like all political structures, it is susceptible to the rot of arrogance and exclusivity.

What to Watch Next

As the political season progresses, there are several concrete developments that will signal which way the wind is blowing:

  • Cabinet Reshuffles: Watch for upcoming appointments in the presidential office. If the President continues to pick from his "Seongnam inner circle," expect the "insider" criticism to intensify. If he reaches out to "Non-Lee" factions, it may signal a genuine attempt at party unity.
  • The Opposition's Rhetoric: Observe whether the People Power Party continues its "Red Scare" tactics regarding the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance. If they shift their focus toward economic policy and governance failures, it could indicate a more effective and threatening strategy for the next election cycle.
  • Internal Party Elections: The battle for the soul of the Democratic Party will be won or lost in the internal elections for leadership positions. The fate of figures like Kim Min-seok will be a key indicator of whether the President’s grip on the party is tightening or loosening.

The name "Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance" makes for a spicy headline, but it is a distraction from the larger structural shifts in Korean power. The real question for the future of South Korea is whether the "New Lee Jae-myung" coalition can transition from a successful political machine into a government that truly serves the broad interests of the nation, or if it will remain a closed circle of loyalists struggling to outrun the ghosts of its own past.
