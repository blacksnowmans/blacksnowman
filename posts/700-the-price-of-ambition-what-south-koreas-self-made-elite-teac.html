The Price of Ambition: What South Korea’s "Self-Made" Elite Teaches Us About Wealth

September 11, 2026

For many South Koreans, the path to financial success feels like an increasingly frantic race. In a society where the cost of living in Seoul is among the highest in the world and economic mobility is increasingly viewed as stagnant, the advice of Ham Ik-byeong—a prominent dermatologist and media figure—has ignited a fiery debate online. At 65, Ham is a multi-millionaire with a net worth exceeding 10 billion won (about $7 million), living in the iconic Tower Palace, a luxury high-rise apartment complex in Seoul’s affluent Gangnam district.

In a recent appearance on the popular YouTube channel "Salaryman Rich People," Ham delivered a message that was less of a success story and more of a cold wake-up call. He argued that most people fail to build wealth not because of a lack of opportunity, but because they are hypocritical: they claim they want to be rich while remaining deathly afraid of the very thing they desire—money. According to Ham, if you want to be wealthy, you must stop "glancing sideways" and look directly at the money.

For a foreign observer, Ham’s blunt rhetoric offers a window into the "crushing" reality of the South Korean capitalistic mindset. It is a philosophy that rejects the modern concept of "work-life balance" in favor of an unrelenting, almost monastic pursuit of asset accumulation. But is this "survival equation" still applicable in a world defined by global uncertainty, or is it a relic of the hyper-growth era that made South Korea an economic miracle?

The Physics of Poverty: Deficit as a Driver

Ham’s theory of wealth is built on a simple, if harsh, premise: the primary fuel for human capacity is "deprivation." Having moved apartments 30 times throughout his life, Ham traces his drive back to a childhood of poverty. When he was establishing his medical practice in the mid-90s—a time when an apartment in the Seoul suburb of Mok-dong cost roughly 18 million won (about $13,000)—he famously delayed his own wedding until he had the funds to secure a home.

He recounts a ten-year period where he worked 365 days a year, skipping every holiday. His quip that his children "knew the face of a dog better than their father's" captures the extreme intensity required to transcend his socioeconomic class. To Ham, the modern youth’s obsession with "work-life balance" is a form of self-sabotage. If you are not willing to endure extreme deficit, you are not truly motivated to escape it.

This perspective is deeply polarizing. Critics argue that such advice ignores the structural barriers faced by today’s generation, who are entering a market where the barrier to entry for housing and investment is exponentially higher than it was in the 1990s. Yet, for those seeking to understand the "hustle culture" that dominates South Korean professional life, Ham’s words serve as a raw reflection of the mentality that built the nation's chaebol-led economy.

The 3-Step Success Formula: Benchmarking the Giants

In a world of infinite choices, Ham suggests that the secret to survival is not to be unique, but to be a master of imitation. He advocates for a three-step formula:

  • Single-Minded Focus: Choose only one priority—money, fame, or power. Trying to chase all three will inevitably lead to failure.
  • Find the Perfect Model: When establishing his dermatology clinic, Ham did not reinvent the wheel. He looked to "Kim & Chang," South Korea’s largest and most successful law firm, to understand how a high-level partnership system functioned.
  • Execute the Template: Ham argues that trying to be "original" is often just a mask for arrogance. If you want to succeed, you should copy the system of the undisputed leader in your field by at least 90%.

This approach highlights a uniquely Korean approach to business: the "fast-follower" strategy applied at the individual level. In this view, the employee who seeks personal "work-life balance" is viewed as a passive cog in a machine, whereas the individual who treats their job as a training ground to eventually "graduate" and start their own venture is the only one truly navigating the capitalist system correctly.

The "Work-Life Balance" Trap

Perhaps the most controversial aspect of Ham’s advice is his blatant disdain for the modern preference for work-life balance (often called "WLB" in Korea). He argues that if you are in your 20s or 30s and prioritizing leisure, you are essentially "burning" your prime asset-building years.

His philosophy on wealth is simple: "Money will set you free." For Ham, wealth is not about buying luxury goods; it is about the "option" to never have to do work you despise. He views the accumulation of capital as a defensive shield against the societal pressures that keep most people stuck in a cycle of commuting and subservience. He challenges parents not to push their children toward academic grades, but to engage them in real-world discussions about how money moves and grows.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea faces a demographic crisis—characterized by a plummeting birth rate and an aging population—the pressure on the 30-40 demographic to maintain this level of "hustle" is causing a quiet, massive social shift.

Moving forward, keep an eye on two specific trends in the Korean market:

  • The Shift from Expansion to Defense: As more people reach middle age in a high-interest-rate environment, we are seeing a move away from "all-in" bets on volatile assets like cryptocurrency and toward conservative, income-generating real estate and traditional savings.
  • The "Exit" Mentality: The increasing rate at which skilled professionals are abandoning corporate "pyramid" jobs for independent contracting or boutique businesses will likely reshape the South Korean labor landscape over the next decade.

Ham Ik-byeong’s advice is not for everyone, and it certainly doesn't account for the systemic inequality that makes his success harder to replicate today. However, it provides an essential, unflinching look at the "survival equation" that drives a significant portion of South Korea's high-achieving population. Whether you view him as a mentor or a symbol of an outdated, grinding era, his influence remains a dominant force in how Koreans think about, earn, and protect their wealth.
