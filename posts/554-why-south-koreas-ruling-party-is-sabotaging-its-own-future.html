Why South Korea's Ruling Party Is Sabotaging Its Own Future

October 01, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is suffering from a strange and frustrating paradox. The progressive camp holds the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung, yet its inner machinery is grinding against itself. Instead of consolidating power to govern effectively, party leaders and factions appear determined to fracture their own base. To an outside observer, it might look like standard political squabbling, but the stakes here are existential for South Korea's current administration. With the crucial 2028 legislative elections looming—an event that will essentially serve as a referendum on the Lee Jae-myung government—the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is trapped in a cycle of self-inflicted wounds, factional infighting, and deepening alienation from its own core supporters.

The Looming Shadow of the 2028 General Election

In South Korean politics, legislative elections midway through a presidential term act as an unsparing report card. The 2028 general election will decide whether the Lee Jae-myung administration retains the political leverage to push through its agenda or becomes a lame duck. For the Democratic Party—the primary progressive political force in South Korea—the path to success should be straightforward: maintain strict risk management, protect the government's baseline of support, and deliver tangible legislative and economic results over the next year.

Instead, the party leadership has pursued policies that narrow and fragment its political coalition. Rather than projecting the confidence and broad-tent responsibility expected of a ruling party, elements within the Democratic Party have indulged in exclusionary gatekeeping. Critics argue that if the party continues to alienate moderates and splinter its own ranks, it is sleepwalking toward a collective political disaster.

Factional Fault Lines and the Rise of the Rebuilding Coalition

The internal friction within the progressive bloc largely stems from lingering bitterness between factions loyal to the current leadership and those aligned with former President Moon Jae-in—the classic "pro-Lee versus pro-Moon" divide. Despite direct appeals from the president to tone down internal hostility and drop derogatory factional labels, the bad blood persists.

This dynamic has manifested in aggressive campaigns targeting figures associated with the previous Moon administration, effectively shrinking the party’s tent from within. When a ruling party spends its energy purging past allies and weaponizing internal suspicion, it forfeits the broad coalition needed to govern. Compounding these tensions are murky controversies over potential party mergers, allegations of fabricated backroom deals regarding leadership succession, and unhealed wounds from botched local election strategies—such as the refusal to pursue unified candidacies in key districts like Pyeongtaek. These missteps have left rank-and-file party members terrified that local failures will become a national template.

The Rebuilding Role of Minor Allies and Disenchanted Party Members

As frustration with the Democratic Party mounts, an ironic shift is occurring within the progressive electorate. Disillusioned party members—known as core partisans who pay regular dues—are increasingly looking outside the main party for straight-talking accountability. Many are turning their attention and sympathy toward the Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller progressive minor party that has stepped into the role of a political gadfly.

While some mainstream commentators dismiss minor progressive parties as disruptive, grassroots supporters view them differently. On the ground, many committed progressives argue that when the Democratic Party becomes too cautious or arrogant to criticize its own missteps, smaller allies must step up to deliver harsh truths. The fear driving this movement is simple: if the Lee Jae-myung government fails in 2028 and power eventually reverts to the conservative People Power Party, it spells disaster not just for the Democratic Party, but for the entire progressive movement.

"If a ruling party refuses to listen to internal dissent and demands blind obedience, it creates the very conditions for its own defeat."

What to Watch Next

As South Korean politics moves toward the next electoral showdowns, several concrete developments will indicate whether the progressive camp can heal its internal fractures or continue its downward slide:

  • Upcoming By-Elections: Keep an eye on upcoming local races, particularly the fiercely contested Seoul mayoral by-election. Polling shifts in the capital will signal whether public fatigue with ruling party infighting has hardened into a permanent loss of voter trust.
  • Electoral Reform Debates: Watch how the major parties handle the ongoing discussions surrounding South Korea's semi-mixed member proportional representation system, and whether they abandon past tactical maneuvers like satellite parties.
  • Intra-Party Realignments: Observe whether mainstream Democratic Party leadership responds to grassroots pressure from dues-paying members, or if defections and shifts in allegiance toward smaller progressive entities like the Rebuilding Korea Party accelerate.
