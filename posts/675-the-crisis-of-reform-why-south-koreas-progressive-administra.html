The Crisis of Reform: Why South Korea’s Progressive Administration is at a Breaking Point

October 04, 2026

For any administration, the term "do-or-die"—or sajeuksaeng in Korean, a historical idiom meaning "one finds life only by seeking death"—is usually a rhetorical flourish. Today, however, it is the defining mood of South Korean politics. Looking at the current state of governance, many observers are pointing to the limits of the administration’s reform agenda. The frustration and disillusionment among its core supporters are no longer quiet murmurs; they have become a deafening signal that the government is in crisis. As economist Choi Bae-geun argues, this instability is not the result of external pressures, but of internal contradictions and a systemic avoidance of the very reforms that brought the current government to power.

The Cracks in the 'Myeong-Seok' Alliance

At the center of this tension is what observers call the "Myeong-Seok" alliance—a political coalition predicated on "cause" (myeongbun) and "pragmatism" (silri). However, this alliance is fracturing under the weight of internal power dynamics. Kim Min-seok, a prominent figure and leader within the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal opposition party), has faced intense criticism for his maneuvers to consolidate power. Detractors argue that his focus on sidelining rivals and his use of divisive rhetoric—such as the "Shincheonji" frame (referencing a controversial religious group often used as a political bogeyman in Korea)—has undermined the unity of the democratic reform bloc.

While the President has recently acknowledged a lack of respect toward the public, the gesture is largely viewed as a superficial attempt to appease his core base. There is a growing suspicion that Kim Min-seok’s vision for "grand integration" is less about unity and more about eliminating competition. This creates a structural conflict: Kim believes the base is tied to him, while the President struggles to balance his own political survival with the demands of a base that feels increasingly alienated. If the administration does not pivot to address these core demands immediately, this internal division could lead to political obsolescence.

The Rhetoric of Reform vs. The Reality of Resistance

The President has often stated, "Reform without resistance or conflict is not reform." Yet, in practice, the administration has consistently distanced itself from substantive reform, citing the very "resistance and pain" that the President once claimed were essential. During an August Cabinet meeting, he noted that while clear-cut reform is popular, it increases resistance and societal pain. To many, this was a confession: the administration is too afraid of the backlash from entrenched interests to push through meaningful change.

This hesitation was further highlighted during the President’s state visit to France in September. Despite invoking the French Revolution, the President cautioned that "excessive reform leads to a backlash." For a reform-minded administration, this feels like an echo of the logic used by previous conservative governments to justify inaction. True reform is inherently painful—it is a process of "stripping the skin," as the Korean saying goes. By avoiding that pain, the administration risks losing the mandate given by the voters who elected them to dismantle the status quo.

The Imperative of Democratic Sovereignty

At its foundation, this administration's legitimacy is tied to its promise to restore constitutional order and realize popular sovereignty. A critical component of this is judicial reform, specifically curbing the outsized power of the prosecution, which has long been accused of weaponizing its authority for political ends. If the administration cannot check the power of the prosecution, its other policy goals will remain hollow.

Furthermore, the leadership’s reaction to its own supporters has been jarring. When the President remarked that he "never expected to hear such things in the remainder of his life" regarding constituent criticism, it revealed a disconnect. By dismissing the cries of supporters as "radical agitation," the government has stifled the very communication that keeps a democracy healthy. A political leader's true "clarity" is found in their ability to empathize with the pain of the vulnerable, not in their ability to ignore it.

What to Watch Next

The administration is currently at a turning point. The upcoming period will determine whether it chooses the path of comfortable decay or the grueling, necessary work of genuine structural reform. To watch this situation unfold, keep an eye on three key indicators:

  • Cabinet Appointments: How the administration handles controversial appointments, such as the vetting of Justice Minister candidates, will signal whether they are still relying on old-school political maneuvering or shifting toward transparency.
  • Policy Prioritization: Watch for whether the government shifts its focus toward tangible, public-facing reforms that directly address economic hardship, rather than internal party power struggles.
  • Communication Style: Whether the President continues to characterize internal criticism as "radical" or begins to engage with the reform bloc’s demands will be the litmus test for whether a reconciliation with their base is even possible.

The core issue remains: does the administration have the courage to face the "do-or-die" moment? If they continue to fear the resistance of the establishment, they will lose the very mandate that brought them to power. The time for flowery rhetoric has passed; the public is waiting for action.
