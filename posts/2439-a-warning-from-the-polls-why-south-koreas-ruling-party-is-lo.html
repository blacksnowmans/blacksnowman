A Warning from the Polls: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Losing Its Grip

November 17, 2026

The mood inside the Blue House and the headquarters of the Democratic Party, South Korea’s liberal ruling party, has grown increasingly somber this November. For any political administration, the second week of the month usually marks a time for consolidating year-end policy goals. Instead, the leadership is staring at a set of numbers that signal a profound crisis of confidence. According to the latest tracking polls, the Democratic Party's approval rating has plummeted to 27.2%, while the People Power Party (PPP), the country’s main conservative opposition, has surged to 41.5%. For a ruling party that prides itself on representing the "will of the people," falling into the 20% range—and trailing the opposition by double digits—is more than a fluctuation; it is a structural emergency.

The situation for President Lee Jae-myung is equally precarious. His job performance approval rating has hit a post-inauguration low of 35.0%, with a staggering 61.9% of respondents expressing disapproval. Perhaps most concerning for the administration is the intensity of this dissatisfaction: more than half of those polled say the president is doing "very poorly." In the high-stakes theater of Korean politics, these figures represent a "dead cross," where the negative sentiment is not just higher than the positive, but is beginning to calcify into a wall of public resistance.

To understand why this matters, one must look beyond the raw percentages. For an international observer, Korean polls can seem volatile, but these specific shifts reveal a fracturing of the progressive coalition that brought President Lee to power. When a ruling party’s core base—specifically those in their 40s and residents of the traditional liberal stronghold of the Honam region—begins to look elsewhere, the very foundation of the government’s mandate is at risk. This is not merely a story about unpopular policies; it is a debate about the soul and direction of the Lee Jae-myung administration.

The Methodology Gap and the Reality of Public Sentiment

In South Korean politics, not all polls are created equal, and the discrepancy between survey methods has become a major talking point for political analysts. The recent 27.2% figure comes from surveys utilizing Automated Response Systems (ARS), which often capture more polarized and reactive sentiments. Experts like Kim Jong-won, CEO of the polling firm EveryResearch, note that ARS surveys tend to reflect the views of highly engaged voters who are more likely to express immediate frustration or "shy" away from responding if they feel their side is failing.

In contrast, telephone interview surveys (CATI) conducted by organizations like Gallup Korea or Embrain Public have occasionally shown the Democratic Party maintaining a lead or a closer margin. For example, in previous weeks, while ARS polls showed a slump, CATI polls placed the Democratic Party in the high 30s. This gap suggests that while the "passive" public might still be leaning liberal, the "active" and vocal public is turning sharply against the government. When the ARS numbers drop this low, it serves as a leading indicator that the government’s "energy"—its ability to mobilize supporters and dominate the national narrative—is evaporating.

The reasons for this decline are multi-faceted. Voters cite failures in real estate policy in the capital region, the rising cost of living that hurts the working class, and a perceived lack of fairness in high-level government appointments. In South Korea, the "fairness" (gongjeong) narrative is a potent political weapon. If a liberal government, which campaigned on equity, is seen as repeating the cronyism of the past, the disillusionment among swing voters is swift and punishing. This "disillusionment effect" is currently manifesting as a "shy" phenomenon, where traditional liberal voters simply stop answering the phone or tell pollsters they are "undecided," effectively removing the floor from the party’s support levels.

Rhyu Si-min’s Warning: Trust, Direction, and Policy

Providing the most incisive—and controversial—analysis of this trend is Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal commentator and former cabinet minister. Rhyu argues that the administration is suffering from a simultaneous breakdown in three critical areas: trust in the person, loyalty to the political line, and the validity of policy. "To understand what the data is saying," Rhyu explains, "you need a framework. You can't just look at the numbers."

Rhyu’s framework suggests that President Lee Jae-myung was not elected because of individual charisma alone, but because he was the standard-bearer for a decades-old progressive "core." In his view, a Korean president’s power comes from the "camp," not the individual. He points out that President Lee’s election victory with 49.1% of the vote was remarkably similar to the late President Roh Moo-hyun’s 48%—a sign that there is a fixed "core" of liberal voters that stays consistent regardless of the candidate.

The current danger, Rhyu warns, is that the administration is "dismantling" the very camp that built it. He draws a sharp parallel to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently imprisoned. Rhyu argues that Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol failed because he alienated his own conservative base shortly after taking office, pushing aside allies like Lee Jun-seok and destroying the coalition that enabled his victory. Rhyu fears the Lee administration is making a similar mistake by attempting to "reconstruct" the party for centrist appeal rather than "extending" the existing progressive house. When the "core" feels abandoned or sees the government wavering on key reforms—such as stripping the prosecution of its investigative powers—they don't just get angry; they withdraw their support, leaving the president vulnerable.

The Rise of the 'Lifeboat' Party

The most tangible evidence of this "core" flight is the sudden rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP), a minor progressive party that has seen its support jump to 10.1% nationally and nearly 15% in the Honam region. This 10% threshold is a psychological milestone in Korean politics. It signals that the RKP is no longer a fringe group but a legitimate alternative for disgruntled progressives.

Rhyu Si-min describes the RKP as a "lifeboat" for the liberal camp. According to survey data, over 90% of RKP supporters hold a negative view of the current Lee Jae-myung administration’s performance. Rhyu argues these aren't people who have switched to the conservative opposition; they are loyal progressives who are using the RKP to send a protest message to the Blue House. "They are saying, 'I'm disappointed in the Democratic Party,' so they pick up the phone and say they support the RKP," Rhyu explains.

This internal migration has sparked a heated debate within the ruling party. Senior Democratic Party lawmakers, such as Kim Min-seok and Kim Young-jin, have pushed back against Rhyu’s "camp-first" rhetoric. Kim Young-jin, a key ally of the president, criticized Rhyu for "throwing grenades into the barracks" and causing internal strife when the party should be focused on unity. These lawmakers argue that for the government to succeed, it must move beyond its base and govern for the entire country, including moderates and conservatives. They see Rhyu’s demand for "purity" and "hardline reform" as a recipe for isolation. This tension—between those who want to double down on the progressive base and those who want to move to the center—is the defining conflict of the Lee administration’s current term.

The 2028 Horizon and the Weight of History

While the next general election is not until 2028, the current polling suggests a grim outlook for the Democratic Party if the trend continues. Rhyu Si-min warns that the opposition People Power Party is currently in a state of flux, but if they manage to form a "One Team" coalition—uniting figures like Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, former party leader Han Dong-hoon, and the influential reformist lawmaker Lee Jun-seok—the Democratic Party will likely face a crushing defeat. The current 41.5% support for the PPP is a warning of what happens when the conservative base senses a weakness in the ruling government.

Beyond the electoral mechanics, there is a heavier historical burden at play. Rhyu Si-min frames the success of the Lee Jae-myung government as a matter of historical duty. He notes that the Korean public has engaged in two "civil revolutions" in recent years: the 2016-17 Candlelight Revolution that ousted Park Geun-hye, and the "Light Revolution" that ended the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Both times, the public entrusted the Democratic Party with the task of fixing the country’s institutions.

"If the Lee Jae-myung administration fails, it is not just the failure of one government," Rhyu warns. "It is a betrayal of two civil revolutions."

The stakes, therefore, are existential. If the public perceives that a second liberal administration in a row has failed to deliver on economic stability and social fairness, the "progressive brand" in South Korea may be damaged for a generation. The 27.2% poll rating is not just a number; it is a ticking clock.

What to Watch Next

As the administration attempts to navigate this low point, several key indicators will determine if they can stage a recovery or if the slide will continue:

  • Personnel Changes: Watch for a cabinet reshuffle or changes in the presidential staff. If the President appoints figures that satisfy the "core" base’s demand for reform, or conversely, figures that signal a genuine olive branch to the center, it could shift the polls.
  • The RKP-DP Relationship: Whether the Democratic Party attempts to absorb the Rebuilding Korea Party or enters into a more hostile rivalry will determine if the liberal vote remains split or reunifies.
  • Economic Deliverables: The government is under immense pressure to produce "tangible results" in housing prices and inflation. If the winter months bring more economic hardship for the working class, the floor of 27% could drop even further.
  • Conservative Consolidation: Keep an eye on the internal power struggles within the People Power Party. If the opposition successfully bridges the gap between its traditional elders and its younger, more moderate wing (led by figures like Lee Jun-seok), the pressure on the Blue House will become overwhelming.
