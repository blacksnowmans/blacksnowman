The Erosion of Trust: President Lee Jae-myung and the Crisis of Internal Politics

October 13, 2026

To watch the current South Korean political landscape is to witness a paradox. In an era where the administration promised a fundamental "separation of investigation and prosecution"—the first such institutional overhaul in 78 years—the atmosphere feels less like a democratic evolution and more like a spiraling cycle of internal repression. As President Lee Jae-myung faces slipping approval ratings, a single comment from a nominee for a powerful new agency has ignited a firestorm, raising questions about whether the reform spirit has been hollowed out from within.

The situation centers on the newly proposed Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA), an organization born from the Democratic Party’s—the ruling party and President Lee’s base—long-standing mandate to reform the nation’s legal system. Yet, as the ink dries on these ambitious plans, the reality on the ground feels uncomfortably familiar to those who remember the past.

The MCIA Nominee and the Shadow of Internal Purges

The controversy began when Kim Ji-yong, the nominee for the inaugural Commissioner of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency, submitted a written response to the National Assembly. In his reply to the People Power Party—the main conservative opposition—he signaled a hardline stance toward internal dissent, specifically targeting Lim Eun-jeong, a regional head within the agency known for her outspoken criticism of the legal establishment.

Kim’s pledge to "firmly establish organizational discipline" by enforcing strict adherence to the law is being interpreted as a prelude to disciplinary action against Lim. For observers, this is a grim signal. If an agency created under the banner of "prosecution reform" is used primarily to silence internal whistleblowers, then the mission of reform has arguably failed. It suggests that while the signboards have been changed, the old mechanisms of power and the desire to control dissent remain firmly intact. If 78 years of institutional transition results only in the suppression of internal critique, one must ask: what was the point of the reform in the first place?

The Rise of "New Lee Jae-myung" Factions

The fear among analysts is that this is not an isolated incident. There is a growing anxiety that the administration, or rather, the radical fringes claiming the mantle of "New Lee Jae-myung" loyalists, is drifting toward a style of "political engineering" that feels hauntingly similar to the darker chapters of recent history—specifically the attempts to target public figures like writer Rhyu Si-min during past legal controversies.

When an administration’s most ardent supporters begin to act as a digital phalanx, labeling opponents as enemies of the state, it creates a toxic environment. If the President himself does not distance his office from these "New Lee Jae-myung" factions, he risks alienating the moderate voters who propelled him to victory. The tragedy of modern politics is often that a government’s most dangerous enemies are not the opposition parties across the aisle, but the arrogance that festers within its own inner circle.

Three Necessary Steps for Presidential Reset

If President Lee hopes to stabilize his sliding approval ratings—which some fear could drop into the 20 percent range—he must pivot away from the path of emotional governance. Three specific actions are required to restore public trust:

  • Cut ties with extremist factions: The President must clearly and publicly disassociate himself from figures like Representative Kim Min-seok and others who use "New Lee Jae-myung" rhetoric to bully and silence moderate supporters. Equivocating between the aggressors and the victims is not "unity"—it is a failure of leadership.
  • Step back from the smartphone: The spectacle of a head of state doom-scrolling through X (formerly Twitter) or YouTube to engage in comment-section wars is incompatible with the dignity of the office. The President’s time is a public asset, not a private toy to be used in digital spats. He must return to the substantive, expert-driven policymaking that defined his earlier career.
  • Embrace professional communication: Official government channels should be used for policy and public reassurance, not as an outlet for personal frustration. When the public has to nervously anticipate which journalist or critic the President will target next on social media, the government’s message is lost in the noise of its own anger.

The Courage to Say "I Was Wrong"

Ultimately, the public does not abandon a leader simply for making mistakes; they abandon a leader who refuses to acknowledge them. The "magic" of political recovery is often found in the simplest of phrases: "I made a bad judgment," "I could not keep this promise," and "I will correct it now."

If President Lee continues to force his supporters to justify every failure, and if he continues to re-appoint figures who resemble the very status quo he promised to dismantle, he will find the foundation of his presidency eroding beneath him. True courage is not found in fighting one’s past statements or doubling down on flawed appointments to save face; it is found in the ability to pivot when the public interest demands it.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the confirmation hearings for the MCIA leadership. If the administration proceeds with a hardline, punitive approach toward internal critics like Lim Eun-jeong, it will serve as a bellwether for the rest of Lee’s term. A government that prioritizes loyalty over integrity will find its path to the next election treacherous; a government that listens to the "stop" signs from its own base, however, may still find a way to navigate back to center.
