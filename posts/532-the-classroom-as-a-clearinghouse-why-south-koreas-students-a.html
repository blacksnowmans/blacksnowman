The Classroom as a Clearinghouse: Why South Korea’s Students Are Abandoning Public Schools

September 10, 2026

In a typical South Korean classroom, the sound of chalk hitting a blackboard is rarely the sound of discovery. For many students, it is the sound of a countdown to their own academic obsolescence. It has been a long time since schools functioned as places of genuine learning; instead, they have devolved into high-stakes testing grounds where students are sorted and discarded based on their preparation—or lack thereof—in the private sector.

One student’s chilling confession, "I have never once dozed off in class in my life," serves as a grim indicator of the pressure cooker that is the modern Korean high school. When students who take meticulous notes and study diligently still cannot improve their grades, the school ceases to be a temple of knowledge and becomes a cold market measuring the efficiency of capital investment.

The Illusion of Public Education in an Age of Private Tutoring

For many, the transition to high school is not merely challenging; it is a source of profound despair. In South Korea, it is considered the "norm" for students to arrive at high school having already completed the first year's curriculum, often venturing into second and third-year material via hagwons (private after-school academies). Students who enter school without this "head start" find themselves in an alien landscape where their peers are already performing advanced applications of concepts they have yet to even encounter.

The pedagogy of the public classroom has adapted to this reality, but to the detriment of the majority. Teachers often calibrate their lessons to the top-tier students who have already mastered the material in private academies. If one or two high-performing students respond to a question, the teacher moves on immediately, leaving the rest of the class to feel invisible. Students have reported incidents where teachers, when asked about skipped material, dismissively reply, "You all know this already, so we’re skipping it." In an environment where admitting ignorance is a social death sentence, the classroom becomes a theater of performance rather than a place of inquiry.

Ultimately, the school acts less as an educator and more as a "stamping office" to verify who has invested the most in private education. The tacit belief that a student must be ready to solve university entrance exam-level problems by the second year of high school effectively denies opportunity to any student who tries to begin their serious studies once they reach the high school gates.

The "Fairness" Myth: When Education Becomes a Metric of Wealth

The recent push to expand Jeongsi—the university admissions track based almost entirely on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT)—was marketed as a move toward greater fairness. However, the data suggests otherwise. Because the CSAT is a high-stakes exam where a single point can determine one's future, it has become the ultimate playground for those with the financial resources to purchase an advantage.

Research from the Korean Educational Development Institute (KEDI) highlights a stark reality: students from the highest income quintile are far more likely to take a "gap year" to study for the entrance exam (an N-su student, or someone who studies for multiple years to improve their score) compared to their lower-income peers. In this "desert of exams," where money equals skill, families without the means to pay for elite, managed prep academies are effectively left behind.

The cost of pursuing education outside the school system is exorbitant. A student studying independently for the CSAT might spend over 1.6 million won (about $1,200) annually on online lecture passes and textbooks—a significant burden for low-income families. Meanwhile, the wealthy purchase peace of mind through "all-inclusive" private boarding academies that cost millions of won per month. As one student noted, "I don't have the confidence to proudly claim that this result was achieved through my own effort alone," highlighting how the commodification of education strips students of their very sense of self-worth.

The Choice to Quit: Is Dropping Out a Strategy for Survival?

Across the country, and particularly in wealthy districts like Gangnam, high school dropout rates are rising. For many students, dropping out is not an act of rebellion, but a calculated survival strategy. If the school exists only to prepare students for the university entrance exam, and if the school is failing to provide that preparation effectively, students are increasingly concluding that the building is redundant.

However, this trend has hit a wall. Major universities have recently increased the weight of "school life records" (internal grades) even in the CSAT-based Jeongsi process. This has created a "trap" for students: they are forced to remain in a classroom they despise because leaving would disqualify them from their target universities. It is a cynical loop that ensures students remain tethered to an institution that they no longer find useful, effectively holding their futures hostage to keep attendance numbers up.

What to Watch Next: The Call for Structural Reform

The tragedy of the current system is not that students are struggling, but that the state has outsourced the responsibility of education to the private market. Programs like the "Dream Tree" center in Iksan—which provides public, community-based mentorship and study support for out-of-school youth—offer a glimpse of what a more humane system could look like. It proves that with minimal public investment, the cycle of isolation can be broken.

To watch moving forward, keep an eye on:

  • Policy Shifts on Internal Grades: How universities balance the inclusion of internal school records in entrance exams will directly impact the dropout rate. If the state continues to use school records as a "leash" to keep students in the classroom, expect higher levels of student burnout and resentment.
  • Public Investment in "Out-of-School" Support: There is growing pressure to move beyond the punitive view of students who leave the public system. Look for debates regarding whether the government will finally provide direct financial and academic support for independent learners, rather than merely encouraging them to return to a failing system.
  • The "N-su" Divide: As the socioeconomic gap in university admissions continues to widen, political parties will face mounting pressure to address the "private education cartel." Any policy that does not address the fundamental reliance on the CSAT—and the private tutoring that supports it—will likely be viewed by the public as a temporary patch rather than a solution.

The ultimate question is whether South Korea can transition from a system that produces winners and losers to one that fosters curiosity. Until then, the classroom will continue to be a place that many students spend their energy trying to leave.
