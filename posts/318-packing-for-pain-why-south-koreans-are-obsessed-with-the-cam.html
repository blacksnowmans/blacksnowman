Packing for Pain: Why South Koreans Are Obsessed With the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Scroll through Instagram feeds in Seoul right now, and you will inevitably land on a photo of a rugged stone path, a yellow scallop shell arrow, or a weary pair of hiking boots. For outsiders, the Camino de Santiago—the ancient pilgrimage network traversing Europe and ending at the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela in northwestern Spain—might look like a romantic European sabbatical. In South Korea, where the trail has exploded into a massive cultural phenomenon with participants ranking among the top nationalities from Asia, it is viewed through a much starker lens: a grueling, voluntary march against one's own physical and mental limits.

The obsession is not treated as a casual vacation trend. Across Korean social media and YouTube, meticulous preparation videos for the Camino routinely rack up views, revealing an intense psychological cocktail of dread and ambition. To understand why thousands of South Koreans are voluntarily packing up their lives to walk hundreds of kilometers across Spain, you have to look at what they are stuffing into their backpacks—and what they are trying to escape.

The Physics of Suffering: Packing the Backpack

Ask anyone who has completed the Camino, and they will tell you the golden rule of the trail: weight is pain. Veteran Korean travelers swap horror stories of buying heavy 1.5-kilogram (3.3 lbs) sleeping bags only to ditch them at the first albergue (the communal pilgrim hostels) because their shoulders simply refused to cooperate. Every single gram carried over an 800-kilometer (approx. 500 miles) trek translates directly to physical agony.

Strategic gear selection borders on an exact science among Korean planners. Choosing a mid-sized 35-liter backpack equipped with an integrated rain cover—often budget-friendly models priced around 48,500 won (about $35)—is considered a vital defensive move. When walking through unpredictable Spanish weather, a soaked pack turns into dead weight, and moisture management becomes non-negotiable. Similarly, a lightweight personal sleeping bag serves a dual purpose: fighting off the sharp spring temperature drops in rural Spain and acting as a protective barrier against bedbugs in shared dormitories. Cutting down on equipment is necessary, but stripping away core survival items is a rookie mistake.

Clothing strategy relies entirely on minimalism and laundry rotation. Instead of packing a separate outfit for every day, experienced travelers pack light, bring a portable washing mesh net, and wash clothes nightly. Technical outdoor wear is blended with versatile everyday items—like breathable leggings or packable down jackets—to maximize utility. Footwear is treated with even greater reverence: combining specialized toe socks with thick hiking socks is a widely shared tactical secret designed to stave off the inevitable blisters.

Survival Essentials Under the Spanish Sun

Walking beneath the punishing glare of the Iberian sun demands more than just casual skincare; it requires a defensive posture. Heavy-duty sunscreen, wide-brimmed hiking hats, and UV-blocking gloves are treated as vital survival gear rather than beauty accessories. Because safety and efficiency often require pilgrims to start walking well before dawn, headlamps are equally non-negotiable for navigating pitch-black rural paths.

Shared spaces also demand coping mechanisms. Albergues are notoriously noisy, making earplugs an essential tool for preserving what little sanity remains after a 30-kilometer day. Meanwhile, medical kits are packed with an anxious thoroughness. Pain relief patches, antiseptic wipes, painkillers, and heavy-duty muscle relaxants fill individual pouches. Many first-timers confess that their medicine kits weigh far more than they intended, yet letting go of that safety net brings too much psychological panic. On a trail where minor foot pain or ignored blisters regularly force promising journeys to an abrupt halt, over-preparation is viewed as the only sensible insurance policy.

Bridging Two Worlds: The Camino Meets the European Holiday

A uniquely practical element of many South Korean itineraries is the dual-purpose trip: tackling the grueling Camino first, followed by a conventional European vacation in cities or resort areas like Switzerland. This requires a masterclass in luggage logistics. Travelers often check a massive 28-inch suitcase filled with emergency comforts—instant rice bowls, dried seaweed soup, spicy kimchi, and instant noodles—stored at a logistics hub or left behind during the hike.

By the time a traveler reaches the final leg of a European journey, physical exhaustion and homesickness frequently collide. Having a stash of familiar food and a portable travel cooker provides an unmatched psychological anchor. This stark contrast plays out in the wardrobe as well. The high-performance windbreakers and moisture-wicking hiking pants worn on the trail are unceremoniously swapped for stylish dresses and denim once the pilgrimage concludes, utilizing packing cubes to compress both lives into manageable modules.

The Romance of Hardship vs. The Reality of Safety

While the cultural narrative around the Camino in South Korea often frames it as a spiritual awakening or a definitive "turning point in life," seasoned travelers increasingly push back against reckless romanticism. Blindly chasing a completion certificate without adequate physical preparation frequently leads to disastrous outcomes, including severe dehydration, navigation errors, and debilitating injuries.

Statistically, while Korean participation continues to rise, so do reports of accidents, injuries, and safety issues along remote stretches like the Pyrenees. The intense, highly structured pre-trip planning seen online is a direct reaction to these risks. The underlying consensus among experienced pilgrims is simple: the trail is an endurance test, not a war zone. Your body is your only vehicle, and listening to its limits is far more important than forcing a finish.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's outbound travel sector continues to recover and mature, expect the demographic profile of Camino-goers to shift further away from traditional gap-year backpackers toward burned-out urban professionals seeking structured disconnection.

  • Look for shifts in travel gear trends: Watch whether ultra-lightweight backpacking gear brands gain further market share in South Korea as consumers prioritize millimeter-level weight savings.
  • Monitor travel insurance and safety services: Keep an eye on specialized medical and emergency evacuation insurance packages tailored specifically for long-distance European walking trails marketed toward Korean consumers.
  • Observe local community responses: Pay attention to online travel communities and forums in Korea for emerging safety warnings regarding specific high-risk stretches of the French and Northern routes.

Ultimately, the weight in a pilgrim's backpack is not just measured in kilograms. It represents a deliberate choice to step away from a hyper-connected, hyper-competitive society in Seoul and trade it for a life boiled down to two simple questions: How far will I walk today, and where will I sleep tonight?
