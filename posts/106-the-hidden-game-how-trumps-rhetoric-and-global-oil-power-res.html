The Hidden Game: How Trump’s Rhetoric and Global Oil Power Reshape Korea’s Future

September 28, 2026

Watching the evening news these days feels less like a summary of global events and more like a high-stakes black comedy. One day, world leaders are locked in bitter, public hostility; the next, they are shaking hands before the cameras as if the previous day’s threats never occurred. Behind the scenes, the frantic scramble to secure national interests continues unabated. When you peel back the layers of the complex US-China relationship and the shifting diplomatic tides between South Korea, the U.S., and Japan, one stark reality emerges: international politics is not about sentiment—it is a brutal, cold-blooded calculation of interests.

A recent, bizarre moment at the White House perfectly encapsulated this theater. During a welcoming ceremony for a U.S.-China summit, President Donald Trump referred to China as a "World War II ally," invoking the "Flying Tigers"—an American volunteer air force unit that fought for China against Japan in the 1940s. The remark was more than a historical irony; it left audiences bewildered and experts scrambling to decipher the underlying strategy. What is truly being played out on this global stage?

Trump’s Strategic Lip Service and the Reality of Power

Trump’s sudden attempt to frame China as an old friend is far too calculated to be a mere gaffe. According to analysts, this is a classic Trumpian transaction: by offering a superficial nod to history, he seeks to extract tangible benefits, particularly regarding global oil prices and leverage ahead of the upcoming U.S. election. He is signaling to President Xi Jinping that he is willing to "play nice" if it serves his immediate political and economic goals.

This reality is mirrored in the shifting patterns of global trade. Consider the recent developments in Mexico, where South Korean companies—numbering around 500 to 2,000 depending on the scale of integration—are becoming deeply embedded. Mexico is positioning itself as a strategic partner, providing a masterclass in how mid-sized powers can navigate the crushing pressure of the US-China rivalry by prioritizing economic pragmatism over ideological alignment.

For South Korea, the lesson is clear: do not be swayed by the performative gestures of superpowers. Whether it is a compliment from a U.S. President or a trade deal in Latin America, these are simply tools for survival. The challenge for Seoul is to develop the "bifocal vision" required to see past the public rhetoric and identify the hard realities of national interest lying underneath.

Oil and the New Hegemony: China’s Economic Leverage

If there is one "big hand" controlling the global economy, it is China. By controlling the volume of its oil imports, Beijing effectively acts as a global thermostat for energy prices. Some market observers have even begun referring to this influence as a "New APEC" (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation)—a recognition that a single policy shift in Beijing can send gas prices and inflation spiraling worldwide.

This is a precarious situation for a nation like South Korea, which remains a resource-poor, trade-dependent economy. Experts warn that if the US-China relationship sours and leads to a strategic oil hoarding spree, prices could easily breach $100 per barrel. At that level, the damage would extend far beyond political posturing; it would be a direct blow to the livelihoods of ordinary citizens and a potential death knell for incumbent administrations.

Even developing nations are forced into a desperate game of survival. Countries like Indonesia find themselves caught between U.S. sanctions and the need for affordable Russian energy. Ironically, if oil prices were to skyrocket, the U.S. would lose the leverage to pressure these smaller nations, as the global economic crisis would force a relaxation of enforcement. It is a harsh ecosystem where economic survival often dictates the limits of diplomatic morality.

The Risk of Military Entanglement in Northeast Asia

A significant point of contention for South Korea has been the intensification of trilateral military exercises between the U.S., South Korea, and Japan. While the current South Korean administration, led by the conservative People Power Party, has emphasized "values-based diplomacy," many observers argue that this military integration is a strategic liability.

The argument is simple but profound: by normalizing these military drills near its borders, South Korea has effectively pushed North Korea, China, and Russia into a tighter, more hostile bloc. Critics suggest that simply adjusting the intensity or frequency of these exercises could serve as a powerful diplomatic signal, potentially reopening the door for dialogue between Washington and Pyongyang. Instead, by prioritizing military proximity to Japan—a country still grappling with its own history of wartime aggression—the current government may be sacrificing its own diplomatic flexibility for a sense of security that is ultimately illusory.

True diplomacy is not about cornering one's neighbor; it is about creating paths for exit and cooperation. By stripping away the incentives for China to cooperate on North Korean issues, Seoul has inadvertently weakened its own negotiating position. We must ask: who is this "security" actually for?

The Middleman’s Role: Diplomacy in the Shadows

The current state of U.S.-Iran relations offers a glimpse into how diplomacy works when direct channels are closed. With no direct negotiations since July, the two nations are like neighbors whose water pipes have burst but who are too proud to speak to one another. Here, a mediator like Qatar becomes essential—not because they are the center of the world, but because they are the only ones willing to carry the messages between the two.

Iran, facing crushing inflation and economic isolation, is desperate for a breakthrough, while the U.S. remains terrified of how rising oil prices might affect its own electoral outcomes. Both sides are playing a game of survival, using intermediaries to manage a conflict they cannot afford to let explode. It is a stark reminder that even in the most ideological conflicts, the primary currency of politics is the "stomach"—the ability to feed one's people and hold onto power.

As we look ahead, South Korea must learn to emulate this pragmatic flexibility. We cannot afford to be trapped by rigid, ideological binary choices. We need to use our own "mediators" and strengthen our economic sovereignty.

What to Watch Next

  • Energy Price Volatility: Monitor China's import quotas and strategic stockpiling. Any sudden shift here is a direct signal of future inflation levels in Asia.
  • Military Drill Adjustments: Watch for changes in the scale of trilateral exercises. A reduction in frequency would be a clear indicator that the government is shifting toward a more flexible, independent diplomatic posture.
  • U.S. Election Policy: As the U.S. election nears, watch how Washington’s rhetoric toward Beijing fluctuates—not based on policy, but on short-term poll numbers.

International politics is a cold mathematical equation. If we fail to solve it ourselves, we will remain nothing more than pawns on a board controlled by larger powers. It is time for a cooler, more precise calculation of our national interest.
