The "Telegramgate" Crisis: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Losing Its Way

September 30, 2026

If you have been scrolling through South Korean political news feeds lately, you would be forgiven for feeling a sense of mounting fatigue. What should be a stable period for the ruling party has instead devolved into a series of head-scratching controversies, leaving even the party’s most dedicated supporters wondering what is happening behind closed doors. At the center of this turmoil is the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal party, which currently holds the presidency under Lee Jae-myung.

The situation crystallized recently when leaked Telegram messages involving Kim Min-seok, a senior representative of the DPK, surfaced. While the administration might hope this blows over as a minor "happening," the fallout reveals a deeper, structural complacency. When the people running the country seem so detached from reality, it is no longer just a communication error—it is a crisis of governance.

The Telegram Leak and the Cost of Silence

On September 20, the political landscape in Seoul was rocked by the leak of private Telegram exchanges involving Kim Min-seok. The messages appeared to show a high-level discussion regarding party reform and sensitive personnel issues, including coordination regarding the then-nominee for Justice Minister, Kim Seung-won. The leak, which included cryptic remarks about "keeping time tight" and coordinating messages for the President, immediately triggered calls for an independent investigation into internal party dealings.

The DPK’s response was, characteristically, silence. This is particularly damaging because just days prior, Representative Kim had publicly praised the very nominee—Kim Seung-won—who would eventually be forced to resign on September 19 following a series of controversies. Despite the public clamor for accountability, the party leadership has refused to address the inconsistency. When the people in power treat public scrutiny as an annoyance to be outwaited rather than an obligation to answer, they lose the moral authority to lead.

This is not an isolated incident. Reports have since emerged showing that sensitive documents, including PowerPoint presentations on accounting irregularities within the party’s Gyeonggi chapter, were being circulated among top brass like Representative Kim and floor leader Han Byung-do. By choosing to ignore these leaks, the DPK leadership is essentially watching their own house burn while pretending the fire is not there.

A Pattern of Personnel Failures

The resignation of Justice Minister nominee Kim Seung-won marked the fifth major personnel failure of the current administration. The political bloodletting has already claimed the position of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik, who stepped down under pressure from the fallout. These are not merely administrative hiccups; they are symptomatic of an administration that has lost its ability to vet its own people.

The main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), has naturally seized on this, demanding re-investigations into allegations of unethical medical trial solicitations and favors granted to the nominee's family. Meanwhile, Representative Kim Min-seok has offered only vague, circular defenses, claiming the party’s handling of personnel was done in a "ruling-party-appropriate manner." This tone-deafness stands in stark contrast to voices like Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae, who has been vocally critical of the party’s tendency to sweep financial and ethical problems under the rug. To the public, the party’s leadership appears to be prioritizing the protection of the President’s image over the integrity of the state.

Chasing New Voters While Abandoning the Core

Every political party needs to expand its tent, and the DPK is no exception. Expanding toward the center and capturing moderate conservatives is the standard playbook for winning elections in South Korea. However, this strategy only works if the party’s core base—the "house rabbits," as they are colloquially called in Korean political discourse—remains satisfied and unified.

The current DPK leadership seems to be operating under the arrogant assumption that their traditional base has nowhere else to go. Recent polling data from agencies like Gallup Korea and Realmeter tells a different story. The party’s support among moderates is sliding, and the opposition is gaining ground not necessarily because they are performing well, but because the DPK is driving its own supporters away.

President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings have been trapped in the 30% range, a "box" that is increasingly hard to break out of. The frustration stems from a perceived elitism: when policies are criticized, the administration rarely accepts feedback, instead dismissing the concerns of their own base. When the President recently spoke about prosecutorial reform in a way that sounded more like a dynastic decree than a democratic process, it alienated the very people who had fought to get him into office. A government that views its most loyal critics as "enemies" or "obstacles" is a government that has lost its way.

The "Pebble" Metaphor: A Tale of Two Philosophies

The internal discord was recently highlighted by a metaphor from liberal political commentator Rhyu Si-min, who compared the President’s most radical, self-defeating supporters to "pebbles in a shoe." He argued that these pebbles, while small, cause constant pain and prevent the President from walking safely. He suggested it was time to "shake out" the shoe.

In a bizarre turn, Representative Kim Min-seok attempted to counter this by suggesting that "pebbles can turn into boulders with time and potential." It was a classic display of missing the point—a semantic word game played while the administration is bleeding support. Rhyu was speaking about the necessity of pruning toxic elements to ensure the government’s survival; Kim was offering a poetic, pseudo-philosophical deflection that does nothing to solve the actual pain of the voter base.

By engaging in these linguistic gymnastics rather than addressing the core of the malaise, the DPK leadership is signaling that they are more interested in winning arguments than winning back the public’s trust. When your supporters are crying out in pain, telling them that the pebble in their shoe will eventually turn into a mountain is not leadership—it is an insult.

What to Watch Next

  • Cabinet Reshuffling: Will the President continue to cycle through nominees without changing the vetting process, or will we see a shift toward more moderate, technocratic appointments?
  • Telegram Follow-ups: Keep an eye on whether opposition lawmakers or independent investigative bodies force a formal inquiry into the leaked internal documents.
  • Support Trends: Watch the polling numbers for the "moderate" segment. If the current trend continues, the DPK may be forced to replace key leadership figures to avoid a total collapse in the upcoming legislative cycle.

Ultimately, power is only legitimate when it is exercised with self-reflection. If the DPK continues to rely on silence and word games to navigate its crises, the cost will be far higher than a few bad headlines—it will be the loss of the trust that forms the very foundation of their mandate.
