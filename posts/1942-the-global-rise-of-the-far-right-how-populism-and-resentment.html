The Global Rise of the Far-Right: How Populism and Resentment Are Reshaping Democracy

November 04, 2026

Across the globe, the political landscape is shifting in a direction that should alarm anyone concerned with the stability of democratic institutions. From the streets of European capitals to the legislative chambers of the Middle East and the political arenas of the Americas, far-right and nationalist movements are gaining momentum. What might look like isolated domestic disputes in various countries are, in fact, part of a synchronized wave of radicalization. Fueled by economic anxiety and cultural friction, these movements are exploiting public dissatisfaction not to reform democracies, but to hollow them out from within.

Germany’s AfD and the Erosion of European Security

In Europe, the normalization of far-right politics has graduated from domestic friction to a direct threat to continental security. Nowhere is this more apparent than in Germany, where the Alternative for Germany (AfD), a far-right nationalist party, has faced mounting scrutiny over its alleged backchannel communications with Moscow. The party’s leadership has made no secret of its opposition to Ukraine’s integration into the European Union and NATO, actively lobbying for a restoration of ties with Vladimir Putin's government.

At the same time, top figures within the AfD have leaned heavily into anti-immigrant rhetoric, explicitly targeting minority groups and blaming them for straining Germany’s social welfare system. This strategy of weaponizing public anxiety over immigration is designed to mobilize disgruntled voters, even as the party’s broader ambitions are periodically tested by internal scandals, such as recent email leaks. When political entities that cozy up to hostile foreign powers and champion xenophobia manage to cross the threshold into mainstream institutional politics, it ceases to be a conventional partisan disagreement. It becomes a systemic vulnerability.

This dynamic is not confined to Germany. In France, economic pressures and anti-immigrant sentiment have driven rural voters and urban residents alike toward populist alternatives, sparking fierce street clashes and drawing sharp rebukes from the government of President Emmanuel Macron. Meanwhile, in the United Kingdom, parties like Reform UK—led by figures such as Nigel Farage—continue to stoke immigration controversies. When prominent figures within these movements face legal scandals, it lays bare the moral hypocrisy underlying their populist appeals: a loud, performative defense of the "common people" masking a disregard for the rule of law.

Israel’s Political Fractures and the Cost of Extremism

The embrace of hardline politics is by no means unique to Europe. In Israel, the government of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has long relied on hard-right and ultra-nationalist factions to maintain power. However, recent electoral shifts have eroded that parliamentary majority, plunging the country’s political future into deep uncertainty.

As Netanyahu attempts to navigate these losses—resisting overtures from the opposition while trying to preserve a coalition with shrinking far-right elements—the societal costs have been immense. In the West Bank, settler violence has surged amid disputes over illegal outposts, pushed forward by extremist groups who feel emboldened by state-level patronage. The situation in Israel offers a sobering lesson in the price of political opportunism: when mainstream leaders co-opt extremists to cling to power, they mortgage the long-term stability of the state for short-term survival.

Worryingly, this has been accompanied by a familiar playbook of institutional distrust. Much like political actors elsewhere who cry "rigged election" or attack the judiciary whenever they face political setbacks, elements of Netanyahu's camp have increasingly cast doubt on democratic mechanisms themselves. When leaders suggest that legal systems or electoral outcomes are fundamentally corrupt simply because they lose, they strike at the foundational trust required to keep a republic functioning.

The Transatlantic Feedback Loop of Populism

It is impossible to understand the global surge of the far-right without looking at the United States. American political polarization and the enduring influence of Trumpism serve as both a financial lifeline and an ideological blueprint for nationalist movements worldwide. Every time election denialism or aggressive anti-establishment rhetoric gains traction in the U.S., it reverberates across borders, emboldening populist counterparts in Europe, Latin America, and beyond.

This cross-border connectivity is amplified by digital platforms, where extremist propaganda, targeted disinformation, and outrage-driven content can be manufactured and distributed at unprecedented speeds. According to recent data shared by international law enforcement agencies, tens of thousands of extremist and terrorist propaganda pieces are flagged online daily—a testament to how effectively fringe movements use modern technology to radicalize youth and organize discontent across national boundaries.

When political discourse descends from policy debate into a contest of grievance and mutual demonization, the ultimate victim is the citizen. The far-right playbook relies on a simple, toxic formula: identify a genuine public hardship—whether inflation, housing costs, or social change—and redirect that frustration away from systemic economic issues and onto vulnerable scapegoats like refugees and minorities.

What to Watch Next

As we monitor these international developments, several concrete indicators will determine whether this wave of far-right resurgence can be contained or if it will continue to dismantle democratic norms:

  • Institutional Resilience in Europe: Watch how mainstream European governments handle legal challenges and security investigations surrounding far-right parties like Germany's AfD, particularly regarding foreign interference.
  • The Trajectory of Middle Eastern Coalitions: Pay close attention to whether Israeli political parties can forge a stable, centrist consensus, or if the reliance on far-right factions will permanently paralyze governance.
  • Electoral Integrity and Pushback: Monitor upcoming elections in major Western democracies to see whether voters begin to reject candidates who undermine judicial independence and electoral systems.
  • Digital Regulation of Extremism: Observe how international agencies and tech platforms cooperate to curb the cross-border flow of radicalizing content designed to exploit economic anxieties.

The temptation to blame the stranger and retreat into exclusionary nationalism grows stronger whenever economies falter and societies feel insecure. But yielding to that temptation dismantles the very walls of protection that free societies have spent generations building. Defending democracy requires more than just noting its flaws; it demands an active, empathetic resistance against the politics of hate.
