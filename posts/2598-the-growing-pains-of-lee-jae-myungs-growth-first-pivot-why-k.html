The Growing Pains of Lee Jae-myung’s "Growth First" Pivot: Why Korea’s Economic Miracle is Stalling

November 21, 2026

Walk through the narrow alleys of Euljiro in central Seoul or the storefronts of Gyeonggi Province, and you will encounter a disconnect that defines South Korea today. On the evening news, the tickers scream about record-breaking semiconductor exports and the "Second Miracle on the Han River." But on the ground, the "For Rent" signs are multiplying, and the lunchtime crowds at local diners are thinning. For many Koreans, the promised "Light Revolution" of the current administration feels increasingly like a distant memory.

President Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), rose to power on a platform of "Basic Society"—a vision where the state guarantees a fundamental quality of life for all citizens, largely through aggressive redistribution and economic justice. However, a year into his term, the political winds have shifted. The "Basic Society" rhetoric has been replaced by a "Growth First, Distribution Later" mantra that sounds remarkably like the conservative playbooks of the 1970s. As the administration pivots to "growing the pie," the everyday economy—the *minsaeng*—is sounding a shrill alarm. Real wages are stagnant, job growth has been halved, and the public is beginning to ask a chilling question: "How is this different from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol?"

The Identity Crisis of the Democratic Party

To understand why this matters, one must look at the DNA of the Democratic Party of Korea. The DPK’s platform is explicitly built on overcoming socio-economic polarization. Its charter promises to guarantee a basic life for all and to complete "economic democratization"—a Korean term for reining in the power of the *chaebol* (the large, family-run conglomerates like Samsung and Hyundai) and protecting small businesses.

During his campaign, President Lee was the champion of these ideals. He spoke of a "Basic Society" where a universal basic income and housing would be the floor, not the ceiling. Yet, those 13 core policy goals have largely vanished from the government's daily messaging. In their place is a logic that would have been at home in the era of the late dictator Park Chung-hee: we must grow the economy first, and the benefits will eventually trickle down.

This shift is more than just a change in vocabulary; it is a fundamental pivot in the government’s theory of change. By prioritizing the "size of the pie" over how it is sliced, the Lee administration is alienating its core constituency—the working class and the self-employed. The disappointment is palpable. For those who believed the removal of the previous administration under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol would usher in a new era of equality, the current focus on "Mega Projects" feels like a betrayal of the party's progressive identity.

The Cold Reality of the Numbers

The government’s optimism is increasingly contradicted by cold, hard data from the Bank of Korea and the Ministry of Employment and Labor. If we compare the current economic indicators to the final, embattled year of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency—a period generally regarded by the current administration as a low point for the nation—the results are startling.

  • Falling Real Wages: Under the final year of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the average real wage (adjusted for inflation) stood at about 4.068 million won (roughly $2,920). Under President Lee, that figure has actually ticked down to 4.066 million won. In a world of rising prices, the Korean worker’s wallet is literally getting thinner.
  • The Job Growth Cliff: The most honest barometer of an economy is the job market. Between January and October of this year, the increase in "regular" or permanent jobs—the gold standard of employment in Korea—was only about 135,000 per month. Under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, that growth was 323,500. Job creation for stable positions has been slashed by 60%.
  • The Youth Crisis: Even after the government expanded the statistical definition of "youth" to include those up to age 34, the numbers are grim. Job losses for the 15-34 age bracket have doubled, jumping from an annual loss of 48,000 under the previous administration to a staggering 113,000 under President Lee.

This is not just a "speed" problem; it is a structural failure. When you combine Korea’s 8.67 million non-regular (temporary or contract) workers with its 5.8 million self-employed citizens, you have nearly 15 million people living in a state of high economic precariousness. For these people, the "Growth First" policy isn't a promise of future wealth; it is a demand for present-day sacrifice that they can no longer afford.

The Semiconductor Trap and the End of Trickle-Down

Why is there such a massive gap between the booming export figures and the misery on the streets? The answer lies in the "Semiconductor Trap." The Lee administration has pinned its hopes on the tech sector, specifically AI chips and high-end manufacturing. While this drives up GDP, it does almost nothing for employment.

In the 1970s, during the era of Park Chung-hee, the "employment inducement coefficient"—a measure of how many jobs are created for every 1 billion won (about $718,000) of GDP growth—was 78. Today, that number is -0.22. In modern, automated semiconductor fabs, billions of dollars in investment can actually result in a net loss of jobs as robots replace human labor.

"Growth today doesn't look like growth in the 70s. Back then, a new factory meant 5,000 new jobs. Today, a new Mega Project means 5,000 new sensors and a handful of PhDs."

This structural reality is why Korea is falling behind neighbors like Taiwan in terms of domestic vitality. In Taiwan, IT manufacturing accounts for 11% of total employment, meaning a chip boom directly raises the wages of a large chunk of the population. In Korea, that figure is a measly 1.9%. The profits from giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix stay trapped at the top. High-income employees at these firms tend to save their bonuses or invest in real estate rather than spending in the local "alleyway" economies, leaving small businesses to wither.

The Mirage of "Mega Projects"

In response to these criticisms, the Lee administration has doubled down on what it calls "Mega Projects." The centerpiece is a massive 113 trillion won (approximately $81 billion) investment plan for Samsung’s operations in Asan. The President often visits these sites, flanked by business titans like Samsung Chairman Lee Jae-yong, promising a "Second Miracle on the Han River."

But for the 7.6 million small business owners in Korea, these grand ceremonies feel like a different world. Small business debt is skyrocketing. Non-performing loans (NPLs) for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) have jumped from 10 trillion won to over 11 trillion won ($7.9 billion) in a single quarter. Households are also hitting a wall, with bad debt rising as people find themselves unable to pay even the interest on their loans.

The government’s reliance on these large-scale construction and industrial projects is a nostalgic attempt to recreate the success of the past. However, the "Second Miracle" cannot be built on the same foundation as the first. The original miracle was fueled by a young, growing workforce and labor-intensive industry. Today’s Korea is the fastest-aging society in the world, with a shrinking workforce and a high-tech sector that doesn't need "mass" labor. Pumping money into Mega Projects without a corresponding plan for direct income support or a robust social safety net is like pouring water into a leaky bucket.

What to Watch Next

The political stakes for President Lee Jae-myung could not be higher. Historically, progressive governments in Korea have struggled when they failed to deliver on the "bread and butter" issues of the middle class. The late President Roh Moo-hyun, despite decent GDP growth, saw his popularity crater when real estate prices soared and the gap between regular and non-regular workers widened.

As we move into the next year of the Lee presidency, there are three key areas to watch:

  • The 2025 Budget Battle: Watch whether the DPK-controlled legislature shifts funding away from "Mega Project" infrastructure and back toward the "Basic Society" programs (like universal vouchers or youth dividends) that were originally promised.
  • Minimum Wage and Labor Relations: With the expert rating for the government’s labor policy sitting at a mediocre 73.4 points, pay close attention to how the administration handles the next round of minimum wage negotiations. If the government continues to favor "flexibility" over wage floors, expect a significant backlash from the labor unions.
  • Small Business Insolvency: Keep an eye on the delinquency rates for "Individual Business" loans. If the bubble of pandemic-era debt finally bursts without a significant government bailout or debt-forgiveness program, the resulting economic shock could paralyze the domestic service sector.

President Lee Jae-myung stands at a crossroads. He can continue to chase the ghost of 20th-century growth, or he can return to the 21st-century vision of a "Basic Society" that his voters actually asked for. Statistics are cold, but the anger of a population that feels its wallet shrinking is very, very warm. If the "pie" keeps growing while the people keep starving, the "Light Revolution" may find itself facing a very dark winter.
