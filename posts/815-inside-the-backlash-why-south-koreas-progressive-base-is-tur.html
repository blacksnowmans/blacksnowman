Inside the Backlash: Why South Korea's Progressive Base is Turning on President Lee Jae-myung

September 19, 2026

When you tune into a live broadcast by Sisatapa TV—a prominent political commentary channel on YouTube—you are immediately struck by a raw, unfiltered tension that mainstream news rarely captures. On a recent special broadcast hosted by producer Lee Jong-won, a panel of sharp political commentators gathered to tear apart South Korean President Lee Jae-myung's latest televised press conference. The chat room was a sea of real-time fury and disillusionment, with peak viewership crossing 10,000 concurrent users. One panelist confessed that the stress of preparing for the broadcast had prompted his wife to plead, "Never do this again." The mood was not one of casual debate, but of shared heartbreak. For a political base that spent years taking political and emotional beatings to defend democratic values, watching the people they put at the very top of power has become an exercise in painful alienation.

This gap between the expectations of the core progressive base and the defensive posture of the current administration has triggered a profound crisis of confidence. Observers watched the president's press conference with a collective white-knuckled hope simply that "no major disaster would happen"—a remarkably low bar for a head of state. Yet, beneath the careful staging and the president's unusually low-key demeanor lay contradictions that have left his staunchest supporters questioning what their years of struggle were actually for. To understand this moment is to understand a widening fracture within South Korea's progressive camp, where the attainment of power has not brought vindication, but a bitter reckoning over what constitutes reform.

A Low Posture Masking Deep Contradictions

The press conference opened with a subdued President Lee. Gone was the defiant, untouchable swagger of the past; in its place was a cautious leader acutely aware that his approval ratings have plummeted to a dismal 33%. Panelist Jeong Jin-ho admitted he spent the broadcast holding his breath, terrified a major gaffe would detonate the event entirely. But the careful posture brought little comfort. When the conversation turned to prosecutorial reform, the reaction among commentators shifted from anxiety to outright shock. Rather than a bold blueprint for systemic change, the president's remarks felt to many like an accidental confession: an admission that they had, in effect, been misleading the public about the true scope of their ambitions.

Cho Seong-eun, another panelist, fought back tears of frustration on air. She voiced a sentiment shared by many core supporters: why manufacture these agonizingly defensive public appearances if the core message is so hollow? Panelist Kim Dadi offered a blunt analogy, comparing the performance to "an abusive husband who, facing imminent divorce and the loss of all his property, suddenly drops to his knees and swears he'll quit drinking." When voters encounter that kind of survival-driven humility, the shock is compounded by memories of past political downfalls. The scene bore an eerie resemblance to historic missteps by past leaders—such as former conservative President Lee Myung-bak's desperate apologies during the 2008 U.S. beef protests, or former President Yoon Suk Yeol's late-term bows as his numbers cratered amid escalating scandals. The common thread is a leadership caught off guard by the depth of public estrangement, yet reluctant to enact genuine structural change.

The internal strain of the administration spilled into plain view with the actions of presidential spokesperson Kang Yu-jung. After a half-hour delay that set off frantic speculation, Kang stepped up to the podium at the tail end of the briefing to voice the unvarnished grievances of the base under the guise of "public opinion." It was a line of questioning that effectively served as a live, televised resignation letter. As the president attempted to sidestep hard questions, Kang repeatedly brought the focus back to the core issues, leaving the room in a heavy silence. Panelists joked grimly that it would be her last day on the job, but the underlying message was deadly serious: the administration's inner circle is fracturing, and the president's traditional supporters are no longer willing to swallow their tongues.

The Dilution of Prosecutorial Reform

To understand the depth of this betrayal, one must revisit what South Korea's progressive grassroots fought for through years of bitter street protests. The core demand of prosecutorial reform was absolute: the complete separation of investigative and indicting powers to strip the country's prosecutors of their unchecked, monopoly-like authority. It was supposed to return the prosecution service to its proper, narrow role under Article 198 of the Criminal Procedure Act. Yet, during the press conference, President Lee made it clear that his ideal vision of reform was merely dividing "investigating prosecutors" and "indicting prosecutors" within the existing framework.

For the base, this was jaw-dropping. Within the notoriously insular fraternity of South Korea's legal establishment, shuffling titles between prosecutors who share the same institutional DNA is not reform—it is an internal redistribution of power. It creates two arms of the same beast rather than dismantling it. Discovering that this lukewarm compromise was the president's goal all along reframes years of intra-party friction. It explains why hardline reformers within the ruling Democratic Party, such as Rep. Jeong Cheong-rae, who pushed for more aggressive overhauls, were increasingly treated as thorns in the administration's side. The progressive movement spent the better part of a decade weathering the trauma of the Cho Kuk scandal, only to watch the architects of their political victory water down their foundational ideals.

This half-measure approach extends to the newly proposed Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA). Billed as a step away from prosecutorial overreach, the creation of a powerful new investigative body under police restructuring looks to critics like the birth of yet another bureaucratic monster. While the rhetoric of "separating investigation from indictment" plays well to a general public fatigued by legal jargon, the core base—who bled on the front lines of past reform battles—sees through the semantic sleight-of-hand. Without democratic guardrails, a beefed-up investigative apparatus risks becoming a new political boomerang that will ultimately target the legitimacy of the administration itself.

We took the hits, yet they preach "both-sidesism."
While core supporters bore the brunt of political warfare, the leadership consistently defaults to a cowardly middle-ground narrative that blames everyone equally. True integration cannot be built by demanding that the battered victim shake hands with the aggressor.

Blaming the System for Staffing Disasters

Another flashpoint during the briefing was the administration's persistent handling of personnel scandals. Faced with a relentless string of controversial and unqualified political appointments, President Lee dismissed the criticisms with a vague and diffuse apology, attributing the failures to "our collective mistakes" rather than a failure of his own judgment. It is the classic corporate dodge of a floundering manager dividing blame by the number of team members, rather than owning a decision as the sole holder of appointment power.

These repeated appointment disasters are not random glitches in a broken vetting system; they are the predictable result of a narrow recruitment pool. The administration consistently favors loyalists who offer uncritical obedience over independent competence, a preference that has systematically produced a string of tone-deaf appointments. By hiding behind the collective pronoun "we," the leadership insults the intelligence of voters who know precisely who signed off on the paperwork.

With Chuseok—Korea's major autumn harvest festival and a critical traditional barometer for public sentiment—fast approaching, this press conference was billed as the administration's final golden window to reset the national narrative. Instead of offering bold course corrections, such as dropping contentious appointments like the newly floated head of the MCIA or purging the prosecutor-turned-aide pipeline, the government offered defensive platitudes. If the administration attempts to coast through the holiday on superficial optics, this press conference will be remembered not as a turning point, but as a political eulogy.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's political landscape enters this tense plateau, the immediate future hinges on concrete actions rather than rhetorical resets. Observers and voters alike should monitor several key developments in the coming weeks:

  • Personnel Overhauls: Watch whether the administration follows through on substantive personnel changes, specifically regarding presidential aides with prosecutorial backgrounds and controversial agency nominees like the proposed MCIA chief.
  • Intra-Party Fractures: Keep an eye on the growing friction within the ruling Democratic Party between hardline reformists and the presidential mainstream, particularly regarding upcoming legislative agendas.
  • Chuseok Public Sentiment: Pay close attention to family dinner table conversations and regional polling data following the Chuseok holiday—traditionally the definitive test of whether a government has successfully managed to turn public opinion around.
  • Grassroots Media Mobilization: Monitor independent channels like Sisatapa TV and progressive digital communities to gauge whether the base's disillusionment hardens into active opposition or cools into resignation.

The frustration radiating from political commentators and everyday citizens alike points to a deeper truth: power won through the promise of systemic change cannot be sustained by maintaining the system's worst habits. Unless the administration abandons its defensive posture and returns to the unvarnished principles of fairness and accountability, the gap between the president and the people who put him there will only continue to grow.
