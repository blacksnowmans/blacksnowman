South Korea’s 9,000-Point Stock Boom Left Brokers Rich and Retail Investors Underwater

November 16, 2026

At 8:40 each morning, when South Korea’s stock market clears automated forced liquidations, the bill for a euphoric spring rally comes due. For Mr. Lee, an office worker in his thirties, the collapse that began in late June erased more than paper gains. Five years earlier, he had lost 240 million won (about $170,000) in a jeonse scam—a uniquely Korean housing lease fraud in which tenants lose massive, supposedly refundable lump-sum deposits instead of paying monthly rent. After recovering just 60 million won (about $43,000) through property auctions, Lee borrowed as much as banks would lend to assemble a 200 million won (about $143,000) stake, putting it into South Korea’s two biggest chipmakers, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. When the benchmark KOSPI index was hitting record highs, his account showed over 100 million won (about $71,000) in profit. Within weeks of the downturn, his return plunged to minus 30 percent.

"Along with the losses, I am still having to pay back the loan interest right now," Lee told investigators.

Lee’s story sits at the center of Episode 361 of MBC’s investigative journalism program Straight, titled "The Betrayal of 9,000 KOSPI." According to the broadcast’s reporting, South Korea’s rapid surge past the 9,300-point mark—and its sudden reversal—exposed far more than a routine market correction. While everyday retail investors, known locally as "ants," borrowed heavily to buy at the top, foreign institutional investors cashed out in massive numbers and domestic securities firms posted record earnings from commissions and high-interest margin loans. In the program’s assessment, the episode reflects deep structural flaws in how South Korean brokerages produce research, profit from leverage, and leave small investors exposed.

When a National Rally Becomes a Debt Trap

To understand why a stock pullback triggered such acute social distress in South Korea, it helps to understand how retail participation has evolved there. With soaring home prices and stagnant wage growth pushing younger workers away from traditional paths to wealth, many turned to bit-tu—a colloquial shorthand for investing with borrowed money. During the first half of the year, as television pundits, YouTube channels, and brokerage reports cheered an unprecedented rally, fear of missing out gripped households across generations.

The fallout documented by Straight reached from campus cafeterias to retirement accounts:

  • Younger workers and students: Mr. Yoo, a university student juggling three part-time jobs to earn roughly 1.4 million won (about $1,000) a month, put 2 million won (about $1,430) of hard-earned savings into stocks and lost half of it. "For a college student, 100,000 or 200,000 won (about $70 to $140) is a lot of money," he said, explaining that he skipped school cafeteria meals for cup noodles to make up the loss.
  • Broken social ties: Mr. Park, an office worker four years into his career, made the largest investment of his life after a friend urged him that the domestic market—slangily called gukjang—was guaranteed to keep rising. Park lost tens of millions of won almost immediately. His friend, who had poured his own assets into SK Hynix using margin leverage, cut off all contact and disappeared after the crash.
  • Wiped-out retirements: A fifty-something investor who had stayed in South Korean equities for more than a decade lost over 2 billion won (about $1.4 million), watching his retirement savings evaporate. "Share prices themselves, as of today, are at a level worse than 11 or 13 years ago," he said, describing severe emotional distress.

For older long-term investors, the collapse felt like a breach of faith with domestic equities. For younger borrowers, it transformed a desperate bid for upward mobility into long-term financial paralysis.

Research Reports That Only Knew How to Say Buy

A central focus of the MBC investigation is the role played by securities analysts during the market’s ascent. In the first half of the year, 25 major South Korean brokerages issued 125 reports raising their target prices on Samsung Electronics and 113 raising targets on SK Hynix. Over that same period, only a single report warned of a decline or reduced weighting for Samsung Electronics, and zero warned of a drop for SK Hynix.

The gap between published targets and actual market peaks was stark. Nomura Securities set a target of 4.7 million won (about $3,360) for SK Hynix, and Hana Investment & Securities set its target at 4.3 million won (about $3,070); the stock’s actual peak stopped at 2,987,000 won (about $2,130). For Samsung Electronics, Nomura projected 670,000 won (about $480) and Daol Investment & Securities projected 585,000 won (about $420), while the shares topped out at 374,500 won (about $268). Across all listed companies in the first half, brokerages published 4,873 upward reports against roughly 1,000 downward ones, often using promotional phrasing such as "just buy," "100 out of 100," and "have more courage."

Only after prices tumbled in the second half did brokerages reverse course, flooding the market with 3,171 downward revisions versus 1,378 upward reports. Academic and fintech data cited in the report underscore how persistent this optimism bias is: an analysis tracking roughly 39,800 domestic brokerage reports against actual share prices six months later found that brokerage forecasts produced larger errors than simply assuming prices would not move at all. Long-term data show that 97.25 percent of domestic brokerage ratings remain "buy."

As the broadcast notes, this pattern is rooted in structural conflicts of interest rather than mere incompetence. In South Korea, brokerage research divisions are tethered to lucrative corporate investment banking businesses—underwriting initial public offerings, corporate bond sales, and capital raises. Analysts who issue an honest "sell" rating risk getting cut off from company briefings and site visits, or costing their firm major corporate mandates. Furthermore, unlike in many overseas markets where institutional research is sold behind paywalls, Korean reports are distributed free through web portals and retail trading apps. Analysts who issue negative calls frequently face intense backlash, leaked personal details, and threatening phone calls from angry retail shareholders. Under pressure from both corporate clients and retail crowds, stopgap regulatory steps such as anonymous reports or target-price gap disclosures have failed to break the habit of relentless optimism.

Who Cashed Out While Retail Investors Bought

While domestic commentary celebrated the KOSPI crossing 9,300, trading data reveal a sharp divide between global institutions and local households. During the first half of the year, foreign investors net-sold 149 trillion won (about $106 billion) worth of KOSPI shares—nearly 130 trillion won (about $93 billion) of it in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix alone. The program points out that Goldman Sachs boasted of its hedge fund clients achieving their highest first-half returns in 20 years, aided by well-timed exits at market peaks.

As Noh Geun-chang, head of Semicon Research, explained in the broadcast, foreign funds were mechanically locking in profits and trimming South Korea’s weight within global portfolios. Stepping in to absorb that supply were South Korean retail investors, who net-bought nearly 100 trillion won (about $71 billion) in shares over the same period.

Domestic brokerages profited on both sides of the surge. Even as branch-level employees privately worried that stocks were overheated and that retail buyers would be left holding the bag once foreign and institutional sellers exited, none of that caution made it into official research reports. Instead, trading volume and leverage drove record profits:

  • Commission windfalls: Between late May and mid-August, retail investors lost 6.3 trillion won (about $4.5 billion) in principal on a single high-risk single-stock exchange-traded fund, yet brokerages collected 81.6 billion won (about $58 million) in trading commissions on that product alone.
  • Record earnings: Combined first-half net profit at South Korea’s top 10 brokerages surpassed 10 trillion won (about $7.1 billion) for the first time, led by 6 trillion won (about $4.3 billion) in the second quarter—2.4 times the previous year’s figure.
  • Margin-loan interest: Thirty domestic brokerages earned 1.34 trillion won (about $960 million) in interest from margin lending to retail clients, up more than 80 percent from a year earlier, at annual rates approaching 8 to 9 percent.

Household Debt, Forced Liquidations, and What to Watch

The crash has spilled directly into South Korea’s broader financial vulnerabilities. During the second quarter, when the KOSPI peaked, total household debt topped 2,000 trillion won (about $1.43 trillion) for the first time. More striking was what drove the expansion: "other loans," which combine credit loans and brokerage margin credit, grew by 12.8 trillion won (about $9.1 billion)—surpassing the 12.2 trillion won (about $8.7 billion) increase in mortgages for the first time on record. Even the Bank of Korea described the shift as a highly unusual distortion fueled by surging stock speculation.

When share prices dropped and collateral fell short, brokerages triggered automatic forced liquidations, known as bandae-maemae, dumping shares at market prices at 8:40 a.m. The daily average number of forced-liquidation accounts doubled in a single month, jumping from about 21,000 in June to 41,000. Those morning sell waves pushed prices lower still, forcing additional accounts into margin calls and pulling down unleveraged investors as well. By June, brokerage margin balances stood at 37.3 trillion won (about $26.6 billion)—2.3 times their level a year and a half earlier, with borrowing rising 2.5 times among people in their twenties and 2.4 times among those in their thirties, outpacing the 2.1-fold increase among borrowers in their forties and fifties.

Looking ahead, three concrete pressure points will determine whether South Korea’s markets and households absorb the shock or sink deeper into mistrust:

  • Structural research reform: Watch whether financial authorities move beyond superficial disclosure rules to address the commercial ties between brokerage investment banking arms and equity analysts—the root cause of the 97 percent "buy" bias.
  • Youth credit strain and consumption: With margin debt concentrated heavily among workers in their twenties and thirties, watch for rising personal credit delinquencies and weaker consumer spending as salaries go toward servicing high-interest loans.
  • Capital flight from domestic equities: With older investors mourning lost retirement savings and younger investors burned by forced liquidations, the key question for South Korea’s market is whether domestic capital will permanently retreat from local stocks unless governance and investor protections noticeably improve.
