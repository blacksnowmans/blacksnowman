The Burden of "Just in Case": Lessons from an Actress’s 1,450km Pilgrimage

September 07, 2026

For most of her career, veteran South Korean actress Shim Hye-jin lived a life defined by the comforts and constraints of fame. Approaching her 30th year in the industry and well into her 50s, she found herself paralyzed by a mundane fear: the simple act of stepping out her front door alone. To break this cycle, she did something radical. She traded the familiar fences of her daily life for the dusty, unforgiving trails of the Camino de Santiago in Spain and the Portuguese Way—a combined distance of 1,450 kilometers (approximately 900 miles).

While many romanticize the pilgrimage as a journey toward profound religious enlightenment, Shim’s reality was far more visceral. It was a journey of muscle cramps, language barriers, and the crushing weight of a backpack packed with "just in case" items. Her experience offers a sharp, practical lesson for anyone feeling weighed down by the anxieties of modern life.

The Physics of Worry: Why We Carry Too Much

Before leaving, Shim spent a month obsessively preparing. Like many, she turned to guidebooks to curate the "perfect" pack. Driven by an amorphous anxiety—the classic "what if I need this later?"—she ended up with a backpack weighing between 15kg and 20kg (about 33 to 44 pounds). On the trail, she stood out not for her athleticism, but for the sheer bulk she hauled, eventually drawing pitying glances from fellow travelers who wondered how she could possibly keep moving.

The realization hit her slowly: the weight in her bag was a direct reflection of her internal anxieties. We do the same in life, carrying emotional baggage that we are terrified to let go of because we mistake it for necessary preparation. It wasn't until her body physically failed—until she literally could not walk another step—that she began the process of "emptying." She learned that shedding physical items was an ironic, but essential, prerequisite to shedding the mental burdens she had been lugging for years. Her conclusion was blunt: "Is the weight of my bag not equal to the weight of my worries?"

"Alone Together": The Art of Healthy Distance

One of the most persistent pressures of social life is the expectation of constant, active engagement. On the trail, however, Shim discovered a different etiquette. She noticed that simply putting on headphones became a silent, universally respected signal: "I need to walk alone right now."

This is a sophisticated form of relationship-building that modern, hyper-connected society often fails to grasp. We fear isolation, yet true, healthy relationships are only possible when we respect the need for solitude. Shim found that you can walk with someone for an entire day without speaking a word, yet feel a profound sense of companionship. This "together but apart" dynamic teaches us that being a good companion isn't about constant conversation or forced cheer; it is about acknowledging the other person's pace and autonomy. Once you learn to respect these boundaries, the "weight" of maintaining a relationship disappears, replaced by a comfortable, effortless connection.

Breaking the Wall of Prejudice

Shim notes that, even on the trail, she initially avoided eye contact with fellow Koreans. It is a cultural habit born of a desire not to intrude, but also of a deep-seated fear of rejection. After a chance encounter with a younger colleague where she finally gathered the courage to say hello, she learned that the other person had been wanting to talk the entire time but was equally afraid of being rebuffed.

We often block ourselves from connection by preemptively assuming that others do not want to be bothered. Shim’s experience is a reminder that the barriers we perceive between ourselves and others are often constructed from our own projections and insecurities. Communication—the simple act of reaching out first—is the ultimate mirror. It forces us to confront who we are, outside of the internal narratives we have spent years constructing.

Moving Toward Kindness

When asked why she would consider returning to the trail, Shim’s answer is simple yet profound: "I want to try being a little kinder."

Her transformation from a woman afraid to leave her home to a pilgrim who has walked nearly a thousand miles is not about physical endurance. It is about the decision to prioritize kindness—both toward others and oneself—as a functional strategy for happiness. She found that when things got difficult, logic and language often failed, but a smile, a hug, or a gentle touch could transcend any border.

As she steps back into her daily life, she carries with her the knowledge that life is not a journey to be "perfectly prepared" for, but a process of shedding what no longer serves us. The Camino taught her that the way to move forward is to lighten the load, respect the space of others, and have the courage to reach out first.

What to watch next:

  • The "One-Item" Test: Apply the trail logic to your daily life. What is one habit, anxiety, or physical object you are carrying "just in case" that is actually slowing you down? Try removing it for one week.
  • The Power of Presence: In your next interaction, try prioritizing a small gesture—a genuine smile or a moment of undivided attention—over trying to "solve" a problem with words.
  • Observing the "Pilgrim's Mindset": Watch how you treat strangers in your own city. If you viewed everyone you encountered as a fellow traveler on a similar path, how would your interactions change?
