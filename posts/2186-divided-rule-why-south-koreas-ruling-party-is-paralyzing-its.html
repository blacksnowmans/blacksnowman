Divided Rule: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Paralyzing Itself From Within

November 10, 2026

Behind the closed doors of the National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are locked in a bitter dispute that has paralyzed their legislative agenda. On paper, the liberal party holds the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung and commands the levers of state power. Yet instead of driving forward an ambitious slate of institutional overhauls, the party is tearing itself apart over an ideological and tactical split: should the government focus primarily on "public livelihood" (minsaeng) relief, or must it prioritize sweeping systemic reforms?

To an outside observer, improving the daily economic lives of ordinary citizens and dismantling entrenched institutional abuses sound like natural partners. But inside South Korean progressive politics, the choice between livelihood and reform has hardened into a fierce factional battleground. It has become a convenient proxy war between loyalists and critics, moderate pragmatists and aggressive reformers, threatening to squander the party's governing momentum.

The False Dilemma: Pitting Daily Bread Against Structural Reform

The core tension within the Democratic Party centers on how lawmakers frame their political mission. Self-described pragmatists insist that South Korea's urgent priority must be immediate economic welfare and stabilizing household balance sheets. They argue that pressing aggressive institutional changes risks alienating swing voters and igniting unnecessary clashes with the conservative establishment.

However, critics within the party see this moderate stance as a cowardly excuse to avoid direct confrontation with vested interests. Postponing structural reform in the name of economic stability, reform-minded figures argue, treats the symptoms of societal dysfunction while leaving the disease untouched. Without dismantling deep-seated corrupt practices and reforming regulatory and legal institutions, any economic relief handed out to citizens will rest on shifting sand.

This middle-of-the-road hesitation leaves the party trapped. By trying to appease moderate voters while promising transformation to its base, the leadership accomplishes neither. Core progressive supporters grow disillusioned by the lack of bold action, while the broader public perceives a hesitant ruling party incapable of delivering tangible economic relief or passing meaningful legislation.

Exacerbating the gridlock is the outsized influence of political influencers and partisan YouTube creators. Rather than exercising independent legislative judgment, many lawmakers find themselves catering to hyper-vocal online subcultures. When politicians govern by monitoring the comment sections of popular streaming channels, genuine policy deliberation is replaced by theatrical loyalty tests, leaving lawmakers held hostage by factional cheerleaders rather than responding to the broader national electorate.

Echoes of the Past: Moderate Compromise and Historical Dead Ends

This dynamic is hardly new in South Korean political history; in fact, progressive party elders have seen this movie before. Today's debate bears an uncanny resemblance to the early 2000s, when a moderate caucus known as the "Gathering for Stable Reform" (Angaemo)—associated with figures such as lawmaker Cho Bae-sook—held sway within the liberal coalition. Promising both stability and progress, the group ended up dragging its feet on core progressive goals, generating factional bickering while delivering little real policy output.

The philosophical roots run even deeper. Decades ago, during the pro-democracy struggles led by figures like former president Kim Young-sam against the authoritarian Yushin system under Park Chung-hee, voices within the opposition repeatedly floated theories of "centrist integration." Proponents argued for moderation and compromise to avoid severe backlashes. Yet historically, moderating demands in the face of entrenched authoritarian institutions failed to deliver meaningful democratization.

Historical precedent demonstrates that public livelihood and institutional reform cannot be separated into two neatly isolated bins. They are two wheels of the same cart. Tackling systemic corruption, curbing corporate abuses, and establishing administrative transparency are prerequisites for an economy that treats working citizens fairly. Attempting to deliver kitchen-table results without institutional repairs represents a failure to understand how governance actually works.

"Loyal Retainers" and the Poison of Factional Hate Politics

Underneath the policy dispute lies a raw struggle for power, often framed simply as "pro-Lee" (loyal to President Lee Jae-myung) versus "anti-Lee." Yet this binary label distorts reality. Many insiders point out an uncomfortable political truth: open opponents can be branded rebels, but it is sycophantic insiders posing as loyalists who pose the gravest threat to a presidency.

Across Korean political history, opportunistic courtiers have historically wrapped themselves in the banner of the leader to secure influence and patronage. A faction calling itself "New Lee" has weaponized aggressive rhetoric, launching virulent attacks against core party activists, moderate veterans, and prominent media figures under the guise of defending the president. By silencing principled internal criticism and blinding the administration to ground-level discontent, these performative loyalists act less like helpful allies and more like classic court flatterers who alienate the broader coalition.

There is a clear precedent for how a governing party must handle toxic fringe factions. During the preceding liberal administration of Moon Jae-in, progressive leaders eventually had to take decisive action to purge toxic online troll groups—derisively dubbed ttongpari (blowflies)—that spread venom and division within the party's own ranks. Cutting ties with these vitriolic networks was painful, but it was essential for maintaining party cohesion. Today, observers argue that President Lee and the ruling party leadership must show similar resolve: cleanly severing ties with abusive bad-faith actors rather than tolerating internal harassment for short-term factional muscle.

What Comes Next for the Ruling Coalition

Despite winning back state power and securing the presidency, the mood among the Democratic Party's grassroots base remains sour. Rank-and-file activists who endured years of bitter political battles still carry a deep sense of frustration. Instead of seeing their victory translate into structural reforms and internal democracy, they watch their party squabble over influence while indulging online outrage machines.

If the party hopes to maintain its governing mandate and prepare for upcoming legislative challenges, senior party figures like lawmaker Kim Min-seok and party elders must construct a coherent governing strategy. The path forward requires three clear moves:

  • Abandoning the artificial choice between reform and welfare: The party must link structural cleanups directly to everyday economic relief, treating reform as the foundation of public welfare rather than a distraction from it.
  • Drawing a hard boundary against toxic partisan influencers: Leadership must actively insulate policy decisions from the whims of hyper-partisan YouTube channels and abusive online mobs, returning policy formulation to formal party and legislative channels.
  • Opening genuine channels for internal party dialogue: Rather than labeling dissent as disloyalty, the leadership must build transparent platforms where both reformist and moderate wings can resolve disagreements through structured debate.

Suppressing internal problems under the cosmetic banner of unity only causes political decay. If the Democratic Party continues to let its internal factions clash over empty labels while stalling real legislative work, it risks turning its hard-won presidential mandate into an era of missed opportunities.
