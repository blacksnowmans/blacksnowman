Apple’s Surrender to Samsung Shows Who Really Holds Power in the AI Boom

November 19, 2026

On Thursday, November 19, 2026, Seoul woke up to the biting cold that reliably arrives with the Suneung—South Korea’s high-stakes national college entrance exam. To clear the morning commute for test-takers, the Korea Exchange pushed back its opening bell by an hour to 10:00 a.m. Inside the trading rooms, however, the mood was already frozen. Foreign investors dumped Korean shares for a fifth straight session, dragging the benchmark KOSPI index down below the psychologically charged 5,000 mark, while fresh warnings from Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller about a possible "big-step" interest rate hike hung over global risk appetite.

Yet right after the delayed 4:30 p.m. close, a report hit the wires that turned that gloom on its head. Apple—long the most ruthless price-squeezer in global electronics—was finalizing terms with Samsung Electronics for a four-to-five-year Long-Term Agreement (LTA) on DRAM memory chips starting in the first half of 2027. Rather than dictating rock-bottom prices, Apple was agreeing to lock in minimum purchase volumes at market-linked rates. In a single day filled with Anthropic’s record-shattering Wall Street debut, Texas power-grid clearances, and U.S.-China summitry in Shenzhen, that quiet capitulation in Seoul told the clearest story about where global capital is actually flowing.

Apple Blinks First in the Memory Chip Crunch

To grasp how thoroughly the semiconductor cycle has flipped, you have to understand Apple’s reputation in Asian supply chains. In Korean business parlance, Apple has long been the ultimate gap—the dominant buyer who dictates terms to subordinate suppliers. For years, Cupertino relied strictly on short-term memory contracts, keeping order windows tight so it could pit suppliers against one another and demand steeper discounts whenever an iPhone generation sold well.

Over a decade ago, Apple famously audited Micron’s books, dragged the U.S. chipmaker into arbitration claiming it had been charged more for NAND flash than rival buyers, and walked away with tens of millions of dollars in settlement money. As the Wall Street Journal has documented, Apple routinely demanded "most-favored-customer" pricing clauses and refused to take delivery of committed volumes during downturns. When Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra recalled the brutal 2023 slump—lamenting that customers had slashed prices to a third of prior levels and crippled industry investment—few in the market needed to guess which customer he meant.

The proposed Samsung deal reverses that power dynamic. Industry expectations point to binding, supplier-friendly clauses: hefty penalties for early cancellation, mandatory take-or-pay commitments for minimum volumes, and potentially advance cash payments. The root cause traces directly back to 2023, when Samsung Electronics’ semiconductor division (DS) posted a 15 trillion won (about $11.3 billion) loss and local rival SK Hynix lost 7 trillion won (about $5.3 billion). Both giants slammed the brakes on capital expenditure, setting the stage for today’s severe shortage.

Apple’s own strategy left it exposed. Because Apple makes its money selling consumer hardware rather than renting cloud compute, it hesitated to join rivals in loudly announcing massive AI data center buildouts, knowing such declarations would only bid up its own DRAM costs. When component prices surged anyway, Apple quietly approached a Chinese memory supplier to cut costs—only to find the Chinese firm bidding higher and sweeping up available supply. Facing the real threat of missing next year’s product launch schedules, Apple had to get in line.

It barely made the cut. SK Hynix has already signed LTAs with 14 customers—including global cloud giants—tying up more than half of its 2027–2028 capacity and selling out 2029 entirely. Micron has locked in 26 Strategic Customer Agreements. Samsung had already closed LTAs with 10 major clients as of the third quarter and was negotiating with 10 more, making it the last major chipmaker with meaningful spare capacity.

For Samsung, the deal is an ideal anchor. Locking in massive volumes over four to five years makes fab utilization and margins far easier to manage than juggling three-year deals with smaller buyers. Unlike hyperscale data center clients such as Amazon Web Services, Apple ships over 300 million consumer devices a year—roughly 200 million iPhones, 60 million iPads, and 20 million Macs—giving Samsung crucial portfolio diversification. The agreement reinforces an extraordinary memory upcycle:

  • Shifting wafer demand: Servers expanded from 38% of total DRAM demand in 2025 to 43% in 2026, squeezing mobile’s share down from 36% to 32%.
  • Record pricing and profits: Benchmark PC DRAM contract prices hit an all-time high of $27.50 in October, up 5.8% from September. In Q3 2026, Samsung’s quarterly operating profit topped 100 trillion won (about $75.1 billion) for the first time in company history as HBM4 high-bandwidth memory sales tripled from the prior quarter, while SK Hynix posted 83.33 trillion won (about $62.6 billion) in operating profit and pledged 100 trillion won in shareholder returns.
  • The "chipflation" paradox: Even though memory now accounts for roughly 40% of an iPhone’s manufacturing cost, fears of consumer sticker shock appear overblown. Counterpoint Research data for Q3 2026 shows global smartphone unit shipments fell 19% year-on-year, yet total revenue rose 3% to $114 billion. Buyers are abandoning cheap phones, not flagship models equipped with stronger on-device AI.

KOSPI at 5,000, Exam Rituals, and Anthropic’s $94 Billion Debut

While Samsung’s supply leverage strengthened behind closed doors, Seoul’s equity market struggled with near-term nerves. On November 19, the KOSPI slid 1.4% to close at 4,996, losing its grip on the 5,000 threshold, while the tech-heavy KOSDAQ fell roughly 1.2% to 654. The won strengthened slightly by 2.80 won to 1,330.90 per dollar thanks to exporter dollar-selling, even as the broader dollar index climbed. Foreign investors offloaded roughly 400 billion won (about $300 million) on the main board and another 200 billion won (about $150 million) on the KOSDAQ, leaving total turnover muted at 5.6 trillion won (about $4.2 billion).

Intraday charts highlighted how tightly Seoul remains tethered to global tech sentiment. After Taiwan’s stock market plunged nearly 5% intraday on foreign selling before paring losses, Korean equities traced the exact same V-shaped arc—sliding around 10:00 a.m. and rebounding around noon in lockstep with Nasdaq futures.

That sensitivity makes South Korea’s annual Suneung trading delay look increasingly out of touch. Pushing market hours to 10:00 a.m.–4:30 p.m. made sense when brokers ran paper order tickets through traffic; in an era of electronic matching engines, tinkering with clock times is pure bureaucratic inertia. Worse, local policymakers periodically float extending trading hours to boost volume—a misplaced priority akin to thinking longer office hours automatically make a company productive. Liquidity follows market transparency, strict disclosure rules, and sound corporate governance, not longer shifts at the exchange.

Just hours after Seoul closed, Wall Street demonstrated where genuine conviction still lives. AI developer Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 a share—6.4% above its $70–$78 target range—raising $94 billion across public and private placements (over $100 billion in total secured capital) at a $1.49 trillion valuation. The deal eclipsed SpaceX as the largest IPO in history and drew more than seven times the demand available.

For months, skeptics warned of an impending AI bubble, yet institutional capital poured in anyway. Crucially, pricing below the $2 trillion valuation once rumored leaves less froth in the stock, while the sixfold wave of unfulfilled orders creates immediate aftermarket buying support ahead of Anthropic’s fast-track entry into the Nasdaq-100 index in roughly 14 trading days. That $100 billion-plus war chest will not sit idle—it will flow directly into acquisitions, data centers, and advanced semiconductors, sustaining the exact demand wave locking up Korean memory capacity through 2029.

Texas Power Grids and the Reality of U.S.-China Chip Diplomacy

AI infrastructure bulls got a second boost from Texas, which hosts roughly 30% of U.S. data center capacity. Following strong Democratic gains in the recent gubernatorial election, investors feared a permitting moratorium over strained electricity and water supplies. Instead, Texas grid operator ERCOT moved faster than expected to resume power-supply approvals, clearing projects including a SpaceX power-generation facility. Because permitting sits with an independent committee focused on grid stability rather than the governor’s desk alone, key projects look set to win approval before the incoming governor takes office on January 19, 2027.

By contrast, investors hoping for a sweeping diplomatic reset at the APEC summit in Shenzhen, China, should temper their expectations. Chinese President Xi Jinping rolled out a warm welcome for U.S. President Donald Trump, and Fox News reported that Trump floated the possibility of allowing Nvidia to sell its latest Blackwell AI chips to China. Yet Washington’s negotiating list spans more than a dozen grievances—from intellectual property and trade deficits to rare earths—and advanced semiconductor controls remain its sharpest lever. Recent revelations of Chinese smuggling and sanctions workarounds have only hardened the U.S. view that cutting off chip access is essential to maintaining strategic leverage.

The severity of China’s silicon bottleneck is already visible in corporate numbers: Japanese neocloud provider Data Selection slashed its revenue guidance tied to Tencent data centers by a staggering 89%. Even with trilateral U.S.-China-Russia discussions touching on trade and a potential Ukraine ceasefire, clashing core interests mean Shenzhen is more likely to yield photo ops than a grand bargain.

A Partial Thaw in China’s Eight-Year Pop-Culture Freeze

For South Korean investors, the bigger diplomatic headline out of the summit was a long-awaited opening with Beijing. Since 2016—when South Korea deployed the U.S. THAAD missile defense system amid the political turmoil surrounding former President Park Geun-hye’s impeachment—China has maintained an unofficial ban on South Korean pop culture, known as the Hanhanryeong. That freeze held firm throughout Yoon Suk Yeol’s tenure, leaving Korean entertainment firms locked out of one of their largest natural markets.

After eight years and eight months, a South Korea-China summit finally produced a breakthrough: the conclusion of Phase 2 negotiations on services and investment under the bilateral Free Trade Agreement. Yet reading the two capitals’ official statements side by side reveals careful hedging. South Korea’s Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy announced the deal explicitly, highlighting Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) treatment for Korean films and games, non-discriminatory pre-censorship rules, and an expansion of co-production eligibility from movies alone to television dramas, animation, variety shows, and documentaries—alongside MFN access in legal, construction, and financial services and joint-venture law firms in China’s free economic zones.

China’s Foreign Ministry, however, merely confirmed the Phase 2 conclusion and cited President Xi’s call for "gradual" cultural exchanges, omitting specifics. Neither side formally declared the Hallyu ban lifted, and key revenue drivers—live K-pop concerts and Korean celebrities appearing on Chinese broadcasts—remain blocked. Skeptics rightly worry Beijing could keep informal non-tariff barriers in place, but securing written MFN protections is still a meaningful institutional first step.

What to Watch Next

  • The fine print on Apple and Samsung: Watch for confirmation of cancellation penalties and advance-payment terms in the DRAM LTA, and whether benchmark PC and server memory prices extend past October’s $27.50 record.
  • Anthropic’s first two weeks on Wall Street: Track whether unfulfilled IPO demand fuels sustained post-listing gains into its Nasdaq-100 inclusion in roughly 14 trading days, and how quickly that capital turns into server orders.
  • December’s Fed meeting and Texas permits: Monitor whether Fed Governor Christopher Waller’s big-step rate warning translates into hawkish action at the December FOMC meeting, and how many Texas data center projects clear ERCOT before the January 19, 2027 gubernatorial inauguration.
  • Beijing’s follow-through: Look for actual Chinese import approvals for Korean dramas and games—rather than diplomatic phrasing—to tell whether the Phase 2 FTA deal truly ends an eight-year chill.
