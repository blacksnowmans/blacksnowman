The "Hyena" Scandal: A Ruling Party Lawmaker’s Outburst and the Fragile State of Korean Political Discourse

November 13, 2026

The hallways of the National Assembly in Seoul are rarely quiet, but on November 12, the air grew exceptionally thick with tension. As a throng of reporters and photographers crowded around Representative Choi Min-hee, a senior member of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the usual choreographed dance between the press and the powerful broke down. Visibly irritated by the presence of the media, Choi did not merely ask for space; she lashed out, labeling the journalists a "pack of hyenas."

In the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, where the ruling party is currently navigating the complexities of the Lee Jae-myung administration, such a remark is more than a simple gaffe. It is a spark in a powder keg. Choi, a member of the party’s Supreme Committee—the top decision-making body—targeted the very people who serve as the eyes and ears of the public. The backlash was instantaneous, resulting in an unprecedented scene: the party leadership bowing in apology and the launch of an internal ethics investigation against one of their own most influential figures.

For an international observer, this might look like a typical political spat. However, to understand why this matters, one must look at the specific history of the actors involved and the precarious balance the Democratic Party is trying to maintain as it governs under President Lee Jae-myung. The incident reveals a widening chasm between the party’s rhetoric on democratic values and the increasingly aggressive "fandom politics" that governs its frontline interactions.

From Media Reformer to Press Antagonist

The irony of Choi Min-hee’s outburst is not lost on the Korean public. Before entering the halls of power, Choi was a celebrated figure in South Korea’s civil society movements. She was often referred to as the "Godmother of Media Reform," having spent decades fighting for press freedom and against the censorship of previous authoritarian regimes. For her to turn around and compare working journalists to scavenging animals represents, for many, a jarring metamorphosis that often accompanies the acquisition of power.

The phrase she used—"a pack of hyenas"—carries a particular sting in the Korean linguistic context. While political supporters of the ruling party often use the derogatory term giregi (a portmanteau of "journalist" and "trash") to dismiss critical reporting, Choi’s use of "hyenas" suggests a predatory, sub-human nature to the act of reporting. It implies that the press is not seeking the truth, but merely looking for a carcass to tear apart.

The reaction from the media was swift and institutional. The Korea Press Photographers Association and the News Photographers Association issued a joint statement condemning the remarks as an assault on the dignity of their profession. They argued that their presence at the National Assembly is not a matter of personal curiosity but a fulfillment of the public’s right to know. By November 16, the Association plans to file a formal criminal defamation complaint against Choi with the police. In South Korea, defamation can be a criminal offense, and for a sitting lawmaker to be sued by the very press corps she oversees is a significant blow to her political standing.

The Ruling Party’s Rapid Damage Control

The Democratic Party, currently the ruling power in South Korea, is acutely aware that it cannot afford a war with the press—at least not one this crude. Kim Min-seok, the Chairman of the Democratic Party and a key ally of President Lee Jae-myung, took the rare step of issuing a formal apology on behalf of the party on the morning of November 12. "We recognize the seriousness of the recent remarks and the controversy they have caused," Kim stated, signaling that the leadership would not be shielding Choi from the consequences of her words.

Kim’s decision to immediately refer Choi to the party’s Ethics Inspection Group (the internal body responsible for disciplining members) is a "red alert" signal. In the past, the party has often been criticized for "circling the wagons" and protecting its own. However, the current political climate is different. The Lee Jae-myung administration is working to solidify its legitimacy and implement a sweeping legislative agenda. Having a high-profile member of the Supreme Committee generating headlines for "hate speech" against journalists is a distraction the Blue House—the South Korean presidential office—cannot tolerate.

Inside the party, the mood is one of frustration. While the Democratic Party’s core base often enjoys "strong" rhetoric against the media, more moderate members worry that such outbursts alienate middle-of-the-road voters. They see Choi’s behavior as symptomatic of a "superiority complex" that can settle in after a major election victory. The concern is that if the ruling party is seen as arrogant and dismissive of democratic institutions like the press, it will lose the moral high ground it claimed during the "Candlelight Revolution" that eventually led to the current political order.

A Second Blunder and the Blue House Rebuttal

As if the "hyena" comment were not enough, Choi Min-hee doubled down on her controversial streak by making a startling claim regarding the nation’s highest office. In a televised appearance, she suggested that President Lee Jae-myung was personally opposed to the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. To the uninitiated, this might sound like a minor procedural detail, but in the context of current Korean politics, it is a bombshell.

Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence, remains a deeply polarizing figure. The Democratic Party and President Lee Jae-myung have framed their administration as a corrective to the "misrule" of the Yoon era. For Choi to suggest that the President is "opposed" to the very process that defined his rise to power—or is perhaps seeking some form of political reconciliation with the imprisoned Mr. Yoon—sent shockwaves through the party base.

The response from the Blue House was unusually blunt. Within 40 minutes of Choi’s broadcast, the presidential office issued a statement of "strong regret." They clarified that the President’s stance on constitutional order and the legal reckoning for Mr. Yoon’s actions has never wavered. This immediate public correction of a ruling party Supreme Committee member by the President’s office is a sign of how isolated Choi has become. It suggests that her "loose cannon" style of communication is now seen as a liability to the President's own messaging.

The Opposition Strikes Back

The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has seized upon the Choi Min-hee scandal to paint the ruling party as a group that has lost its way. PPP leaders have been quick to point out the hypocrisy of a party that preaches "press freedom" while its leaders call reporters scavengers. They have called for Choi’s immediate resignation from her party posts and have even suggested that the National Assembly should take up a formal motion for her expulsion.

The opposition’s strategy is clear: link Choi’s behavior to the broader culture of the Democratic Party. They argue that the DP has become a "party of extremists" where the loudest, most aggressive voices are rewarded by the "fandom" base, while the actual work of governance and civil discourse is ignored. For the PPP, this is a golden opportunity to win back moderate voters who may be tired of the constant political warfare and the perceived arrogance of the ruling camp.

This situation also hampers the possibility of "bipartisan cooperation" (협치) in the National Assembly. With several major economic and social welfare bills pending, the atmosphere of mutual hostility fueled by these remarks makes it nearly impossible for the two sides to sit down and negotiate. The "hyena" comment has essentially frozen the legislative process as both sides retreat into their respective ideological corners.

What to Watch Next

The fallout from Choi Min-hee’s comments will likely dominate the political news cycle for weeks to come. Here are the concrete developments to monitor:

  • The Legal Battle: On November 16, the Photojournalists Association is expected to file its complaint. How the police and the prosecution handle this case will be a major test. If the case proceeds to a formal indictment, Choi’s political career could be in jeopardy.
  • The Ethics Inspection: The Democratic Party’s internal investigation will be a barometer of the party’s internal discipline. If they issue a light warning, they risk further public backlash. If they suspend her party rights, it would be a significant demotion for a Supreme Committee member.
  • The "Fandom" Reaction: Watch the ruling party’s online communities. If the core base rallies around Choi and attacks the party leadership for "betraying" her, it could signal a deeper rift within the Democratic Party between the pragmatic leadership and the hardline supporters.
  • Blue House Relations: The speed with which the Blue House corrected Choi’s remarks about President Lee and Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol suggests a cooling of relations. Any future appearances or appointments involving Choi will be scrutinized to see if she has been permanently sidelined.

Ultimately, this scandal is a reminder that in South Korea’s vibrant but volatile democracy, words have immediate and heavy consequences. For the ruling Democratic Party, the task now is to prove that it can govern with the dignity and respect for institutions that it once demanded of its predecessors. If it fails to rein in the rhetoric of its most senior members, it may find that the "hyenas" it so fears are, in fact, the voters waiting at the ballot box.
