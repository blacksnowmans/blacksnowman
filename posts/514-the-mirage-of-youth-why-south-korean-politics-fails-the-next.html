The Mirage of Youth: Why South Korean Politics Fails the Next Generation

September 30, 2026

Whenever South Korean political parties hold a press conference to introduce their new recruits, the same buzzwords are inevitably trotted out: "freshness," "future generation," and "new energy." But look past the glossy brochures and carefully curated social media photos, and the reality of youth politics in Korea is far grimmer. The barrier to entry for young people isn't a lack of talent or ambition—it is a rigid, suffocating cartel that forces them to choose between irrelevance and becoming a carbon copy of the establishment.

As Seongnam City Council member Lee Se-mi recently noted, much of local politics in South Korea has devolved into a form of "family play." For young politicians, success rarely comes from legislative innovation or grassroots policy work. Instead, it comes from curating proximity to power—flaunting ties to a "hyung-nim" (a powerful, older mentor figure) who can bypass bureaucratic red tape to handle constituent complaints. It is a system built not on democratic deliberation, but on patronage and personal connections. We must ask: Is this the "new energy" we were promised?

The Culture of "Hyung-nim" Leadership

In the current South Korean political ecosystem, the primary skill taught to young aspirants is not how to write policy, but how to become subservient. The "hyung-nim" system is pervasive. When a constituent has a problem, a savvy young politician doesn't seek a systemic fix; they brag about having drinks with a high-ranking National Assembly member or a powerful local party chairman, promising that their "elder brother" will pull some strings to solve it.

This transforms politics from a public service into a display of private influence. Young politicians quickly learn that their political survival depends entirely on their ability to please these gatekeepers. If they want a nomination for an election, they must stay in the "line" of their mentor. This creates a cycle where the very people expected to disrupt the status quo are the ones most incentivized to protect it. They are not merely failing to challenge the old guard; they are becoming its most eager accomplices.

The "Selection" System vs. Grassroots Empowerment

A critical issue in Korea is the total absence of a healthy cultivation system for new talent. Most young people don't enter politics through organic community engagement or by building a unique personal narrative. Instead, they are "selected" (or "tapped") by senior politicians. This "kan-taek" (royal selection) culture is the antithesis of democratic growth.

Under this model, independent voices are systematically filtered out. Only those who mirror the logic of the establishment and demonstrate absolute loyalty are promoted. In a system where "purity of loyalty" is a higher political asset than expertise in local governance or economic policy, it is no surprise that youth politics feels scripted and hollow. This is not an accident—it is a structural barrier. Established politicians view independent-minded young leaders as a threat, so they create a bottleneck that only admits those willing to serve as appendages to the machine.

A Note on Real-World Stakes: We must be honest about the cost of this culture. A young politician who has never solved a minor neighborhood issue on their own—who relies entirely on a mentor's favor—is fundamentally unprepared to design the future of a nation. They are not acting as politicians; they are acting as assistants.

Lessons from Denmark: The Power of Bottom-Up Nominations

To understand what is missing in Korea, we can look at models like Denmark, where a bottom-up nomination process forces candidates to answer to local party members and voters rather than central party elites. In this system, there is no "hyung-nim" to hide behind. If a young candidate wants to run, they must secure support from their community by presenting a vision that actually addresses the residents' needs.

In South Korea, the process is starkly top-down. Nominations are often dictated by a handful of power brokers in the capital. While young Danish politicians are busy building deep roots in their districts, Korean youth politicians are often relegated to roles like "party spokesperson," serving primarily as messengers for central power figures. Without a fundamental shift in how candidates are nominated, injecting "young blood" into the party will do nothing to cure its structural aging.

Beyond "Youth" as a Marketing Label

The failure to integrate young people into politics is also an educational failure. In Korea, politics is treated as a taboo or an abstract, unreachable concept in school. When students are not trained in debate, compromise, and policy-making through local "youth assemblies," they grow into a generation that views political engagement with cynicism or fear. This apathy is the lifeblood of the current establishment, which prefers a disinterested public over an engaged one.

Furthermore, the media and political parties often treat "youth" as a demographic category rather than a generation with distinct lived experiences. When issues like university application system failures or job market volatility arise, the establishment often treats them as administrative glitches to be smoothed over with temporary fixes, failing to see the structural inequality that drives the frustration. True communication would involve analyzing the systemic contradictions that leave young people behind, not just offering condescending, one-off support programs.

What to Watch Next

If we want to fix this, we need to stop romanticizing the "youth" label and start demanding a change in the mechanics of power. Here is what to look for in the coming months:

  • Changes in Nomination Rules: Watch for internal movements within major parties (such as the conservative People Power Party or the liberal Democratic Party) that push for open primaries or decentralized candidate selection. Anything less is just a reshuffling of the same old gatekeepers.
  • Local-Level Policy Success: Look for young politicians who prioritize "field politics"—those who have documented successes in local infrastructure, safety, or community welfare without relying on the influence of a "hyung-nim."
  • The Growth of Youth Assemblies: Watch whether local governments actually grant young people budget-making authority or real policy influence, rather than keeping these programs as tokenistic photo opportunities.

Real change requires the courage to dismantle the cartel. True generational shift isn't about how many people under 40 are in a party; it's about whether those people have the freedom to act independently of the status quo. If the system remains closed to those who haven't pledged loyalty to a master, then the system itself is the problem—and it is time for it to be overhauled.
