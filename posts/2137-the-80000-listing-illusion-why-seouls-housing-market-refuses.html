The 80,000-Listing Illusion: Why Seoul’s Housing Market Refuses to Cool Down

November 09, 2026

Imagine walking past the storefronts of real estate agencies in Seoul today. For the first time in years, the glass windows are plastered with white paper notices advertising available apartments. Over the past three months, the volume of properties listed for sale in South Korea’s capital has surged by 33 percent, pushing total inventory past the 80,000 mark. In almost any major housing market in North America or Europe, a 33 percent jump in supply over a single quarter would trigger immediate price cuts. Sellers would compete for scarce buyers, asking prices would soften, and real estate coverage would herald an impending downturn.

Yet inside Seoul’s notoriously stubborn property ecosystem, something far more complex is unfolding. Apartment prices are not falling in tandem with rising inventory. Instead, across broad swathes of the metropolis, prices remain stubbornly firm or continue to inch upward. While headline reports point to a "slowing pace of appreciation," the overall market trajectory remains positive. The capital is caught in a high-stakes standoff: homeowners refuse to discount their properties, while prospective buyers sit on the sidelines waiting for a correction that has yet to materialize. Understanding why 80,000 listed properties have failed to bring down prices requires looking beneath the superficial numbers—into recent tax overhauls, geographic splits within the city, and a crucial tax deadline approaching next spring.

The Standoff Behind the 80,000 Accumulating Listings

The catalyst behind this sudden flood of inventory traces directly back to the government’s August 3 tax reform package. Designed by President Lee Jae-myung’s administration to stimulate transaction volume and encourage multi-home owners to unload excess properties, the policy shift prompted a wave of listings. Homeowners who had been holding off on selling recalculated their holding and capital gains tax liabilities and decided to test the waters. Within 90 days, Seoul’s active apartment listings expanded by a staggering 33 percent.

However, an increase in listed properties does not automatically translate to a supply glut of motivated sellers. A closer examination of the 80,000 active listings reveals a fundamental mismatch between quantity and intent. The vast majority of these listings do not represent urgent distress sales. Instead, owners have placed their properties on the market at top-of-the-market asking prices, adopting an attitude of "I will sell only if I get my price."

This dynamic has created what local market analysts call a "transaction cliff"—a sharp contraction in trading volume caused by a massive spread between asking prices and bid prices. Buyers look at the headlines of surging listings and expect deep discounts, choosing to wait out the market. Sellers look at long-term urban supply constraints and hold out for premium valuations. Because neither side is willing to blink, transactions have slowed to a crawl, causing unsold inventory to stack up on paper without driving down execution prices.

Deciphering the Micro-Divergence Across Seoul’s Districts

To understand why prices have not dropped, one must look closely at how price statistics are reported. Media coverage frequently emphasizes that Seoul's housing price growth is decelerating. It is true that weekly price gains have slowed from 0.1 percent down to 0.03 percent. However, a slowing growth rate is still growth; it means prices are rising at a more modest tempo, not turning negative. Interpreting a 33 percent inventory increase as a sign of an imminent crash misreads the reality on the ground.

Furthermore, Seoul's real estate market is far from uniform. A sharp divergence is opening up between the city's elite enclaves and its outer neighborhoods:

  • The Gangnam 3-Gu (Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa): In these wealthy districts south of the Han River—traditionally the bellwether of South Korean property sentiment—inventory buildup has finally begun to produce isolated discounted trades. Some high-end complexes are seeing modest price adjustments as owners adjust expectations after a prolonged rally.
  • Outer Districts and Mid-Tier Enclaves: Outside the prime southern districts, the picture looks vastly different. Supported by robust end-user demand from families looking for primary residences, apartments in middle-income neighborhoods are holding firm, with several complexes continuing to log record-high transaction prices.

Historically, price adjustments in Seoul follow one of two patterns: either price softness in Gangnam eventually ripples outward to the rest of the city, or outer districts catch up to fill the valuation gap. With Gangnam showing early signs of fatigue while outer districts remain resilient, the broader market remains in a delicate holding pattern.

The Looming Spring Test: Tax Day and the Ghost of 2021

Market participants and industry analysts agree that the true test of Seoul's housing market will arrive next spring. The key variable is the June 1 assessment date for the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (known locally as Jongbuse), South Korea's national tax levied on high-value property holdings.

As the June 1 threshold approaches, holding costs for multi-property owners will become concrete rather than theoretical. For individuals holding multiple high-value apartments in Seoul, annual tax bills can run into tens of thousands of dollars (tens of millions of Korean won). Between the first quarter of next year and May 31, owners who are currently "testing the market" with high asking prices will face a hard choice: lower their prices to complete a sale before the tax assessment date, or commit to paying heavy holding taxes for another year.

Yet market veterans urge caution before assuming that a wave of spring fire sales is guaranteed, pointing to the lessons of 2021. During that cycle, aggressive tax hikes and strict mortgage rules under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency similarly led to a temporary spike in apartment listings. Analysts at the time predicted an inevitable price collapse. Instead, when homeowners realized buyers would not meet their asking prices, they did not slash prices; they simply withdrew their listings entirely. Many opted to gift properties to family members or convert them into long-term lease arrangements, causing available inventory to vanish almost overnight and driving prices back up.

Market Takeaway: A high volume of active listings only depresses prices when sellers are forced to liquidate. If owners possess the financial capacity to withdraw listings and absorb tax penalties, inventory can evaporate just as quickly as it accumulated.

The current government under President Lee Jae-myung is working to resolve the structural distortions and tax entanglements inherited from previous policy regimes. However, for these regulatory adjustments to achieve genuine market stabilization, the financial penalties for holding unsold excess property must be heavy enough to overcome sellers' willingness to hold out.

What to Watch Next

For international investors, expats, and prospective buyers navigating Seoul's housing market, the coming months will provide critical signals regarding where prices are ultimately headed. Key indicators to monitor include:

  • Listing Withdrawals vs. Closed Sales (Q1 to May): Watch whether the current inventory of 80,000 properties begins clearing through actual price-discounted transactions, or if listings begin dropping abruptly as owners withdraw properties ahead of the June 1 tax date.
  • Transmission from Gangnam to Outer Districts: Track whether the modest price adjustments currently observed in Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa spread to working- and middle-class districts, or if outer-district demand remains strong enough to absorb citywide supply.
  • Execution of Tax Policy: Pay close attention to any fine-tuning of the Jongbuse holding tax implementation guidelines by the Lee Jae-myung administration as the spring deadline nears. Any signal of regulatory easing could instantly encourage sellers to pull inventory off the market.

For now, Seoul’s 80,000 apartment listings represent a tense truce between buyers and sellers rather than a market breakdown. Until actual transaction prices begin trending downward across all districts, the headline inventory surge remains an illusion of supply rather than a guarantee of affordability.
