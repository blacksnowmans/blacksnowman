Closing Ranks: Inside the Democratic Party’s Push to Cement Power, Align Policies, and Break South Korea’s Legislative Gridlock

November 13, 2026

Behind closed doors at the presidential residence on November 12, a three-hour dinner took place between President Lee Jae-myung and top leadership of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), including party leader Kim Min-seok and floor leader Han Byung-do. The mood was reportedly warm and relaxed, with President Lee sharing anecdotes from his own past experience leading the party to console Kim over the heavy burden of party management. Yet behind the relaxed atmosphere lay an urgent political mandate: closing ranks between the executive branch and the legislature to extinguish rumors of internal division and press ahead with a stalled policy agenda.

For observers of South Korean politics, the timing of this dinner was anything but coincidental. The ruling party has found itself squeezed between an uncooperative opposition, persistent debates within its own ranks over tax reform, and growing public frustration over slow judicial proceedings involving key figures from the previous government. By projecting absolute unity with President Lee, the DPK leadership sought to send a clear message: the party and the administration are operating as a single unit, and they are preparing to use their legislative majority to break through the opposition's blockade.

Alignment at the Top: Resolving the Real Estate Tax Debate

For weeks, the conservative main opposition People Power Party (PPP) had tried to drive a wedge between the presidential office and the DPK, claiming that the two sides were offering contradictory visions on key economic policies. Floor Leader Han Byung-do addressed these attacks head-on, repeatedly telling reporters that there was "zero friction" between the party and the presidential office. The three-hour dinner at the Blue House served as a formal political declaration to cement that single front.

The most significant internal policy issue settled by this high-level alignment is the fate of the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (often referred to in Korea as Jongbuse). The tax, designed to curb real estate speculation by levying heavy duties on high-value property owners, has long been a lightning rod in South Korean politics. Lawmakers representing districts in Seoul and the surrounding metropolitan area—where home prices are highest and voter sensitivity to property taxes is acute—had been quietly lobbying for tax relief to soothe discontent ahead of upcoming electoral battles.

Party leader Kim Min-seok effectively put an end to those internal debates. Emphasizing that "consistency is the cornerstone of effective real estate policy," Kim drew a firm line against further tax rollbacks. This stance reaffirms the DPK’s core economic principle against granting tax cuts to the wealthy, prioritizing long-term market stability over short-term political expediency. While some metropolitan lawmakers remain concerned about local voter pushback, the party leadership made it clear that maintaining credibility and policy discipline alongside President Lee’s national agenda takes precedence over localized political calculations.

The Legislative Standoff: Setting a Line in the Sand for November 19

While the ruling camp unified its internal strategy, the situation inside the National Assembly reached a boiling point. A scheduled plenary session on November 12 collapsed entirely after the opposition People Power Party refused to attend. Floor Leader Han Byung-do unleashed a blistering attack on the conservative opposition, accusing them of turning their backs on urgent livelihood issues while concentrating solely on filibusters and out-of-parliament protests.

Han went so far as to claim that the PPP was stalling the legislature while waiting for instructions from former president Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached, removed from office, and is currently incarcerated. In response to the legislative deadlock, the DPK declared a hard deadline: it will force open a plenary session on November 19 to pass long-overdue livelihood and budget measures, with or without opposition participation.

Two major legislative initiatives highlight the political and moral stakes of this upcoming session:

  • The Democracy Contributor Act: Long a priority for the DPK, this bill aims to honor and provide official state recognition to individuals who suffered or sacrificed during South Korea's historical pro-democracy movements. DPK leaders recently met with families of fallen activists who have been holding sit-in protests at the National Assembly. While the PPP opposes the measure—arguing that it risks honoring individuals previously convicted under the National Security Act—Kim Min-seok publicly apologized to the families for past delays and vowed that the party would pass the law on November 19 regardless of political resistance.
  • The Equality Act: Lawmaker Park Joo-min has reignited the decades-long debate over general anti-discrimination legislation by introducing the Equality Act. To overcome strong resistance from conservative Protestant groups—a powerful voting block in Korean politics—Park carefully drafted the bill to exclude criminal penalties or restrictions on religious sermons. Highlighting his own Christian background, Park framed the legislation as fully aligned with Christian ethics, setting a target to pass the landmark human rights law in the first half of next year.

Reforming Financial Power and Reclaiming the Youth Vote

Beyond high-stakes ideological battles, the DPK is attempting to show tangible governance results by tackling structural economic reform and targeted welfare expansion. Primary among these is a major overhaul of South Korea's massive financial holding companies, led by DPK lawmaker Kwon Chil-seung.

The proposed legislation aims to eliminate the entrenched practice of financial company CEOs effectively granting themselves consecutive reappointments through friendly board appointments. The DPK plan proposes requiring selection committees to consist entirely of 100% independent directors and expanding shareholder oversight rights. By breaking up the self-perpetuating power structures of major financial conglomerates, the party hopes to increase corporate transparency and align financial governance with international standards.

Simultaneously, the DPK is directing significant fiscal resources toward younger demographics. The party announced a 53.5% year-on-year increase in budget allocations for youth employment and asset-building programs. In Seoul, municipal DPK lawmakers have taken aim at systemic failures in the "Youth Safe Housing" initiative, demanding immediate city intervention to resolve widespread deposit non-refund incidents that have left young tenants financially stranded.

This macro-to-micro approach extends deep into regional politics. DPK local committees are actively campaigning for specialized economic zones in 김포 (Gimpo), advocating for mandatory living wage ordinances in 대구 (Daegu), and demanding federal support for impoverished urban neighborhoods in 대전 (Daejeon). By pairing structural financial reform with localized policy intervention, the DPK is trying to demonstrate that its legislative dominance yields concrete economic benefits.

Clash with the Judiciary: Trial Delays and Internal Party Friction

Perhaps the most politically explosive front opened by the DPK involves its escalating conflict with the judiciary. Ruling party leadership has launched sharp attacks against Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, accusing the courts of intentionally delaying trials involving high-profile officials from the previous administration—specifically raising alarms over the potential release of former prime minister Han Duck-soo.

Floor Leader Han Byung-do openly questioned whether Chief Justice Cho’s judiciary was attempting to facilitate a resurrection of "anti-democratic forces." With frustration growing over slow legal proceedings, senior DPK lawmakers have floated the idea of launching a formal parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court management, while hardline supporters have demanded Cho’s immediate impeachment.

This judicial offensive, however, has exposed delicate fractures within the DPK itself. Lawmaker Park Hee-seung sparked intense controversy among the party's grassroots base during a heated exchange with supporters, where he argued that impeaching sitting judges is a practice seen only in "underdeveloped countries." Although Park later clarified that he was urging procedural caution, his comments drew fierce backlash from party loyalists who view judicial reform as an urgent necessity.

This internal friction underscores the political tightrope the DPK leadership must walk. It must satisfy a highly motivated base that demands swift accountability for figures tied to the previous administration while simultaneously maintaining institutional credibility and avoiding accusations of judicial overreach.

What to Watch Next as Year-End Battles Loom

As South Korea enters the crucial end-of-year legislative window, the DPK’s ability to turn its unified executive-legislative platform into concrete results will be put to the test. Over the coming weeks, political observers should focus on three key indicators:

  • The November 19 Plenary Session: Watch whether the DPK succeeds in convening the session unilaterally and whether it fulfills its pledge to pass the Democracy Contributor Act amidst expected PPP filibusters and procedural disruptions.
  • Budget Negotiations and Tax Consistency: Pay close attention to whether the leadership maintains its strict stance against Jongbuse real estate tax cuts as lawmakers face increasing pressure during final national budget reconciliations.
  • The Judiciary Battle: Monitor whether the DPK moves forward with a formal parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, and how the leadership manages internal dissent over judicial reform strategies.
