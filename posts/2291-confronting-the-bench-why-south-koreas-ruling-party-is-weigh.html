Confronting the Bench: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Weighing the Impeachment of the Chief Justice

November 13, 2026

Inside the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly in Seoul, the political atmosphere has reached an acute boiling point. Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are locked in fierce debate over an extraordinary constitutional showdown: whether to move forward with the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the head of the country’s Supreme Court. At the heart of this confrontation lies a rapidly closing window to block the potential release from custody of Han Duck-soo, the former prime minister who served during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol.

For observers outside South Korea, the prospect of a parliament moving to impeach the head of the judiciary might look like an institutional breakdown. Yet within the ruling camp, it is framed as the ultimate test of accountability. With the National Assembly scheduled to convene a pivotal plenary session on the 19th, lawmakers argue that the country faces a historic fork in the road: either exert legislative power to check a judiciary perceived as protecting past establishment figures, or risk letting public trust in the legal system crumble entirely.

The Flashpoint: Han Duck-soo’s Release and Judicial Pushback

Public outrage ignited in recent days as legal procedures signaled that Han Duck-soo could soon walk free. Han, who served as prime minister under Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol before the latter was impeached, removed from office, and subsequently imprisoned, is viewed by the ruling party and its base as a core figure bearing heavy responsibility for the governance failures and abuses of that era. To see the courts pave the way for his release has been interpreted by supporters of the ruling Democratic Party not merely as judicial leniency, but as outright resistance to post-impeachment accountability.

Compounding this anger are defensive rumblings reported from within the judiciary itself, where some judges reportedly lamented that "impeaching judges is something only backward countries do." That attitude drew a blistering response from Representative Noh Jong-myun, a prominent Democratic Party lawmaker. Noh countered that impeaching errant judges is not the mark of a developing nation; rather, a country becomes backward when it leaves judges who commit impeachable acts completely unchecked.

Under the South Korean Constitution, impeachment is the primary institutional mechanism granted to the legislature to rein in judicial overreach and abuse. Proponents of impeachment argue that if Chief Justice Cho allows political considerations rather than strict adherence to the law to facilitate Han Duck-soo’s release, the judiciary is manufacturing the very grounds for its own impeachment. As Noh pointed out, the upcoming plenary session on the 19th represents the "golden hour" for parliamentary action. Missing that window would effectively forfeit the political and procedural leverage needed to challenge the court's direction.

Internal Debates: Parliamentary Inquiry versus Direct Impeachment

While the sentiment among grassroots party supporters is overwhelmingly in favor of confronting the judiciary, the Democratic Party’s internal deliberations have been anything but simple. A crucial party caucus meeting on November 5 exposed sharp tactical divisions between the leadership and reform-minded backbenchers.

Initially, party leaders favored launching a formal parliamentary investigation—known in South Korea as a gukjeong josa—into judicial conduct before considering any punitive measures. However, this cautious roadmap met fierce resistance from three first-term lawmakers, including Representative Noh. They argued that a parliamentary inquiry would inevitably degenerate into a toothless formality. Expecting institutional non-cooperation and deliberate delays, Noh warned that a protracted inquiry would simply bleed away political momentum, allowing the judiciary to run out the clock.

Rather than hiding behind an inquiry, Noh insisted, the party needed to pinpoint clear violations and move straight toward impeachment. This internal pushback was fostered in part by President Lee Jae-myung, who had explicitly urged Democratic Party lawmakers to "debate fiercely" among themselves rather than settle for quiet consensus. The debate culminated in a pivotal three-hour dinner meeting between President Lee and the party's top parliamentary leadership, a session that helped steady the party's strategy and reaffirm a more decisive posture against judicial stonewalling.

Yet internal discipline remains a critical challenge. Representative Han Min-soo has repeatedly underscored that the arithmetic of a successful impeachment vote hinges entirely on maintaining a disciplined legislative bloc. While the Democratic Party and its allies hold enough seats to pass an impeachment motion, Han cautioned against wavering members who might calculate their personal political interests over collective principle. For lawmakers such as Han Min-soo, Choi Min-hee, and Lee Sung-yoon, presenting a united front on the Assembly floor is the only viable path to executing a fast-track strategy.

The Legal Dilemma: Proving Constitutional Violations against Cho Hee-dae

Targeting the head of the judiciary carries immense institutional stakes, and both Noh Jong-myun and Han Min-soo acknowledge that political determination alone cannot substitute for legal rigor. Impeachment cannot function as an emotional outlet; it demands verifiable, documented proof that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae violated the Constitution or statutory law in a manner grave enough to warrant removal.

Political commentators, including broadcast host Kim Eo-jun, have voiced concern about the potential for a severe political backlash. An aggressive campaign against the Supreme Court risks alienating moderate voters if framed successfully by the conservative opposition, the People Power Party, as an unconstitutional assault on judicial independence. Noh and Han concede this danger, maintaining that moving without airtight legal evidence would be a trap. However, they equally reject the notion that fear of backlash should justify inaction.

"Impeachment without clear evidence is a fast track to defeat, but failing to pursue impeachment when evidence exists is a cowardly retreat."

The strategic debate therefore revolves around pace and legitimacy. A parliamentary inquiry might help gather documentary evidence, but its timeline is painfully slow. Direct impeachment requires moving at full speed while simultaneously presenting the case to the public to demonstrate that the judiciary is not an untouchable sanctuary above the law.

What Comes Next: The 19th and the Battle for Accountability

The coming days will test the limits of legislative power in South Korea's evolving political landscape. As the Democratic Party prepares for the decisive plenary session on the 19th, observers should watch several concrete developments:

  • The Official Party Platform: Whether Democratic Party floor leaders formally adopt the motion to impeach Chief Justice Cho as a binding party line, or whether internal dissent forces a phased approach linked to judicial rulings on Han Duck-soo.
  • Party Cohesion and Vote Counts: How effectively the leadership reins in centrist and cautious lawmakers to ensure no unforced defections take place during a high-stakes roll-call vote.
  • Judicial Reactions: Any formal statements or procedural steps taken by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s Supreme Court in response to the pressure building from the legislature.
  • Public Sentiment: Whether civic groups and broader public opinion rally behind the impeachment push as an extension of post-Yoon democratic accountability, or whether concerns over judicial stability begin to complicate the ruling party's narrative.

What unfolds on the National Assembly floor on the 19th will be far more than routine partisan maneuvering. It will signal whether South Korea’s ruling majority is prepared to fundamentally redefine the boundaries of judicial accountability, asserting that even the country's highest judges remain subject to the ultimate authority of the Constitution.
