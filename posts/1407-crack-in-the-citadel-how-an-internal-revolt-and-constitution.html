Crack in the Citadel: How an Internal Revolt and Constitutional Disputes Are Shaking South Korea’s Ruling Camp

October 22, 2026

Barely twenty-four hours after law enforcement officers executed a surprise search-and-seizure warrant on his office, Lee Jong-won sat down in front of a live camera. As the head of Sisatapa, one of South Korea’s most influential political YouTube channels, Lee had spent years in the political trenches. He had rallied hundreds of thousands of online followers, mobilized grassroots donors, and fought around the clock to help elect President Lee Jae-myung. But on this night, there were no defenses or partisan excuses. Visibly furious, Lee declared a total break with the administration he had helped create.

"I no longer have a shred of hope," Lee told his viewers. "I have zero desire to defend these people anymore." Vowing to bring down what he called a corrupt inner circle, he promised to release hard evidence exposing backroom dealings between presidential aides and regional developers. It was a stunning spectacle: a key media foot-soldier of South Korea's progressive movement turning his weapons directly at the heart of executive power.

To outside observers, YouTube commentators turning on politicians might look like minor internet drama. In South Korea, however, hyper-partisan digital platforms function as critical mobilization engines for major political parties. When a figure like Lee Jong-won publicly breaks ranks, it signals a far deeper structural crisis. Beneath the surface of the Lee Jae-myung administration lies a fraught power dynamic marked by internal factional purges, questionable constitutional maneuvers, and a widening rift between the government and the movement that brought it to power.

From Loyal Megaphones to Internal Whistleblowers

The immediate spark for this public fallout was the law enforcement raid on Lee Jong-won’s studio, an act he categorized not as a legitimate legal investigation, but as a political hit job targeted at non-mainstream elements within the progressive camp. Yet the ammunition Lee threatens to use goes far beyond personal grievance.

During his broadcast, Lee alleged that senior officials within the Presidential Office’s political affairs division engaged in illicit golf outings with local construction and real estate developers in Cheongju, a city in Chungcheongbuk-do Province. Crucially, Lee claimed to possess receipts and physical evidence showing that these developers picked up the bill for lavish green fees, meals, and entertainment. In South Korea, where the Improper Solicitation and Graft Act sets strict legal limits on hospitality for public servants regardless of explicit favors, such allegations carry explosive weight. If substantiated, allegations of presidential secretaries fraternizing with regional property developers could spark a major influence-peddling scandal.

What makes this rupture particularly dangerous for the administration is the shift in narrative. Lee did not merely criticize individual officials; he turned his sights on the aggressive online loyalists—often dubbed the "New Lee" faction—who enforce strict orthodoxy within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. "Defending this administration has become an act of complicity in crime," Lee warned. "I refuse to be an accomplice."

This sentiment reflects a growing exhaustion among legacy progressive commentators. For months, figures who once stood at the vanguard of anti-conservative protests have found themselves under attack by radical loyalist groups for failing to show absolute submission to the current presidential enclave. When internal criticism is systematically vilified as betrayal, the alliance inevitably begins to devour itself from within.

Constitutional Norms and an AI-Powered Defense

As the political feud spilled into the public domain, a separate legal controversy further eroded the administration's standing among constitutional scholars. The controversy centers on the regular attendance of law enforcement chiefs at State Council meetings.

On October 7, top officials from South Korea’s primary investigative agencies—including the Commissioner of the National Police Agency, the Acting Head of the Public Prosecution Office, and the Acting Director of the newly created Serious Crime Investigation Office—were seated at a formal State Council gathering. Under Article 88 of South Korea’s Constitution, the State Council is designated as the nation's supreme policy deliberation body, strictly reserved for the President, the Prime Minister, and Cabinet Ministers. Sub-cabinet agency heads may only attend under rare, exceptional circumstances to answer questions regarding specific, pending agenda items.

Critics across the political spectrum quickly pointed out that granting top prosecutors and police chiefs regular seats alongside the President seriously compromises the institutional separation of powers. If investigative chiefs sit face-to-face with executive power on a weekly basis, their political neutrality is inherently undermined. Even during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—law enforcement heads were not given permanent access to regular State Council proceedings. Former lawmaker Na Kyung-won of the main conservative opposition People Power Party captured the outrage, stating: "Investigative agencies are not presidential staffers. Stop summoning them."

Article 88 & Judicial Neutrality: South Korea's Constitution mandates a clear division between political policy-making bodies and independent investigative authorities. Converting police and prosecutorial directors into regular cabinet attendees threatens the core principle of rule of law.

The administration's response to this criticism only fueled the fire. Taking to social media, President Lee revealed that he had queried Google’s generative AI model, Gemini, on the matter, claiming the AI verified that inviting law enforcement heads to the cabinet was entirely constitutional. The attempt to validate executive actions using an AI prompt drew widespread ridicule. Even former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon chimed in, remarking, "Even if Gemini's grandfather says it's okay, this violates basic constitutional common sense."

This procedural overreach has already produced tangible political friction. The newly formed Serious Crime Investigation Office—which recently received an additional 6.8 billion KRW (about $5 million) in state budget funding—launched a controversial jurisdictional probe into Gyeonggi Province Governor Chu Mi-ae, a heavyweight figure who previously served as Justice Minister. Legal experts have noted that the agency appears to be exceeding its legal jurisdiction to target internal political rivals, prompting accusations that state investigative machinery is being weaponized for political domestic control.

Ideological Purges and Echoes of 1997

The aggressive posture of the ruling camp's dominant faction is not just creating legal headaches; it is alienating the intellectual and political figures who historically built South Korea’s progressive base. Prominent commentators, including former minister and author Rhyu Si-min, along with veteran lawmakers who led legislative battles against past conservative governments, have increasingly faced coordinated harassment and online smear campaigns from radical supporters.

This dynamic led political analyst Cho Seong-eun to deliver a scathing critique that resonated across the political spectrum: "If you remove the single event of declaring martial law, it is genuinely hard to see how the Lee Jae-myung administration fundamentally differs from the regime of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol." Cho noted with bitter irony that even Mr. Yoon, during his deeply unpopular tenure, did not conduct such systematic ideological purges against his own core supporters.

To historians of South Korean politics, this factional cannibalism carries a haunting sense of deja vu. It strongly echoes the final months of Kim Young-sam’s presidency in 1997. Ahead of that pivotal presidential election, President Kim attempted to centralize control by marginalizing rival internal figures and suppressing prospective successors within his party. Rather than consolidating power, his actions fractured the ruling conservative coalition, causing bitter floor fights and third-party breakaways that ultimately paved the way for opposition leader Kim Dae-jung to win the presidency.

By attempting to silence legacy factions associated with former presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in, the current inner circle risks committing the same strategic blunder. When a party demands total sycophancy and dismantles its own big tent, it sacrifices the broad coalition required to sustain long-term governance.

What Lies Beneath the 61% Approval Headline

On the surface, the administration appears politically secure. According to the 172nd National Barometer Survey (NBS)—conducted jointly by Embrain Public, Kstat Research, Korea Research, and Hankook Research—President Lee’s job approval rating stands at a solid 61%, with negative evaluations at 29%. Driven by a massive 77% support rate among voters in their 50s, these headline numbers give the ruling party an aura of invincibility.

A closer examination of the demographic and policy data, however, reveals significant vulnerabilities:

  • Youth Disaffection: Approval among voters aged 18–29 drops dramatically to 41%, with 52% of young voters identifying as politically independent.
  • Economic and Housing Frustration: Negative ratings on housing and real estate policy stand at 51%, far outstripping positive views (36%). High inflation and soaring living costs continue to strain working-class households.
  • Controversial Appointments: The nomination of conservative former lawmaker Lee Hye-hoon to head the Ministry of Planning and Budget drew sharp public backlash, with 42% of survey respondents rating the decision as a mistake.

High headline approval figures can easily create an illusion of stability. If the government relies on partisan cheerleading while ignoring underlying economic distress and constitutional erosion, its public backing can evaporate rapidly when crisis hits.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will test whether these internal fractures remain contained or escalate into a full-scale political crisis for the ruling party. Foreign observers monitoring South Korea's political landscape should focus on several concrete indicators:

  • Publication of the Cheongju Golf Receipts: Watch whether Lee Jong-won fulfills his pledge to publish financial evidence regarding presidential secretaries and regional real estate developers. Any formal probe under the Graft Act would deal a direct blow to executive ethics.
  • Legal Challenges to Cabinet Procedures: Keep an eye on constitutional motions or parliamentary inquiries brought by the opposition People Power Party regarding the presence of non-cabinet investigative agency heads at State Council meetings.
  • The Investigation into Governor Chu Mi-ae: The trajectory of the Serious Crime Investigation Office’s probe into Governor Chu will show whether the agency operates independently or continues to clash with rival factions inside the Democratic Party.
  • Legislative Real Estate Measures: Watch how the administration handles mounting public anger over property prices and housing supply ahead of upcoming legislative milestones, particularly as youth voter detachment deepens.
