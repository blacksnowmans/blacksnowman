Navigating Volatile Markets: Why a Korean Full-Time Investor Is Betting Big on US Tech and Dollar Assets

September 08, 2026

Checking a brokerage account in South Korea lately can feel like an exercise in frustration. While global headlines trumpet record highs in the US stock market, local retail investors often find their portfolios stuck in a frustrating range or bleeding value. Financial media swings wildly between baseless optimism and apocalyptic panic, leaving everyday savers wondering how to protect their principal let alone grow it.

Into this anxiety steps Son Joo-bu, a high-profile full-time private investor who left his corporate job at 41. Managing roughly 3.1 billion won (about $2.2 million) in financial assets exclusively outside of real estate, he pulled in an estimated 1.5 billion to 2 billion won (about $1.1 million to $1.4 million) from US equities alone over the past year. In a recent appearance on the popular financial YouTube channel Donjjulnam, Son offered a perspective that cuts against the grain of conventional personal finance advice: he argues that the current dip in exchange rates and market volatility is not a crisis, but an ideal window to reallocate cash.

The Trap of Local Currency and the Case for Dollar Assets

Many investors panic over short-term fluctuations in the won-dollar exchange rate. Son argues this is the wrong frame of reference. Rather than trading currencies like a day trader, investors should approach foreign exchange through the lens of long-term diversification.

Son draws on a harsh personal lesson from his years working overseas as an expatriate for a tobacco company in Russia. In 2017, while on parental leave there, he witnessed the collapse of the Russian ruble firsthand. The exchange rate skyrocketed from about 30 rubles to the dollar to over 70 rubles, rendering the local currency virtually worthless. Dealerships were wiped clean of cars, and luxury goods vanished as ordinary citizens scrambled desperately to trade collapsing paper money for hard assets. That experience opened his eyes to the vulnerability of the typical South Korean household balance sheet.

The vast majority of South Koreans keep 70% to 90% of their net worth tied up in domestic real estate and Korean won. This concentrates nearly all financial risk in a single, local currency. To mitigate this, Son advises that individuals must hold at least 30% to 50% of their portfolio in dollar-denominated assets, regardless of short-term exchange rate noise. With recent economic indicators creating a temporary softening in the dollar, he points out that this slight strengthening of the won offers an opportune entry point for buying US stocks.

Why the US Market Becomes the Default Choice

Son keeps only about 100 million won (about $70,000) of his 3.1 billion won portfolio in Korean equities—a fraction of his overall holdings. While he readily acknowledges the engineering prowess of Korean giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, he favors US markets for two main reasons: the near-total absence of geopolitical risk, and a corporate culture that treats minority shareholders with respect rather than as an afterthought.

Looking ahead to structural shifts like space-based data centers, Son sees the US market holding an unshakeable lead. When the space data center era matures, he highlights American chipmaker Micron Technology as a primary beneficiary due to its domestic US manufacturing base and deep ties with NASA. Combined with the US tax structure—where capital gains taxes are deferred until shares are sold—the American market functions essentially as a low-cost compounding machine for long-term investors.

Demolishing the Myth of Semiconductor Overproduction

A perennial fear in the tech sector is the threat of overproduction. When companies like SK Hynix announce plans to multiply production capacity over the next few years, many fear a brutal memory chip price crash is just around the corner. Son counters that the speed of supply growth is entirely overshadowed by an explosive surge in real-world demand.

Consider humanoid robots: a single humanoid unit requires roughly the equivalent memory content of 100 iPhones. If the world ships around 1 million humanoid robots by 2030, that single application will absorb memory demand equivalent to 100 million iPhones. Add in Elon Musk’s SpaceX initiatives for orbital data centers, and the scale shifts again. Just as Wall Street initially dismissed Apple as a mere handset vendor trading at low price-to-earnings multiples before it evolved into an indispensable platform, memory chips are transitioning from cyclical commodities into customized, mission-critical infrastructure.

This conviction explains Son’s dramatic portfolio shift. While his holdings were once heavily concentrated in Nvidia, he has trimmed that exposure down to 20% to 30%, channeling roughly 70% into memory chip makers like Micron. Textbook diversification takes a back seat when an investor develops overwhelming conviction in a secular industrial trend.

Dynamic Asset Allocation and Weaponizing Market Panic

For everyday workers starting with a modest nest egg, Son advocates a structural "root, trunk, and flower" strategy. Stable, low-volatility dividend payers like the Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD) form the roots; broad-market trackers like the S&P 500 form the trunk; and high-growth or aerospace themes form the flowers.

However, relying purely on slow-and-steady dividend growth can test anyone's patience. To accelerate returns, Son utilizes a tactical overlay based on the Cboe Volatility Index (VIX) and CNN Fear & Greed Index. When geopolitical shocks push the VIX past 30—signalling acute market panic—defensive assets like SCHD often hold their ground or gently drift upward while broader markets sell off.

During these moments of peak market fear, Son liquidates portions of his defensive holdings to aggressively buy beaten-down growth assets or leveraged ETFs like ProShares Ultra QQQ (QLD) at cyclical bottoms. Once market calm returns and the VIX drops back below 20, he rotates back into defensive positions. Successfully executing this tactical rotation even once a year can compound excess annual returns significantly.

Looking Ahead: What to Watch

For investors looking to apply these lessons without diving into full-time trading, the path forward requires discipline rather than frantic market timing:

  • Monitor Currency Windows: Treat dollar asset accumulation as a structural necessity rather than a currency-trading gamble, using periods of won strength to scale into foreign holdings.
  • Track Volatility Metrics: Keep a close eye on the VIX index. Treat readings above 30 not as a signal to panic-sell, but as an indicator to look for oversold structural growth stories.
  • Focus on Secular Infrastructure: Look past short-term supply cycles in semiconductors to evaluate how emerging fields like robotics and AI infrastructure will drive generational demand for memory.
  • Manage Tax Shelters Wisely: Be mindful of strict annual withdrawal limits in specialized Korean pension accounts, ensuring that government tax perks do not inadvertently trap your future liquidity.

Ultimately, navigating volatile markets requires looking past daily chart fluctuations and focusing on the underlying mechanics of global technological and economic shifts.
