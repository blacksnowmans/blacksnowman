The Arrogance of Power: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Losing Its Grip on the Young

November 06, 2026

Inside the National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul’s political nerve center, party strategists are staring at numbers that should terrify them. South Korea’s governing Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), which holds a legislative majority alongside the presidency, has watched its once-commanding public support slip into the fragile 40-percent range. The warning sirens are blaring from an unexpected source: the party’s own venerable elder statesmen.

Former Prime Minister Kim Boo-kyum, widely respected across party lines as a moderate bridge-builder, recently delivered an unsparing indictment that has shaken the party establishment. His critique cuts straight to the core of the ruling camp’s dilemma: having attained executive and legislative power, the party became drunk on grand political narratives—institutional reforms, historical justice, peace initiatives—while ordinary citizens, particularly workers in their twenties and thirties, watched their living standards deteriorate. In that vacuum of tangible delivery, Kim warned, arrogance flourished, toxic factional purity tests took root, and public fatigue reached a breaking point.

With general elections looming, the progressive establishment under President Lee Jae-myung faces an existential crossroad. Kim’s critique is not the wistful nostalgia of a retired politician; it exposes a structural crisis threatening South Korea's governing coalition. To understand why the Democratic Party is losing ground, one must examine how the gap between sweeping moral rhetoric and kitchen-table economics alienated younger voters, how radicalized factions hijacked party culture, and how institutional legal battles continue to cast a shadow over statecraft.

Grand Narratives vs. Bread-and-Butter Reality

For years, South Korea’s democratic progressive movement leaned on a familiar playbook. It presented itself as the moral champion of democratic consolidation, human rights, balanced regional development, and judicial transparency. These themes resonated deeply across generations that lived through authoritarian rule and mobilized in the historic street protests of the past decades.

Yet for a 30-something wage earner navigating Seoul’s brutal housing market and an insecure labor landscape, these lofty ideals sound increasingly like an excuse. While politicians spent their airtime arguing over macro-level reforms, economic inequality worsened. Skyrocketing real estate values in metropolitan areas made homeownership an impossible dream for those without intergenerational wealth. The ethical superiority that party leaders projected began to look less like principled conviction and more like self-satisfied complacency.

The party’s tone-deaf public messaging has repeatedly fueled this resentment. A telling example surfaced recently over housing data. Presidential staff publicly touted real estate stabilization, pointing to modest price drops in Seoul’s ultra-wealthy "Gangnam Big Three" districts—Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa—where luxury properties slipped from 10 billion won (about $7.5 million) to 8 billion won (about $6 million). To an ordinary salaryman struggling to secure a modest lease in northern Seoul, Incheon, or Gyeonggi Province—where everyday rents and prices surged by trillions of won in aggregate—celebrating multi-million-dollar discounts for the super-rich sounded less like good governance and more like mockery.

As revolutionary shifts in artificial intelligence and global industrial restructuring sweep through Korea's export-reliant economy, voters demand practical safety nets and future industries, not ideological lectures. Former Prime Minister Kim’s rebuke struck a nerve precisely because it articulated what younger voters have felt for months: high-minded speeches do not put food on the table or pay the monthly rent.

The Ghost of Purity Politics: Radical Fandom and Internal Purges

Beyond disconnected economic messaging, the Democratic Party is grappling with a malignant internal culture: the rise of an exclusionary, hyper-partisan fandom. This phenomenon is not entirely new, but its current iteration, dubbed by political insiders as the militant "New Lee Jae-myung" faction, threatens to drive away moderate voters entirely.

Veteran party observers recall the early years of the former Moon Jae-in administration, when an ultranationalist and fanatical fringe—derisively termed "dung flies" (ttongpari) by critics—wreaked havoc inside the progressive camp. They attacked any colleague who deviated from absolute loyalty, hurling slurs at moderate lawmakers and waging vicious online harassment campaigns against internal rivals, including Lee Jae-myung himself when he served as Mayor of Seongnam and Governor of Gyeonggi Province. That toxic element eventually isolated itself, with remnants absurdly drifting toward conservative camps, including the campaign apparatus of Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was later impeached, removed from office, and subsequently imprisoned.

Today, the script has flipped, but the destructive dynamics remain identical. A new wave of online militants wearing the banner of party loyalty has engaged in merciless internal attacks against moderate or critical Democratic lawmakers, branding them "traitors" and subjecting them to coordinated intimidation. By styling themselves as the sole arbiters of ideological purity, these groups alienate the pragmatic center.

Academic research on Korean public opinion underlines the danger. An analysis of over 200 Gallup Korea surveys indicates that sudden increases in uncommitted independent voters and drops in ruling party support occur most consistently when aggressive party factions muzzle dissent and drown out diverse policy debates. With negative job performance evaluations for the administration sitting stubbornly at 45 percent, relying exclusively on an echo chamber of hardcore partisans is a recipe for electoral disaster. Party elders argue that unless President Lee and legislative leaders draw a strict line against these factional gatekeepers before the election campaign hits full stride, moderate voters will simply stay home on polling day.

From Micro-Blogging to YouTube: The Pitfalls of Presidential Communication

Recognizing the growing friction with the wider public, the presidential office has attempted to overhaul its communication strategy. Historically, President Lee Jae-myung relied heavily on X (formerly Twitter) as his political weapon of choice, using short, punchy, combative posts to frame national debates. During peak periods, the president posted dozens of times a month—over 80 times in a single summer month alone.

That digital pugilism, however, came at a heavy price. Snappy 140-character salvos stripped nuance from complex statecraft, produced inflammatory soundbites like pledges of "total financial ruin" (paegamangsin) for rule-breakers, and stoked endless political skirmishes. In response, the administration has dialed down rapid-fire micro-blogging—presidential posts fell by more than half, to around 30 per month—and pivoted toward long-form, explanatory formats, including conversational YouTube panel discussions and collaborations with KTV, the state public broadcaster.

While shifting to explanatory video allows policymakers to unpack policy context, the medium brings its own hazards:

  • The Echo Chamber Trap: If these broadcasts become platforms for uncritical cheerleading and bureaucratic self-praise, they deepen public cynicism rather than easing it.
  • Algorithmic Exposure: Unlike text on X, which was mostly read by hyper-attuned political junkies, short video clips circulate widely on algorithmic feeds, landing directly on the screens of older demographics and unaligned voters. Any unscripted misstep on sensitive cultural or labor issues can spark nationwide backlash in hours.
  • The Search for Philosophy, Not Omniscience: Voters do not expect a head of state to recite every bureaucratic minutia like an all-knowing policy technocrat. They look for core governing values, humility, and an acknowledgment of public hardship.

Recent polling from the National Barometer Survey (NBS) captures this fragility. While presidential approval climbed back to around 50 percent after dipping dangerously close to the 40 percent mark, public perception remains split down the middle: 48 percent believe the country is moving in the right direction, while 45 percent say it is on the wrong track. A mere format change will not sway that 45 percent if the underlying empathy is missing.

The Legal Quagmire and Institutional Hurdles

Looming over the Democratic Party's political prospects is the complex web of judicial risks inherited from past political campaigns and ongoing institutional feuds. The broader legal environment remains tense following the conviction of Yoon Suk-yeol for violating the Public Official Election Act by spreading false information during his presidential campaign—specifically regarding meetings with questionable shamanic figures and legal interventions on behalf of associates.

In that landmark ruling, the judiciary reaffirmed a stringent legal doctrine: guilt does not depend on a candidate’s subjective, internal excuses, but on how ordinary voters understand the contested claims. While conservative commentators argue that this strict standard will inevitably entrap other political figures, legal scholars point out that this has been long-standing judicial precedent, not an arbitrary test concocted out of thin air.

Nonetheless, structural vulnerabilities within South Korea’s Public Official Election Act remain a headache for the legislature. Article 250 penalizes false statements concerning a candidate’s background, qualifications, and personal wealth—clear, verifiable facts. However, the statute also criminalizes misstatements regarding a candidate's undefined "actions" (haeng-wi). During contentious prosecutions involving municipal development projects like Daejang-dong, investigative authorities stretched this vague term to indict figures over psychological memory, treating statements like "I did not know the official well" as an affirmative "action." Legal reform to define these broad clauses is overdue, preventing routine campaign rhetoric from being criminalized at the discretion of politicized prosecutors.

At the same time, the institutional tools meant to check prosecutorial abuses are sputtering. The Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO)—created to handle politically sensitive abuses of power—remains hobbled by severe personnel caps and structural resistance. As the ruling party and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, trade charges of prosecutorial manipulation and judicial politicization, the judicial arena continues to drain legislative energy away from urgent socioeconomic reforms.

What to Watch Next

The Democratic Party now stands before a narrow window of opportunity to reset its course before the electorate renders its verdict. Over the coming weeks, several concrete moves will signal whether the party has absorbed Kim Boo-kyum's warnings or chosen ideological self-isolation:

  • Candidate Nominations and Factional Disciplines: Watch whether the party leadership uses upcoming parliamentary candidate screenings to sideline hyper-aggressive militants, or whether radical fandoms successfully purge moderate, reform-minded incumbents.
  • Pivots in Economic and Housing Policy: Look for concrete legislative shifts away from macroeconomic self-congratulation toward tangible relief packages for young renters, junior workers, and families battered by inflation.
  • Tone and Format of Presidential Engagements: Monitor whether President Lee Jae-myung’s new YouTube-centered dialogue includes critical, independent voices who challenge state policy on air, or descends into a defensive public-relations exercise.

Holding power in South Korea's hyper-competitive democracy has never been a guarantee of enduring popularity. If the governing party cannot trade moral self-righteousness for tangible relief in the daily lives of its citizens, the electorate will have no hesitation in delivering a harsh reprimand at the ballot box.
