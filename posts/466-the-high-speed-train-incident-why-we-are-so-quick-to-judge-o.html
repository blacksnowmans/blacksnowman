The High-Speed Train Incident: Why We Are So Quick to Judge Others

September 08, 2026

When did we become a society so quick to raise our voices at strangers? Public spaces that should be peaceful—our commutes, our transit, our shared daily environments—have become flashpoints where minor friction triggers our deepest defensive instincts. In confined spaces like a subway car or a high-speed train, where there is nowhere to escape, a single conflict can infect the emotions of everyone nearby in an instant. A recent incident on a KTX train—South Korea’s high-speed rail network—traveling from Seoul to Daejeon provided a jarring look at this reality. It wasn’t a battle between villains, but rather a clash of humans struggling to protect their own rights and vulnerabilities. It forces us to ask: how easily do we judge and condemn others based on the thinnest sliver of evidence? If we do not look for the truth hidden behind our hasty judgments, our "good intentions" can quickly morph into public violence.

The Anatomy of a Train-Car Conflict

The incident began as a simple disruption of peace. A young university student was engaged in a heated, expletive-laden argument with a man in his 60s. To the casual observer, the optics were clear: a rude, aggressive youth was verbally abusing an elder. The surrounding passengers felt an immediate, visceral reaction. Many, including myself, felt an urge to intervene on behalf of the older man. "How could a young person speak to an elder like that?" was the sentiment filling the air. When an older man in a nearby seat finally snapped and shouted at the student, the entire cabin erupted in a chorus of condemnation. The student was branded, shamed, and forced into silence by the collective pressure of the crowd.

The truth, however, emerged five minutes later when a conductor arrived to check the tickets. The older man was sitting in the student's reserved seat using a ticket for the wrong date. The "disrespectful" student had been fighting for his own paid property; the "victim" was, in fact, the one in the wrong. The crowd, who had moments before been so eager to act as judges, had been victims of the fundamental attribution error—a psychological phenomenon where we attribute others' actions to their character while ignoring the situational context. In the high-pressure, enclosed environment of the train, our "amygdala hijack"—a state where the brain’s emotional center overrides rational thought—had turned the entire carriage into a theater of collective, misplaced anger.

The Illusion of the "Pregnancy Seat"

This behavior is not an isolated incident; it is a recurring motif in Korean social life. Consider the ubiquitous subway trope: a young man spots a woman with a "baby bump" sitting in a seat designated for pregnant passengers. Driven by chivalry and social duty, he politely offers his seat, only to have the woman reply, "I’m not actually pregnant." The resulting silence is deafening, and the man’s sudden exit at the next station is a testament to the shame of a good intention gone wrong.

While these moments are often played for laughs, they reveal a deeper, more troubling trend. According to surveys by the National Human Rights Commission of Korea (a government agency dedicated to protecting individual rights), over half of the population feels that social discrimination and hatred have worsened significantly since the pandemic. We are living in a climate where we feel compelled to label, judge, and "correct" others based on mere seconds of observation. We are not just reading books by their covers; we are writing entire, false biographies based on a single glance.

The Danger of "Performative Justice"

The tragedy in these scenarios is that the impulse behind the crowd’s anger is often, at its core, a desire for order and fairness. The passengers on the KTX weren't inherently malicious; they were trying to uphold what they perceived as a moral code. However, when we intervene in situations we don’t understand, we often turn a private disagreement into a public conflict. We see this in larger social issues in Korea, such as the long-standing protests over the Jeju Naval Base or environmental disputes in Miryang. When third parties—the "crowd"—intervene based on superficial information, they often escalate an interest-based conflict into an intractable value-based war.

By failing to consider the context, our sense of justice becomes a form of violence. We strip away the humanity of the person we are targeting, reducing them to a stereotype. Whether it is an act of "seat-shaming" or a viral internet takedown, the mechanism is the same: we refuse to acknowledge the "unseen story" behind the person’s behavior.

Finding Space Between Stimulus and Response

As the psychologist Viktor Frankl famously noted, there is a space between stimulus and response, and in that space lies our freedom and our power to choose our reaction. In the context of Korean society, where social pressure to conform is intense and the pace of life is blistering, this space is shrinking. We must practice the discipline of pausing before we judge. When we feel that sudden surge of righteous indignation, we should ask ourselves: "Am I perhaps about to offer a seat to someone who isn't pregnant?"

Moving forward, we must learn to close our "internal courtrooms." The next time you encounter someone whose behavior defies your expectations, try to imagine the "unseen story" they are carrying. Perhaps their rudeness is a mask for exhaustion, or their aggression is a defense against a life that has treated them poorly. We cannot know for certain, and that uncertainty is exactly why we must withhold judgment. True social maturity in an age of digital noise and rapid-fire opinions is the ability to say, "I do not have enough information to judge this." By expanding the space between our observations and our reactions, we might find that the world—and the people sharing our train carriage—is far more complex, and far more deserving of our grace, than we ever imagined.

What to watch next: Pay attention to how news agencies and social media influencers frame "viral" public confrontations. When you see a video of a stranger behaving "badly," look for the missing context before you join the chorus of the crowd. The most radical act of kindness you can perform today is to simply reserve your judgment.
