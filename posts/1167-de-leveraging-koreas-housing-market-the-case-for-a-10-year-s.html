De-leveraging Korea’s Housing Market: The Case for a 10-Year Sunset on Cheap Jeonse Loans

October 15, 2026

To understand the peculiar fragility of South Korea’s property market, one must look not at standard mortgages, but at a rental arrangement that exists virtually nowhere else on earth: jeonse. Under this system, instead of paying monthly rent, a tenant hands over a massive lump-sum deposit—frequently equal to 60 to 80 percent of the home’s purchase price—to the landlord for a two-year lease. The landlord keeps the interest earned on that cash, returning the principal in full when the lease expires. For decades, it was celebrated as a uniquely Korean vehicle for social mobility: tenants saved money by avoiding monthly rent, while landlords secured interest-free financing.

In recent years, however, that virtuous cycle transformed into an engine of runaway speculation. As commercial banks, backed by state credit guarantees, poured trillions of won into low-interest jeonse loans, what was once intended as working-class housing welfare morphed into a liquidity firehose. Armed with tenant deposits financed by cheap bank debt, speculative buyers engaged in widespread "gap investment" (gap-tuja)—buying up apartments with minimal personal capital by covering the gap between the purchase price and the oversized jeonse deposit. Today, growing calls within Korean policy circles demand a radical reckoning: dismantling the endless flow of jeonse credit before it triggers a systemic debt crisis.

How State-Backed Rental Loans Fueled a Speculative Engine

The distortion at the heart of modern Korean real estate stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of what a jeonse deposit actually is. Legally, it is a debt: an unsecured loan extended by a tenant to a property owner, which must be returned down to the last won at the end of the tenancy. In practice, however, speculative landlords came to treat this money as their own personal windfall.

During market upswings, landlords used incoming deposits to purchase additional apartments, creating leveraged chains of residential properties. When a lease expired, they simply relied on a new tenant paying an even higher deposit to pay back the outgoing one. This rollover chain functioned smoothly as long as housing valuations climbed and bank credit remained loose.

The ultimate fuel for this spiral was government policy. By expanding state guarantees for jeonse loans, previous administrations lowered borrowing barriers for ordinary citizens under the banner of tenant welfare. Yet instead of making housing affordable, the flood of accessible credit simply allowed landlords to hike jeonse prices higher. The higher the deposit grew, the easier it became for gap speculators to purchase homes with almost no cash down. Far from helping young families build wealth, state-subsidized rental credit pushed the dream of actual homeownership further out of reach by artificially propping up underlying property values.

The 10-Year Roadmap: Capping Borrowing Without Crashing the Market

Dismantling this debt apparatus cannot happen overnight. Abruptly cutting off the credit spigot would trigger what analysts describe as a cardiac arrest in the housing market: freezing mobility, stranding tenants who rely on loans to secure leases, and precipitating sudden insolvencies among property owners. Because of this, momentum is growing behind a structured, long-term soft-landing proposal: gradually capping jeonse loans at 300 million won (roughly $225,000) over a decade-long transition.

A ten-year timeline offers market participants predictable parameters to reshape their balance sheets. When buyers and landlords recognize that state-subsidized credit is no longer an bottomless spring, the speculative fever of "all-in borrowing" (widely termed yeongkkeul, or gathering every soul-cent) loses its rationale. Setting a clear, decreasing cap sends an unmistakable signal to the market that the era of artificial price inflation is closing.

Crucially, such a roadmap requires protective buffers for existing renters. The proposal outlines transitional grace periods of two to four years, calibrated to match standard two-year residential lease cycles. By ensuring that current tenants are not suddenly evicted or forced into distressed refinancing mid-lease, policymakers can reduce street-level resistance while engineering a structural decline in aggregate credit.

As credit ceilings tighten, the domino effect is straightforward:

  • Reduced Tenant Purchasing Power: Renters will no longer be able to bid up deposits using cheap leverage.
  • Downward Pressure on Jeonse Prices: With deposits falling, landlords lose the ability to roll over unsupportable liabilities.
  • Forced Liquidation by Speculators: Highly leveraged gap investors will be compelled to offload properties onto the open market.
  • Broad Price Correction: A surge in resale listings combined with reduced leverage stabilizes sale prices, paving the way for end-users to purchase homes at rational valuations.

The Ghost of 2022 and the Moral Hazard of Landlord Bailouts

The urgency behind these proposals is rooted in the trauma of 2022 and 2023. During that period, rising interest rates stalled the market, triggering a nationwide wave of "reverse jeonse" (yeok-jeonse). Across Seoul and surrounding Gyeonggi Province, landlords found that new market deposits had fallen below the level of their existing liabilities. Because many had squandered the original deposits on additional properties or private spending, they were utterly incapable of returning the money to departing tenants.

The state's response during that crisis has since faced sharp criticism. Rather than allowing the market to purge bad actors, policymakers opened up emergency credit lines, granting special low-cost "return loans" to landlords so they could refund their tenants. In effect, public balance sheets were deployed to insulate private speculators from their own reckless leverage. It was an exercise in pure moral hazard, delaying inevitable market corrections by using fresh debt to paper over old debt.

To prevent this cycle from repeating, structural reformers argue that moral appeals are not enough; mechanical constraints must be implemented. One primary solution gaining traction is a mandatory escrow system. Under this framework, whenever a jeonse deposit exceeds a specific threshold—such as 60 to 70 percent of the property's estimated market value—the excess funds would be legally required to sit in a locked escrow account. Landlords would be prohibited from touching those funds for secondary investments, converting the deposit from a speculative piggy bank into an untouchable reserve held strictly for repayment.

The Political Impasse and What to Watch Next

If the structural distortions of jeonse credit are so evident, why has substantive reform languished? The obstacle is political fear. Any restriction on borrowing directly impacts non-homeowners and younger middle-class voters who depend on loans to secure urban shelter. In Korea's hyper-competitive electoral landscape, tightening rental credit invites immediate pushback, offering the conservative opposition—the People Power Party—and conservative editorial boards an easy avenue to attack the ruling party for suffocating ordinary citizens.

Yet continually punting the issue merely builds pressure within the country's broader household debt bubble. For the current government under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, addressing jeonse excess cannot be treated as an isolated financial regulation. To succeed without political self-immolation, it must be paired with broader macroeconomic signaling: credible commitments to expand long-term affordable public rental housing, revisions to property taxation, and unyielding enforcement against speculative landlord cartels.

In the coming months, observers of the Korean economy should track several critical indicators:

  • Escrow Legislation: Watch whether lawmakers introduce concrete statutory proposals to mandate escrow accounts for high-ratio jeonse contracts.
  • Regulatory Shifts at the Financial Services Commission: Look for signals that financial regulators are gradually including jeonse borrowings inside institutional debt-service caps, rather than exempting them as welfare instruments.
  • Auction and Distress Rates: Monitor whether the share of foreclosed apartments resulting from failed deposit returns continues to decline or begins surfacing in regional property hubs.

The fundamental dilemma of Korean housing is whether the state has the stomach to deflate an illusion built on public credit. Capping jeonse loans will undoubtedly induce painful adjustments for landlords accustomed to free capital, as well as tenants adjusting their housing expectations. But without a disciplined retreat from debt-fueled tenancy, South Korea risks preserving a high-stakes financial pyramid where the working class permanently finances the speculative dreams of the asset-rich.
