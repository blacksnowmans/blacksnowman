The $20 Million Rejection: Why Faker Chose Korean Legacy Over Chinese Gold

October 16, 2026

Imagine a twenty-year-old sitting across from a contract that offers him more money in a single year than most successful professionals earn in several lifetimes. In the high-stakes world of e-sports, where careers are often shorter than a college degree, the logical move is to "cash out." Yet, for Lee Sang-hyeok—known globally by his in-game moniker, Faker—the logic of the market has always played second fiddle to the logic of legacy. For over a decade, the "Unkillable Demon King" has been the centerpiece of T1, South Korea’s most storied professional gaming organization, despite a relentless barrage of "blank check" offers from rival leagues in China and North America.

The rumors of a Chinese "blank check" for Faker have long transcended mere gossip; they serve as a symbol of the massive capital flows within the global e-sports market. To understand why the greatest player in the history of League of Legends (LoL) turned down an estimated $20 million annual salary to remain in Seoul, one must look beyond simple "loyalty." It is a story of personal philosophy, the strategic preservation of South Korea’s "soft power," and a unique business partnership that has redefined how professional athletes are treated in the digital age.

The Great Exodus and the King Who Stayed

To understand the weight of Faker’s decision, we must go back to the winter of 2014, a period known in the gaming community as the "Korean Exodus." At the time, South Korea had firmly established itself as the world’s dominant force in League of Legends. The two sister teams of the Samsung Galaxy organization—Samsung White and Samsung Blue—had just finished first and in the top four, respectively, at the World Championship.

However, the victory was short-lived. Flush with cash from tech giants and billionaire investors, teams in the LPL (China’s professional League of Legends league) began a predatory hiring spree. They offered Korean players salaries that were double, triple, or even five times what they were earning at home. Almost overnight, every single member of both Samsung teams left for China. The LCK (League of Legends Champions Korea, the domestic top-flight league) was gutted of its championship-level talent.

Faker, then only in his second year of professional play, was the ultimate prize. Had he boarded a plane to Shanghai in 2014, the LCK might have collapsed into a developmental "farm league" for wealthier foreign markets. Instead, he chose to stay with SK Telecom T1 (now simply T1, a joint venture between the Korean telecommunications giant SK Telecom and the American media conglomerate Comcast Spectacor). By staying, he gave the Korean league a foundation to rebuild. His decision wasn't just about a team; it was a bet on the ecosystem that raised him. He proved that money couldn't buy the "home-ground" advantage of the world’s most competitive training environment.

Deconstructing the $20 Million Annual Offer

For years, the "blank check" story was treated as an urban legend until Joe Marsh, the CEO of T1, confirmed the scale of the temptation. During a 2021 podcast, Marsh revealed that after Faker became a free agent, Chinese teams offered him deals worth upwards of $20 million (approximately 24 billion KRW) per year. To put that in perspective, that is a salary comparable to top-tier stars in the NBA or Major League Baseball, for a player in a league where the average career lasts only three to four years.

The discrepancy between Faker’s market value and his personal lifestyle is staggering. In various interviews, Faker has noted that his parents manage his finances and that his personal monthly spending is often as low as $150 to $220 (about 200,000 to 300,000 KRW). When a person is content with a convenience store meal and basic necessities, the difference between a $5 million salary and a $20 million salary becomes an abstract number rather than a life-changing shift.

"If I only thought about the money, moving to China would have been attractive. But I decided it was better to stay, considering many factors beyond just the bank account."

This "money-second" mindset allowed Faker to focus on what he calls "the next stage." For him, the goal shifted from personal wealth to the responsibility of being a national icon. Oh Kyeong-sik, a high-ranking executive at T1 who has sat across the table from Faker during multiple negotiations, noted that even in his early twenties, Faker was acutely aware of his role as a representative of South Korea. He didn't just see himself as a gamer; he saw himself as a piece of the country's cultural identity, much like a top-tier diplomat or a world-class athlete in the Olympics.

From Employee to "Part-Owner": A Revolutionary Contract

T1 realized early on that they could never keep Faker through salary alone. If a bidding war broke out, the sheer size of the Chinese market would eventually win. To keep the "Unkillable Demon King" in Seoul, they had to offer something more permanent than a paycheck: equity and a post-career future.

In February 2020, T1 announced a landmark deal that granted Faker a "Part-Owner" (equity) stake in the organization. This was a revolutionary move in e-sports. It transformed Faker from a highly-paid contractor into a partner who shares in the team's long-term success. The contract ensures that even after he eventually hangs up his mouse and keyboard, he will move directly into a leadership or managerial role within the company.

This strategic move aligned Faker’s personal legacy with the brand’s survival. It also reflected a broader shift in how South Korea views its digital assets. In a nation where the "soft power" of K-pop and K-dramas is a key economic driver, Faker is viewed as a "public good." His decision to stay is often compared to the patriotic grit of historical figures. In the current political climate under the Lee Jae-myung administration, there is a renewed emphasis on protecting national intellectual property and cultural leaders. Faker’s refusal to "export" his talent to China is seen as a victory for domestic cultural sovereignty.

This stands in stark contrast to the previous era of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi, whose tenure was marked by social division and the eventual turmoil that led to his impeachment. During those chaotic years, figures like Faker provided a sense of national pride and stability that transcended politics. While Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi remains in prison, Faker’s legacy continues to grow, untainted by the scandals that rocked the country’s traditional power structures.

The Philosophy of the "Empty Water Bottle"

What keeps a man motivated when he has already won five World Championships (including his most recent "V5" victory) and has more money than he can spend? Faker’s answer is humility and growth. He famously uses the metaphor of an "empty water bottle" to describe his approach to life. If a bottle is full, it cannot take in anything new. By considering himself "empty"—essentially always a student—he remains open to the constant changes in the game’s meta-strategy.

This internal drive is why he recently signed a contract extension that will keep him with T1 through 2029. By the time that contract ends, Faker will be in his early thirties—an age once considered "ancient" for a professional gamer. His longevity is a testament to his rigorous self-care, including meditation, reading philosophy and psychology books, and maintaining a mental distance from the "toxic" elements of online fame.

  • Growth over Gold: Faker prioritizes being in the most competitive environment to ensure he continues to improve.
  • Altruistic Goals: He has stated that his current motivation is to provide joy to his fans and to maintain the prestige of Korean e-sports.
  • One-Club Loyalty: By staying with T1 for his entire career, he has built a "brand" that is worth more than any single-year salary offer from China.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward the 2025 season and beyond, the "Faker effect" will continue to be the primary barometer for the health of the global e-sports market. Here are the key factors to monitor:

  • The "One-Club Man" Precedent: Will other young Korean stars follow Faker's lead and prioritize domestic legacy, or will the rising salaries in North America and the Middle East (especially with the advent of the E-sports World Cup in Saudi Arabia) trigger a second "Exodus"?
  • T1's Business Evolution: As a part-owner, how will Faker influence T1's expansion into global media? His transition from player to executive will be a case study for the entire sports world.
  • Government Support: Watch for the Lee Jae-myung administration to potentially introduce more robust protections or incentives for "national asset" players to keep them within the domestic LCK league.
  • The 2029 Horizon: Faker's current contract is essentially a "lifetime" commitment. Every match he plays from now on sets a new record for longevity at the highest level of competition.

In the end, Faker’s rejection of the $20 million "blank check" was not just a rejection of money. it was an affirmation that some things—like being the face of a nation’s favorite pastime and building a legacy that will last decades—simply do not have a price tag.
