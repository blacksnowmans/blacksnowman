Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party: How a Religious Sect Conspiracy Is Tearing the Democratic Party Apart

November 07, 2026

For months, the grassroots members of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) have simmered with fury. In party chat rooms, district offices, and online forums, ordinary members found themselves slapped with one of the most toxic labels in modern South Korean public life: followers of Shincheonji, a fringe, secretive religious sect notorious across the country. The accusation did not come from conservative opposition agitators or fringe online trolls. It was ignited by Kim Min-seok, now the party’s leader, during a bitterly contested internal leadership convention.

What began as an explosive campaign attack has now metastasized into a full-blown judicial and constitutional crisis within the ruling party. Convinced that the leadership used fabricated cult-connection rumors to sway internal party votes and purge internal rivals, an organized cohort of party members is bypassing standard political decorum and taking their own party chief to court. With criminal complaints, injunction requests to suspend the party leader's authority, and class-action efforts underway, the dispute exposes deep fault lines inside the governing camp at a moment when South Korea faces pivotal legislative and judicial turning points.

A Manufactured Smear in a Bitter Party Race

The controversy traces its roots to July 26, 2026, when Kim Min-seok appeared on an MBC radio broadcast during the party convention campaign. Kim publicly stoked suspicion by claiming that "allegations of Shincheonji's potential interference in this convention are grounded in fact." He boldly promised to unveil definitive proof once the official convention kicked off, positioning himself as a defender of party purity against covert infiltration.

In South Korean politics, an association with Shincheonji—officially the Shincheonji Church of Jesus—is radioactive. The group has long faced intense public hostility and scrutiny over allegations of aggressive proselytizing, clandestine infiltration of established institutions, and high-profile legal controversies. By introducing this explosive element into an intra-party election, Kim raised the stakes dramatically. He aligned with figures such as commentator Kang Jin-goo to craft a narrative that portrayed his internal opponents—most notably high-profile lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae and rank-and-file members skeptical of Kim's faction—as pawns or sympathizers of the sect, framing them under an aggressive "anti-Lee Jae-myung factionalism" narrative.

Yet the promised evidence never materialized. Instead of presenting concrete proof, Kim abruptly shifted tone under the guise of making a "magnanimous decision for party unity." To thousands of members who had spent four months enduring insults and witch hunts—including coarse slurs directed at figures like Jung Cheong-rae demanding to know whether they were secretly taking orders from Shincheonji founder Lee Man-hee—the sudden pivot felt less like statesmanship and more like a calculated retreat after the damage had already been done.

Aggrieved members argue this was not merely careless campaign rhetoric, but an intentional strategy to distort voter perception and secure the leadership. The controversy has also highlighted factional calculations among sitting lawmakers. Lawmakers aligned with Kim, including Kim Dong-ah, Jeon Yong-gi, Jung Jin-wook, and Yoon Jong-gun, quickly mobilized to defend the party chief, issuing statements praising his calls for unity. To grassroots activists, however, this defense appeared to be driven by self-preservation ahead of future parliamentary candidate nominations rather than an honest reckoning with internal election integrity.

The Grassroots Revolt: Criminal Complaints and an Injunction

Disillusioned with party leadership, the dissenting faction is moving the fight into the legal arena under Article 52 of South Korea's Political Parties Act. The statute explicitly prohibits candidates in internal party leadership elections from publishing false facts regarding a competitor's identity, career, or disciplinary record for the purpose of getting elected or defeating another candidate, penalizing violations with up to three years in prison or fines up to 6 million won (about $4,500).

Under established Supreme Court precedents, such as ruling 2025Do4697, disseminating false claims that pollute the objective judgment of primary electors is treated as a grave offense against internal democratic processes. The legal standard assesses the overarching impression conveyed to voters, and critics argue Kim’s unsubstantiated allegations poisoned the primary environment.

Led by attorney Lee Jae-il, the legal team representing the party members has reportedly completed digital forensic extraction at the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency's digital evidence analysis lab to substantiate their claims. Their legal offensive is structured along three specific tracks:

  • Criminal Prosecution: Filing formal criminal complaints under the Political Parties Act and examining potential violations of the Personal Information Protection Act regarding alleged leaks of the party membership database.
  • Preliminary Injunction: Submitting an urgent motion to the court to suspend Kim Min-seok’s official duties as party leader, arguing that a mandate won through fraudulent campaign narratives is fundamentally illegitimate.
  • Internal Disciplinary Petitions: Petitioning the Democratic Party’s Ethics Tribunal to strip Kim of his membership rights, expel him, or formally unseat him from the party apparatus.

To establish institutional legitimacy and counter accusations that the effort is merely an astroturfed political sabotage campaign, organizers are assembling a 1,000-member collective litigation panel. Participating members must verify their party credentials through the party's official registry and personally pay an individual filing stamp fee of 33,000 won (roughly $25). Organizers intend to pair these filings with large-scale grassroots demonstrations, signaling that they will settle for nothing less than a formal judicial ruling or Kim's resignation.

Misguided Priorities Amid High-Stakes National Trials

This internal civil war comes at an extraordinarily fraught moment for the governing party. Rather than resolving internal member grievances or focusing on the upcoming national budget deliberations, the Democratic Party leadership has been expending immense legislative capital pushing a contentious "fabricated indictment special counsel bill" and floating the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

For many observers, this legislative posturing represents poor strategic timing. The budget session directly affects public livelihoods, and tying up the National Assembly in procedural standoffs over special counsel appointments risks alienating swing voters. Grassroots supporters are particularly exasperated: party leaders are asking activists to rally behind special counsel initiatives while simultaneously ignoring the smear campaigns waged against those very same members back home.

This political theater unfolds in the shadow of major judicial developments concerning the conservative opposition. All eyes are on the imminent appellate verdict for Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was removed from office following his impeachment and is currently imprisoned. Mr. Yoon faces charges under the Public Official Election Act.

If the appellate court upholds the lower court’s prison sentence, the consequences for the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) will be existential. Under South Korean election law, an upheld conviction would force the PPP to reimburse approximately 39.7 billion won (roughly $30 million) in state-provided election campaign subsidies. Such a massive financial clawback would threaten the institutional viability of the conservative party. However, deep public skepticism surrounds the judiciary, fueled by rumors that key witnesses like Yoon Dae-jin have altered testimony to soften the legal blow and avoid the severe fine thresholds that trigger the subsidy refund.

Selective Justice and What to Watch Next

Underlying the broader frustration of the party base is a persistent sense of structural inequality in how the legal system and the press treat political families. Grassroots activists frequently contrast the relentless, invasive investigations endured by the family of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk—whose daughter Cho Min was publicly scrutinized down to her high school credentials—with the muted response to allegations surrounding prominent conservative figures.

In particular, accusations concerning the daughter of prominent conservative figure Han Dong-hoon remain a point of severe contention. Allegations have surfaced regarding credentials for applications to competitions like the Technovation challenge in the United States, with claims that an app was commissioned domestically for 2 million won (around $1,500) under a relative’s name to secure municipal honors. While President Lee Jae-myung has repeatedly called for equal standards of prosecutorial scrutiny regardless of political faction, the perception remains that established legal elites enjoy institutional protection.

The Democratic Party finds itself caught between grand national narratives and toxic internal dysfunction. Moving forward, observers should watch several concrete milestones that will decide whether this conflict blows over or triggers a structural party split:

  • The Injunction Motion: How quickly the civil court hears the petition to suspend Kim Min-seok's leadership duties, and whether judges view the primary-stage Shincheonji claims as sufficient grounds to intervene in internal party affairs.
  • Ethics Tribunal Response: Whether the DPK Ethics Tribunal takes up the member petitions or suppresses them to protect leadership stability, which could drive disillusioned members into wider street protests.
  • Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s Appellate Ruling: The legal outcome for Mr. Yoon, which will dictate whether the opposition People Power Party faces financial ruin and determine how aggressively the DPK leadership can leverage judicial reform issues.
  • Budget Session Standoffs: Whether the ruling party can pivot from factional infighting to pass the national budget, or if internal paralysis will derail President Lee Jae-myung’s legislative agenda heading into the coming year.
