Why Your Brain is Not a Robot: Reimagining Mental Health in South Korea

October 04, 2026

For most modern South Koreans, the concept of "mental health" has evolved from a taboo subject to a matter of daily survival. Yet, despite this shift, there remains a pervasive, stubborn belief that our minds are separate from our bodies—a "pilot" controlling a machine. When we feel overwhelmed, society often resorts to tired platitudes: "Just change your mindset" or "You need more willpower."

Kim Chong-gi, a prominent psychiatrist who gained public recognition through his appearances on the popular reality show Heart Signal 4, recently addressed these misconceptions on the YouTube channel Japdahan Balgyeon ("Miscellaneous Discoveries"). His message is blunt: the mind is not a disembodied entity. It is the physical manifestation of the brain. When we dismiss mental suffering as a lack of willpower, we aren't just being unhelpful—we are engaging in a form of victim-blaming that allows society to ignore the systemic pressures creating these crises in the first place.

The Myth of the Mind-Reader

Because of his media appearances, where he analyzed the subtle interpersonal dynamics of reality show contestants, many Koreans perceive Dr. Kim as a sort of psychological "mind-reader." Patients often enter his clinic with an air of defensive tension, fearing that a psychiatrist is peering into their deepest traumas or moral failings.

Dr. Kim is quick to dismantle this image. He emphasizes that a psychiatrist’s role is not to manipulate, profile, or "solve" a human being. Instead, their primary duty is to act as a partner in recovery. A good doctor is not someone who possesses secret insights into your soul, but someone who provides a safe, non-judgmental space for you to speak. In a society that often demands people "suck it up" and keep their hardships private, the most radical act a psychiatrist performs is simply to listen with genuine intent. The clinic, he argues, is not a place where you go to be "found out," but a place where you go to safely offload the burdens you can no longer carry alone.

The "Humanist" Turn in Diagnosis

Recent data indicates that diagnosed cases of depression in South Korea have surged by over 30% in the last five years. While many attribute this to a harsher, more competitive society, Dr. Kim offers a more nuanced, "humanist" interpretation. Human biology has not fundamentally changed in half a decade; rather, our culture has become more attuned to the reality of suffering.

In the past, South Korean society prioritized visible growth and economic performance above all else. Today, there is an increasing social mandate to acknowledge individual psychological states. The spike in depression diagnoses is not necessarily evidence of a "sicker" population, but rather a sign that pains once hidden behind a veneer of stoicism are finally being recognized and treated as legitimate medical conditions. This represents a significant, positive maturation of Korean society: we are finally moving away from the culture of "endure it" toward a culture of "let’s get professional help."

The Weight of Relational Trauma

The transition between the private and public spheres often hits a breaking point during South Korea’s major holidays. Dr. Kim notes that his clinic is consistently fully booked in the weeks surrounding these dates. While older generations (those in their 50s and 60s) have often reached a point of weary resignation regarding traditional family hierarchies—such as the historically fraught relationship between mothers-in-law and daughters-in-law—those in their 30s and 40s are currently caught in the crossfire.

For this younger cohort, the struggle is active. They are still attempting to negotiate boundaries, demand fairness, and reconcile their modern values with traditional expectations. This creates a unique form of psychological friction that, if left unaddressed, frequently manifests as clinical depression. It is a mistake to dismiss this as mere "holiday stress." When a person is constantly pulled between their societal role and their personal dignity, the mind reaches a state of metabolic exhaustion.

Some look to "laughter therapy"—a popular, if informal, community-based practice in Korea—as a quick fix. While laughter is a potent survival strategy and a way to temporarily lower the temperature of a tense situation, Dr. Kim clarifies that it is not a cure for depression. It is a lifeline, not a solution. It helps make the unbearable momentarily tolerable, but professional medical intervention remains the necessary structural support to keep the "drowning" from going under.

The Brain is Not a Moral Failure

The most crucial takeaway from Dr. Kim’s practice is the rejection of dualism. Many patients ask, "Is my depression a real illness, or is it just in my head?" The answer, as far as modern neuroscience is concerned, is that they are the same thing. To tell a depressed person to "be more positive" is no different than telling someone with a broken leg to "walk with more willpower."

Clinical depression involves actual changes in how the brain processes energy and regulates its metabolism. When a doctor looks at brain imaging, they see these changes as clearly as a physician sees a fracture on an X-ray. By moving the concept of "mental health" out of the abstract realm of willpower and into the biological realm of the brain, we strip away the shame. When you realize your brain is currently struggling to function, you stop blaming yourself for "failing" and start treating your condition with the same care you would accord to any other physical injury.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea continues to grapple with these issues, we should watch for three major developments:

  • Changes in Corporate Culture: Look for how major South Korean companies (the chaebols) adapt their HR policies. As the connection between mental health and productivity becomes scientifically undeniable, we may see more robust, mandatory mental health leave policies that treat psychological burnout as seriously as physical injury.
  • The Destigmatization of Medication: Keep an eye on how pharmaceutical treatments for depression are discussed in media. If the narrative continues to shift toward viewing antidepressants as "aids for a struggling organ" rather than "crutches for the weak," we can expect the number of those seeking help to continue rising—which, ironically, is a sign of a healthier, more honest society.
  • Community-Based Support Systems: Pay attention to the rise of grassroots counseling spaces outside of hospital settings. If the "listening" culture advocated by experts like Dr. Kim takes root in schools and community centers, it could significantly reduce the burden on high-level psychiatric care while preventing minor stresses from escalating into full-blown crises.

Ultimately, your brain is not an enemy to be conquered by sheer force of will, nor is it a robot that can be reset with a command. It is a physical organ that requires rest, maintenance, and occasionally, external support. Recognizing this is not a sign of weakness—it is the ultimate act of self-preservation.
