South Korea's Judiciary Under Fire: Privilege, Expense Scandals, and the Urgency of Reform

October 06, 2026

For many people outside South Korea, the country is best known for its dynamic pop culture, cutting-edge technology, and economic prowess. But beneath the polished exterior of Asia's fourth-largest economy lies a persistent domestic frustration: the widening gap between the daily economic struggles of ordinary citizens and the insulated, unaccountable lifestyles of the nation’s elite. While everyday Koreans grapple with rising living costs, recent revelations have laid bare a culture of entitlement, expense-account abuses, and political maneuvering among South Korea’s highest-ranking judicial figures. This is not merely a story of political gossip; it is a deep-seated institutional crisis that raises fundamental questions about the rule of law and the true meaning of fairness in a democracy.

The Double Standard of the Judiciary and Prosecutor’s Office

To understand why public anger is boiling over, one must look closely at how South Korea’s legal and investigative apparatus operates when its own members are implicated in wrongdoing. Two prominent controversies involving senior legal officials have brought these systemic flaws into sharp relief.

The first involves the prosecution of Jeong Jin-woong, a senior prosecutor who physically assaulted a high-profile suspect—then-prosecutor Han Dong-hoon—during a 2020 investigative procedure. Under South Korean law, a public official committing physical abuse during an arrest faces serious felony charges carrying up to five years in prison. Yet, rather than upholding an objective standard of justice, internal investigative bodies appeared more concerned with protecting their own. Documents and internal whistleblowing revealed deep divisions within the Seoul High Court Prosecutor's Office, with key officials like candidate Kim Ji-yong facing heavy scrutiny over how the indictment and subsequent reviews were handled. Critics argue that the application of the law shifted to accommodate political alignments, proving that those who wield state power are frequently judged by an entirely different standard than ordinary citizens.

This protectionist instinct was further illustrated by technical and administrative roadblocks surrounding the forensic examination of Han Dong-hoon's iPhone during the same era. Despite public demands for a thorough investigation, critical digital forensics were effectively stalled under the pretext of technical limitations and budgeting constraints within the Supreme Prosecutors' Office. When public officials with the power of the purse deliberately obstruct investigative procedures to shield key figures, the entire machinery of a fair legal system begins to collapse. For the average citizen, who faces severe legal scrutiny for minor infractions, watching the country's top legal minds weaponize technicalities and personal loyalties to dodge accountability is deeply demoralizing.

Luxury Dining on the Taxpayer's Dime

If opaque investigations into prosecutorial misconduct reveal systemic bias, the recent expense-account scandals involving the nation's highest court display an astonishing degree of moral hazard. In October, during parliamentary audits by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, South Korea's top judicial leadership faced intense questioning over lavish meals funded by public money.

Disclosures brought to light by lawmakers revealed that senior judicial officials indulged in high-end "omakase" meals at exclusive private rooms, running up bills of about $55 (75,000 KRW) per person. Under South Korean administrative spending guidelines, official meal expenses exceeding roughly $37 (50,000 KRW) require strict itemized documentation, including the names and affiliations of all attendees. To bypass these inconvenient transparency rules, officials allegedly engaged in blatant falsification and splitting of receipts—turning expensive private banquets into fictitious, low-cost gatherings spread across multiple entries.

Even more scandalous was the existence of an informal cash stipend system for Supreme Court Justices. Documents indicated that top justices and court administration officials received regular off-the-books cash allowances, totaling thousands of dollars annually, drawn from general operational budgets rather than standard executive expense accounts. When confronted with these revelations, Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae offered a defense that shocked the public: he claimed he merely attended the meals and delegated all financial administration to subordinates, effectively washing his hands of any personal responsibility.

In a country where ordinary workers face strict internal audits over minor office expenses, the spectacle of the nation's chief judge dismissing luxury dining and undocumented cash stipends as administrative trivia is viewed as a profound betrayal. It highlights a culture of entrenched privilege where accountability flows strictly downward while perks flow upward.

The Constitutional Battleground and the Demand for Structural Overhaul

These mounting scandals have transformed simmering discontent into a concerted political and social demand for sweeping judicial reform. At the heart of this movement is a critique of the judiciary's absolute concentration of administrative and legal power, epitomized by the National Court Administration.

Reformers argue that the judiciary cannot be left to police itself. Proposals for meaningful change include completely dismantling the National Court Administration, spinning off purely administrative functions like property registration into independent agencies, and expanding citizen participation through expanded jury and lay-judge systems. By breaking up the insular corporate culture of the courts, proponents hope to strip away the feudal privileges long enjoyed by the legal elite.

This institutional friction has also spilled over into a direct constitutional clash between the judiciary and the executive branch. Recent disputes over the appointment and recommendation powers for Supreme Court Justices—specifically regarding the interpretation of Article 104 of the South Korean Constitution—have exposed an institutional egoism within the courts. Chief Justice Cho and his allies have attempted to frame the judiciary's recommendation power as an absolute veto over the president's constitutional prerogative to appoint justices. Critics contend this is a symptom of an overinflated institutional ego, wherein the courts attempt to place themselves above the democratically elected branches of government.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this critical juncture, several key developments will determine whether these scandals lead to genuine reform or simply fade into political theater:

  • Legislative Moves on Judicial Reform: Pay close attention to the Democratic Party and other opposition blocs in the National Assembly. Whether they muster the political will to pursue structural bills—such as dismantling the National Court Administration or initiating impeachment proceedings against uncooperative judicial leaders—will be a major test of legislative resolve.
  • Public Sentiment and Civil Society Pressure: Watch for how public opinion crystallizes around upcoming budget reviews and parliamentary hearings. Continued civic outrage is the primary catalyst preventing political elites from sweeping these financial scandals under the rug.
  • Executive-Judiciary Relations: Monitor future appointments to the Supreme Court and constitutional bodies. How the presidential office and the courts negotiate appointment authorities will signal whether the judiciary is willing to compromise or intends to press its institutional standoff further.

Ultimately, South Korea's judicial crisis is a stark reminder that formal laws and democratic constitutions are only as strong as the integrity of the people who enforce them. As the public demands answers for unaccountable spending and shielded investigations, the nation stands at a crossroads between protecting an entrenched legal aristocracy and forging a genuinely fair and transparent republic.
