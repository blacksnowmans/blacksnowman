The Dark Side of Korea’s Running Craze: Why Chasing Personal Records Is Straining Young Hearts

October 14, 2026

On any given weeknight in Seoul, the paved paths hugging the Han River and its tributaries resemble a high-speed commuter corridor. Lit by riverfront lampposts and the glow of smartwatches, hundreds of recreational athletes dash in synchronized cadence. In recent years, running has transformed from an unglamorous solitary workout for middle-aged joggers into one of South Korea’s most fashionable pastimes. Young professionals, organized into sleek urban "running crews," flaunt matching designer activewear, post their splits on social media, and train aggressively for full marathons. Yet behind this vibrant national embrace of cardiovascular fitness, cardiologists are raising an urgent alarm: the relentless pursuit of speed, distance, and grit is pushing human hearts past their breaking point.

The warning has grown louder as headlines of runners collapsing mid-race pierce the enthusiasm. A particularly jarring case involved a promising runner in his twenties—a graduate student at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), one of the country’s elite research institutions—who suffered fatal cardiac arrest just short of the finish line. He was an accomplished amateur capable of a "sub-3" marathon (finishing 42.195 kilometers in under three hours), a benchmark that demands extraordinary endurance. His sudden death punctured the widespread assumption that youth and athletic discipline provide an impenetrable shield against cardiovascular catastrophe.

According to Dr. Lee Jong-young, a professor of cardiovascular medicine at Hallym University Sacred Heart Hospital, the belief that endless high-intensity running is universally beneficial is a dangerous misconception. While running burns fat, strengthens cardiopulmonary capacity, lowers blood pressure, and prevents clots, the blanket application of extreme exercise carries profound physiological hazards. In Dr. Lee's assessment, out of ten thousand people who run, running cannot be assumed to be a safe shield for all ten thousand.

From Health Savior to Vascular Danger: The Illusion of High-Intensity Running

The popular fitness narrative treats running as an unalloyed good: the more you sweat, the heavier you breathe, and the longer you endure, the healthier you become. However, pushing the cardiovascular system into extreme exertion triggers what specialists describe as a state of extreme metabolic stress. In this zone, the body struggles to process fatigue byproducts, and the circulatory system experiences relentless mechanical strain.

Cardiologists compare chronically overtaxed blood vessels to an overstretched rubber band. When an elastic band is repeatedly pulled to its absolute limit, the inner structural fibers loosen, fray, and develop micro-tears. Inside the human body, enduring sustained maximum heart rates subjects arterial walls to violent oscillations of rapid expansion and contraction. Over time, vessels lose their innate compliance and harden—a condition directly tied to arteriosclerosis and, in catastrophic scenarios, arterial dissection.

A major cultural driver behind this physiological wear-and-tear is South Korea's deeply ingrained ethos of perseverance, often referred to as overcoming adversity through sheer will. Recreational runners frequently adopt an all-or-nothing mindset, gritting their teeth through severe physical distress under the conviction that pain is merely weakness leaving the body. But when internal metabolic pathways collapse under excessive load, willpower cannot prevent vascular trauma. What began as an investment in longevity becomes a self-inflicted hazard.

The Trap of Youth: Why 20-Somethings Face Silent Cardiovascular Risks

The assumption that heart disease belongs exclusively to older generations is crumbling in hospital wards across South Korea. Outpatient clinics are seeing an influx of patients in their twenties and thirties presenting with dangerously elevated systemic inflammation markers and severe dyslipidemia, despite their young age.

The modern urban lifestyle explains much of this vulnerability. Many young Korean workers juggle prolonged desk hours, irregular sleep schedules, persistent occupational stress, and convenience-store diets heavy in processed foods. Their baseline cardiovascular health is often compromised before they ever lace up their sneakers. When someone whose underlying vascular system is already inflamed abruptly plunges into intensive long-distance training, they are effectively running on unstable terrain.

Compounding this baseline fragility is the physical transformation that happens when an untrained heart is forced into chronic high-output labor. To compensate for unaccustomed, punishing workloads, the heart muscle hypertrophies—it grows abnormally thick and enlarged:

  • Short-term compensation: In a runner’s twenties and thirties, high muscle tone allows an enlarged heart to pump effectively, masking structural changes.
  • The natural decline of aging: Cardiac muscle contractility naturally decreases by roughly 1% per year as part of the normal aging process.
  • The late-life structural trap: A heart that has been excessively enlarged struggles far more when contractility drops. Just like a stretched, worn-out tire, a bulky, dilated heart loses pumping efficiency, sharply raising the long-term risk of congestive heart failure.

As Dr. Lee warns, a smaller, normal-sized heart can still pump blood efficiently even when its muscle power declines with age; an abnormally enlarged heart, once weakened, cannot maintain that function.

Hard Numbers and the Myth of Running as a Universal Panacea

The danger is not merely theoretical; it is grounded in clinical literature. Data published in the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) examining sudden cardiac events among millions of marathon participants documented 176 instances of cardiac arrest on race courses. While the overall incidence appears numerically low—roughly 0.60 cases per 100,000 runners—the distribution reveals critical risk factors:

  • The rate of cardiac arrest among full-marathon runners was more than double that seen among half-marathon participants.
  • The overwhelming primary cause of these mid-race arrests was underlying coronary artery disease.
  • Male participants faced a significantly higher incidence of cardiac emergencies than female participants.

Despite improvements in emergency medical response that have cut on-site mortality rates compared to past decades, resuscitation after a collapse does not undo the severe shock inflicted on the cardiovascular system.

Perhaps the most dangerous manifestation of exercise zealotry is the decision to replace medical treatment with high-mileage runs. Cardiologists frequently encounter patients diagnosed with hypertension or hyperlipidemia who unilaterally discontinue their prescribed medications, convinced that running will cure their chronic conditions. While moderate aerobic conditioning does help regulate blood pressure, running a marathon with untreated hypertension causes blood pressure to spike to hazardous levels mid-stride, turning what should have been therapy into a cardiovascular trigger.

Checking the Engine: Practical Screenings and Everyday Benchmarks

To avoid turning a healthy pursuit into a medical emergency, medical experts recommend shifting the metric of success away from speed and toward physical feedback. Rather than pushing until breathlessness turns into dizziness, runners should aim for moderate intensity—a zone where light sweat forms, breathing quickens, and the body feels pleasantly stimulated rather than completely depleted.

Recognizing early distress signals requires monitoring the entire body rather than staring obsessively at a pacing app. Uncharacteristic shortness of breath, unexplained abdominal fullness or indigestion, an unusually heavy sensation in one leg, or erratic pulse readings on a wearable monitor are clear signals from the heart to stop immediately. Pushing through these warnings to beat a clock or impress a running crew can prove fatal.

Fortunately, verifying cardiac readiness does not require exorbitant spending, especially in South Korea’s medical environment:

  • Affordable clinical screening: Under South Korea's accessible National Health Insurance framework, standard diagnostic tools such as an exercise stress test (treadmill test) and an echocardiogram carry out-of-pocket costs of only a few tens of thousands of won (roughly $20 to $50). It costs far less than a single pair of high-end carbon-plated marathon shoes to objectively assess whether one's heart can tolerate heavy exertion.
  • The everyday stair-climbing baseline: For an immediate, cost-free self-assessment, runners can test themselves on apartment building stairs. If an individual can climb multiple flights of stairs smoothly without crushing chest pain or catastrophic shortness of breath, their heart can likely tolerate modest distance running. If a few flights cause acute chest tightening, track workouts should be halted until a specialist is consulted.
  • Screening for family history: Anyone with a known family history of early heart disease or unexplained fainting must undergo objective clinical evaluation rather than chalking up extreme breathlessness to poor stamina.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's amateur running culture matures, the focus is beginning to shift from raw participation numbers to safety and systemic oversight. Observers should watch several concrete developments in the coming race seasons:

  • Event safety standards: Whether municipal organizers and private race directors in Seoul and other major cities will begin requiring mandatory health questionnaires or baseline medical checks for high-mileage entries, particularly for full-marathon distances.
  • Emergency protocols: The density and deployment speed of automated external defibrillator (AED) response teams along popular public urban running routes such as the Han River parks and Cheonggyecheon.
  • Cultural moderation: Whether urban running clubs begin prioritizing structured, low-to-moderate-intensity training sessions over competitive segment-hunting on fitness apps, encouraging members to treat rest as a discipline rather than a defeat.

Running remains one of the most accessible and rewarding sports ever conceived. But its benefits endure only when the runner respects the biological engine beneath the chest. Before taking the next stride toward a personal record, every runner must ask whether their heart is thriving on the effort—or pleading for relief.
