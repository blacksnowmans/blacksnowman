South Korea’s Legislative Warfare: Impeachment Threats, Prosecutorial Reform, and a $590 Billion Budget Hanging in the Balance

November 09, 2026

Imagine standing outside the National Assembly precinct in Yeouido, Seoul, on a chilly November morning. On one side of the avenue, conservative activists and lawmakers from the opposition People Power Party line the pavement holding bright banners and calling for presidential impeachment. A short drive away near the presidential office, solo demonstrators stage high-profile vigil protests. Inside the Capitol chambers, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea gather in tense caucus meetings, bracing for an all-out legislative war over the nation’s upcoming budget and a sweeping judicial reform bill.

This scene encapsulates the deep political impasse seizing South Korea as the year comes to a close. At the epicenter of the confrontation is a high-stakes legislative agenda: a proposed special counsel investigation into past prosecutorial abuses, alongside a massive 820 trillion won (roughly $590 billion) national budget for the upcoming fiscal year. With the main opposition party taking its grievances to the streets and raising the specter of presidential impeachment, the National Assembly has devolved into a bitter standoff where critical economic and social policy measures risk being frozen entirely.

From Assembly Chambers to Street Rallies: The Opposition’s Escalating Impeachment Strategy

To understand why political tensions have reached a boiling point, international readers must look at how the balance of power in Seoul has shifted in recent months. Following the impeachment and removal of former leader Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently serving a prison sentence—the presidency passed to Lee Jae-myung of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). Now operating as the main opposition, the conservative People Power Party (PPP) finds itself battling both a DPK-led administration and a firm liberal majority in the unicameral National Assembly.

Outmatched inside parliamentary committee rooms, the PPP has increasingly turned to extra-parliamentary tactics. Lawmakers such as Representative Jang Dong-hyeok have escalated the fight by staging one-person protests outside the presidential office, explicitly raising calls for President Lee Jae-myung’s impeachment. At street rallies, conservative figures have deployed sharp slogans, accusing the government of riding roughshod over constitutional norms.

The response from the ruling Democratic Party has been swift and severe. Representative Han Byung-do, a senior DPK lawmaker, publicly condemned the opposition’s rhetoric, stating that PPP leaders "have lost their minds" and crossed a dangerous line. Han characterized the opposition's street campaign as a "parade of extreme rhetoric," accusing the conservative party of shameless hypocrisy and attempting to destabilize a constitutionally elected administration simply to rally its core base.

For outside observers, this dynamic illustrates a familiar pattern in South Korean parliamentary politics: when a minority party loses institutional leverage inside the legislature, it often pivots to public rallies and high-decibel rhetoric to keep its supporters engaged and delay government initiatives. However, invoking presidential impeachment so early in a term carries steep political risks, potentially alienating moderate voters who are looking for economic stability rather than permanent partisan conflict.

The Core Flashpoint: The Special Counsel Bill on Fabricated Prosecutions

While budget debates frequently cause friction in parliamentary systems, the immediate spark for the current legislative shutdown is a specific piece of reform legislation: the Special Counsel Bill on Fabricated Prosecutions.

The proposed draft law seeks to establish an independent special prosecutor to investigate allegations of prosecutorial misconduct, targeted investigations, and evidence manipulation conducted by state prosecutors during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. For the Democratic Party, the bill is a necessary measure to enact judicial reform and ensure institutional accountability. DPK strategists argue that during Yoon's presidency, prosecutorial authority was weaponized to harass political rivals and manufacture charges, leaving deep scars on the country's legal apparatus. From the DPK’s perspective, an independent special counsel is not a tool of vengeance, but a vital mechanism to restore public confidence in the judicial system.

The opposition People Power Party views the legislation in a fundamentally different light. Led by Party Leader Han Dong-hoon, the PPP has aggressively denounced the bill as a "privilege law" tailored to protect ruling party figures and rewrite the legal record. Opposition leaders have labeled the draft measure an "escape hatch" designed to erase opposition parliamentary strength and undermine lawful judicial rulings.

To block the bill from reaching a final floor vote, the PPP has threatened to mount a filibuster—an unlimited parliamentary debate intended to stall assembly proceedings—complemented by ongoing street rallies. This fierce clash highlights South Korea’s long-standing structural rift: the immense power historically wielded by the state prosecution service, and the persistent struggle over who controls, restrains, or reforms it.

Legislative Gridlock: A $590 Billion Budget Caught in the Crossfire

The consequences of this political gridlock extend well beyond partisan rhetoric. Hanging in the balance is the government’s annual budget proposal for the coming year, totaling 820 trillion won (approximately $590 billion USD).

In addition to the national budget, several major legislative measures remain bogged down in committee:

  • The Democratic Reform Veterans Act: A bill intended to provide formal state recognition and benefits to historical democracy activists.
  • The Housing Supply Package: A set of three real estate laws designed to accelerate residential development and stabilize housing costs for working-class citizens.
  • Economic Support Initiatives: Appropriations meant to bolster social welfare programs and stimulate economic growth amid global head winds.

The Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling DPK maintain that the timely passage of the $590 billion budget is essential to maintaining fiscal momentum, assisting low-income households, and investing in key infrastructure. They accuse the PPP of taking the national economy hostage by linking routine budget approvals to the withdrawal of judicial reform bills.

The PPP, on the other hand, contends that it cannot co-sign massive government spending plans while the ruling party uses its legislative majority to push controversial reform packages without bipartisan consensus. With the legal deadline for budget approval approaching, the National Assembly faces the real threat of a total legislative stoppage. If budget negotiations collapse entirely, emergency spending provisions would take effect, halting new public investment programs and delaying crucial social services.

What Lies Ahead: Key Benchmarks to Watch in Seoul

As the National Assembly enters the final stretch of its winter session, the confrontation between the ruling majority and the conservative opposition will reach a decisive moment. Here are the key developments to watch in the coming weeks:

  • The Budget Statutory Deadline: Watch whether the Democratic Party uses its parliamentary majority to pass the 820 trillion won ($590 billion) spending package unilaterally, or if last-minute negotiations produce a compromise before fiscal deadlines force a legislative lapse.
  • The Filibuster Mechanics: Monitor how the People Power Party executes its promised filibuster against the Special Counsel Bill on Fabricated Prosecutions. Under National Assembly rules, a three-fifths majority can vote to end a filibuster after 24 hours, making the timing of the vote a critical tactical maneuver for the DPK leadership.
  • Public Reaction to Street Campaigns: Track whether the opposition's street demonstrations build broader public momentum or trigger a backlash among moderate voters weary of legislative paralysis. Decreasing public support could force the PPP to recalibrate its strategy and return to committee rooms.
  • Fate of Social and Economic Bills: Keep an eye on the progress of the Housing Supply Package and the Democratic Reform Veterans Act, which will serve as clear indicators of whether routine governance can survive the broader battle over judicial reform.

The coming weeks will test the strength and adaptability of South Korea’s democratic institutions. Whether the National Assembly can move past street demonstrations and legislative standoffs will decide not only the fate of a $590 billion budget, but also the future direction of judicial accountability and economic governance in Seoul.
