Apple's Foldable iPhone Challenges Samsung's Monopoly

September 10, 2026

When the livestream for Apple's latest hardware showcase wrapped up in the pre-dawn hours of September 10, tech enthusiasts around the world stared at their screens in disbelief. While everyone had braced themselves for the usual lineup of incremental smartphone and wearable updates, Apple dropped a heavy curveball: its first-ever foldable device, unofficially dubbed the iPhone Duo. Watching live review streams, one common reaction echoed across the internet: you find yourself thinking Apple has lost its mind, only to realize they quietly packed in almost every single feature Android power users had been begging Samsung to add to its Galaxy Z Fold series for years. For an industry that long assumed Apple would take a cautious, wait-and-see approach to the folding phone category, this move signals a serious challenge to Samsung Electronics, South Korea's consumer electronics titan that has dominated the foldable market since its inception.

Initial rumors pointed to a base price of $1,999 in the United States—roughly 2.68 million won. However, those expectations evaporated when local release details for South Korea were confirmed. The starting price for the domestic market has been set at a staggering 3,290,000 won (about $2,450). With pre-orders slated for October 16 and an official release on October 23, consumers are left grappling with a tough question: is any phone truly worth this kind of money? Even accounting for shifting currency exchange rates and local value-added taxes, South Korean buyers face a noticeably higher price tag than shoppers in the US, China, or Japan. Yet, looking past the eye-watering cost, the engineering packed into this device reveals that Apple has systematically targeted every longstanding frustration users had with Samsung's folding lineup. What kind of beast has Apple built, and how does it change the playing field?

The D-Shaped Form Factor and Software Polish

When early computer-aided design leaks surfaced online, the global tech community erupted in skepticism. The device featured an asymmetrical, 'D'-shaped corner profile, leading many commentators to write it off as bizarre and poorly balanced. Critics pointed out the awkward empty space on one side, questioning Apple's design logic. However, once official renders and hands-on units materialized, public sentiment flipped entirely. That initial asymmetry, once viewed as a handicap, was reinterpreted as a bold and distinct hardware identity.

The off-center camera placement on the cover display initially drew widespread grocers. Yet, rather than forcing a strict symmetrical layout, Apple found visual balance within an asymmetric footprint, yielding a surprisingly comfortable grip and distinct visual flavor. Still, reality sets in when examining the hard numbers. According to official specifications, the iPhone Duo measures 117.8mm by 164.6mm with a 5.2mm thickness when unfolded, scaling down to 117.8mm by 84.1mm and 11.3mm when folded. While the engineering team clearly worked hard to trim the thickness down, the 254g (0.56 lbs) weight is difficult to ignore. Slipping it into a pocket makes its heft immediately noticeable, proving that sleek dimensions and physical weight remain in a constant tug-of-war.

The true magic of the device reveals itself the moment the screen transitions. Shifting from the outer cover display to the expansive inner screen triggers a subtle, blurred expansion animation that feels remarkably fluid. In comparison, competing folding devices—including previous entries from Chinese manufacturers and Samsung's Galaxy Fold lineup—typically execute this transition with a harsh, jarring stutter when waking the main panel. Apple’s software engineering effectively erases the physical divide between the two displays.

This software-first philosophy extends deeply into daily usability. Placing the phone onto a MagSafe wireless charger instantly triggers a StandBy clock and calendar interface, even when rested flat. The cover display's Dynamic Island has also been heavily re-engineered to make the most of the narrow exterior screen estate. Features like animating cute graphics on the outer screen to capture a toddler's attention, or using machine learning to auto-frame FaceTime calls, demonstrate how refined software can elevate awkward hardware limitations into an elegant user experience.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The asymmetrical 'D' shape sheds early concerns to deliver a striking, highly recognizable visual profile.
  • Fluid, blurred screen-transition animations give the software experience a distinct edge over existing foldables.
  • An unfolded thickness of just 5.2mm keeps the profile razor-thin, though the 254g weight leaves room for ergonomic improvement.

Under-Display Cameras and Desktop-Class Silicon

Powering on the internal display reveals one of the device's most striking traits: the absolute absence of a camera cutout. Scanning across the massive canvas shows no punch holes or distracting notches, leaving an uninterrupted expanse of glass. This clean look is driven by a refined Under-Display Camera (UDC) system, signaling a massive leap forward in hidden sensor technology.

The irony behind this component lies in its supply chain. The OLED panels used in the iPhone Duo are manufactured by Samsung Display. While Samsung heavily championed UDC technology from the Galaxy Fold 3 through the Fold 6, it notably dropped the feature from its newer Fold 7, reverting to a visible punch-hole camera to optimize selfie quality and reduce manufacturing costs. Samsung previously argued that layering display pixels over a camera degrades light transmittance, muddying photo clarity. However, with Apple launching a folding device that successfully deploys a clean UDC layout, the competitive pressure on Samsung’s upcoming hardware releases will be immense.

This hardware hardware ambition is matched by raw processing power. Drawing architectural DNA from Apple's M4 tablet chips—built on an advanced 3-nanometer process boasting massive multi-core leaps and dedicated Neural Engine capabilities—the iPhone Duo behaves less like a novelty smartphone and more like a mobile workstation. Pushing through 8K video timelines or handling heavy RAW photo exports in real-time highlights a level of thermal and computational headroom that blurs the lines between desktops, tablets, and phones.

📊 By the Numbers

The unfolded profile measures a razor-thin 5.2mm while tipping the scales at 254g. Utilizing Samsung Display panels equipped with an invisible UDC layout, the device houses M4-class architecture capable of delivering multi-core scores approaching 14,500 points right inside a folding form factor.

The Stylus Dilemma: Apple Pencil vs. Samsung S Pen

Perhaps the most shocking revelation during the announcement was the inclusion of stylus support. To hit aggressive weight and thickness targets, Samsung made a calculated trade-off, stripping S Pen support entirely from recent iterations of its foldable line. Adding digitizer layers underneath the screen inherently adds bulk. Yet, Apple managed to integrate stylus capability without compromising its slim chassis—though current support is limited to the legacy, USB-C compatible pencil rather than the newer Apple Pencil Pro.

This exposes a fundamental technological divide between the two ecosystems. Samsung’s Galaxy S Pen relies on Electro-Magnetic Resonance (EMR) technology, meaning the stylus requires no internal battery and remains feather-light. However, it demands a dedicated, heavy digitizer grid installed directly beneath the display panel—a structural requirement that adds both thickness and weight, heavily tied to Samsung's strong industry ties with Wacom.

Apple, conversely, utilizes an Active Electrostatic (AES) approach, bypassing the need for a thick underlying digitizer layer. Instead, it reads stylus inputs through the device's standard capacitive touch sensors, a method similar to those used by Chinese brands like Huawei and Honor. While this keeps the phone remarkably thin, it forces users to accept compromises regarding accessory compatibility and long-term screen resilience.

🔥 Criticisms on Stylus Support and Panel Durability

While stylus integration is a welcome win, restricting compatibility to an older C-type pencil rather than the flagship Apple Pencil Pro feels like a cost-cutting measure disguised as a feature. Furthermore, despite Apple integrating its anti-reflective nano-texture glass treatments to improve scratch resistance by roughly 40%, folding inner polymer screens remain soft compared to standard glass. Heavy, prolonged stylus use will inevitably lead to surface micro-abrasions over time. For all its engineering triumphs, Apple still failed to provide a built-in physical silo to dock the pen, leaving users with a half-measured accessory ecosystem.

Thermal Management, Audio, and the Reality Check

Media consumption on the iPhone Duo benefits immensely from its physical footprint. Unfolding the device reveals a symmetric dual-speaker layout that delivers a rich, room-filling soundstage far surpassing standard smartphone audio. Meanwhile, intensive tasks like 3D gaming or 4K rendering are kept in check by a built-in vapor chamber—a cooling mechanism that allows the device to sustain performance levels up to 35% higher than traditional pro-tier slab phones without aggressively throttling.

Yet, enthusiasm hits a brick wall when factoring in the real-world ergonomics. Weighing in at 254g, the device is noticeably heavier than competing foldables on the market. For casual users who do not edit video or push heavy graphical loads on the go, carrying a heavier, thicker chassis loaded with specialized thermal piping raises fair questions about whether the engineering trade-offs were entirely necessary.

Then comes the matter of pricing. While a US retail price of $1,999 is steep, the domestic South Korean launch price of 3,290,000 won creates a massive barrier to entry. While previous Samsung foldables often launched with relatively favorable regional pricing in South Korea, the iPhone Duo commands a significantly higher premium locally than it does overseas. Even after factoring in high exchange rates and local taxes, paying upwards of 3 million won makes this luxury hardware a tough sell for the mainstream consumer market.

💭 Editor’s Note: Can the Market Bear a 3.29 Million Won Price Tag?

Technological advancement is always thrilling, but when innovation translates directly into exorbitant costs, it forces a moment of reflection. The iPhone Duo introduces genuine milestones: seamless UDC integration, active stylus support, and sophisticated thermal cooling inside a folding body. Yet, its 254g heft and astronomical price tag serve as a blunt reminder of corporate pricing power in an era where owning a top-tier smartphone rivals the cost of a used car. Whether this device represents a transformative leap in personal computing or simply an aggressive test of brand loyalty remains to be seen. One thing is certain: Samsung can no longer afford to sit comfortably on the sidelines.
