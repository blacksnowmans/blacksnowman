What Came Out of Hongeo's Stomach: How a Normal Appetite Almost Fooled Me

October 22, 2026

When the veterinarian showed me what had just been extracted from my cat’s stomach, my jaw dropped in sheer disbelief. Laid out before me were two full drawstrings from my own trousers, alongside an enormous, tangled mass of unidentified cord. It was a terrifying amount of fabric and rope for any animal to ingest, let alone an orange-and-white cat who had spent the preceding days greeting me at the door, chasing his toys, and demanding his meals with his usual enthusiastic purr. Looking at that soaking pile of cloth, I realized just how close we had come to disaster, and how easily I could have missed the warning signs entirely.

My cat, Hongeo, is a 5-year-old, roughly 6.5 kg orange-and-white boy. Under normal circumstances, he is remarkably sturdy. He rarely ever vomits hairballs, his appetite is consistently hearty, and his energy levels are steady. But looking back, the whole ordeal started quietly, disguised beneath what seemed like minor, intermittent indigestion.

The Deceptive Normalcy: Daily Retching and a Healthy Appetite

The trouble began right around the time Hongeo returned from a two-week stay at another home. Almost immediately, I started noticing an unfamiliar, repetitive behavior. It was not a violent, prolonged illness, but rather a short, dry retching that occurred every single day.

  • Brief gagging fits: Hongeo would suddenly stretch his neck forward and dry-heave or retch for about 5 to 10 seconds.
  • Small, delayed vomits: On a Saturday and again on the following Monday, he brought up a small amount of digested kibble—no more than one or two spoonfuls—roughly four hours after eating.
  • Unchanged behavior: Despite these episodes, his appetite remained completely normal, and his daily activity levels never dipped.

Because he was still asking for breakfast, playing with his favorite toys, and rubbing against my legs, I hesitated to assume the worst. I initially wondered if it was a mild digestive upset or an unusually stubborn hairball. I even consulted an acquaintance who is a veterinarian and monitored him closely. But when the gagging episodes refused to stop, my intuition urged me not to ignore them. I realized that the true danger signal was not the volume of vomit, but the persistent recurrence of an entirely new, abnormal behavior.

In hindsight, Hongeo's normal appetite was the most deceptive factor of all. Many cat owners believe that a pet with an ingested object will immediately stop eating and collapse into lethargy. But as long as an object remains within the stomach without completely obstructing the passage into the small intestine, a cat may still feel hungry and digest portions of their meals. Hongeo’s enthusiasm for food masked a ticking time bomb.

Diagnostics at Suwon SKY Animal Medical Center

Deciding not to take any further chances, I brought Hongeo in for an evaluation. We arrived at Suwon SKY Animal Medical Center around 1:00 PM.

The medical team began with an X-ray examination. The initial radiograph suggested that there might be a foreign body inside the stomach, but standard X-rays have clear limitations when it comes to soft, pliable materials like cloth or thread. They simply do not cast the clear, dense shadows that metals or dense bones do.

To obtain a definitive answer, an abdominal ultrasound was performed next. The ultrasound confirmed our fears: there was indeed a foreign body lodged inside Hongeo’s stomach.

The only silver lining at that moment was that the scan showed the foreign material had not yet extended into his intestines. This distinction was critical. Linear foreign bodies—such as yarn, ribbons, cords, and clothing strings—are among the most dangerous things a cat can swallow. If one end of a string enters the intestines while the other remains anchored in the stomach, the natural peristaltic contractions of the gut can cause the delicate intestines to crawl up along the cord like fabric bunched along a drawstring. This can quickly lead to intestinal plication, severe laceration, or complete perforation of the bowel wall. Knowing the material was still confined to Hongeo's stomach gave us a fighting chance to intervene before life-threatening complications occurred.

The Procedure: Gastroscopy with a Backup Plan for Surgery

Dr. Min-seong Kim thoroughly explained the test results and our treatment path. The clinical strategy was precise: they would place Hongeo under general anesthesia and attempt to retrieve the objects using gastroscopy (an upper endoscopy). However, endoscopic retrieval is not guaranteed. Depending on the size, orientation, and entanglement of the objects, or whether the stomach wall had suffered trauma, it might prove impossible to safely pull the items back up through the esophagus. If endoscopy proved unsafe or unsuccessful, the surgical team was prepared to transition immediately into open abdominal surgery (laparotomy/gastrotomy).

Waiting during the procedure was agonizing. Fortunately, the clinic director successfully extracted the foreign material through the endoscope, sparing Hongeo the extensive incisions, pain, and long recovery period associated with open surgery.

When the items were brought out, the sheer volume was staggering. There were two distinct drawstrings pulled from my own sweatpants, along with an extensive, tangled bundle of mystery cord whose exact origin remains unknown to this day. Even though I had occasionally seen Hongeo bat at loose strings in the past, I had never imagined he would consume them in such quantities. Seeing that wet mass made it startlingly clear that an owner must never rely on energy levels alone to rule out a gastrointestinal foreign body.

Waking Up and Returning Home

After the endoscopy was completed, Hongeo was monitored closely as he gradually emerged from the general anesthesia.

We had arrived at the hospital at 1:00 PM, and by approximately 7:30 PM that evening, Hongeo was cleared to be discharged. Our total time at the clinic was roughly six and a half hours. I felt an immense sense of gratitude toward the clinic director for the skill required to maneuver such large, tangled cords safely out through an endoscope, and toward Dr. Kim for walking me through every diagnostic step and contingency plan.

Once we arrived home, Hongeo was still somewhat groggy from the anesthetic drugs. As he walked out of his carrier, I noticed a slight limp on his left hind leg. While mild unsteadiness is common after anesthesia, a persistent limp on one specific limb cannot simply be brushed off as post-anesthetic haze. It was an unexpected development that required follow-up communication with the clinic to monitor his condition properly.

Post-Procedure Care and Crucial Takeaways

Feeding management after an endoscopy requires strict caution. The veterinary staff instructed me to feed Hongeo only about one-third of his usual food portion for his initial post-procedure meal.

Hongeo was starving and persistently begged for his usual bowl of food, but his stomach lining had just endured the friction of foreign bodies and the passage of endoscopic tools. Overwhelming his digestive tract immediately could have triggered severe vomiting or irritation. Post-endoscopy feeding recommendations depend heavily on mucosal irritation and the specifics of the procedure, so following the attending veterinarian's precise instructions is vital.

This experience taught me several invaluable lessons that I believe every cat owner needs to remember:

  • Appetite does not rule out an obstruction: A cat can have a substantial foreign body resting in their stomach while still maintaining a healthy appetite and bright energy. Do not wait for lethargy to appear before seeking help.
  • Take repetitive minor retching seriously: Occasional hairballs happen, but daily gagging, neck-stretching, or regular delayed vomiting—even in spoonful-sized quantities—signals that something is amiss.
  • Linear items are exceptionally dangerous: Strings, drawstrings, cords, and thread must be kept entirely out of reach. If you ever see a string emerging from your cat's mouth or anus, never pull it yourself, as doing so can slice through the gastrointestinal tract. Get to a vet immediately.
  • Endoscopy requires proper evaluation: Not every foreign body can or should be removed via an endoscope. The location of the item, whether it has reached the intestines, and the readiness of the clinic to convert to open surgery if needed are critical factors in safe treatment.

I am profoundly relieved that we caught Hongeo's condition before those long cords migrated past the pylorus and into his intestines. Hongeo is back home, resting and healing, and every drawstring in my household has been tucked securely out of his reach.

Note: This article is a personal account of my cat Hongeo’s medical experience and is not intended to serve as professional veterinary advice. Every case of foreign body ingestion is unique; always consult your own veterinarian promptly if you suspect your pet has swallowed a foreign object or is displaying unusual symptoms.
