Reviving a Dissident Giant: Why South Korea’s Stage Is Calling for the Spirit of Rev. Moon Ik-hwan

October 18, 2026

On an autumn morning in 1989, inside a heavily guarded Seoul courtroom, an elderly woman who looked frail enough to be blown away by a gust of wind stood up from the gallery. Her son, a 70-year-old Presbyterian pastor with snow-white hair, had just been led into the dock bound in prison ropes. He had committed what South Korea’s military-backed establishment considered an unforgivable crime: an unauthorized, clandestine journey across the Cold War frontier to Pyongyang to discuss peaceful reunification with North Korea. As murmurs rippled through the room, ninety-year-old Kim Shin-muk filled the hall with a booming roar: "Ik-hwan! Hold your head high! Face this trial like Jesus climbing Golgotha!"

For decades, the Reverend Moon Ik-hwan was precisely that—a walking cross in South Korea’s turbulent march toward democracy and peace. Yet more than thirty years after his death in 1994, the historical memory of this towering dissident has found itself caught in ideological crossfire. While conservative administrations steadily rolled back his presence in national textbooks—culminating in an almost total erasure during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office before he was impeached and imprisoned for insurrection—Moon’s family and contemporaries have concluded that political monuments and scholarly monographs are no longer enough to keep his spirit alive.

Instead, they are turning to the theater. A newly announced playwriting competition is offering an unusually generous grand purse of about $75,000 (100 million won) to summon Moon’s fierce, complex life back onto the stage. It is not merely a memorial prize, but a calculated cultural intervention designed to rescue a founding father of Korean civil society from ideological oblivion.

The $75,000 Search for a Living Icon

The logistics of the open call reveal an unusual degree of urgency and ambition. Rather than restricting submissions to standard two-act spoken plays, the organizers have flung the doors wide open to any theatrical medium capable of commanding an audience: musicals, verse dramas, liturgical theater, and experimental music plays. There are no seniority barriers; established playwrights and unproduced novices will compete under pseudonyms, evaluated purely on the vitality of their text. Submissions will be accepted on a rolling basis until December 31, 2027—giving writers nearly two full years to grapple with their subject.

The institutional backing signals that this will not be a polite, one-off memorial tribute. The jury is chaired by Lee Chang-dong, the internationally celebrated auteur and former South Korean Minister of Culture best known for cinematic masterpieces like Peppermint Candy and Burning. Furthermore, Munhakdongne, one of South Korea’s preeminent literary publishing houses, has guaranteed that five or six shortlisted scripts will be published together in a single volume. The objective is to seed a lasting repertoire that can be performed across the independent playhouses of Daehangno—Seoul’s bustling theater district—as well as in university auditoriums and church halls across the country.

Behind this open call lies a painful, 30-year family struggle. Moon’s two sons—the late theater director Moon Ok-geun and the veteran screen actor Moon Sung-keun—had tried to bring their father’s life to the stage since 1990. Moon Ok-geun visited his father in prison, searching for an imaginative spark by asking if he had ever crossed paths as a child in Manchuria with his future schoolmates, the revered dissident journalist Jang Jun-ha and the iconic martyr-poet Yun Dong-ju. The pastor smiled gently and replied, "No, we met separately."

For fifteen years after his brother’s death, Moon Sung-keun sat with a pen, trying to write the drama himself. He consistently failed. As a son who had watched his father endure police beatings and cold cells, the emotional proximity was paralyzing. Recognizing that filial piety was clouding dramatic honesty, the family has now stepped aside, handing the raw material over to the unsparing eye of professional dramatists.

From "The Slamming Door" to Street Dissident

The man South Korea remembers was an electrifying orator who could bring tens of thousands of pro-democracy protesters to tears on teargas-choked boulevards. Yet before his political awakening in the mid-1970s, Moon was the absolute antithesis of a populist hero. As an Old Testament scholar at Hanshin University, his campus nickname was "Moon the Door-Slammer" (Moon-i-kwang). If a student was ten minutes late submitting a term paper, Moon would slam the lecture hall door shut in their face without hesitation.

Suffering from severe hypertension that cost him hearing in one ear in his late forties, he was an irritable, rigid intellectual. At home, he reigned as an austere Confucian-Christian patriarch whose children tiptoed around the house to avoid triggering a tempest. Moon was agonizingly aware of his own petty, short-tempered nature, and he spent his middle age locked in bitter internal self-reproach.

He was also a man haunted by ghosts. His childhood friend Yun Dong-ju had died in a Japanese prison at age twenty-seven, having written some of the most haunting poetry in the Korean language. Moon carried survivor’s guilt like an open wound. He channeled that pain into an all-consuming academic mission: leading the historic Catholic-Protestant joint translation of the Old Testament into modern Korean, a groundbreaking ecumenical project initiated in 1968. Moon treated the translation as an absolute moral vow, working night and day in his study even as South Korea descended into the brutal authoritarianism of Park Chung-hee’s Yushin regime.

The dam finally broke in August 1975, when his lifelong compatriot, dissident leader Jang Jun-ha, died under deeply suspicious circumstances at the foot of a cliff. The scholarly sanctuary collapsed. On March 1, 1976, Moon drafted the historic "Declaration for Democratic National Salvation" at Myeongdong Cathedral. He deliberately omitted his own name from the public list of signatories, hoping to stay out of prison just long enough to finish the final chapters of the Old Testament translation. But when a co-conspirator with a heart condition faced life-threatening interrogation, Moon’s brother revealed the truth to investigators. Moon was arrested, thrust into solitary confinement, and severed from his academic quietude forever.

Remarkably, the brutal crucible of prison did not embitter him; it dismantled his neuroses. The rigid academic who once slammed doors emerged from his first prison sentence as a warm, magnanimous figure whose home was left permanently unlocked for wandering students, factory workers, and fugitives. He had remade his very character to serve the needs of a battered society.

Ten Years in Chains: The Unfinished Battle Over Korean Division

Moon Ik-hwan’s subsequent life became a mirror of South Korea’s democratic agony. Over the final seventeen years of his life, he spent a staggering ten years and three months behind bars. His six distinct prison terms were handed down with grim symmetry: twice under Park Chung-hee, twice under Chun Doo-hwan, and twice under Roh Tae-woo. Every time the regime changed hands, the state threw Moon back into a cell.

Yet Moon was never a partisan politician; he functioned as what director Lee Chang-dong described as a "true clown"—a traditional, sacred performer (gwangdae) who absorbs the communal sorrow of the people and transmutes it into defiant, celebratory energy. In the early 1990s, after evading undercover intelligence agents for hours, a seventy-year-old Moon finally arrived at a late-night labor union rally. Instead of launching into an austere political lecture, he grabbed the microphone and asked the exhausted, solemn blue-collar workers how often they made passionate love to their spouses, roaring: "Love deepens the more you share it! I still do it once a week! Love each other fiercely!" The room erupted in laughter and tears, shattering the grim solemnity of the authoritarian era.

When the 1980 Gwangju Uprising catalyzed a militant, youth-led labor movement, Moon did not retreat into generational conservatism. Instead, he founded the United People’s Movement for Democratic Unification (Mintongryeon) in 1985, building a bridge between venerable intellectual dissenters and the firebrand student radicals on the barricades.

While South Korea achieved procedural democracy following the mass uprisings of June 1987, Moon’s legacy remains contentious precisely because his second cause—peaceful national reunification—remains unresolved. While his pro-democracy struggle can be comfortably acknowledged, his 1989 flight to North Korea to meet Kim Il-sung remains an explosive cultural tripwire. Under conservative administrations, including during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, state-sanctioned narratives marginalized Moon’s contribution to inter-Korean dialogue. Because the Cold War division continues to govern modern Korean politics, Moon cannot simply be packed away as a finished museum piece; he remains a live nerve.

The Art of Empty Space

When Moon Sung-keun initially informed his father that he wanted to become an actor, the pastor was unexpectedly delighted. But as his son gained fame on the silver screen, the elder Moon offered a singular piece of advice that perplexed the young artist for decades: "In acting, there must always be empty space (yeobaek)."

To an actor trained to fill every second with fierce, unbroken intensity, the advice seemed counterintuitive. Only decades later did the younger Moon realize what his father meant: true power lies in holding back self-righteous certainty, creating a breathing room where the audience can step in, reflect, and share the burden of the emotion. Moon Ik-hwan’s own life had been an exercise in that artistic and spiritual restraint—holding his tongue to finish a holy translation, enduring a decade of cold concrete without hatred, and stepping down from the pedestal of academic prestige to sit alongside union laborers.

In South Korea’s contemporary political landscape—dominated by hyper-polarized cable commentary, bitter generational divides, and skyrocketing economic pressures on working families—that spacious moral leadership is sorely missed. As the Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung navigates governance against a recalcitrant conservative opposition, the broader progressive movement is grappling with its own historical memory and the absence of moral giants.

What remains to be seen over the next three years is whether South Korea’s playwrights can rise above hagiography. The true test of this competition will not be whether it produces a reverent monument to a dead martyr, but whether an artist can capture the friction, the petty faults, the humor, and the harrowing inner terror of an intellectual who chose to set himself on fire so that others might see in the dark. The deadline of December 2027 gives the theatrical world enough time to create that space—and the stages of Daehangno are waiting.
