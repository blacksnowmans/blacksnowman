Impeaching the Chief Justice: Inside South Korea's High-Stakes Battle Over Judicial Power

November 24, 2026

When a senior judge with a quarter-century of service on the bench publicly turns against their own institution, it is a sign that normal constitutional friction has escalated into a structural crisis. Deep within the internal network of South Korea's judiciary, a 25-year veteran judge recently published a sharp critique questioning a controversial ruling involving former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. The post pointedly accused the court of performing a delicate balancing act—clearing specific charges while leaving calculated ambiguities to grant a political exit strategy. It was an extraordinary rupture from an institution famous for its tight-lipped insular culture.

Outside court buildings, the political fallout has been even swifter. In the halls of the National Assembly, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are preparing to take a step rarely seen in the republic's history: drafting a formal motion to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. What began as simmering frustration over judicial slowness and alleged political bias has now erupted into a full-scale constitutional showdown over the limits of judicial authority.

Cracks in the Gavel: Internal Dissent and Public Discontent

The move to impeach Chief Justice Cho is no longer mere political rhetoric; it has entered an operational phase. Democratic Party lawmaker Yang Bu-nam confirmed in a recent broadcast interview that a formal impeachment bill is scheduled for completion within days. Leading the effort alongside Yang are prominent party figures including Representative Park Joo-min and Representative No Jong-myeon. Together, they represent a unified push by the ruling party—currently led under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—to hold the head of the judiciary accountable for what they term a systemic breakdown in judicial integrity.

To understand why this move carries such weight, foreign observers must look beyond standard partisan squabbling. For decades, South Korea's judiciary operated behind an impenetrable shield of "judicial independence." While designed to protect judges from authoritarian coercion, critics argue this shield gradually turned the court system into a sanctuary immune from public oversight. The growing public skepticism toward the courts is not an isolated phenomenon; it is rooted in how judges handled politically sensitive trials during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, particularly cases connected to charges of insurrection and constitutional disruption.

When judges appear to stall critical proceedings or offer conveniently timed procedural leniency to powerful figures, the perception of strict political neutrality crumbles. The veteran judge's internal whistleblower post regarding the Han Duck-soo ruling hit a raw nerve precisely because it echoed what many citizens already suspected: that legal logic was being manipulated for political calculus. When internal officers begin arguing that judicial opinions are acting as handmaidens to power rather than impartial arbiters, the moral authority of the high court reaches a dangerous tipping point.

The Legal Strategy: Targeting Delays Instead of Verdicts

Impeaching a sitting Chief Justice is a legal minefield. If the National Assembly seeks to remove the head of the judiciary simply because lawmakers dislike the outcome of a specific verdict, it risks triggering a constitutional disaster and violating the doctrine of separation of powers. The Democratic Party's impeachment task force is acutely aware of this hazard, and its legal strategy reflects a calculated attempt to bypass that trap.

Instead of challenging the substance of court rulings, the draft impeachment motion focuses exclusively on procedural abuse and administrative misconduct. At the heart of their argument is the alleged violation of statutory trial timelines, often referred to in South Korean legal circles as the "633 principle"—a procedural guideline stipulating that initial trials should conclude within six months, with subsequent appellate stages taking no longer than three months each. The task force argues that Chief Justice Cho and court leadership intentionally dragged out proceedings, delayed the scheduling of key hearings, and misused their administrative control over trial calendars.

Key procedural grounds driving the draft impeachment motion include:

  • Systematic Trial Delays: Willful non-compliance with statutory trial period guidelines, effectively withholding timely decisions on high-profile cases.
  • Manipulation of Hearing Dates: Misuse of docket management power to stall or reschedule critical sentencing dates for political expediency.
  • Infringement on Constitutional Rights: Denying the public and involved parties their constitutional right to a swift and fair trial through intentional inaction.

By framing the issue as an arbitrary abuse of procedural management rather than a conflict over legal interpretation, the Democratic Party aims to build a rock-solid constitutional argument. The party has set a tight internal deadline, aiming to shepherd the impeachment motion through the National Assembly floor before the end of the year. For the ruling party, establishing this precedent is presented as an essential step toward restoring public confidence in a court system that answers to the constitution rather than to judicial privilege.

Debunking the Framing: Media Warfare and Court Paralysis

As the momentum for impeachment builds, conservative news outlets and opposition commentators have launched a fierce counter-offensive. Their core narrative presents the impeachment as an unconstitutional assault on judicial autonomy—an attempt by the ruling party to bully the bench into favorable rulings. Conservative commentators further warn that removing the Chief Justice will throw the Supreme Court into absolute chaos, paralyzing its operational capacity.

One specific argument circulating in conservative media centers on judicial training cohorts. Out of the 11 sitting Supreme Court justices, two were classmates of Yoon Suk-yeol during their time at the Judicial Research and Training Institute—the state-run institution through which all South Korean legal professionals historically passed. Conservative commentators contend that if these justices are forced to recuse themselves due to potential conflicts of interest in ongoing cases related to Yoon Suk-yeol's insurrection trial, the apex court will lack the quorum necessary to function, effectively freezing the high court.

Lawmakers championing the impeachment push back aggressively against this narrative, calling it a fabricated distraction designed to protect key figures from legal accountability. Historically, personal acquaintance or shared training class backgrounds have rarely forced automatic recusal or halted proceedings in major political cases. For instance, similar cohort connections did not stop the bench from ruling on previous high-profile cases involving President Lee Jae-myung. The argument that the Supreme Court will collapse into paralysis is, from the perspective of the impeachment's proponents, a tactical maneuver meant to muddy the waters and delay trials related to the primary figures of the previous period of unrest.

The core conflict comes down to two fundamentally opposed views of the bench. Conservative critics frame any political action against the Chief Justice as an authoritarian threat to judicial independence. Supporters of impeachment counter that judicial independence was never intended to be an absolute immunity shield for administrative negligence and deliberate delays. When the bench abuses its control over trial schedules to influence political outcomes, they argue, it forfeits the trust upon which its authority relies.

What to Watch Next

The struggle over the Supreme Court Chief Justice marks a pivotal moment for South Korea's legal institutions, with implications that will resonate long after the current legislative session ends. Over the coming weeks, several concrete milestones will indicate which way the scales will tip:

  • Finalization of the Draft Motion: Watch for the official release of the impeachment text by Democratic Party representatives Yang Bu-nam, Park Joo-min, and No Jong-myeon to examine the exact statutory violations cited.
  • National Assembly Floor Vote: Monitor the legislative calendar as the Democratic Party attempts to secure a full parliamentary vote before the end of the year.
  • Reactions from Within the Judiciary: Track whether more judges break rank on internal court networks, or if the judiciary unifies behind Chief Justice Cho in defense of institutional autonomy.
  • Movement on Delayed Trials: Observe how the Supreme Court handles pending high-stakes dockets, particularly the stalled proceedings surrounding Yoon Suk-yeol's insurrection charges.

If the National Assembly passes the impeachment motion, it will send the case to the Constitutional Court for a final verdict, pushing South Korea into uncharted legal territory. Whether this high-stakes gamble ultimately cleanses the judiciary or further deepens the nation's political divides will depend entirely on how convincingly the argument for procedural accountability is made in the weeks ahead.
