The Day the Numbers Screamed: Understanding the KOSPI’s Brutal Deleveraging Cycle

October 15, 2026

As soon as the market opened, the numbers began to scream. On the trading screens of Seoul, the KOSPI—South Korea’s benchmark stock index—plunged nearly 9% in a matter of hours. Representative blue-chip stocks, the pillars of the national economy, collapsed by double digits. Watching a brokerage account balance melt away in real-time is a visceral horror that words struggle to capture. In offices across the country, investors stared at their monitors in a suffocating silence, broken only by the frantic ringing of phones. "Where is the bottom?" and "Should I sell everything now?" became the desperate refrains of a market in freefall.

In just two days, hundreds of billions of dollars in market capitalization evaporated. This wasn't a decline that could be explained away by tidy macroeconomic theories or textbook models. It was the sound of a tower built on debt finally buckling under its own weight. To understand why the Korean market experienced such a violent spasm, one must look past the headlines of "global tech sell-offs" and into the unique, debt-fueled architecture of the Korean retail trading scene. This was not just a market correction; it was a systemic purge of leverage that had reached a breaking point.

The Detonator: A Forced Liquidation of Debt-Fueled Dreams

For months, the KOSPI had been propped up by a dangerous amount of Shin-yong—the Korean term for margin trading or credit-based purchases. As the index climbed, fueled by the global AI craze, retail investors poured into the market using borrowed money. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the world’s leading memory chip makers, became the primary targets for this leverage. At its peak, the margin debt tied to just these two stocks approached 10 trillion won (about $7.2 billion).

The tragedy of leverage is that it works beautifully on the way up but acts as a guillotine on the way down. In South Korea, brokerage firms typically require a collateral maintenance ratio of 140%. If the value of the stocks falls below this threshold, and the investor cannot provide more cash, the brokerages trigger "Bandae-maemae"—automatic, forced liquidations at the next day's market open. These are market-price sell orders that happen regardless of the stock's actual value or the investor's wishes.

"The bottom of this market will only be found when the debt in Samsung and Hynix is fully purged."

This grim realization has become common among market veterans. When the initial drop occurred, it triggered a first wave of margin calls. This selling drove prices lower, which triggered a second wave, then a third. This "one-two-three punch" of forced liquidations creates a feedback loop of panic. Statistics from the Korea Financial Investment Association recently showed margin debt exceeding 32 trillion won (about $23 billion), a historic high. Until this mountain of debt is ground down—ideally back toward the 4 trillion won level for the major tech stocks—the market remains on a hair-trigger. For those trading with their own cash, the pain is psychological; for those on margin, the pain is terminal.

The Vicious Cycle: On the way up, leverage is a drug that amplifies gains. On the way down, forced liquidations tear into the flesh of even those who hold their stocks outright, as the sheer volume of mechanical selling drags the entire index into the abyss.

The Psychology of 73% Fear

In the middle of such a crash, sentiment becomes a leading indicator. Data from seasoned investor communities showed that roughly 73% of participants felt the market was in "extreme danger." Usually, when even the most hardened, long-term investors are terrified, the market is nearing its emotional floor. Historically, when fear levels hit these heights—such as during the early days of the 2020 pandemic or brief geopolitical scares in 2026—a technical rebound followed almost immediately.

The irony of the stock market is that it is often safest when it feels the most dangerous. At the point where 7 out of 10 investors are ready to surrender, the "selling climax" is reached. The short-sellers begin to buy back to lock in profits, and "vulture" investors who have been sitting on cash start to pick through the wreckage. In the volatile 5,000 to 7,000 point range that the KOSPI has inhabited recently, these moments of peak public outcry have historically been the most profitable entry points for those with the stomach to buy.

However, for the average person, "buying the dip" during a 9% crash feels less like a strategy and more like catching a falling knife. The advice often given by Korean market analysts is to "buy while being hit"—meaning, do not try to time the exact bottom. If you wait for the "all clear" signal, you will likely miss the 5% or 7% rebound that happens in a single afternoon. The goal isn't to be perfect; it's to be present when the tide turns.

Critiquing the Lee Jae-myung Administration’s Response

As the market crumbled, the response from the government of President Lee Jae-myung was met with widespread skepticism. The Financial Services Commission (FSC)—the government agency responsible for financial oversight—held emergency meetings, only to issue a familiar and hollow refrain: "The fundamentals of the Korean economy remain solid."

To many investors, this "out-of-body" rhetoric feels like a betrayal. There is a long, dark history in Korean politics of officials claiming "fundamentals are fine" right before a catastrophe, most notably before the 1997 IMF crisis. Currently, the administration is grappling with the economic aftershocks of the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is now imprisoned following his impeachment. While the Lee Jae-myung government has been quick to intervene in the real estate market to protect apartment prices, it has been curiously passive as the stock market—where 14 million Koreans hold their savings—deteriorated.

The most pointed criticism involves the "Stock Market Stabilization Fund" (Jeung-an Fund). This is a pool of roughly 10 trillion won (about $7.2 billion) contributed by financial institutions and exchanges to act as a buffer during crashes. Historically used during the 2008 Lehman Brothers crisis and the 2004 credit card crisis, the fund has been "ready" for years but never deployed during this latest rout.

Policy Inertia: Why is the government willing to pour tens of trillions into bailing out construction companies and real estate projects, yet refuses to use the 10 trillion won stabilization fund to catch the falling KOSPI? The fund should not be viewed as a charity for speculators, but as a strategic tool to buy undervalued national assets and break the cycle of forced liquidations.

The call from the market is simple: the government should stop offering "verbal interventions" and start buying. If the KOSPI is truly undervalued at these levels, as the FSC claims, then the Stabilization Fund should have no fear of purchasing Samsung Electronics or Hyundai Motor at these prices. Using the fund to absorb the "passive" selling pressure would provide the floor the market so desperately needs.

The AI Bubble vs. Semiconductor Reality

Beyond Korea’s domestic debt issues, the crash was exacerbated by a global debate over the "AI Bubble." Market skeptics pointed to companies like Anthropic, which saw 40% quarterly growth but failed to ignite the market because the "run-rate"—the slope of the growth curve—is finally starting to flatten. There are valid concerns about power shortages for data centers and the sheer capital expenditure required to keep the AI dream alive.

However, the actual data from the semiconductor sector tells a more nuanced story. TSMC, the Taiwanese foundry giant, recently posted a net profit margin of over 50%. The demand for high-end chips still exceeds supply. For South Korea, the focus is on HBM (High Bandwidth Memory). This is a specialized type of memory essential for AI processors. Producing HBM is difficult; it requires stacking chips and has a much lower "yield" (the percentage of functional chips) than standard RAM.

This technical bottleneck is actually a blessing in disguise for Samsung Electronics. Because HBM takes up so much manufacturing capacity, it reduces the overall supply of standard memory, keeping prices stable. Samsung’s Book Value Per Share (BPS)—essentially what the company would be worth if it were liquidated today—is projected to hit 100,000 won (about $72) by the end of the year. With the stock trading significantly below its historical valuation multiples, the "fundamental" argument for a recovery is strong, even if the "psychological" argument is currently weak.

  • Samsung Valuation: The stock is approaching its historical Price-to-Book (PBR) floor.
  • Dividend Safety: Large Korean firms, under pressure to improve shareholder returns and facing inheritance tax obligations for their founding families, are unlikely to cut dividends.
  • Supply Constraints: The transition to HBM production is structurally limiting the supply of traditional DRAM, which should prevent a total collapse in chip prices.

The Strategy of "Body-Blocking" the Crash

In the face of such a violent market, what is a retail investor to do? The most effective, albeit painful, strategy is what some call "body-blocking" (mom-bbang). This means refusing to be shaken out of positions by temporary price movements. If you are not using margin, you cannot be forced to sell. Time is the only asset that the market cannot take away from you.

The worst mistake during a 9% drop is "horizontal movement"—selling a stock that has crashed to buy another one that has crashed slightly less. This often results in being "whipsawed," where you sell at the bottom of one stock only to miss its rebound while the new stock continues to fall. For those with cash, the current market offers dividend yields that are becoming impossible to ignore. Some preferred shares of blue-chip companies like Hyundai Motor or major Korean banks are now offering dividend yields of 7% to 9%, far outpacing bank deposit rates.

What to watch next:

  • The 140% Trigger: Watch the opening 30 minutes of the market. If the index stabilizes after the initial wave of forced liquidations, it suggests the leverage purge is nearing completion.
  • The Stabilization Fund: Any announcement of the actual deployment of the 10 trillion won fund will be the "bazooka" that ends the panic.
  • Foreign Net Buying: Look for when foreign institutional investors stop their program selling and begin bottom-fishing in the semiconductor sector.

The KOSPI’s 9% plunge was a brutal lesson in the dangers of debt. We have seen that those who build on borrowed time are eventually forced to pay the price. Yet, amid the ruins, the underlying companies—the ones actually making the chips, cars, and batteries the world needs—remain intact. The question for the investor is whether they will be paralyzed by the "screaming numbers" on the screen, or whether they will recognize the rare, if painful, opportunity to buy a piece of Korea’s future at a fire-sale price.
