Two Faces of Korean Justice: From Midnight Protests to Closed-Door Privileges

October 19, 2026

A little over a decade ago in downtown Seoul, the setting of the sun turned ordinary citizens into presumptive criminals. During the massive 2008 candlelight demonstrations against U.S. beef imports, police cordons moved in as twilight fell over Gwanghwamun Square. Officers with shields and water cannons laced with tear gas began herding people into transport vans. Thousands of office workers, university students, and retirees were detained simply because South Korea's Assembly and Demonstration Act strictly prohibited outdoor rallies after dark. On the asphalt, blocking the vans with an attorney badge held high, stood Park Joo-min—then a young public-interest attorney, now a prominent lawmaker for the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's current ruling party.

Park spent over a year challenging that blanket curfew in the Constitutional Court, eventually winning a landmark ruling of unconstitutionality that dismantled the ban. That single decision cleared the legal ground for the peaceful, illuminated vigils that later shaped modern Korean political history. Yet today, looking at the halls of power in Seocho-dong—Seoul's judicial and prosecutorial nerve center—and Seoul City Hall, the contrast could hardly be starker. While civil rights advocates once risked arrest on cold pavement to secure basic liberties, today's institutions face growing public fury over allegations of political favoritism, swept-under-the-rug audits, and fiscal mismanagement.

The Street Lawyer Who Dismantled a Post-Sunset Curfew

To understand the depth of public cynicism toward the justice system today, one has to recall how aggressively the state once wielded procedural statutes against ordinary speech. Article 10 of the Assembly and Demonstration Act had long been used as a cudgel to choke off demonstrations once working hours ended. Conservative administrations maintained that nighttime gatherings inevitably invited public disorder, framing peaceful chanting as criminal defiance.

Park Joo-min argued the basic constitutional proposition that free assembly cannot cease simply because the sun drops below the horizon. His legal fight tore down that legacy provision, allowing nighttime vigils to occur lawfully. Behind that high-profile constitutional triumph lay relentless, grinding labor. Working through Minbyun (Lawyers for a Democratic Society), South Korea's leading progressive legal association, Park spent years anchoring the rear lines of rallies, staying until dawn to shield protesters from unlawful police battery.

When detentions occurred, his work shifted to precinct lockups across the capital. Park spent endless nights traveling between police holding cells to advise frightened detainees and prevent coercive interrogations. His workload was so relentless that Minbyun awarded him its "King of Visits" title—given to the attorney who logged the most detainee visits—two years running. His life was so consumed by frontline advocacy that even his private life collided with it: on the day set for formal pre-wedding family meetings with his fiancée, also a dedicated Minbyun labor attorney, she failed to appear because police had just loaded her into a paddy wagon for standing with striking factory workers.

Selective Justice: High-Profile Inquiries That Ground to a Halt

The contrast between that street-level defense of constitutional rights and the conduct of elite prosecutors during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was later removed from office by impeachment and is now in prison—could not be more glaring. Rather than applying equal pressure under the law, critics argue that the prosecution service selectively shielded powerful figures around the ruling establishment.

Nowhere was this more visible than in the inquiries involving Mr. Yoon's spouse, Kim Keon-hee. Despite nationwide uproar surrounding the acceptance of a luxury Christian Dior handbag and allegations concerning the Deutsche Motors stock manipulation case, investigators effectively gave the first lady extraordinary deference. Senior prosecutors from the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office traveled directly to a secure presidential security facility rather than summoning her to a public prosecution building. Investigators reportedly surrendered their mobile phones before conducting the interview—an accommodation branded by critics as "emperor-style questioning." Case handlers later cleared her of charges, insisting the facts did not support an indictment, fueling accusations that prosecutors acted more as defense counsel than impartial magistrates.

A similar perception of selective restraint emerged regarding the academic credentials of the daughter of Han Dong-hoon, the former leader of the conservative People Power Party (South Korea's main opposition party) and former justice minister under Mr. Yoon. The controversy centered on allegations that an outsourced third-party application was submitted as her own project in international competitions, ghostwritten papers, and submissions to dubious open-access journals to pad college admissions portfolios. Yet the police never sought basic search-and-seizure warrants, conducting only a single written inquiry with Han's spouse before declining to refer the case for prosecution. Testimony during parliamentary audits later revealed that higher echelons at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office and the Ministry of Justice failed to forward mutual legal assistance requests to overseas institutions, cutting off avenues to verify foreign admissions documentation.

The Hangang River Bus Scandal and City Hall's Cover-Up Accusations

Institutional decay is not confined to the courts and prosecutor's offices; it has also cast a shadow over Seoul City Hall. Conservative Mayor Oh Se-hoon, currently serving his fifth overall term, has championed the "Hangang River Bus"—a commuter water-transit fleet intended as the centerpiece of his ambitious waterfront development agenda. But the multi-million-dollar initiative has become embroiled in allegations of kickbacks and administrative tampering.

Parliamentary inquiries and investigative reports uncovered an irregular financial transfer during vessel construction. Gadeok Heavy Industries, the shipyard contracted to build the passenger vessels, transferred funds through a suspected paper firm called Seongwoo ENG, involving roughly 320 million won (about $240,000) in uncharacteristic brokerage fees. Industry observers pointed out that such commissions are highly anomalous in routine domestic shipbuilding, raising alarms that the money functioned as an under-the-table rebate aimed at high-ranking officials at the Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation (SH) or the municipal government.

The situation escalated from suspected commercial graft to an administrative scandal when whistleblowers revealed what happened inside the Seoul Metropolitan Government's internal audit committee. After uncovering the suspicious money trail, the inspection team drafted a formal request for a police criminal investigation and briefed Mayor Oh in person on two separate occasions. Instead of greenlighting an external probe, the head of the audit committee allegedly instructed investigators to destroy all working documents and case files, asking why the city should "spoon-feed the police." While City Hall claimed it submitted the matter to the national Board of Audit and Inspection, the submission was reduced to a single-page notice focusing merely on delivery delays, completely omitting the bribery allegations and kickback testimonies.

Eroding Trust in the Supreme Court

Even the apex of the judicial hierarchy has not escaped ethical scrutiny. Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and Court Administration Chief Roh Kyung-pil have been referred to investigators at the anti-corruption division of the Major Crimes Investigation Office over allegations of abuse of authority and embezzlement involving official operational expense cards.

Controversies over spending on lavish omakase dinners, paired with revelations that an appellate bench managed to review 70,000 pages of complex electronic case records within a mere two-day window, have severely tarnished public confidence in the nation's highest court. The judiciary derives its authority entirely from public trust in its impartiality and intellectual rigor; when the Chief Justice faces criminal scrutiny for discretionary spending while decisions are rendered at speeds that defy thorough review, the foundation of judicial legitimacy cracks.

What to Watch Next

South Korea stands at an institutional crossroads where the public's tolerance for prosecutorial double standards and municipal cover-ups has worn thin. Over the coming months, several key developments will signal whether accountability can be restored:

  • Transfer of Shelved Inquiries: Watch whether cases once quietly closed—including the allegations surrounding Han Dong-hoon's daughter's portfolio—gain traction as files are reviewed by the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency and potential independent investigative units.
  • The Hangang Bus Kickback Inquiry: Whistleblower records preserved on password-protected hard drives will determine whether prosecutors launch a formal probe into SH officials and Mayor Oh's senior aides regarding the missing 320 million won ($240,000).
  • Reckoning for Seocho-dong Prosecutors: With special counsel probes and oversight hearings targeting the prosecutors who conducted private questioning of Kim Keon-hee, disciplinary and legal fallout may establish new baselines for how investigative power can be checked.
  • Chief Justice Cho's Expense Probe: The Major Crimes Investigation Office's handling of the criminal referral against Supreme Court leadership will be a critical barometer of whether the judiciary's highest officers remain accountable to the laws they administer.
