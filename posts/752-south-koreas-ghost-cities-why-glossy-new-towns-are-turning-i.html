South Korea’s Ghost Cities: Why Glossy New Towns are Turning into Concrete Ruins

September 14, 2026

Step off a highway about an hour and forty minutes outside of Seoul, past the edges of the capital’s endless commuter sprawl, and you enter a surreal landscape. To one side stretches a brilliant, man-made wave park and clean, empty asphalt that catches the afternoon sun. To the other side sits a row of glass-fronted commercial buildings with doors choked by mildew, floors overgrown with waist-high weeds, and faded "For Lease" signs hanging by shreds of dry adhesive. This is Siheung Turtle Island, a waterfront leisure complex that has become one of South Korea's most notorious "ghost towns," where retail vacancy rates hover around an astonishing 87%.

For decades, the Korean real estate market operated on an article of faith: if you build a gleaming new development with pretty architectural renderings, people and money will follow. Local governments, state-backed corporations, and private developers partnered up to turn reclaimed tidal flats and quiet peninsulas into futuristic hubs for tourism, shopping, and high-density living. But as South Korea faces a sharp demographic downturn, these once-hyped projects are colliding with a harsh economic reality. Turtle Island and similar developments like Incheon's Midan City reveal the dark side of a hyper-growth mindset that prioritized speculative land sales over sustainable urban planning.

The Anatomy of a Failed Waterfront Development

Turtle Island began in 2018 as an ambitious joint venture between the city of Siheung, the state-run Korea Water Resources Corporation (K-water), and private construction firms. The vision was a marine leisure complex centered around Asia’s largest man-made surf lagoon, Wave Park. The infrastructure itself is genuinely impressive—the artificial waves are world-class, rivaling setups in Hawaii or Dubai. Yet, walking through the nearby commercial districts feels less like visiting a Mediterranean resort and more like wandering through an abandoned movie set.

The core issue lies in a fundamental structural imbalance. According to data from the Siheung Institute for Urban Affairs, commercial zones account for an eye-popping 49.9% of Turtle Island's total area. For comparison, the national average for commercial land use in South Korea sits at just 1.9%, while densely packed Seoul stands at 4.3%. Local authorities carved up and sold off small retail spaces to maximize short-term land-sale profits and fund environmental cleanup costs around Lake Sihwa—reportedly dumping about 400 billion won ($290 million) worth of financial burdens onto the project. They created a retail district with almost no permanent residential population to support it, completely isolated by a lack of fast highway connections.

The human cost of this miscalculation has fallen squarely on everyday investors. Small business owners and retirees who sank their life savings into these storefronts, trusting government-backed promotional materials, now find themselves trapped. Without a steady influx of residents or reliable transit links—such as stalled regional highway extensions—relying purely on summer tourists to sustain an entire economy has proven to be a losing bet.

Midan City and the Trap of Speculative Megaprojects

While Turtle Island suffers from oversupplied retail space and poor access, Incheon’s Midan City represents a different kind of urban tragedy: a megaproject frozen mid-construction. Located on Yeongjong Island, near Incheon International Airport, Midan City was meant to feature a sprawling 1.5 trillion won ($1.1 billion) luxury hotel and casino resort complex led by China's R&F Group starting around 2015. Instead, the venture was hammered by geopolitical tensions, strict Chinese regulations on overseas gambling, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Today, Midan City’s incomplete concrete skeletons stand as bleak monuments to speculative overreach. After dark, the area plunges into a stillness where local convenience store owners report seeing virtually no foot traffic after 7:30 PM. The underlying strategy was flawed from the start: relying almost entirely on foreign tourists to fill a massive resort anchored by a foreigner-only casino, in a region already competing with other gaming venues on the same island. When the developers and major stakeholders pulled back, local contractors and residents were left holding the bag.

Local politicians have frequently used these distressed developments as campaign backdrops, promising a renaissance ahead of national elections, only for attention to evaporate the moment the ballots are counted. Meanwhile, developers and executives have faced fraud accusations over misleading franchising pitches, while ordinary buyers watch their investments turn to dust.

The Aging Pains of First-Generation New Towns

It is not just newly minted ghost towns that are struggling with shifting economic tides; even established areas are feeling the squeeze. Ilsan’s La Festa, once celebrated as South Korea’s first major outdoor street shopping mall when it opened in 2003, offers a glimpse into the aging process of the country's first-generation new towns. Once a bustling cultural heart where premium commercial spaces commanded heavy key money deposits, La Festa now grows quiet by 9 PM.

The decline of Ilsan's commercial districts highlights how quickly consumer habits have evolved. Newer retail hubs drew away foot traffic, online shopping decimated physical storefronts, and an aging local population altered spending patterns. Interestingly, many merchants have resorted to moving up from expensive ground-floor spaces to cheaper second-floor units just to keep their businesses alive.

Unlike Turtle Island, which was born empty, Ilsan’s commercial areas spent two decades serving as a genuine community hub where people built real memories. But the underlying lesson remains the same: cities are living organisms that require constant adaptation, not static assets guaranteed to appreciate forever.

What to Watch Next

The crumbling facades of Turtle Island and Midan City serve as a stark warning for South Korea's real estate and urban policy sectors. As the country grapples with one of the lowest birth rates in the world and a shrinking working-age population, the era of building speculative new towns on the assumption of endless expansion is drawing to a close.

  • Infrastructure Commitments: Watch for actual funding and ground-breaking dates on long-delayed transit links, such as the second capital region outer ring expressway segments near Siheung and Incheon. Without roads and rail, these areas cannot organically grow.
  • Zoning Overhauls: Monitor whether local governments will finally heed urban planners' warnings by re-zoning excess commercial plots into residential, cultural, or green spaces to lower vacancy pressures.
  • Legal and Financial Fallout: Keep an eye on ongoing lawsuits between retail buyers, developers, and local authorities, which could set legal precedents for how public-private partnership failures are handled in South Korea's secondary property markets.

Ultimately, these ghost towns demonstrate that a glossy architectural rendering cannot replace a genuine community. Until policymakers and developers shift their focus away from short-term land-sale profits and toward sustainable, human-centric urban design, South Korea’s concrete ruins will remain quiet reminders of the limits of developmentalism.
