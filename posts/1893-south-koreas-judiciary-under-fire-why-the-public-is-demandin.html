South Korea's Judiciary Under Fire: Why the Public is Demanding a Probe into the Chief Justice

November 03, 2026

The political fallout from South Korea's short-lived December 3rd martial law crisis has long since moved past the streets and into the country's courtrooms. But instead of providing a sense of closure, the judiciary has become the epicenter of a bitter new struggle over accountability. Recent months have seen former military commanders who marched on the National Assembly acquitted in their first trials, while allegations swirl that the nation's top judges are quietly shielding the architects of the aborted insurrection.

For an outside observer, it can be dizzying to watch a democracy that successfully ousted an authoritarian president via impeachment now tear itself apart over the integrity of its own legal system. Yet, at the heart of this confrontation is a profound public fear: that the institutions designed to uphold the constitution are instead being weaponized to quietly dismantle it from within. With a recent poll showing that roughly 60% of South Koreans favor a parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the clash between the bench and the public has reached a dangerous boiling point.

The Public Fracture: Why Citizens and Even Opposition Voters Want Answers

Recent polling paints a stark picture of public distrust in South Korea's judicial branch. When asked whether Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae should face a parliamentary probe, about 60% of respondents said yes, while only 30% opposed it. What makes these numbers remarkable is their breadth across political lines. While support is near 90% among voters backing the ruling Democratic Party—the party of President Lee Jae-myung—roughly 25% of supporters of the conservative main opposition, the People Power Party, also agreed that an investigation is necessary. Geographic divides tell a similar story: even in Daegu and Gyeongsangbuk-do, the conservative heartland, 50% of respondents backed an inquiry.

At the same time, public sentiment regarding an outright impeachment of the Chief Justice is more closely split, hovering near a 47.9% to 47.9% tie. While the ruling party's base remains deeply frustrated with the slow pace of justice regarding the December 3rd martial law attempt, there is also a palpable sense of fatigue and caution regarding the unprecedented step of impeaching the head of the judiciary. This split highlights a critical warning for lawmakers: without clear, tangible evidence brought to light through a rigorous parliamentary investigation, public momentum risks stalling.

The Martial Law Acquittals and the Judiciary's Credibility Crisis

Public anger was poured onto an already smoldering fire following a series of shocking first-instance court rulings that acquitted former military generals who commanded troops during the December 3rd crisis. The legal reasoning offered by the court strained credulity: judges accepted the defendants' claims that they were simply "too busy to watch television" at the time, meaning they allegedly did not hear why martial law was being declared and lacked the physical time to review the emergency decrees. Consequently, the court ruled, they could not be proven to have understood the illegality of their actions.

To the average South Korean, the logic is absurd. Senior military commanders watched troops scale the walls of the National Assembly and smash windows, yet the court accepted the defense that they were unaware of any wrongdoing. Legal critics argue that if a commanding officer can escape liability for a military assault on the legislature simply by claiming ignorance or following orders, future coups will essentially be granted a legal green light.

This ruling is widely viewed by legal analysts as part of a broader ideological shift within the court system since Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae took office. Critics inside and outside the legal profession argue that the judiciary has moved from an institution of impartial justice to a protective bulwark for the remnants of the fallen administration.

A "Low-Intensity Judicial Coup" and Battles on the Courtroom Intranet

If the December 3rd martial law declaration was an overt, high-intensity attempt to seize power by force, what critics term a "low-intensity judicial coup" is unfolding through legal maneuvering. Though imprisoned, former president Yoon석열 (Yoon Suk Yeol) left behind a judicial landscape stacked with sympathetic figures. Through delayed trials, soft sentences, and procedural roadblocks, the court system is increasingly seen as running interference for the forces behind the crisis.

Evidence of internal friction has even spilled onto "Courtnet," the internal online network used by South Korean judges. In a recent post, a senior judge at Jeongdok Library publicly criticized the Chief Justice's approach to filling Supreme Court vacancies, accusing the leadership of trying to transform the constitutional power to decline a nomination into an aggressive tool to force specific, ideologically aligned judges onto the bench. Such rare public whistleblowing from within the judiciary underscores fears that the nation's highest court is being deliberately shaped into a safe haven for authoritarian sympathizers.

The Historical Echoes of 1980 and What Lies Ahead

For South Korea, this moment carries heavy historical echoes. When the military dictatorship of Chun Doo-hwan was dismantled in the 1980s, the failure to fully and ruthlessly purge its systemic enablers allowed those networks to quietly survive for decades—ultimately resurfacing in the authoritarian impulses of Yoon석열's presidency. History suggests that incomplete accountability is merely a delayed fuse.

As South Korea approaches a crucial stretch of court proceedings and looming judicial retirements, the stakes could hardly be higher. The National Assembly's power to launch a parliamentary investigation remains one of the few democratic checks available to pierce the traditional veil of judicial independence when that independence is alleged to shield insurrection.

To watch next, observers should monitor whether the ruling Democratic Party can successfully coordinate with progressive judges and legal unions to launch a formal parliamentary inquiry into the judiciary's handling of martial law cases. If the legislature fails to act before key judicial timelines run out, South Korea risks watching the architects of its constitutional crisis legally scrubbed clean by the very courts sworn to protect it.
