The Price of Alliance: South Korea Caught Between Washington’s Demands and a Domestic Survival Crisis

September 10, 2026

Walking through the streets of Seoul today, the atmosphere is heavy. For many young Koreans, the daily struggle to make ends meet has become an exhausting, uphill battle. Yet, while the domestic situation feels precarious, the international stage offers little relief. In fact, South Korea is currently being squeezed by its most significant ally, the United States, in a way that feels less like a partnership and more like a shakedown.

When reports recently emerged that the U.S. has set a November deadline for South Korea to deploy troops to the Strait of Hormuz—a volatile maritime chokepoint in the Middle East—many were left in disbelief. It is a bitter irony: after years of Washington signaling a pivot away from Middle Eastern entanglements to reduce their own military footprint, they are now pressuring Seoul to fill that void with South Korean lives and equipment. To many on the ground here, it feels as though the U.S. is creating a mess and then demanding that its allies take on the burden of cleaning it up.

The $350 Billion Question: Economic Extortion or Diplomacy?

The military pressure is only half of the story. The trade relationship is currently under extreme strain. Last year, in a desperate bid to avoid a 25% "tariff bomb" from the U.S., South Korea agreed to a massive package of investments—including roughly $11 million (15 billion won) for mask manufacturing and $20 billion in strategic investments, totaling an eye-watering $350 billion commitment. Now, Washington is reportedly pushing to expand those commitments even further.

According to confidential reports shared with the National Assembly’s Trade, Industry, Energy, SMEs, and Startups Committee (the parliamentary body overseeing industrial policy), the U.S. is demanding investment scales that far exceed the initial $350 billion agreement. For many, this has crossed the line from standard diplomatic negotiation into something approaching legalized extortion.

The most egregious example is the pressure to invest in the Alaska LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas) project. This is a venture that industry giants—ExxonMobil, BP, and ConocoPhillips—have all walked away from. The math simply doesn't work; the cost of extraction is too high, and the project is fundamentally uncompetitive. Because the major energy firms refuse to touch the project due to its financial risk, the U.S. is essentially trying to force Seoul to foot the bill for their failed venture. It is no wonder that the "MG generation" (a catch-all term for South Korea's younger demographics) is expressing outrage, labeling these demands as nothing short of robbery.

Geopolitics at the Expense of Sovereignty

The South Korean government, tasked with defending the nation’s interests, appears to be struggling on a tilted playing field. The Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy has been under fire for its handling of these negotiations. While U.S. Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick has publicly spoken of "cautious" policies regarding semiconductor tariffs, the subtext is clear: Washington expects more tribute in the form of tax dollars.

The Alaskan gas project is a microcosm of this imbalance. Even though the economic data has warned against this project for years, the U.S. is using the leverage of the "alliance" to force Seoul and Tokyo to take on a project that the private sector knows is a dud. Meanwhile, when South Korea brings up its own strategic goals—such as the development of nuclear-powered submarines or the transfer of wartime operational control (a long-standing goal to regain full military command from the U.S. in the event of a conflict)—Washington consistently employs stall tactics. The message being sent is clear: Seoul is expected to pay up for American interests while its own security priorities remain secondary.

The Struggle of the 97-Line: A Mirror to the Nation’s Woes

While the state battles with international giants, the youth of South Korea are fighting their own war against structural inequality. A new film, "97 Hyeja, Drifting" (directed by Jung Ki-hyuk), has captured the national imagination by portraying the raw, unvarnished reality faced by those born in 1997. The film follows Hyeja, a young woman working in Gangnam—the affluent heart of Seoul—who cannot afford to actually live there.

The film serves as a stinging critique of a society where the promise of upward mobility has been hollowed out. Hyeja spends the movie trying to collect money owed to her, a journey that symbolizes the desperation of a generation that feels the social contract has been broken. The film is resonating because it isn't just fiction; it’s a reflection of the millions of young people trapped between stagnant wages and the skyrocketing cost of urban housing. In a city where your commute is dictated by your inability to pay rent, the "Drifting" of the title is a literal reality for many.

The film’s success lies in its refusal to sugarcoat the frustration of the youth. It captures the tension between the "old" and "young" generations, yet it also finds a moment of genuine solidarity between the protagonist and an older mentor character, Hee-sook. It is a powerful reminder that while politicians may try to use "generational conflict" to divide and conquer, the reality for the average citizen—young or old—is a shared struggle against a system that prioritizes corporate and foreign interests over the lives of its own people.

What to Watch Next

As the November deadline for the Hormuz deployment approaches, the stakes for the South Korean government could not be higher. Here is what to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • The Response to Troop Deployment: Watch for whether the Ministry of Defense bows to U.S. pressure or insists on a more neutral stance, given the lack of public support for a Middle Eastern engagement.
  • Investment Negotiations: Look for any official statements regarding the "Alaska LNG" project. If the government commits funds to this, expect a fierce backlash from both the opposition party and the public.
  • Public Sentiment and Cinema: The cultural conversation ignited by "97 Hyeja, Drifting" suggests that the public is becoming increasingly intolerant of domestic policies that prioritize global giants while ignoring the housing and employment crises at home.

At the end of the day, a true alliance is built on mutual respect, not economic exploitation and military coercion. If the South Korean government continues to treat these demands as inevitable, it risks losing the trust of the very generation that will define the country's future. It is time to ask: can a nation truly be a sovereign power if it allows its youth to drift while its wealth flows into the pockets of the powerful?
