The Midnight Repost: Why President Lee Jae-myung Is Waging an Online Civil War Against His Own Party

October 19, 2026

Late at night, when most heads of state have retired or are reviewing briefing folders for the morning, South Korea’s presidential account on X (formerly Twitter) lit up. President Lee Jae-myung had just posted a formal message praising his Minister of Trade, Industry and Energy, Kim Jung-kwan, for grueling negotiations over strategic investments. But minutes later, the timeline took a jarring turn: the president directly reposted an entry from an anonymous partisan account named "Jaemstice."

The post was neither official policy nor statesmanlike rhetoric. It featured a screenshot of a news article paired with crude vitriol targeting SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won, describing the head of one of South Korea's largest conglomerates as an "incompetent old man who begged to be Bank of Korea governor." For seasoned observers of South Korean politics, this was not an accidental brush of a thumb on a smartphone screen. It was a digital flare fired into the dark—a deliberate signal to an army of hardcore online loyalists that open season had been declared.

To understand why a sitting president with the full machinery of state communication at his disposal would resort to signal-boosting anonymous internet vitriol, one must look past the immediate outrage. This was not a social media gaffe. It is the visible surface of a high-stakes, ruthless internal struggle to purge the traditional establishment of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party and rebuild it from the ground up as a personal political vehicle.

The Mechanics of 'Target-Marking': From Statecraft to Digital Mobilization

In South Korean political parlance, what President Lee executed is known as jwa-pyo jjiki—literally "marking the coordinates." By publicly amplifying an aggressive, anonymous fringe account, the head of state signaled to his most zealous online followers exactly whom they should swarm. The targets were clear: SK Chairman Chey Tae-won, critical news outlets, and economic commentators like Professor Choi Bae-geun whose broadcasts had rattled the administration.

This tactic represents a profound rupture with the traditional conduct of the South Korean presidency. Historically, even when faced with fierce blowback, former progressive presidents maintained strict institutional decorum. Roh Moo-hyun engaged critics in spirited, direct debate; his successor Moon Jae-in absorbed relentless hostility with disciplined, often infuriating silence. Neither used the presidential bully pulpit to unleash digital mobs on private citizens or internal party dissenters.

The strategy carries severe institutional costs. According to findings by the Pew Research Center, 77 percent of South Koreans believe social media has deepened political polarization—the third highest figure among 19 advanced nations surveyed. Yet President Lee has leaned heavily into direct-to-masses digital mobilization, publishing well over 100 messages on X every month. As Chang Duck-jin, a sociology professor at Seoul National University, has observed, when a leader relies excessively on "going public" through algorithmic echo chambers, they risk trapping themselves in praise loops, ultimately becoming isolated decision-makers deaf to broader public sentiment.

With Lee's approval ratings sagging into the 30 percent range amid economic friction—sluggish job growth, persistent consumer inflation, and real estate headaches—ordinary governance logic would dictate building a broader governing coalition. Instead, the administration appears consumed by internal party consolidation.

The Clash of Two DNAs: Insurgent Realism vs. Democratic Party Tradition

To understand the depth of this factional civil war, foreign observers need to understand what the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) actually is. The party traces its emotional lineage through three towering figures: Kim Dae-jung, the Nobel Peace Prize laureate who endured death sentences under military dictatorships; Roh Moo-hyun, who shattered regionalist barriers with populist idealism; and Moon Jae-in, who took office on the back of the massive 2016–2017 candlelight protests that ousted the conservative establishment.

This history forged a distinct organizational identity, often called the "Democratic Party DNA," anchored by the so-called "pro-Roh" and "pro-Moon" factions. This wing of the party values procedural deliberation, historic liberal solidarity, and institutional dignity.

President Lee Jae-myung, by contrast, entered the mainstream party apparatus as an outsider. Critics within the party have long noted that early in his career in Seongnam, Lee reportedly explored seeking a nomination from the conservative Grand National Party (the historic ancestor of today's main opposition, the People Power Party). Throughout his rise from provincial mayor to provincial governor and ultimately to the Blue House, Lee operated as a hard-nosed, pragmatic machine politician whose loyalty was to electoral efficacy rather than historical party ideology.

Now in power, the friction between Lee's dominant loyalist wing—the "pro-Lee" (chin-myeong) faction—and the legacy pro-Moon and pro-Roh mainstream has turned existential. For Lee's inner circle, governing effectively requires total ideological and organizational discipline within the party. For the party's historic base, Lee's hostile takeover feels like an alien graft using their historic party solely as a vessel for personal power.

A Calculated Purge: Engineering the Departure of the Old Guard

Over the past eighteen months, this tension has manifested in an aggressive campaign against legacy progressive figures. Online agitators and pro-Lee YouTube influencers coined disparaging collective labels to diminish the moral authority of the party's past leadership—systematically tarring former President Moon Jae-in, Rebuilding Korea Party leader Cho Kuk, political commentator Kim Eo-jun, and former minister Rhyu Si-min.

Party rhetoric followed suit. Political strategist Lee Dong-young declared early on that the new generation of Lee loyalists carried "no debt of gratitude to Roh Moo-hyun, Moon Jae-in, or Cho Kuk." Official party forums, such as a symposium organized by lawmaker Lee Un-ju, became venues where the legacies of Roh and Moon were openly mocked, alarming veteran members who view those presidencies as the party's sacred foundation.

The operational climax of this campaign arrived during the party's leadership race. The pro-Lee leadership pushed through controversial procedural changes, specifically a preferential voting system criticized internally as a mechanism designed to produce preordained minority-rule outcomes. Through this mechanism, loyalist Kim Min-seok was installed as party leader, successfully sidelining internal competition from figures like lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae.

The behind-the-scenes calculation of Lee's strategists was cynical but straightforward: by shutting out the pro-Moon and pro-Roh elements and subjecting them to relentless online hostility, the party leadership hoped to provoke a mass exodus. If hundreds of thousands of traditional, fee-paying party members (gwon-li dangwon) tore up their membership cards in frustration, the party apparatus would be permanently cleared of resistance. The leadership could then reshape the party's base—even floating ideas of absorbing fractured fragments from the conservative People Power Party and moderate splinter groups following the political realignments that sent former conservative leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol to prison after his impeachment.

This culminated in jaw-dropping rhetoric from party operatives like Lee Dong-young, who publicly demanded that long-time loyalists leave: "It is time to replace the pro-Moon and pro-Cho voter base that has had its run. Leave, leave—let us change the core." A political party actively ordering its own core voters to leave was an anomaly without precedent in modern Korean democracy.

The Defection That Never Came: Why the Plan Broke Down

The strategy encountered an unexpected barrier: the rank-and-file refused to leave.

When the controversial leadership vote concluded, the widely predicted stampede toward the exits was halted by two figures. First, defeated candidate Jung Cheong-rae made an emotional appeal to his heartbroken supporters:

"If you leave the party, how lonely will I be?"

Second, and most decisively, veteran liberal intellectual Rhyu Si-min stepped in. Rather than blessing a breakaway faction, Rhyu praised the party's dedicated base as the true owners of the institution. He urged members not to vent their anger by defecting, but to launch a grassroots "3 Million Fee-Paying Member Movement" to defend the party from within. The planned mass defection dissolved overnight.

Internal leaks from mid-October revealed genuine dismay inside the presidential office and party leadership: "Why aren't they leaving?" Instead of inheriting an empty house they could redecorate at will, the pro-Lee faction found itself locked in the same building with an energized, resentful, and dug-in internal opposition.

The ensuing chill was palpable during an awkward, long-delayed Blue House luncheon between President Lee and former President Moon Jae-in. While Lee had previously hosted conservative commentators to project an image of bipartisan outreach, his meeting with his progressive predecessor was notably strained. Crucially, former First Lady Kim Jung-sook did not attend—a conspicuous absence widely read across Seoul as an intentional, silent rebuke against the ongoing campaign to erase Moon's legacy.

What to Watch Next: The Fragile Geometry of Lee's Power

President Lee's late-night reposting of an anonymous attack account is not an isolated temper tantrum; it is the nervous tick of an administration whose internal consolidation timetable has broken down. Having failed to induce a clean split, the leadership is trapped between declining general public support and a hostile internal base.

Moving forward, several critical dynamics will determine whether this internal feud paralyzes the ruling party:

  • The Battle for the Fee-Paying Membership: Watch the numbers in the DPK's dues-paying base. If the pro-Moon grassroots successfully recruit hundreds of thousands of new members under Rhyu Si-min's banner, the current leadership will face an existential threat in upcoming candidate nomination processes.
  • Legislative Friction: As economic measures and judicial appointments head to the National Assembly, observe whether sidelined non-Lee lawmakers begin withholding votes or breaking party discipline on key presidential priorities.
  • Digital Escalation vs. Public Fatigue: With presidential approval stuck in the 30s, continue monitoring whether the Blue House escalates its reliance on direct, militant online messaging, or if moderate cabinet officials push back against social media habits that alienate mainstream voters.

Power in South Korean politics is fleeting, and the patience of the electorate is unforgiving. A leader who treats long-standing allies as enemies to be evicted through digital bullying risks waking up to find that the house he sought to conquer has become an inescapable bunker.
