The Fragile Flower of Public Support: South Korea's Ruling Party Faces a Reckoning on Reform and Stagnant Approval Ratings

September 10, 2026

Step into the current political climate of Seoul, where the atmosphere inside the progressive camp feels less like a celebration of power and more like a waiting room for an inevitable storm. President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s main liberal party—finds his approval ratings pinned at 43.3%, trapped for eight consecutive weeks in a stubborn low-40s box. To an outside observer, a 43% approval rating might sound respectable enough. But in the volatile arena of South Korean politics, it represents a chilling disconnect between the presidential office and the fierce, highly engaged base that put him there. As political commentators and commentators dissect the numbers, a sharper, more uncomfortable truth is emerging: the machinery of government is beginning to drift away from the very people who built it.

The Illusion of Easy Reassurance and the Politics of Authenticity

Rumors recently circulated through political circles that the president is weighing a direct, personal appeal to soothe the bruised feelings of his supporters. Yet the belief that a well-crafted speech or a televised address can neatly stitch up these divisions is a fundamental misjudgment. The friction did not materialize overnight. It has accumulated through a series of internal party battles: controversies surrounding ranked-choice voting during the national convention, contentious regional visits by lawmakers, and backroom maneuvers on high-profile regional projects.

When news broke that the administration was searching for the right "direct expression" to address public frustration, critics were quick to point out that the messaging missed the mark entirely. What the base demands is not a masterclass in political theater, but a dose of raw, unvarnished accountability. Voters have grown exhausted by polished rhetoric. As political analysts have observed, even if a formal apology is off the table, what is desperately missing is an honest, unvarnished explanation of why certain compromises had to be made. The Democratic Party’s core supporters are not blind loyalists; they are strategic actors. If the administration takes actions that undermine core democratic values, these same voters are fully prepared to turn on the leadership, transforming overnight from passionate defenders into harsh critics. Dismissing them as unquestioning followers is a costly political arrogance.

Looking back at previous progressive administrations under figures like Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in, steep drops in support rarely arrived without warning. They are the cumulative product of asymmetric information and a growing chasm in communication. Today, the alienation felt by the party’s base is born not from a lack of personal favors, but from a creeping sense that internal democratic procedures—promises kept sacred during party rule-making—are being bent for expediency. Patching over these cracks without fixing the foundation only plants the fuse for a much larger explosion down the road.

The Prosecutor's Trap and the Perils of Institutional Speed

While the Blue House wrestles with its sliding support, a parallel crisis is quietly unfolding in the legislature, centered on the perennial battleground of judicial reform. The establishment of the new Public Prosecution Office—intended to strip prosecutors of their sweeping investigative powers and redistribute them—has turned into a bureaucratic minefield.

During a stormy session of the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, opposition broke out after key committee leaders revealed they had been left completely in the dark regarding the Ministry of Justice's newly drafted organizational plan. The acting justice minister offered little more than a vague apology. This was no mere administrative oversight. The Ministry’s decision to bypass the National Assembly by coordinating behind closed doors with other government bodies represents a calculated effort to blunt the core objectives of prosecution reform.

The timetable pushed by the bureaucracy reveals a tightly orchestrated trap: a deputy ministers' meeting, a cabinet vote, a series of prosecutor personnel shuffles, and an aggressive target launch date of October 2nd. This compressed schedule leaves a dangerously narrow window between personnel appointments and agency launch, virtually guaranteeing institutional chaos. Critics have likened the approach to trying to perform surgery with a kitchen spoon. Instead of a precise scalpel removing a tumor, administrative shortcuts threaten to butcher the entire reform agenda.

Compounding the danger is the looming transfer of some 140,000 backlog cases from the prosecutors' offices to the national police. Without adequate staffing or realistic transition periods, the setup threatens to overwhelm the police force, instantly handing critics a narrative of institutional incompetence. With thousands of prosecution personnel slated to transfer directly into the new structure essentially intact, critics warn that the reform risks becoming a bureaucratic shell game—changing the sign on the door while preserving entrenched institutional privilege.

Cultivating the Garden: Why Politics Needs Its Base More Than Ever

In South Korean political lore, the relationship between a politician and their supporters is often described through a vivid botanical metaphor: the base is the flower that produces the honey, while political brokers are the bees that fly away the moment the nectar runs dry. Power attracts opportunists by the thousands, but when the political winter sets in, those fair-weather operators vanish into the cold.

This is the central dilemma facing the current administration. Reform cannot be executed as a top-down technical exercise detached from the people who demanded it. The impatience of the party faithful—manifested in tearful appeals at press conferences begging leaders not to compromise on reform—is not a nuisance to be managed. It is the lifeblood of the movement. When politicians chase administrative convenience and institutional compromises to appease entrenched state agencies, they trade away the moral authority that brought them to power.

True political leadership requires the courage to pause, step back, and endure short-term friction to preserve long-term principles. If the Democratic Party rushes blindly toward arbitrary deadlines like the October launch of the new prosecution agency, it risks unraveling decades of hard-won reform history in a single legislative misstep.

What to Watch Next

As this political drama plays out over the coming weeks, several concrete markers will determine whether the administration can right the ship or whether its reform agenda will capsize:

  • The Legislative Response: Watch whether the Democratic Party establishes a dedicated task force to review and potentially halt the Ministry of Justice’s accelerated organizational roadmap for the new prosecution agency.
  • Personnel and Transition Deadlines: Keep a close eye on the scheduled late-September prosecutor appointments and whether lawmakers successfully demand an extended grace period for the transfer of the 140,000 backlog cases to the police.
  • Communication Shifts from the Blue House: Look past standard press releases to see if the administration adopts a more candid, direct tone regarding policy compromises, which will signal whether they truly grasp the depth of grassroots alienation.
  • Approval Rating Trajectory: Monitor whether the president’s polling numbers break out of the 40% box or continue to drift downward, which will serve as the ultimate barometer of public trust heading into the autumn legislative session.
