The $750 Divide: Why the iPhone Duo and Galaxy Z Fold 8 Cost So Differently in Korea

September 10, 2026

When Apple unveiled the "iPhone Duo"—its long-awaited first foray into the foldable smartphone market—at Apple Park in Cupertino on September 9, 2026, the global tech community was buzzing. But in South Korea, the reaction was less about the hinge technology and more about the sticker shock. When the local pricing was announced, social media feeds were immediately flooded with a singular question: "Why is there a $750 (1 million won) price gap between this and the Galaxy Z Fold 8?"

The numbers are staggering. The entry-level 256GB iPhone Duo starts at roughly $2,450 (3.29 million won). For those eyeing the top-tier 2TB model, the price tag balloons to over $3,800 (5.09 million won)—marking the first time a mass-market smartphone has crossed the $3,700 barrier in Korea. Meanwhile, Samsung Electronics, the hometown giant, priced its latest foldable, the Galaxy Z Fold 8 (256GB), at roughly $1,700 (2.27 million won).

For two flagship devices that retail for nearly identical prices in the United States, this massive disparity in Korea feels like a cold slap to local consumers. Is it just the exchange rate, or is there something more calculated—and perhaps more predatory—behind the pricing strategies of these two tech titans?

The Illusion of the $1,999 Price Tag

To understand why Korean consumers feel cheated, we have to look past the marketing. In the U.S., the iPhone Duo is priced at $1,999, while the Galaxy Z Fold 8 sits at $1,899. The actual price difference for a U.S. buyer is just $100 (about $75). So, why does that $75 gap explode into a $750 chasm once the devices cross the Pacific?

The answer lies in how these companies manipulate exchange rates and "risk premiums." In the U.S., sales tax varies by state and is added at checkout, whereas Korean prices are "final," including a 10% Value Added Tax (VAT). Even when backing out the VAT to compare the base prices, it becomes clear that Apple is using a "shadow exchange rate" of nearly 1,500 won to the dollar—significantly higher than the actual market rate of roughly 1,340 won.

Apple has a history of using conservative pricing buffers in Korea to protect against currency volatility or potential regulatory fines from the Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC). By hedging aggressively, they ensure that even if the won weakens, their margins remain untouched. While Apple also commands higher prices in parts of Europe, the Korean market often acts as a laboratory for these premium-tier markups, where brand loyalty is high enough that Apple feels little pressure to align its local pricing with global market conditions.

Samsung’s Defensive Strategy: Pricing for Survival

If Apple is pricing for profit protection, Samsung is pricing for survival. When you calculate the U.S. price of the Galaxy Z Fold 8 against the current exchange rate and add the 10% VAT, the theoretical price in Korea should be around $2,100 (2.8 million won). Yet, Samsung launched the device at roughly $1,700 (2.27 million won).

In essence, Samsung is applying a "virtual exchange rate" of roughly 1,090 won to the dollar. It is a rare, aggressive move for a company to slash over $390 (520,000 won) off the expected price of a flagship device. This is not charity; it is a defensive wall. With fierce competition from Chinese manufacturers like Huawei, whose folding devices are rapidly improving, and now with Apple entering the foldable space, Samsung cannot afford to lose its domestic stronghold.

Samsung’s strategy is a direct response to a market that is both highly sensitive to price and fiercely competitive. By keeping the Z Fold 8 under the $1,750 (2.3 million won) psychological barrier, they are effectively baiting Apple’s potential customers. They are betting that while some enthusiasts will pay a premium for the Apple ecosystem, the average consumer will see the $750 gap and conclude that the iPhone Duo simply isn't worth the extra cost.

The Verdict: Who is the Real Loser?

The $750 gap is a collision of two polar-opposite strategies: Apple’s "cushioned" high-margin pricing versus Samsung’s "market-share" aggressive pricing. For the consumer, it highlights a recurring frustration. Korean buyers, who live in one of the world’s most advanced digital environments, often feel they are treated as a captive audience for global companies' price-gouging experiments.

Whether the iPhone Duo’s features—the A20 Pro chip, a 7.6-inch Super Retina XDR display, and integrated Apple Pencil support—justify a $3,800 price point for the top model is a debate that will likely rage until the next upgrade cycle. But the data shows a clear trend: the cost of being an early adopter is becoming unsustainable. As the line between a smartphone and a portable workstation blurs, the prices are rising to levels that make even the most dedicated tech enthusiasts pause.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the KFTC. With consumer anger mounting over discriminatory pricing, regulatory bodies are under increasing pressure to investigate whether these "brand premiums" violate fair trade practices. Additionally, watch the sales figures for Q4 2026; if the iPhone Duo significantly outsells the Galaxy Z Fold 8 despite the massive price gap, it will prove that Apple’s pricing power is truly immune to market logic—and that the era of the $4,000 smartphone is officially here to stay.
