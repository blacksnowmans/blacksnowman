The President on the Front Line: Why Lee Jae-myung’s Direct Style is Splitting South Korean Opinion

November 10, 2026

Late on a weekday evening in Seoul, the blue light of a smartphone illuminates the face of the nation’s most powerful man. Instead of resting or delegating to a press secretary, President Lee Jae-myung is typing. He is drafting a message for his millions of followers on X (formerly Twitter) and Facebook, or perhaps preparing for a surprise YouTube live stream. To his supporters, this is the ultimate act of democratic transparency—a leader bypassing "distorted" mainstream media to speak directly to the people. To his critics, it is the sign of a government where the institutional system has collapsed, leaving a solitary leader to fight every battle alone.

In November 2026, this tension has reached a breaking point. South Korea finds itself in a bizarre political reality where the President’s approval rating depends entirely on who you ask. In one survey, he is a wildly popular reformer enjoying a 61% mandate; in another, he is struggling to maintain even 35% support. This statistical schism is not just a quirk of math; it is a symptom of a deeply polarized nation grappling with the legacy of the previous administration’s collapse and the unconventional style of the man now tasked with fixing it.

A Tale of Two Polls: The 25-Point Gap

To understand the current state of South Korean politics, one must look at the wild divergence in public opinion data from the first week of November 2026. Two major polling entities have released numbers that seem to describe two different countries. Poll Flower (Yeoron-josa-kkot), a polling organization often viewed as capturing the sentiment of the progressive base, reported that President Lee’s approval rating hit a robust 61.3% in its automated response (ARS) survey. Even its more conservative telephone interview method showed a comfortable 55.5%.

However, Next Meter, another prominent polling firm, painted a much grimmer picture. Their data showed the President’s approval at just 35.9%, with a staggering 60.1% of respondents disapproving of his performance. This 25-percentage-point gap between the two top-tier surveys is nearly unprecedented in South Korean political history. It suggests that while the President’s "hardcore" supporters are more energized than ever, the broader public—including moderates and those feeling the pinch of a sluggish economy—is growing increasingly cynical.

The partisan divide extends to the legislature as well. The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the President’s party, currently holds a slim lead or is neck-and-neck with the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition. Despite the total collapse of the previous administration under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment and removal from office—the conservative PPP has maintained a baseline support of around 37% to 39%. This indicates that the "shaken" conservative base has regrouped, feeding off the dissatisfaction of middle-of-the-road voters who feel the current government’s reform drive has not yet improved their daily lives.

The Digital Fortress: Risks of SNS Governance

At the heart of this divide is President Lee’s unique communication strategy. Since taking office, Lee has treated social media not as a marketing tool, but as a "lifeline." He frequently uses digital platforms to debunk "fake news" and explain complex policy decisions in real-time. This approach was born out of a deep-seated distrust of the traditional press corps, which Lee and his allies believe remains biased toward the old guard.

However, there is a growing consensus that this "one-man show" is becoming a liability. When the President acts as his own primary spokesperson, he becomes the lightning rod for every controversy. In a healthy democratic system, the Cabinet ministers and the Presidential Office staff—senior secretaries and advisors—are supposed to act as a shield. They should be the ones in the trenches, defending policies and taking the heat for unpopular decisions, allowing the President to remain a unifying, "big picture" figure.

Instead, the current administration often looks like a vacuum. When a crisis hits, the ministers and DPK leadership often wait for the President’s social media feed to set the tone before they speak. This creates a dangerous feedback loop where the President is overworked and the institutional "muscles" of the government begin to atrophy. If the President is the only one speaking, the administration becomes isolated within the echo chamber of its most vocal digital supporters, losing touch with the "silent majority" who do not spend their evenings on political YouTube channels.

The "Idol" Problem: Warning Against Blind Worship

Perhaps the most shocking moment of the month came from the President himself. In a candid social media post, Lee warned his followers that while political leaders need support, it should never cross the line into "religious-like praise or worship." He was essentially telling his most loyal fans—the ones who carried him to the Blue House—to calm down.

This message sent shockwaves through the progressive camp. For years, South Korean liberal politics has been driven by powerful "fandoms." We saw this with the late President Roh Moo-hyun’s "Nosamo" and the dedicated supporters of former President Moon Jae-in. These groups are the infantry of the movement, but they can also be exclusionary, attacking anyone within the party who dares to offer constructive criticism.

Lee’s warning was interpreted by many as a shot across the bow of specific influential figures, such as the prominent liberal writer and commentator Rhyu Si-min, whose followers are known for their fierce loyalty. Within the DPK, the message has sparked a messy internal debate. Was the President trying to bridge the gap with moderates? Or was he trying to distance himself from the more radical elements of his base that make him appear "scary" to undecided voters? The ambiguity of the message, while perhaps intended to be a philosophical reflection on democracy, has instead created a political headache, proving once again that the President’s direct words often create more friction than they resolve.

Beyond the Base: The Bread-and-Butter Mandate

Ultimately, the survival of the Lee Jae-myung administration will not be decided by a Facebook post or a YouTube live stream. It will be decided by the price of a basket of groceries and the availability of stable housing. For many 30-somethings in Korea, the political drama in Seoul feels worlds away from their struggle to survive in a high-inflation, low-growth economy.

The high approval ratings in the "Poll Flower" ARS surveys reflect the passion of those who want to see a total "cleaning" of the old system—the prosecution, the media, and the corporate cartels. But the low numbers in the "Next Meter" polls reflect the fatigue of the general public. To these voters, the constant political warfare feels like a distraction from the "Min-saeng" (people's livelihoods) that Lee promised to prioritize during his campaign.

The shadow of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s failed presidency still looms large. The current government was swept into power on a wave of anger and a demand for a return to normalcy. However, "not being the other guy" is not a long-term governing strategy. As the administration enters its third year, the novelty of a "communicator-in-chief" is wearing thin. The public is looking for the "efficacy" of reform—tangible proof that their lives are better because the DPK is in power.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the final weeks of 2026, there are three critical areas to monitor to see if President Lee can bridge the gap between his base and the broader public:

  • Cabinet Empowerment: Watch for a potential reshuffle or a change in tone from the Presidential Office. If we see ministers like the Minister of Economy and Finance taking the lead on major announcements instead of the President’s SNS, it will signal a shift toward a more sustainable, institutionalized governing style.
  • The "Rebuilding Korea Party" Factor: Keep an eye on the minor progressive parties, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party led by Cho Kuk. They currently hold about 7% of the vote. If they begin to peel away more of the DPK’s traditional base, the President may feel pressured to lean even further into his "direct communication" style to keep his supporters from defecting.
  • Legislative Breakthroughs: The DPK holds the majority, but it has struggled to pass landmark welfare and labor reforms. If the party can move past internal factionalism—exacerbated by the "worship" controversy—and deliver a major legislative win for the working class, we may see the Next Meter poll numbers begin to climb.
"A leader’s words should be like a heavy stone that anchors the ship of state, not a spark that sets the sails on fire."

President Lee Jae-myung is a man of undeniable conviction and a master of the digital age. But as the 2026 poll numbers show, a president who stands alone on the front line is a president at risk. For his government to succeed, he must learn to step back, let his system work, and turn the energy of his fandom into the steady machinery of a functioning republic.
