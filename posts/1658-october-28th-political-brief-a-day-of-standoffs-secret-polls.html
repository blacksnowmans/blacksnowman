October 28th Political Brief: A Day of Standoffs, Secret Polls, and Shifting Coalitions

October 29, 2026

If you spent October 28th watching the news cycle in Seoul, the prevailing sensation was that of being trapped in the eye of a storm. From the halls of the National Assembly to the offices of the Presidential Residence, political tensions reached a fever pitch, characterized by a series of cascading crises that left even seasoned observers struggling to predict the next turn of events. For those trying to make sense of the noise, the day was a stark reminder of how deeply polarized and volatile the current political landscape has become under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

The Judiciary Under Siege: The Battle Over Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

The most explosive issue of the day was the escalating standoff regarding a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, Cho Hee-dae. The Democratic Party (the ruling party, currently led by supporters of President Lee Jae-myung) has been pushing for a formal investigation, claiming the grounds for such a probe are more than sufficient.

Conversely, the People Power Party—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—has framed this move as an act of "political violence" that threatens the independence of the judiciary. Even Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has publicly condemned the inquiry as a crackdown on the rule of law. Within the ruling party itself, however, there is quiet anxiety that a botched investigation could backfire, creating a precarious atmosphere as the separation of powers is openly tested.

Adding to the chaos was a physical confrontation during a site inspection of election ballots. Amidst the intense friction, accusations of electoral fraud were shouted, leading to police intervention. While the opposition points to these outbursts as evidence of systemic issues, the ruling party insists that the opposition is merely using the incident to fuel distrust in the democratic process.

President Lee’s Digital Diplomacy and Policy Controversies

President Lee Jae-myung found himself at the center of a different firestorm after he engaged directly with users on "FM Korea," a prominent conservative online community. While some praised the President for attempting to engage with a demographic that typically leans against his administration, his comments regarding migrant workers quickly overshadowed the outreach.

The President suggested that the government would review the potential abolition of preferential policies for multicultural families. This sparked an immediate backlash from smaller progressive parties and the Rebuilding Korea Party, who characterized the remarks as "ignorant" and a "slur" against migrant laborers. The controversy highlights a recurring tension: while digital engagement is a bold attempt at transparency, the President's words carry immense weight, and critics argue that policies should not be shaped by, or appear to justify, discrimination against vulnerable populations.

In a separate move to consolidate support, President Lee hosted senior lawmakers from his party for a 130-minute dinner, emphasizing the need for the ruling party to align its rhetoric and actions with the "eyes of the people." The message was clear: despite some positive economic indicators, the administration is feeling the pressure to ensure that the benefits of any recovery are felt by the average citizen.

The Hidden Costs of Public Opinion

Perhaps the most damning news for the administration was the revelation regarding the President’s office’s use of public funds for polling. It was disclosed that the office spent approximately $2.6 million (3.6 billion KRW) on 181 separate opinion polls. The issue, however, is not the cost itself, but the secrecy: the administration has refused to disclose the subjects of these polls or the agencies commissioned to conduct them.

In a democracy, government transparency is the baseline for public trust. By shielding these polls from public view, the administration has fueled suspicions that it is either "cherry-picking" data to suit its agenda or attempting to manipulate public perception behind a curtain of administrative opacity. This "black-box" approach to governance is increasingly being used by the opposition as evidence that the administration lacks the accountability it promised.

Shifting Sands in the Opposition

While the ruling party navigates these controversies, the opposition People Power Party is experiencing its own internal power struggles. A proxy war for party leadership is taking shape, with Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon and former party leader Han Dong-hoon emerging as the primary poles of influence. Meanwhile, Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party has begun positioning himself as a "kingmaker," adding yet another layer of complexity to the conservative coalition’s future.

Amidst this, the opposition continues to push for a special counsel investigation into alleged "fabricated indictments" by the prosecution. This is setting the stage for another round of high-stakes confrontation in the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee. As the parties trade blows, the public is left with a sense of "political fatigue," as the legislative agenda appears increasingly dominated by partisan warfare rather than the pressing needs of the economy or social welfare.

What to Watch Next

  • The Outcome of the Special Counsel Push: Keep a close eye on the Legislation and Judiciary Committee to see if the opposition can successfully force a vote on the special counsel bill, which would likely freeze legislative proceedings entirely.
  • Transparency Demands: Public pressure on the Presidential Residence to disclose the details of the $2.6 million polling budget is likely to increase. If the administration continues to keep these documents private, expect the "secrecy" narrative to become a central theme in the next round of parliamentary questioning.
  • Opposition Realignment: Watch for signs of whether the conservative block led by Oh Se-hoon and others can unify, or if the internal jockeying for influence will further fracture the opposition’s ability to act as a cohesive counterweight to the Lee administration.

Ultimately, October 28th served as a microcosm of the current political era in Korea: a period where the machinery of government is constantly clashing with the complexities of public sentiment. Whether the administration can pivot from reactive crisis management to proactive governance remains the defining question of this term.
