Lee Jae-myung’s Populist Pivot: Why South Korea’s President is Courting the Far-Right Online

November 03, 2026

At approximately 4:00 PM on a recent afternoon, South Koreans scrolling through their social media feeds witnessed a jarring moment in the country’s digital political history. President Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the Democratic Party—South Korea’s main liberal party—took to X (formerly Twitter) to retweet a post from a forum known as Efemia Korea, or "Femco." To a casual observer, it might have looked like a simple interaction. To those familiar with the landscape of Korean internet subcultures, it was a political earthquake.

Femco is not just any forum; it is the digital headquarters for South Korea’s "angry young men," a demographic defined by a fierce commitment to a narrow definition of meritocracy, a rejection of feminism, and increasingly, a hostility toward multiculturalism. The post the President shared questioned why "multicultural families"—a term used in Korea to describe families with at least one foreign-born parent—were given priority access to public postpartum care centers over the families of "national merit recipients" (veterans and those who served the state). President Lee’s response was swift and supportive: "Thank you for the good point," he wrote, adding that his administration would consider abolishing such "preferential" treatments for immigrants.

For a president who belongs to the lineage of Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—leaders who championed human rights, inclusivity, and the protection of the marginalized—this move marks a radical and dangerous departure. It is a pivot toward a brand of "online populism" that seeks to consolidate power by feeding the grievances of a specific, vocal community at the expense of social cohesion and the rights of foreign residents who keep the Korean economy afloat.

The Postpartum Center Controversy and the Myth of "Preferential Treatment"

The spark for this latest firestorm is the "public postpartum care center," a government-subsidized facility designed to help mothers recover after childbirth. Given South Korea’s status as the country with the world’s lowest birth rate, these centers are highly sought after. Local ordinances often prioritize certain groups for entry, including low-income families, families with multiple children, and multicultural families.

By retweeting a post that framed this as an insult to national veterans, President Lee bypassed a complex policy discussion in favor of a populist "us vs. them" narrative. He suggested that multicultural benefits might be unnecessary or even harmful to native Koreans. "I will deeply review whether we need to continue favoring multicultural families or if it is time to abolish these preferential systems," the President stated, mentioning that he would coordinate with local governments to amend enforcement decrees.

The problem with this stance is that it ignores the legal and social reality of why these protections exist. The Multicultural Families Support Act was designed as a minimum safety net to help marriage immigrants and their children integrate into a society that remains deeply homogenous and often discriminatory. These are not "luxuries"; they are survival mechanisms. By validating the sentiment that immigrants are "stealing" spots from "deserving" Koreans, the President is effectively weaponizing the resentment of young men on Femco to justify dismantling minority protections.

Ignoring the Backbone of the Economy: The Migrant Labor Reality

Perhaps more damaging than the social media rhetoric is the economic justification President Lee has provided for his shift in tone. He recently argued that foreign workers do not significantly contribute to the Korean economy because they send their earnings back to their home countries rather than spending them locally. This argument is a staple of far-right anti-immigrant rhetoric globally, but it is demonstrably false in the Korean context.

Foreign workers currently make up about 3% of South Korea’s total workforce. While that may seem small, their concentration is critical. They are the backbone of the "3D" industries—Dirty, Difficult, and Dangerous—that young Koreans increasingly avoid. From the construction sites of Seoul to the shipyards of Ulsan and the seasonal farms in rural provinces, the Korean economy would grind to a halt without this labor.

  • Agriculture and Fisheries: In many rural districts, the harvest is entirely dependent on seasonal foreign laborers. Without them, food prices would skyrocket as domestic production fails.
  • Manufacturing: Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are facing a dire labor shortage. Foreign workers fill the gap in factories that sustain the supply chains of giants like Samsung and Hyundai.
  • Consumer Contribution: Contrary to the President's claim, migrant workers pay taxes and spend a significant portion of their income on local rent, food, and services. The value they produce for Korean industry far outweighs the remittances they send home.

The human cost of this labor was laid bare during the Aricell factory fire in Hwaseong earlier this year. Aricell, a lithium battery manufacturer, suffered a catastrophic blaze that killed 23 workers. Of those victims, 18 were foreign nationals. These individuals were working in high-risk environments, often with minimal safety training, to power Korea’s high-tech exports. To characterize such workers as a drain on the economy is not just a factual error; it is a moral failure that ignores the blood and sweat they contribute to their adopted country.

The "New Lee Jae-myung" and the Erasure of Liberal Values

Why would a liberal president take such a path? The answer lies in a calculated internal power struggle within the Democratic Party. For decades, the party has been defined by the "Big Three" legacies: the democracy-building of Kim Dae-jung, the anti-authoritarianism of Roh Moo-hyun, and the inclusive governance of Moon Jae-in.

However, a new faction—the so-called "New Lee Jae-myung" group—is attempting to purge these traditional influences. This group views the old liberal establishment as an obstacle to their specific brand of power. They are increasingly influenced by the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance, a hardline political faction that has historically been accused of using undemocratic methods to seize control of party structures. This is a "déjà vu" of the 2012 Unified Progressive Party (UPP) crisis, where a similar factional takeover led to the party's eventual dissolution.

In this new framework, traditional liberal intellectuals and loyal supporters of former President Moon are no longer "comrades"; they are targets. President Lee has gone as far as to label his own supporters "unaccountable extremists" when they criticize his slow progress on judicial reform or his pivot to the right. In a recent SNS post, he wrote: "Following the impatient and desperate desire for reform of some citizens... and attacking comrades who are trying to achieve real reform... is irresponsible extremism."

This is a striking irony. While the President uses the most sensitive and accommodating language to engage with anti-immigrant posters on Femco, he uses "extremist" and "agitator" to describe the very people who stood in the cold with candles to put him in power. By alienating the party’s core—the "pro-Moon" (Moonpa) and the "pro-Roh" factions—he is attempting to build a new base centered on populist appeal to the 2030 male demographic, even if it means adopting the rhetoric of the far-right.

The Crackdown on Dissent and the Silence of Intellectuals

This political shift is being accompanied by a chilling effect on free speech and intellectual critique. Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal writer and former minister who has been a staunch ally of the Democratic Party for decades, has recently become a target. When Rhyu offered "tough love" critiques of the administration’s direction, the response from the President's circle was not dialogue, but hostility.

The administration has even used state power to pressure critical media. There have been instances of search and seizures against journalists who reported on internal friction within the Blue House (the President's office). When the President's office stated they "did not wish for punishment" for Rhyu Si-min during a legal dispute, it was framed not as an act of grace, but as an act of arrogance—as if the President was "allowing" an intellectual to speak only so long as he stayed within bounds.

The result is a government that feels increasingly "out of control." President Lee’s late-night social media habits, where he personally engages in emotional arguments with citizens, suggest a leader who is isolated from professional counsel and reacting purely on instinct and populist data. As one critic noted, "The President can no longer control himself, and now he is lashing out at the media and his own supporters."

"If the President had listened to the critiques of people like Rhyu Si-min with an open heart, his approval ratings would not be in freefall. Instead, he treats every critic as an enemy to be purged."

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves into the next phase of this administration, the central question is whether the Democratic Party can survive this internal identity crisis. The "New Lee Jae-myung" era is currently defined by a rejection of the party’s historical roots in favor of a volatile online populism.

Keep an eye on these three developments:

  • Legislative Changes to Multicultural Acts: Watch for actual bills introduced by the Democratic Party to reduce benefits for foreign residents. This will be the first concrete test of whether the President’s SNS rhetoric becomes national policy.
  • The Internal Party Purge: As local elections approach, pay attention to the candidate selection process within the Democratic Party. If traditional "pro-Moon" or "pro-Roh" figures are systematically excluded in favor of "New Lee" loyalists, the fracture may become permanent.
  • Approval Ratings Among the 2030 Demographic: The President is betting that courting Femco will win him the young male vote. If this group remains cynical while his traditional base continues to desert him, the administration may find itself without any reliable coalition at all.

South Korea’s democracy has always been vibrant because of its "organized power of awakened citizens," a phrase coined by Roh Moo-hyun. By labeling those same citizens as extremists and courting the politics of hate, President Lee is playing a high-stakes game that could leave the country more divided than ever before.
