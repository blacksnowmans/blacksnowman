The Year That Built a Presidency: How an Unlikely Alliance Forged Lee Jae-myung’s Economic Doctrine

October 18, 2026

In South Korean political parlance, few labels carry as much quiet cynicism as polifessor—a blend of "politician" and "professor" used to describe academics who hover around campaign headquarters every election cycle, trading ideological white papers for parliamentary nominations or cabinet portfolios. By the summer of 2020, as the world slid into pandemic isolation and supply chains fractured under rising U.S.-China friction, South Korea’s political mainstream was saturated with them. Yet, inside the provincial government complex of Gyeonggi Province, an entirely different kind of political vetting was taking place in total silence.

Choi Bae-keun, an outspoken progressive economist who had helped establish the Platform Party (a satellite party allied with the Democratic Party of Korea for the 2020 general election), had decided to withdraw from direct politics. Disillusioned by the transactional opportunism of Seoul’s political district, Yeouido, Choi had retreated to public intellectual work, analyzing macro trends and warning of systemic domestic crises on his independent platform, Choi Bae-keun TV. Then came an unexpected outreach from Gyeonggi Province, then led by Governor Lee Jae-myung.

Rather than jumping at the access to power, Choi insisted on a rigorous, year-long testing period. He set out to examine whether the provincial governor was simply another smooth-talking politician reciting staff-written talking points, or an executive with the nerve to confront the country’s entrenched bureaucratic and financial cartels. The intellectual partnership forged during that twelve-month audit would ultimately lay the ideological foundation for the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

Beyond the 'Polifessor' Trap: A Year-Long Policy Audit

The relationship between intellectuals and political power in modern South Korea has often been fraught. From the democratic breakthroughs under Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun to the presidency of Moon Jae-in, progressive administrations have repeatedly found their reform agendas blunted by elite resistance—most notably from the fiscal bureaucracy and institutional finance. For an economist wary of political opportunism, backing any candidate carried substantial professional and reputational risk.

Choi’s starting point was simple: look past the rhetoric and audit the administrative data. Unlike many of his peers in Yeouido who came through elite bureaucratic or academic tracks, Lee Jae-myung possessed an unconventional trajectory. A former juvenile factory worker who educated himself to become a human rights lawyer, Lee cut his administrative teeth as mayor of Seongnam, a satellite city southeast of Seoul. There, he declared a local debt moratorium, aggressively restructured municipal obligations, and funded landmark welfare programs including youth dividends and free school uniforms.

As governor of Gyeonggi Province—South Korea’s most populous province with over 13 million residents—Lee scaled up this executive approach. Choi watched closely as Lee’s provincial office tackled complex, ground-level reforms:

  • Demolishing entrenched illegal operations: Forcefully clearing unauthorized commercial establishments that had monopolized public mountain streams and valleys for decades, replacing intimidation with strict legal enforcement.
  • Supply chain rebalancing: Direct provincial interventions to build out dedicated semiconductor industrial spaces and diversify supply chains away from over-reliance on China amid global geopolitical realignments.
  • Micro-economic circulation: Implementing local currency programs and a public food delivery application to break the pricing power of private platform monopolies and channel consumer spending directly into mom-and-pop storefronts.

The results were visible not just in administrative spreadsheets, but in public approval ratings that hovered consistently near 50 percent throughout volatile political cycles. To Choi, this demonstrated that Lee was not engaging in empty populism; he possessed an intuitive grasp of ground-level economics derived from his own working-class background, paired with the bureaucratic leverage needed to execute policy against organized resistance.

The Messengers of Trust and the Battle Over Pandemic Relief

Intellectual alliances rarely succeed on policy papers alone; they require personal trust and effective interlocutors. When Choi first engaged with Gyeonggi Province, he was met not with political swagger, but with genuine intellectual engagement led by Kim Nam-joon, then a key provincial secretary and now a member of the National Assembly representing the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Kim approached the academic with an open mandate, soliciting candid criticism rather than agreeable soundbites.

To translate macroeconomic frameworks into workable provincial regulations, Governor Lee brought in Dr. Kim Jae-yong, a former student council president at Hanyang University who had built decades of mutual trust with Choi. Dr. Kim served as an intellectual translator, ensuring that bold fiscal and monetary proposals were not diluted as they passed through the administrative machinery.

The definitive test of this alliance came during the fierce national debate over COVID-19 relief in the summer of 2020. The South Korean economy was seizing up, with neighborhood merchants facing catastrophic cash flow shortages. Inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, two diametrically opposed schools of thought collided:

Targeted Relief: Championed by former Prime Minister Lee Nak-yon and backed by the fiscal conservatism of the Ministry of Economy and Finance, this approach insisted on strict means-testing, distributing relief funds only to selected low-income brackets under the banner of "fiscal soundness."

Universal Economic Stimulus: Advocated by Governor Lee Jae-myung and backed by Choi Bae-keun, this approach argued that relief funds should be distributed universally in the form of expiring local currency to prime the domestic economic pump and stimulate immediate consumer velocity.

Governor Lee challenged the national consensus by implementing universal disaster basic income at the provincial level in Gyeonggi, publicly pressuring the central government to abandon narrow means-testing. He argued that excluding taxpayers who contribute the most erodes social solidarity and creates perverse administrative costs. Subsequent transaction data vindicated this strategy: consumer spending and small merchant revenues rapidly rebounded toward pre-pandemic levels after universal distributions, proving the multiplier effect of local currency.

For Choi, watching Lee stake his political survival against the conservative fiscal establishment proved that the governor possessed the rare political courage required to steer a nation through structural crisis.

Rewriting the Rules: The 'Basic Series' and Monetary Rethink

Beyond emergency crisis management, Choi and Lee shared a deeper, more radical diagnosis: South Korea’s traditional economic engine had reached the end of its structural lifespan. The post-war export-led development model—driven by corporate conglomerates, leveraged real estate speculation, and commercial banks profiting from collateralized mortgage lending—was generating unsustainable inequality and choking domestic consumption.

This diagnosis gave birth to Lee’s signature "Basic Series" platform:

  • Basic Income: Providing a universal baseline of economic support to sustain consumption in an era of automation and volatile labor markets.
  • Basic Finance: Ensuring low-income citizens access to low-interest, long-term credit lines backed by public institutions, rescuing vulnerable households from predatory private lenders.
  • Basic Housing: Expanding high-quality, long-term public rental housing to treat shelter as a public utility rather than an engine for speculative asset accumulation.

Underpinning this platform was a fundamental rethink of monetary and fiscal policy, including the institutional role of the Bank of Korea. Choi argued that a central bank fixated exclusively on narrow inflation targeting while commercial banks feast on property speculation abandons its broader duty to societal stability. State fiscal policy and central banking mechanisms, they argued, must actively coordinate to channel capital into productive social infrastructure and high-tech manufacturing.

Choi frequently drew parallels to Britain’s Industrial Revolution, noting that 19th-century British industrial preeminence was not merely the result of the steam engine, but of centuries of systemic institutional, educational, and legal modernizations that preceded it. In the eyes of Lee’s advisory circle, the Fourth Industrial Revolution, artificial intelligence, and the global green transition require a comparable civilizational overhaul—not superficial tax tinkering.

This structural vision stood in stark contrast to the period under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose term was marked by deep cuts to national research and development (R&D) budgets, uncoordinated tax reductions for high earners, and an adherence to austerity that severely eroded public livelihoods. That disastrous policy detour, which ended in Mr. Yoon’s impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment, underscored to progressive economists how quickly a nation’s economic fundamentals can decay when leadership lacks a coherent developmental philosophy.

What to Watch Next

Now that the ideas forged in Gyeonggi Province form the governing doctrine of the Lee Jae-myung administration, the stakes have shifted from policy debate to nationwide execution. For international investors, regional analysts, and domestic market participants, several concrete indicators will reveal how effectively this doctrine is being realized:

  • Capital Market and Corporate Governance Reforms: Watch for aggressive legislative efforts by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea to overhaul domestic capital markets, address corporate governance discounts, and regulate split-off listings that disadvantage retail shareholders.
  • Fiscal Coordination with the Bank of Korea: Monitor institutional shifts in monetary policy rhetoric, specifically whether the central bank moves toward closer strategic alignment with government industrial strategies and targeted liquidity support for vulnerable economic sectors.
  • Scaling the 'Basic Series' Infrastructure: Track the legislative roll-out of public credit facilities and long-term public rental models against resistance from the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, and private commercial banking lobbies.

The transformation of South Korea’s economic model from a debt-driven, real estate-reliant system into an innovation-focused social investment state is no longer an academic thought experiment. It is the official policy of the republic, tested by a skeptical economist four years ago, and now being stress-tested by the reality of executive power.
