Beyond the Rate Hysteria: Why Today's Interest Rates Are Fueling Growth, Not Killing It

September 01, 2026

Open any Korean financial news comment section or stock forum today, and you will find a consistent, panic-stricken refrain: “Interest rates are ticking up—it’s over.” For decades, investors have been shackled to the dogma that rising interest rates are the inevitable death knell for stock prices. When the yield on the 10-year US Treasury note climbs, the collective consensus is that the market is headed for a cliff. But as the financial landscape undergoes a radical shift, we have to ask ourselves: are we clinging to an outdated textbook while the real architects of the market are playing a completely different game?

The Death of the “Global Savings Glut”

For years, markets were governed by what former Federal Reserve Chair Ben Bernanke famously termed a “global savings glut.” In that era, the world was awash with excess capital, and with few places to deploy it, money flooded into US Treasuries. This kept interest rates artificially low for a long time, allowing companies to borrow cheaply and focus on stock buybacks and dividends rather than genuine innovation.

That era has definitively ended. Today, we are witnessing an explosion in demand for capital, driven by a massive technological revolution—most notably in Artificial Intelligence (AI). We have entered an era of “investment surging,” where companies must pour astronomical sums into infrastructure and R&D just to stay competitive. When there are high-return opportunities everywhere, it is illogical to expect interest rates to remain suppressed. Today’s interest rates are no longer just arbitrary numbers set by the Federal Reserve; they are a pulse check on how aggressively our economy is chasing growth.

ROE: The Power That Outpaces Rising Costs

The standard fear is that rising rates increase corporate interest expenses, thereby eating into earnings. That is a mathematically sound observation, but it ignores the quality of the companies driving today’s market. The current AI sector is demonstrating a Return on Equity (ROE)—a measure of how effectively a company uses shareholder capital—that is light-years ahead of the stagnant sectors of the past.

If a company can generate value far in excess of its borrowing costs, higher interest rates are not a catastrophe; they are a hurdle that filters the best from the rest. In a high-growth environment, it is arguably more economically sound for capital to gravitate toward companies that can justify their cost of debt through massive scale and innovation, rather than rewarding companies that only survive because borrowing costs are near zero.

The Myth of the Yen Carry Trade

When Japanese interest rates recently ticked above 3%, the market immediately reached for its favorite horror story: the “Yen Carry Trade” liquidation. The logic goes that investors who borrowed cheap yen to buy US assets will be forced to sell, triggering a massive market crash. It is a compelling, frightening narrative, but it fundamentally underestimates the US economy.

The US market is no longer fragile enough to be undone by shifting Japanese capital flows. Its core strength now relies on the intensity of its own internal growth engine. Even if Japanese institutional investors sell some Treasuries, the depth of the US market and the sheer momentum of its dominant tech giants provide a buffer that is often overlooked. Markets love to consume fear, and headlines about a "liquidation bomb" generate clicks, but they rarely reflect the reality that policymakers are already stress-testing for a 5% environment—and they are doing so because they believe the economy has the muscle to handle it.

The Opportunity in High-Cost Eras

For South Korean investors, who often look to the Federal Reserve’s decisions with a sense of passive anxiety, this is a moment to pivot. Historically, the Korean market has mirrored the Fed’s moves, often remaining trapped in defensive postures focused on housing market stability or inflation control. However, while the US is using higher rates as fuel for an AI-driven, high-growth era, many investors elsewhere are still flailing in the dark, waiting for a "return to normal" that may never come.

High-interest rate environments are, in fact, the ultimate filter. They systematically eliminate “zombie companies”—those that relied on cheap, easy debt to sustain inefficient operations. When the cost of capital is high, only the companies with proven, superior profitability (high ROE) can thrive. For the disciplined investor, this is not a time to retreat; it is a time to identify the companies that are not just surviving, but monopolizing their sectors despite the higher cost of doing business.

What to Watch Next

Stop obsessing over the headline interest rate and start looking at the margins. The most critical metric for the coming months is not whether the Fed cuts rates by 0.25%, but how companies maintain their gross margins—often 60% to 70% in the tech sector—in the face of rising expenses. If a company can improve its AI service margins by 1%, that is far more impactful to its intrinsic value than a minor fluctuation in the 10-year Treasury yield.

As we head into the next year, ignore the "doom and gloom" commentary that suggests interest rate cuts are the only path to a bull market. Often, rate cuts are a sign of a failing economy. Conversely, a market that holds firm while rates stay elevated is a sign of true structural strength. The battle moving forward will not be about the height of interest rates; it will be a fight to see which companies have the operational grit to dominate the high-cost landscape. Don't waste your time waiting for the interest rate storm to pass; learn how to build a portfolio that thrives in the rain.
