Beyond the Romantic Facade: What Portugal’s History Teaches Us About Survival and Modernity

September 07, 2026

Walking through the streets of Portugal, travelers are often struck by a strange sensation that time has somehow stopped. To some, it is pure romance; to others, it is the lingering, delayed landscape left behind by a long authoritarian regime. From the precise, grid-like squares of Lisbon rebuilt under the iron will of the Marquis of Pombal to Porto's weathered bookstores that inspired the magical halls of Harry Potter, the country wears its dual identity on its sleeve: the golden age of maritime exploration on one side, and a cool, shadowed modern history on the other.

The Day the Earth Moved: Lisbon’s 1755 Disaster and the Birth of Modern Reason

On November 1, 1755, as citizens gathered for All Saints' Day mass, a massive earthquake with a magnitude estimated at 8.5 or higher struck the city. Roughly 85 percent of Lisbon's buildings collapsed, and those who fled to the safety of the docks were swallowed by a subsequent tsunami. The disaster claimed tens of thousands of lives, delivering a fatal blow to the dominant Christian worldview of Enlightenment-era Europe. When thinkers asked why an omnipotent God would permit such carnage on a holy morning, it sparked a massive philosophical shift, forcing intellectuals to question divine providence and embrace human agency and reason.

The Marquis of Pombal, the statesman who took charge of the crisis, issued a blunt command: "Bury the dead and feed the living." He pushed through a reconstruction project of Lisbon that was so rigorous it bordered on authoritarian. He even had building models tested with marching soldiers to study earthquake resistance—laying the early groundwork for modern disaster management and urban planning. Today, visitors admire Lisbon’s orderly stone paving and grand public squares as romantic backdrops, but beneath that charm lies the cold, rationalist vision of a leader who forcibly dismantled religious fatalism to impose scientific logic.

Disasters often leave behind mourning, but looking closely at Lisbon's history reveals how radically catastrophes can reshape human society. The moment religious authority crumbled and science rushed in to fill the void, the great machinery of modernity began to turn. The lingering dissonance one feels walking Lisbon’s stone alleys may well stem from the raw survival and forced reforms embedded in its foundation.

The Surviving Echoes of the Alfama District

Perched safely on a rocky mass of land, Lisbon’s historic Alfama district miraculously escaped the worst of the 1755 earthquake. Once a poor neighborhood populated by dockworkers and sailors, it now invites travelers into a labyrinth of frozen-in-time alleys. Walking its narrow, winding paths, one can feel the stubborn resilience of low-rise homes that held their ground while high-rises tumbled down around them.

A visit to the Fado Museum in Alfama provides essential context for understanding the country's musical soul. Fado, a traditional working-class genre dating back to the 14th century, is heavy with *saudade*—a deep, melancholic longing akin to the Korean concept of *han*, born from women waiting for sailors who might never return from the sea. Whether dining at a modest local spot or a high-end restaurant, the defining feature of a live Fado performance is its raw immediacy. Without complex sound systems or electronic amplification, the performance relies entirely on a single 12-string Portuguese guitar and a singer's voice. In the hush of the room, as the singer's voice bends with sorrow, listeners realize why Lisbon is fundamentally a city shaped by yearning.

The Long Shadow of Dictatorship and the Academic Spirit of Coimbra

In the 15th century, Portugal commanded the globe with astonishing maritime might, a legacy still proudly displayed on monuments like the Discoveries Monument in Lisbon. Yet in the 20th century, the authoritarian ruler António de Salazar pursued a deliberate policy of underdevelopment, famously suppressing higher education and slowing industrialization under the pretext that universities only bred subversion. This policy delayed Portugal's modernization by more than four decades, leaving deep structural marks that still contribute to a slower pace of digital transformation today.

While tourists often view offline bookstores and unhurried streets as romantic, those traits are partly the hangover of a regime that sought to stunt the public's capacity for independent thought. At the same time, local residents pushed out to the urban fringes by foreign capital and the explosion of short-term rentals face the harsh bite of gentrification. Romance for the visitor can easily be a crisis for the local resident.

Between Lisbon and Porto lies Coimbra, the historic university city that once served as the capital of the Kingdom of Portugal. While Lisbon’s Fado leans toward female-led sorrow, Coimbra’s traditional music developed as male-voiced serenades. Walking past the ancient university buildings and the stunning Joanina Library offers a glimpse into the intellectual resistance that quietly endured even under Salazar’s authoritarian thumb. Coimbra provides a sobering intellectual pause for travelers caught between the buzz of Lisbon and the charm of Porto.

Porto’s Slow-Paced Art and Culinary Awakening

Steeper and slower-paced than Lisbon, Porto sits along the river where buskers play to crowds and the sweet aroma of aged wine drifts across the iron bridge from the Gaia district. Beyond high-end dining, Porto’s culinary soul shines in simple dishes executed with deep care. Take, for instance, octopus ceviche—octopus boiled tender and dressed simply with lime juice, olive oil, and fresh herbs. For visitors accustomed to heavy, spicy dipping sauces, this preparation elevates the natural texture and fragrant herbs of the seafood, proving how much potential lies in stripping a recipe down to its core ingredients.

Artistically, Porto delivers shocks of brilliance just as potent as its food. At the National Museum of Soares dos Reis, the intense gaze of Aurélia de Souza’s self-portrait penetrates the viewer with uncompromising energy. De Souza, who devoted her life entirely to her brushes and canvases while rejecting traditional marriage, stands as a testament to an artistic spirit that refused to bow to an era of censorship and repression. Later, stepping into the "Arab Room" at the Porto Stock Exchange Palace (*Palácio da Bolsa*), visitors encounter breathtaking Mudejar-style interior decoration that showcases the complex friction and eventual blending of Islamic and Christian aesthetics.

What We Seek When We Travel

Traveling through Portugal prompts a recurring question: Why do we chase romance so relentlessly?

Whether looking at the ruins of the 1755 earthquake, the suppressive quiet of the Salazar years, or the trade rivalries that birthed port wine, every picturesque surface rests upon a foundation of hard-won survival. Pombal’s grand squares and De Souza’s fierce self-portraits are ultimately human records written in the face of despair.

The timeless quality that travelers feel in Lisbon and Porto is not an accident; it is a quiet respect for the long darkness these cities have weathered. In an era dominated by hyper-fast digital lives, Portugal's slow pace feels less like falling behind and more like an intentional recovery of self. For those planning a visit, wandering blindly through the narrow alleys of Alfama reveals something far richer than mere ancient ruins: the stubborn, brilliant vitality of a people who carved a path through the dark and brought the light back with them.
