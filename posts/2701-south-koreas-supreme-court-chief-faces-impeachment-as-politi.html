South Korea’s Supreme Court Chief Faces Impeachment as Political Warfare Engulfs the Judiciary

November 24, 2026

On November 23, South Korea’s political landscape was dominated by a single flashpoint: an escalating showdown over the independence of the nation’s highest court. Across more than 16 major stories tracked on Google News that day, Korean broadcasters and newspapers reported a deepening standoff between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the conservative opposition over an aggressive push to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae.

According to reports from Busan Ilbo and MBC News, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea has set an urgent timetable to finalize an impeachment motion against Jo within the week and pass it in a National Assembly plenary session before the end of December. Framing the effort as a "historical calling," as reported by KBS News, DPK leaders argue that legislative intervention is necessary to restore judicial neutrality. In response, Jang Dong-hyuk, leader of the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), dismissed the drive as a "bulletproof impeachment" and a "jailbreak impeachment" designed to shield President Lee Jae-myung from suspended criminal proceedings, according to Money Today and Break News. By nightfall, the dispute had widened beyond the Chief Justice himself, with the opposition threatening legal action against an individual trial judge and urging judges nationwide to resist.

For outside observers, the clash can look like an arcane procedural fight. In reality, it exposes a fierce struggle over the separation of powers in South Korea—one that pits an assertive ruling party and sitting president against conservative opponents demanding the courts check executive power, leaving the judiciary trapped in the middle.

Why the Ruling Party Is Targeting the Chief Justice

To understand the stakes, it helps to look at South Korea’s current political alignment. President Lee Jae-myung leads the government from the Democratic Party of Korea, which commands the ruling majority in the National Assembly, while the People Power Party serves as the main conservative opposition alongside smaller groups such as the Reform Party. Under South Korea’s constitutional system, a parliamentary vote to impeach a senior official like the Supreme Court Chief Justice immediately suspends that official from duty while the Constitutional Court deliberates on whether to remove them permanently.

The DPK’s push to move within a seven-day drafting window and a 30-day legislative timeline reflects deep distrust of the Supreme Court’s current leadership. As KBS News and Korean political briefings noted, DPK leaders contend that since taking office, Chief Justice Jo’s rulings and administrative direction have strayed from democratic values and damaged judicial impartiality. Party officials have repeatedly raised suspicions of improper alignment with prosecutorial power and questioned the fairness of trials connected to past administrations.

From the ruling party’s perspective, using the legislature’s impeachment power is an essential check on a judiciary it believes has grown politically compromised. Yet as political analysts cited in Korean commentary point out, impeaching the head of the judicial branch carries enormous constitutional weight. Unless the DPK establishes clear, legally grounded grounds for removal rather than relying on broad political rhetoric, the party risks validating opposition accusations that it is undermining judicial independence. As MBC News reported, the debate has already descended into extreme rhetoric, with rival camps trading accusations of "subversion of the constitutional order" and "insurrection."

The Opposition’s Counterattack: Frozen Trials and ‘Jailbreak’ Claims

The conservative opposition has seized on the timing and target of the DPK’s move to mount a sharp counteroffensive. According to Money Today and Break News, PPP leader Jang Dong-hyuk accused the ruling party of holding the entire judiciary hostage to protect President Lee Jae-myung. Jang’s labels—"bulletproof impeachment" (a familiar Korean political term for using parliamentary power to block criminal accountability) and "jailbreak impeachment"—are meant to cast the DPK’s effort not as judicial reform, but as an attempt to intimidate judges overseeing cases tied to the president.

At the center of the opposition’s argument are three to four core criminal trials involving President Lee that are currently delayed or suspended. Reports from Sisa Journal and Gyeonggi Ilbo note that both the People Power Party and the Reform Party have demanded the immediate resumption of those proceedings alongside the withdrawal of the Jo impeachment motion. In a public message addressed to judges across the country, Jang declared that restarting President Lee’s trials is the only way to protect South Korea, framing the defense of Chief Justice Jo as synonymous with defending the rule of law.

Criticism of the impeachment plan has also come from outside the main conservative bloc. According to Freezine News, Reform Party lawmaker Lee Ki-in denounced the DPK’s push as a "farce to shift the political narrative" away from governing challenges and accused the ruling party of committing an insurrectionary act by turning a constitutional branch into a political bargaining chip. As Ulsan Maeil observed in its coverage, the relentless back-and-forth has transformed the courts themselves into South Korea’s primary political battleground.

Pressure on Individual Judges Tests Judicial Independence

What makes the current standoff especially volatile, according to Korean legal observers and political briefings, is that pressure on the courts is no longer confined to the Chief Justice. Both sides have escalated in ways that directly touch individual judges and the internal culture of the bench.

JTBC reported that the People Power Party dismissed the DPK’s impeachment push as an "empty impeachment" (literally a "tin-can impeachment") and countered by announcing plans to file a criminal complaint against Judge Lee Jin-kwan for "distorting the law"—a charge alleging that a judge intentionally misapplied legal statutes in a ruling. Meanwhile, Channel A reported that Jang Dong-hyuk publicly called on judges nationwide to "resist" the ruling party’s pressure, with rhetoric even touching on the possibility of collective judicial resignations.

Even within conservative ranks, the tactic of targeting individual judges drew pushback. According to Gyeonggi Ilbo, Daegu Mayor Hong Joon-pyo—a prominent conservative figure with a legal background—criticized his own party’s plan to pursue Judge Lee Jin-kwan, calling it a "bizarre legal tactic." Hong warned against turning legal mechanisms into instruments of political retaliation, arguing that weaponizing complaints and impeachments threatens to distort the entire justice system.

"The court should not be a place that cleans up the mess politics fails to solve, but a sanctuary that upholds our community’s standards—and that sanctuary is burning." — Commentary from the Korean political briefing

In the assessment of Korean commentators reviewing the November 23 coverage, neither side’s escalation leaves room for neutral adjudication. Threatening a Supreme Court Chief Justice with removal over contested rulings while urging sitting judges to mobilize politically—and threatening unfavorable judges with criminal complaints—risks eroding public confidence in the courts regardless of who prevails. At the same time, the all-consuming focus on impeachments and suspended trials has pushed pressing economic concerns, including inflation and a sluggish domestic economy during a bleak November downturn, off the National Assembly’s agenda.

What to Watch in the Weeks Ahead

With both parties entrenched in irreconcilable positions, the coming days and weeks will determine whether South Korea enters a full-scale constitutional crisis. Observers watching Seoul should track four concrete developments:

  • The seven-day drafting deadline: Whether the Democratic Party of Korea completes and formally introduces its impeachment motion against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae within the week, and what specific legal violations the text cites to justify removal.
  • The year-end floor vote: Whether the DPK pushes the motion through a National Assembly plenary session before the end of December. Passage would immediately suspend Jo from office while the Constitutional Court reviews the case, raising the risk of paralysis in judicial administration.
  • Escalation against individual judges: Whether the People Power Party follows through on its threatened criminal complaint against Judge Lee Jin-kwan, and whether rank-and-file judges respond to Jang Dong-hyuk’s call for resistance or maintain silence to guard their neutrality.
  • Parliamentary boycotts and street mobilization: Whether the PPP responds to a formal impeachment vote with National Assembly boycotts or large-scale street protests, turning an institutional dispute over the courts into a broader confrontation over the Lee Jae-myung government.

For now, the distance between the two camps appears unbridgeable. Whether the ruling party pulls back under opposition and public scrutiny or presses ahead into a Constitutional Court showdown will shape not only the fate of Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, but also the durability of South Korea’s judicial independence.
