The Shifting Sands of South Korean Media and Politics

September 23, 2026

If you turn on YouTube in South Korea these days, you are likely to encounter "Kim Ou-joon’s Humility is Hard: News Factory." It has become a digital juggernaut, and it recently caused a seismic stir. In the "2026 South Korea Credibility Survey"—conducted by the polling firm Korea Gallup and commissioned by the current affairs magazine SisaIN—this program eclipsed the long-dominant main evening news broadcasts of the major public networks to take the top spot as the most trusted news program in the country.

For those who have followed the local media landscape, this is a remarkable reversal of fortune. Just a few years ago, the common grievance among progressive commentators was the "tilted playing field," where legacy media outlets held all the power. Today, a new media platform—one without a single physical transmission tower—has toppled the icons of terrestrial broadcasting. The speed of this evolution has been breathtaking.

However, while the shifting tectonic plates of the media landscape are noteworthy, they are not the only things changing. The political climate remains precarious, filled with what Koreans call "pebbles in one's shoes"—small but irritating obstacles ranging from diplomatic security hurdles to infighting within the judiciary. It is time to peel back the layers of recent headlines and look at what is truly at stake.

The Rise of the "Towerless" Broadcaster

The SisaIN poll results shocked the South Korean media establishment. Kim Ou-joon’s Humility is Hard: News Factory recorded a 6.3% trust rating, surpassing MBC’s Newsdesk (5.9%) and KBS’s News 9 (4.9%).

This achievement was built without the massive capital or national infrastructure of the legacy networks; it was driven entirely by smartphones and YouTube algorithms. It validates the long-standing strategy of Kim Ou-joon, who founded the alternative media outlet Ddanzi Ilbo in 1998. His goal was never to be just another private YouTube channel, but to build a "terrestrial network without a transmission tower." Achieving this in less than four years proves he has successfully challenged the traditional gatekeepers.

Yet, the data contains a sobering reality. While the public is increasingly polarized—with people either fiercely trusting or deeply distrusting specific outlets like MBC or the conservative-leaning Chosun Ilbo—there is a massive segment of the population that is not listening at all. In the survey, 65.1% of respondents identified no specific YouTube channel as "trusted." This suggests that the majority of the public, particularly those in the political center, are weary of the aggressive, ideological framing found in current affairs YouTube. If a channel wants to be a true "voice of the people," it cannot simply preach to the converted; it must find a way to reach that vast, silent majority.

Diplomacy: Breaking Free from the "Avatar" Trap

Looking toward foreign policy, South Korea finds itself in a delicate spot ahead of the upcoming U.S.-China summit. While China has gained confidence in its long-term rivalry with the U.S., South Korea’s security establishment remains stuck in a defensive, reactive posture. The government is constantly scrambling to address U.S. agendas—be it demands for increased defense cost-sharing, military deployments, or participating in semiconductor blocks against China.

As noted by experts, diplomatic success is often determined by who sets the agenda. By constantly worrying about "not appearing anti-American," the current administration often fails to put its own cards on the table. Whether it is trade disputes or regional security, the history of the current administration’s diplomacy has been one of cautious silence rather than assertive negotiation. To reclaim sovereignty, the government needs a clear strategic vision that prioritizes the Korean Peninsula's interests over the role of a passive junior partner. Without a shift toward a proactive diplomatic strategy, the nation risks being treated as a mere pawn on the chessboard of greater powers.

Judicial Integrity Under Scrutiny

The South Korean judiciary—the institution tasked with upholding the rule of law—is facing a crisis of moral authority. Recent reports from MBC have revealed that Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and other high-ranking officials allegedly manipulated receipts for "official business expenses" to hide extravagant dining habits.

The details are damning: in an attempt to circumvent spending limits and anti-corruption laws (such as the Kim Young-ran Act, which limits the cost of meals for public officials), officials reportedly inflated the number of attendees at dinners held at high-end sushi bars and luxury hotels. In one instance, a dinner bill for an upscale restaurant was recorded as costing roughly $20 (28,000 KRW) per person, when the actual minimum price for a meal at that establishment is nearly $55 (75,000 KRW). When these discrepancies were exposed, the Court Administration dismissed them as "simple clerical errors." This lack of accountability—from the very body that demands strict adherence to the law—has fueled public anger and eroded trust in the judicial system.

The Road Ahead: Governance and Public Sentiment

The recent resignation of Kim Seung-won, a nominee for Justice Minister, following allegations involving past misconduct and an attempt to silence whistleblowers, serves as a reminder that the current administration's vetting process is far from foolproof. Protecting flawed candidates at the cost of the government’s overall moral standing is a gamble that rarely pays off.

On a more positive note for the ruling party, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings saw a modest rebound to 34.8% recently, ending a nine-week slide. His direct communication during a press conference helped stabilize his support base. However, the data also shows that 25.3% of the public now feel "they liked the President before, but dislike him now." This group represents the disillusioned center, and they are not easily won back.

Ultimately, the public is tired of political theater. Whether it is media wars, diplomatic posturing, or judicial scandals, what citizens consistently demand is the recovery of the "livelihood economy"—lower prices and more stable financial prospects. As the country moves forward, the administration’s ability to move beyond rhetoric and deliver tangible economic relief will be the true test of its longevity. The political landscape is volatile, and in this environment, leadership is defined not by how one handles victory, but by how one addresses the "pebbles" of discontent beneath the public's feet.
