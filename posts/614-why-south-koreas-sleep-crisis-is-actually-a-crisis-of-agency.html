Black Snowman

Why South Korea's Sleep Crisis Is Actually a Crisis of Agency and Connection

September 10, 2026

Picture the scene: It is past midnight in a bustling Seoul neighborhood. Neon signs still flash outside a high-rise apartment, where an office worker stares blankly at a glowing smartphone screen, feeling a heavy knot of anxiety in their chest. The usual narrative blames modern stress—demanding bosses, financial insecurity, or existential dread—for driving away sleep. People toss and turn, convinced that their worries are keeping them awake. But according to cognitive psychologists like Professor Kim Kyung-il, this familiar cause-and-effect is entirely backward. Stress does not cause insomnia. Rather, chronic sleep deprivation amplifies stress and deepens loneliness, leaving people to bumble through life like drivers forcing a car forward with a ruined engine, blaming the machine's vibrations instead of the lack of maintenance.

In South Korea, where rapid economic growth was built on the backs of generations who famously sacrificed sleep to build a modern nation from the ashes of war, rest has long been treated as a luxury or, worse, a sign of laziness. Yet as burnout reaches an inflection point, psychologists and cultural commentators are arguing that reclaiming sleep is no longer just a matter of personal wellness. It is a prerequisite for mental clarity, social health, and even democratic stability.

The Paradox of High Agency and the Cost of Exhaustion

South Korean society is famously dynamic, driven by a cultural trait that psychologists term "agency"—the intense desire to assert one's presence and exert influence over the surrounding world. Cultural psychologist Han Min has often pointed out that the uniquely Korean habit of demanding accountability in public spaces—such as challenging authority figures or shouting "Do you know who I am?" in hospitals or police stations—stems from this hyper-developed sense of individual agency. It is an active, assertive way of navigating life.

The problem arises when this agency becomes overactive. Constantly trying to shape the world and manage every moving part of one's environment consumes enormous energy. To fuel this relentless drive, people willingly sacrifice the first thing on the chopping block: sleep. For decades, surviving the fierce competition of Korean society meant working through the exhaustion. However, society has crossed a critical threshold. Pushing harder without basic rest is no longer a viable survival strategy; it is a fast track to collective burnout.

Sleep acts as a barometer of human control. Paradoxically, while high-agency individuals want to control their lives, a lack of sleep is the very thing that strips away self-control. Sleep-deprived individuals struggle to show basic patience toward others, and pro-social behaviors plummet. Studies even show that voter turnout drops significantly in sleep-deprived populations, suggesting that chronic exhaustion quietly erodes the foundation of civic life. When people lose self-control due to fatigue, public discourse turns sharp and reactive. Viewing rest as essential is not just about personal health; it is about maintaining the basic civility of society.

Moving Beyond 'Blacking Out' to Healthy Rest

For many overworked adults, sleep is not a peaceful transition but a violent surrender—what experts describe as simply "passing out" from sheer exhaustion. Boasting about hitting the pillow and instantly blacking out is often worn as a badge of honor, but sleep specialists warn that forcing the brain into a sudden shutdown is akin to pulling the emergency brake on a speeding vehicle. Over 350,000 years of human evolution, night fell gradually, and the human brain evolved to ease into darkness.

True sleep requires preparation. Dimming lights and stepping away from stimulating screens an hour before bed is a basic courtesy to the brain. Treating sleep as an afterthought rather than a vital 30 percent of human existence shapes how a person functions the next day. Those who drift off peacefully wake up ready to engage with the world constructively, while those who collapse into unconsciousness often wake up irritable and emotionally depleted.

Interestingly, good sleep is closely tied to a sense of daily growth. People who learn something new, tackle a challenge, and look forward to tomorrow find that sleep acts as a sweet reward. In contrast, monotonous routines and stagnant days leave the brain with little reason to rest deeply. South Korea’s notoriously long working hours leave little room for personal learning or anticipation, trapping workers in a cycle where empty days lead to restless nights.

The Digital Leash and the Power of Aimless Connection

One of the greatest disruptors of modern rest is the smartphone left glowing on the nightstand. Bringing a phone to bed invites a flood of micro-stressors—checking messages, scrolling through news, and reacting to market swings—effectively signaling to the brain that it is under threat and must stay alert. Creating physical distance by leaving the phone in another room overnight is one of the simplest and most effective remedies for insomnia.

Beyond digital clutter, researchers point to human connection as the most fundamental driver of sleep quality. During the COVID-19 pandemic, insomnia spiked not just because of isolation, but because human interactions became entirely transactional. When people only communicate when there is a specific task or "business" to discuss, they begin to view themselves merely as functional tools.

By contrast, casual, aimless check-ins—asking someone how they are doing with no underlying agenda—validate human existence. Studies show that people cannot sleep soundly if they feel fundamentally disconnected from those around them. Simple rituals, such as expressing genuine gratitude or offering a soft apology to a loved one before closing one's eyes, calm the nervous system far more effectively than any sleeping pill.

Resetting the Internal Clock

Fixing sleep hygiene requires looking beyond the bedroom and into the morning. Exposing oneself to natural sunlight within an hour or two of waking up is essential for resetting the body’s 24-hour circadian rhythm. For thousands of years, humans synchronized their lives with the sun. Ignoring this natural rhythm by stumbling through mornings in dark, indoor spaces leaves the brain confused when night finally falls.

Ultimately, managing sleep requires self-awareness. Just as personality tests like MBTI are widely discussed in South Korea, understanding one's unique sleep patterns—whether one is an early bird, a night owl, a long sleeper, or a short sleeper—is an important step toward respecting individual diversity. Tracking sleep patterns and building a sustainable routine can fundamentally change how a person experiences daily life.

"True rest is not a mechanical shutdown, but a ritual that reaffirms our connection to ourselves and others. When we learn to respect our boundaries, we reclaim the peace we need to face tomorrow."
