Inside South Korea’s Fried Chicken Crisis: Why a $22 Order Leaves Store Owners with Barely $1.50

November 06, 2026

Imagine standing in front of a deep fryer heated to 180°C for fifteen hours a day, breathing in heavy oil smoke in a cramped kitchen, turning out fifty orders of fried chicken every single night. At the end of the month, your sales register shows an impressive gross revenue of 50 million Korean won (around $37,000 USD). To an outsider, business looks booming. But after settling invoices for raw poultry, paying rent, and handing over massive cuts to delivery apps, you walk away with less than 3 million won (about $2,200 USD)—an income that barely meets minimum wage, working out to less than 2,000 won (roughly $1.50 USD) in actual net profit per bird sold.

In South Korea, fried chicken is far more than a fast-food choice. It is a cultural staple, an essential component of the iconic nightlife culture, and historically the most popular second-act career for retirees and self-employed entrepreneurs. Yet today, as menu prices inch toward the psychological threshold of 30,000 won (around $22 USD) per order, consumers are furious at what feels like price-gouging, while store owners are driven to tears claiming they have nothing left to live on. Where did all the money evaporate? The crisis crippling South Korea's neighborhood chicken shops is not simply a matter of agricultural inflation. It is a structural tragedy spawned by a double squeeze: monopolistic delivery platform fee structures on one side, and predatory supply pricing by corporate franchise headquarters on the other.

A recent recorded discussion between business media mentor Eun Hyun-jang and Pi Se-jun, president of the National Korean Chicken Franchisee Association (an independent collective bargaining group for restaurant owners), exposed the stark reality beneath the industry's polished exterior. Their detailed breakdown of store balance sheets reveals how sweat equity is being systematically siphoned off by corporate middlemen and tech giants.

Why Plummeting Raw Chicken Prices Never Reach the Menu

When news reports broadcast that wholesale market prices for raw chicken in South Korea dropped by 37.7 percent, hungry consumers naturally expect menu prices on their delivery apps to follow suit. Instead, prices remain stubbornly high. When customers scold local shopkeepers for greed, franchisees are caught in an unfair bind: despite wholesale market drops, the wholesale price at which corporate franchise headquarters supply processed chicken to local outlets fell by a mere 9.2 percent—less than a quarter of the market decline.

The Barrier of Long Supply Chains and Locked-in Contracts

To understand this disparity, one must look at how raw poultry moves from farm to fryer in Korea. A chicken travels from raising farms to slaughterhouses, through wholesale distributors, into corporate franchise headquarters, and through regional cold-chain logistics centers before finally arriving at a store's kitchen door. Every link in this chain adds processing, packaging, and transport costs. Corporate headquarters frequently justify sticky prices by claiming that raw chicken represents only a fraction of total overhead, asserting that a 30 percent drop in a 2,000-won base bird amounts to only a few hundred won once cutting, brining, and transport fees are added.

Yet as Pi Se-jun of the franchisee association points out, headquarters leverage their market power to create asymmetric contracts. When raw chicken prices spike, franchisors rush to raise supply fees to store owners almost instantly. But when raw poultry prices plummet, headquarters drag their feet, holding supply prices steady to pocket the windfall margin. Store owners, bound by strict franchise agreements, are legally forbidden from sourcing raw chicken from open wholesale markets. Their purchasing rights are completely locked down by corporate rule.

The Trap of Hidden Margins and Corporate Dominance

This dynamic highlights a notorious fixture of the Korean franchise model: the "difference franchise fee" (cha-aek gamaeng-geum). Rather than charging store owners a transparent, flat monthly royalty fee, many corporate headquarters embed their profit margins directly into mandatory raw materials—charging elevated prices for proprietary poultry, frying oil, batter powder, and even branded cardboard packaging. This creates an illusion that franchisees pay zero royalties, while in reality corporate headquarters extract heavy profits on every single ingredient delivered.

When franchise owners attempt to band together to demand fair pricing, corporate headquarters often fight back aggressively. A glaring case involved Goobne Chicken, one of South Korea's major oven-roasted chicken franchises. Allegations surfaced that Goobne corporate management attempted to undermine the existing independent franchisee association by forming a separate, company-aligned puppet organization to split franchisee solidarity. When corporate leaders view store owners not as long-term partners but as captive customers for their wholesale logistics division, market price reductions inevitably evaporate long before reaching the neighborhood kitchen.

The Brutal Receipts: Breaking Down a $17 Chicken

To see how store margins disappear, consider the financial breakdown of a single fried chicken order priced at 23,000 won (approximately $17 USD), a standard price point in urban South Korea today.

  • Raw Ingredients (51% / ~$8.60 USD): Processed chicken, batter powder, frying oil, dipping sauces, pickled radish, and packaging boxes consume about 11,700 won.
  • Delivery & Platform Costs (25% / ~$4.20 USD): Intermediate broker commissions from delivery apps, app listing fees, and outsourced motorcycle delivery agency fees eat up roughly 5,750 won.
  • Operating Overhead (24% / ~$4.20 USD): The remaining balance must cover store rent, electricity, gas, utility bills, employee wages, and local business taxes.

After paying out all mandatory overhead from that remaining 24 percent, a franchisee is frequently left with less than 2,000 won ($1.50 USD) per order. For delivery-only storefronts that do not benefit from high-margin alcohol sales typical of sit-down pubs, the business model turns downright toxic.

The Illusion of Cash Flow and the Tax Trap

What keeps many operators running despite microscopic profit margins is what experts call the cash flow illusion. Because delivery platforms deposit daily sales receipts directly into store bank accounts, store owners see a continuous stream of cash coming in. Many mistake this gross revenue for personal earnings, using it to pay off immediate supplier bills or living expenses.

The trap springs when quarterly Value-Added Tax (VAT) and annual comprehensive income tax bills arrive. Because gross revenue figures look substantial—often topping 50 million won ($37,000 USD) a month—tax assessments are high. But because operating margins are razor-thin, owners lack the cash reserves to pay their tax debts. Many end up taking out high-interest personal loans or maxing out credit cards just to settle taxes, setting off a downward spiral toward bank account seizures and bankruptcy. Business advisor Eun Hyun-jang notes that thousands of hardworking operators fail not because of poor food quality, but because opaque settlement systems mask their true insolvency until it is too late.

This dynamic is reflected in national statistics: within two to three years of opening, the closure rate for small food service franchises in South Korea reaches 14.74 percent. For delivery-focused outlets, transferring a failing store is nearly impossible because prospective buyers are well aware that the business model offers no real return on capital.

How Delivery Platforms Stripped Franchisees of Control

If corporate headquarters extract profit from the top of the supply chain, giant tech delivery platforms dictate the terms on the ground. Major delivery apps have evolved from simple ordering directories into omnipotent control towers that dictate store operations, customer interactions, and delivery logistics.

Undermining Takeout and Forcing App Reliance

Traditionally, store owners preserved their profit margins by encouraging local neighborhood customers to pick up orders in person, offering self-pickup discounts of around 4,000 won (~$3 USD) while saving on delivery fees. However, delivery apps routinely roll out massive platform-subsidized brand promotion events offering $5 to $6 off delivery orders. Customers naturally choose to order delivery from their couches rather than walking to the shop. But because store owners are pressured to co-fund these app-based discount campaigns while simultaneously paying delivery brokerage fees, their profit margins are completely wiped out.

Loss of Delivery Rights and Data Monopoly

The shift toward platform-operated delivery services has stripped store owners of basic operational control. Under these systems, the platform monopolizes motorcycle dispatching. Even if a store owner is standing idle in their shop and has an available scooter, app algorithms forbid them from delivering their own store's orders. In a surreal twist of modern platform labor, some chicken store owners resort to signing up as app riders to deliver food for neighboring restaurants during off-peak hours just to make extra margin, while paying third-party platform riders to deliver chicken from their own kitchen.

Furthermore, delivery apps maintain a total monopoly on customer data. Franchisees receive orders without customer phone numbers or addresses, preventing small business owners from building local customer lists or sending direct promotional messages. When delivery delays occur or food packages are damaged in transit, platform algorithms frequently grant instant customer cancellations, forcing the store owner to absorb 100 percent of the financial loss for both food and delivery costs without adequate recourse.

Political attention on this structural imbalance has intensified under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Government task forces and regulatory bodies have begun debating mandatory platform co-existence frameworks and fee caps to curb corporate dominance. These efforts mark a shift from the policy landscape under former president Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration faced criticism for leaving platform fee hikes and unfair cost-shifting largely unaddressed before he was impeached and imprisoned.

What to Watch Next in Korea’s Small Business Ecosystem

The crisis in South Korea’s fried chicken sector serves as a bellwether for the broader global gig economy and franchise food market. As neighborhood shops fight for survival beneath the weight of corporate inflation and platform fees, several key developments will determine whether small business owners can regain their footing:

  • Legislative Intervention on Platform Fees: Watch whether the Lee Jae-myung administration passes binding regulatory caps on delivery app commission rates and restricts platforms from shifting "free delivery" promotional costs onto local merchants.
  • Franchise Transparency Reforms: Monitor regulatory pushes by the Fair Trade Commission to mandate full disclosure of corporate "difference franchise fees" (cha-aek gamaeng-geum), forcing franchisors to separate true logistics costs from hidden markups.
  • Independent Franchisee Organizing: Pay attention to how franchisee associations, such as the National Korean Chicken Franchisee Association, organize legal challenges and collective bargaining actions against unfair corporate supply contracts and puppet vendor groups.
  • Direct Consumer Takeout Shifts: Observe whether store owners can successfully build independent direct-ordering networks or local cooperative apps to bypass dominant tech intermediaries and reconnect directly with neighborhood buyers.
