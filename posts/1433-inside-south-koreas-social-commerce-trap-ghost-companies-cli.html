Inside South Korea’s Social Commerce Trap: Ghost Companies, Click Farms, and the Dark Side of Influencer 'Group Buys'

October 24, 2026

If you spend any time scrolling through South Korean Instagram, your feed will inevitably serve you a familiar aesthetic: warm, muted tones, carefully styled coffee tables, and a friendly creator talking directly into the front-facing camera like an older sister or trusted confidant. In Korea, this parasocial intimacy is rarely just for fun; it is the engine of a multi-million-dollar retail phenomenon known as gonggu—short for gongdong gumaee, or group purchasing.

The pitch is always urgent and personal: "A holy grail item I personally tested for months," or "An all-time lowest price that will never return after this batch closes." Below the post, thousands of heart emojis and adoring comments flood the screen. For millions of young Korean shoppers, trusting an influencer has replaced trusting traditional brand advertising. But as an eye-opening investigation by Tracking 60 Minutes, the flagship investigative program on public broadcaster KBS, recently revealed, that trust has curdled into a hunting ground for organized deception, ghost shell companies, and digital manipulation rings.

The Retro iPhone Craze and the Disappearing Supplier

For college student Joo Ga-young, the trap began with a trend. Across Seoul's university campuses earlier this year, discontinued older iPhone models—specifically the iPhone XS and iPhone 6S—suddenly became must-have lifestyle accessories. Gen Z buyers were enchanted by the soft, CCD-camera-like color grading of the older lenses, creating a rush for vintage tech. Anxious that she was falling behind the trend, Joo stumbled across a curated Instagram storefront run by an influencer named Kim Min-ah.

Kim had already run five successful rounds of vintage iPhone group purchases alongside her regular stream of diet supplements and beauty products. She posted glossy photos, maintained detailed highlight reels answering technical questions, and interacted warmly with followers. Convinced by Kim's track record and approachable demeanor, Joo clicked the link in Kim’s profile and wire-transferred 129,000 won (about $95) directly from her bank account—the only payment method accepted.

The delivery deadline came and went. The package never arrived. When Joo returned to the influencer's page to investigate, she spotted an ominous comment from a previous customer: "I bought during Round 5. When is it shipping?" Kim was actively collecting cash for a sixth round of sales while earlier buyers were still waiting in the dark. Rather than halting sales or addressing the bottleneck, Kim continued posting glamorous new promotional images. When Joo sent a direct message demanding answers, she was swiftly blocked.

When KBS journalists tracked down Kim Min-ah, the influencer insisted she was merely another victim. Claiming she was duped by her supplier, Kim argued that her only role was pressing the vendor for updates, pointing out that previous batches had shipped without incident. Yet behind the scenes, Kim had pocketed massive tiered kickbacks. Wholesalers in this market offer creators escalating commission rates: starting at 10% for modest sales, the cut climbs steadily to 15% once revenue crosses 15 million won, and hits a staggering 18% for total sales over 40 million won (about $30,000). Influencers were handed pre-packaged sample photographs and marketing copy, doing little more than copy-pasting assets to collect tens of thousands of dollars without verifying inventory or supplier reliability.

The supplier itself was an illusion. Visiting the registered corporate address, reporters found not a logistics warehouse but a dusty, single-desk shared office space renting for a nominal deposit. The building manager confirmed that such virtual offices are routinely used by fly-by-night e-commerce operators who flee the moment complaints arise. Phone lines were already disconnected.

Investigators at Goyang Police Station, northwest of Seoul, uncovered an even more cynical pattern: the operation was a family-run shell game. The primary operator had previously been sanctioned by the Fair Trade Commission (FTC)—South Korea's antitrust and consumer protection watchdog—for non-delivery and refusal to issue refunds. To evade enforcement, the operator simply shut down the business, re-registered a brand-new entity using his mother's name, and resumed identical sales. Over 1,000 victims, mostly teenagers and college students scraping together monthly living allowances, were left stranded in crowded KakaoTalk group chats with virtually no immediate recourse.

The Click-Farm Economy: Buying 100,000 Views for Seven Dollars

How do obscure products and dubious suppliers capture national attention so quickly? The KBS investigation revealed that Korea's social media algorithms are being systematically hijacked by cheap, industrial-scale traffic manipulation.

In online commerce, metrics like view counts and "likes" are the ultimate currency. They signal social proof to shoppers and trigger platform recommendation engines to blast posts to wider audiences. In South Korea's digital marketing underbelly, this currency is sold on the open market. KBS reporters contacted an engagement vendor to test the system: placing an order for a newly uploaded video sitting at a meager 179 views. Within seconds of refreshing the browser, the counter jumped to 3,647. Under an hour later, the video had rocketed past 102,000 views. The cost to create this viral illusion was just 10,000 won—roughly $7.50.

These figures are pumped out around the clock by automated click farms using thousands of proxy IP addresses and bot accounts. Yet machine-generated traffic leaves telltale scars in the data. Social media data analyst Kim Tae-ju broke down the forensic profile of these boosted posts for the broadcast:

  • Absurd Replay Multiples: One viral promotional video logged 110,000 total views, but deep analytical insights showed only 12,234 unique viewers. A normal audience consumes a short clip roughly once or twice; here, automated bots were looping the content nearly ten times per script cycle.
  • Impossible Watch Times: The video was exactly 8 seconds long, yet the platform registered an average watch time of 16 seconds—a statistical glitch characteristic of macro scripts that fail to properly register playback completion.
  • Bizarre Geographic Routing: For an Instagram clip promoting a local beef soup restaurant in central Seoul, platform analytics revealed that 31.9% of the audience came from the United States, 27.4% from Brazil, and 12.3% from India. Ghost servers halfway across the world were artificially inflating the profile of a neighborhood eatery.

Squeezing the High Street: "Empty Boxes" and Small Business Fraud

While students lose pocket money to influencer scams, small business owners struggling through a prolonged domestic economic slump are being pushed to financial ruin by predatory digital marketing agencies.

Desperate for foot traffic, restaurant operators reported paying upwards of 3.74 million won (about $2,800) to marketing outfits promising guaranteed viral reach across dozens of managed accounts. In one case, a restaurant saw its promotional video hit 60,000 views and 500 saves, yet dining rooms remained entirely empty. Over the following two months, the owner's gross revenue actually dropped by 7 million won (around $5,200). When merchants requested the back-end analytical data to inspect viewer demographics, the agencies refused, claiming the data constituted a "trade secret."

Worse still is the wholesale corruption of Korea’s e-commerce review ecosystems. On major portals like Naver—South Korea's dominant search engine and online shopping hub—and specialized platforms like Coupang and fashion giant Musinsa, customer reviews require verified purchase and delivery confirmation. To bypass this safeguard, marketing rings operate sprawling "empty box" review rings.

"You can only review an item once the carrier registers delivery," a former participant explained. "So they send you a link to pay for an expensive cordless vacuum or a bed with your own credit card. A few days later, a tiny box arrives containing nothing but air. You post a glowing five-star review pretending you loved the product, and the vendor refunds your card and pays you a 1,000-won commission."

According to industry insiders, the fraud has grown so brazen that some agencies no longer even bother shipping empty cardboard. Instead, they bribe local logistics couriers to scan barcodes and mark tracking numbers as "delivered" in the carrier database without a parcel ever leaving the depot. Concurrently, automated macro scripts click targets up to 10,000 times a day to catapult merchants from page ten of search rankings into the coveted top three, forcing competitors into an endless bidding war just to stay visible.

When struggling entrepreneurs try to escape, agencies weaponize contract fine print. Oh Seong-geun, a veteran auto body technician with 20 years in business, was cold-called with an offer to secure top-tier keyword placement on Naver for "10,000 won a month," totaling 120,000 won a year. After reading his credit card number over the phone, he discovered a charge for 2.28 million won (about $1,700)—the agency had quietly locked him into an unauthorized five-year contract. When he immediately demanded a cancellation, the firm demanded a 750,000 won penalty fee, promised to return the balance, and promptly disconnected their numbers and vanished.

Accountability in the Age of Algorithm Arbitrage

The recurring tragedy in Korea's social commerce boom is the regulatory vacuum surrounding creator liability. Whenever a group-buy ring collapses, influencers invariably claim to be helpless intermediaries, putting all legal culpability on phantom suppliers while keeping their lucrative 10% to 18% commissions.

Under South Korea's Act on the Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce, however, legal experts argue this defense is wearing thin. Creators who solicit sales under their own identity, provide exclusive checkout links, outline purchasing conditions, and take substantial cuts of gross turnover are not passive billboards; they function as de facto mail-order distributors. South Korean legal experts increasingly stress that consumers caught in these group-buy collapses should not waste time engaging in emotional disputes in comment sections, where they face instant account blocks. Instead, gathering proof of payment and correspondence, identifying business registration numbers, and filing dual civil and criminal complaints that hold both the supplier and the influencer jointly accountable under e-commerce statutes is often the only realistic path to restitution.

Looking ahead, watch whether the Fair Trade Commission and the National Police Agency move to tighten statutory definitions for platform-based intermediaries. With automated click farms distorting everyday market signals and parasocial relationships being weaponized against young buyers and cash-strapped small business owners alike, the pressure on regulators to pierce the corporate veil of ghost suppliers—and strip predatory creators of their immunity—has never been higher.
