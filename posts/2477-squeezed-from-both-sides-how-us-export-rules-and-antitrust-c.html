Squeezed From Both Sides: How US Export Rules and Antitrust Courts Are Cornering South Korea’s Chip Giants

November 18, 2026

For executives in Pangyo and Seoul—the twin nerve centers of South Korea’s semiconductor industry—making the world’s fastest memory chips is no longer the hardest part of the job. That title now belongs to navigating Washington. Within days, South Korea’s two dominant chipmakers, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, found themselves staring down two very different threats from the United States: an aggressive expansion of export controls designed to seal off every digital backdoor to China, and a US court ruling that forces them into what could become a years-long antitrust trial.

To readers outside Asia, this can look like just another round of trade friction or routine corporate litigation. Inside South Korea, it feels existential. Semiconductors are the bedrock of the country’s export-driven economy, and Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together command an overwhelming share of the global memory market, including the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips that power artificial intelligence servers. Because their manufacturing footprint stretches across China and Southeast Asia while their core equipment, software, and top customers sit in the United States, any shift in Washington’s legal or geopolitical posture hits Seoul immediately—and there is rarely an easy exit.

Washington Moves From Border Checks to the Cloud

Until recently, US semiconductor export controls focused mostly on stopping physical crates of advanced chips from crossing into China. Now the US Department of Commerce has made clear that physical borders are not enough. Under its tightened framework, Washington is moving to block Chinese tech firms from accessing high-performance AI computing power remotely through overseas cloud providers and third-country data centers.

When direct chip shipments were choked off, Chinese companies turned to data centers in Southeast Asia and Europe to rent computing capacity and keep training large language models. By targeting remote access itself, US regulators are attempting to sever that workaround at the root. The Commerce Department has also issued a blunt warning to Nvidia, the dominant US designer of AI processors: supply chips to a third-country operator that leases more than half of its GPU capacity to Chinese firms, and Nvidia risks having its own supply lines cut off.

At the same time, Washington has ended flexible "fast-track" export procedures for five countries across Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Brazil—jurisdictions viewed by US officials as potential transit corridors into China—replacing them with strict, case-by-case licensing reviews. That shift lands directly on South Korea’s foundries and outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) companies, which handle chip packaging and finishing. Many Korean firms operate lines in Southeast Asia or route global orders through the region. Slower licensing means higher logistics bills, delayed deliveries, and mounting uncertainty over basic plant operations. In Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province, an industrial belt in southeastern South Korea packed with advanced component suppliers, industry insiders warn that a single regulatory change in Washington now threatens to stall core regional production lines.

Crucially, these rules are backed by criminal and administrative enforcement. A formal export-control probe by the US Commerce and Justice departments into a firm called Megaspeed has sent a chill across the wider supply chain. For giant conglomerates, compliance means armies of lawyers; for smaller Korean distributors and sobuijang—South Korea’s shorthand for specialized materials, parts, and equipment suppliers—having to scrutinize every shipment and end-user can be financially crippling.

What Washington frames as national security increasingly operates as extraterritorial policing, forcing allied firms to audit not only who buys their components, but how downstream customers run their business models.

A "Lockstep" Lawsuit Returns to Haunt Memory Makers

Just as Korean chipmakers were recalculating their global supply routes, a US court delivered a second blow. The court rejected a motion by Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, and US rival Micron to dismiss a class-action lawsuit accusing them of conspiring to inflate prices for DRAM—the standard working memory inside servers, PCs, and smartphones.

In allowing the case to proceed, the court pointed to allegations that the three manufacturers moved in "lockstep" when adjusting prices and output. Plaintiffs claim the trio deliberately turned down long-term contracts for commodity DRAM and distorted spot-market prices by curbing supply. With dismissal off the table, the case now enters the discovery phase, raising the prospect that sensitive internal emails, executive communications, and board meeting minutes will be dragged into open court.

For observers unfamiliar with the memory industry, the irony is sharp. Unlike contract chipmakers that build custom processors to order, memory manufacturers operate in a brutal commodity cycle. Factories cost tens of billions of dollars to build and cannot simply be switched off; when demand slumps, slashing production is the standard—and often only—way to stop bleeding cash. Here, the very output cuts used to survive a severe market downturn have come back as antitrust ammunition.

The financial stakes are daunting. Under US antitrust law, confirmed collusion carries heavy damages, with analysts warning that potential payouts could run into trillions of won—equivalent to billions of dollars. Even before any verdict, a multi-year legal fight drains executive focus, weighs on share prices—with brokerages already trimming targets for SK Hynix—and threatens to tie the hands of memory executives during the next inevitable downturn. If cutting supply in a glut becomes legally hazardous, the industry’s core mechanism for managing boom-and-bust cycles could break down.

Factory "Hostages" and the Korea Discount

While both Korean giants face the antitrust case, the geopolitical squeeze hits SK Hynix’s manufacturing map with particular force. A large share of SK Hynix’s memory output still comes from plants inside China, built with trillions of won (billions of dollars) in capital. Although Washington has granted conditional waivers allowing equipment to enter those Chinese facilities, those permissions resemble temporary, revocable passes rather than permanent guarantees. If the US moves to restrict where chips fabricated in China can be exported—even to third countries—plants once prized for their scale risk turning into stranded geopolitical hostages.

The anxiety extends to South Korea’s crown jewel: high-bandwidth memory, the stacked memory chips essential to AI accelerators. US regulators are closely watching whether HBM produced by Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix is leaking into China’s AI clusters via intermediaries. Both companies now expend enormous administrative effort on end-user verification. A single proven violation could trigger bans on using US chipmaking tools and software—a fatal blow to any modern fab. Worse still, Korean executives worry that aggressive US restrictions are accelerating Beijing’s drive for semiconductor self-reliance, threatening to erode Korea’s long-term market share without guaranteeing lasting security gains.

Financial markets have been quick to price in the political risk. Whenever Washington unveils fresh export curbs, foreign investors sell Asian semiconductor bellwethers like Samsung Electronics and Taiwan’s TSMC. In Seoul, this reinforces what local investors call the "Korea discount"—the chronic tendency of South Korean stocks to trade at lower valuations than global peers despite strong technology and earnings. When regulatory wind shifts can erase customers overnight, strong quarterly profits are no longer enough to reassure global funds.

Meanwhile, the broader AI ecosystem is already mutating around these constraints. OpenAI’s partnership with AMD shows how major tech buyers are working to dilute Nvidia’s monopoly and hedge supply-chain risk. Yet if geopolitical friction causes global tech giants to slow down data-center capital spending altogether, Asian and European markets alike face a broader demand cliff—a scenario that hits export-heavy South Korea harder than almost anywhere else.

What to Watch Next

South Korea’s semiconductor giants cannot out-engineer politics, and the next year will test whether Seoul and its corporate champions can adapt faster than Washington’s rules evolve. Outside observers should keep a close eye on three concrete developments:

  • Whether Seoul pushes back or pivots: Watch how the South Korean government and industry associations engage Washington over the five-country licensing tightening and cloud restrictions—and whether Korean firms accelerate shifting packaging and testing lines out of exposed Southeast Asian corridors.
  • Discovery and settlement signals in the DRAM case: As US lawyers begin combing through internal documents from Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, and Micron, look for early signs of whether the companies seek a costly settlement to contain reputational damage, and whether they hesitate to trim output if memory prices soften again.
  • Enforcement ripple effects and hyperscaler spending: Follow the trajectory of the US Megaspeed investigation and global Big Tech data-center budgets. If compliance burdens overwhelm smaller Korean component suppliers while cloud giants tap the brakes on AI infrastructure, South Korea’s broader export engine will feel the chill well beyond its two headline chipmakers.
