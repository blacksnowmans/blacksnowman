The Cannibalization of South Korea’s Right: Why a Conservative Icon Warns His Own Party Is Headed for Extinction

November 09, 2026

When the veteran polemicist Cho Gap-je speaks, South Korea’s conservative establishment usually listens. For decades, the septuagenarian commentator has been a bellwether of the traditional right—unforgiving toward progressives, deeply nationalist, and fiercely committed to preserving conservative dominance in Seoul. Yet in recent days, Cho delivered one of the most blistering assessments of his own political camp heard in years.

Aiming squarely at the leadership of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, Cho issued a blunt diagnosis: "The foolish far-right is merely food for the shrewd radical left." Referring directly to key party leaders like Jang Dong-hyeok and Jeong Jeom-sik, Cho warned that with such leadership figures, the conservatives will never beat the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) "even if they die and wake up." His grievance was not ideological apostasy, but rather a catastrophic drift toward delusion: a party establishment paralyzed by fringe conspiracy theories, convinced that past elections were rigged, and reliant on vitriolic internet agitators rather than viable policy.

For an opposition struggling to find its footing after being swept out of executive power, Cho’s broadside has exposed an uncomfortable reality. Rather than mounting a credible defense against the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the South Korean right is teetering on self-marginalization—trapped between ideological grievance and a growing global tide of reactionary populist politics.

Conspiracy Theories and the Opposition's Paralyzed Core

To understand the panic among traditional conservative thinkers, one must look at how the PPP has navigated life in opposition. Following the impeachment and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the conservative party was expected to rebuild itself around competence, fiscal stewardship, and constitutional rectitude. Instead, significant segments of its base and public-facing apparatus have doubled down on electoral denialism and allegations that criminal proceedings against former officials were entirely fabricated.

Cho argued that both claims—unsubstantiated claims of stolen elections and allegations of rigged prosecutions—constitute anti-constitutional agitation. His warning was unambiguous: unless the party leadership purges itself of these fringe elements and their rhetoric before the upcoming legislative contests, the conservative movement faces structural obsolescence.

"The foolish far-right is merely food for the shrewd radical left. A party chained to stolen-election myths cannot offer a constitutional alternative."

Yet rather than pivoting to parliamentary statecraft, the PPP has increasingly retreated to the streets and digital broadcast studios. Party spokespeople have launched sharp rhetorical offensives, weaponizing recent volatility in the benchmark KOSPI index and foreign exchange markets to accuse President Lee Jae-myung’s administration of total economic failure. While opposition scrutiny of monetary and economic policy is standard democratic practice, the PPP has coupled this with theatrical escalation. The party has denounced independent special counsel legislation as a "jailbreak bill" and publicly insulted special counsel candidates as political "lackeys" (ttolmani).

This combative posturing prompted DPK Lawmaker Han Byung-do to scoff at the opposition's lack of self-awareness, asking how a party that recently flirted with presidential impeachment rhetoric could lecture the nation on governance without first reckoning with its own past misrule. By preferring rhetorical fireworks to sober policy alternatives, the PPP risks alienating the moderate swing voters who ultimately decide South Korean elections.

Calculations on the Flanks: Realignments and Overseas Trips

While the PPP remains entangled in its own echo chamber, alternative factions within the broader conservative camp are maneuvering to capitalize on the vacuum. The Reform Party, a minor splinter outfit formed by center-right figures looking to break free from traditional partisan machinery, is aggressively repositioning itself.

Kim Chul-geun, the Reform Party's secretary general, recently abandoned plans to run in the capital city of Seoul, choosing instead to contest a seat in Yongin. The shift toward this key hub in southern Gyeonggi Province is highly calculated: the area is dense with young professionals, tech corridors, and semiconductor infrastructure. It represents precisely the demographic of educated, forward-looking moderates who feel abandoned by both the progressive establishment and the conspiratorial far-right.

At the same time, high-profile cross-party maneuvers hint at underlying tectonic shifts. PPP Lawmaker Kim Gi-hyeon and Reform Party Lawmaker Lee Jun-seok are scheduled to travel together to the United Kingdom on November 16. The joint overseas trip has set off intense speculation across Yeouido—Seoul’s political district—that key power brokers are quietly testing the waters for a broader opposition realignment or a grand conservative restructuring.

Any such realignment, however, faces a minefield of personal feuds and legal hostilities. Lee Jun-seok has simultaneously drawn a hard line against cultural provocateurs, confirming he will pursue legal action against star Korean history lecturer Jeon Han-gil following a string of inflammatory personal attacks. By refusing to settle out of court, Lee signaled that his brand of politics will not extend leniency to reactionary personal slander—a micro-clash that mirrors the broader civil war engulfing the right.

The Echo of Scandals Past: The Monetized Fringe

The PPP’s current vulnerability cannot be separated from the lingering wreckage of the previous administration. A recent investigative report by domestic broadcaster JTBC revealed that during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, the sister of a prominent hardline right-wing YouTuber obtained a post within the presidential office—and managed to maintain an illicit secondary private job for profit while drawing a state salary.

The scandal illustrates the transactional ecosystem that flourished during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in power, where far-right internet broadcasters who weaponized polarization were rewarded with institutional access and public influence. This marriage of digital outrage, private monetization, and state power left a toxic legacy. While progressive critics view these revelations as proof of systemic corruption, older conservative pragmatists like Cho view them as an existential embarrassment that destroyed the party's moral authority.

A Shared Pathology: How South Korea Reflects Global Radicalization

The domestic travails of South Korea's right are not occurring in an ideological vacuum. The same dynamics hollowing out the PPP—the monetization of rage, deep suspicion of democratic procedures, and the glorification of street confrontation—are rattling democracies across the globe.

  • Lethal violence in Europe: In Kraków, Poland, a 25-year-old military cadet steeped in extremist ideology opened fire in the historic Central Square, killing six people and wounding more than a dozen in what authorities classified as an act of far-right terrorism.
  • Institutional disruption in Germany: The Alternative for Germany (AfD) has advanced increasingly radical proposals, floating an international conference on Ukraine that excludes Ukraine entirely and agitating for the dismantlement of the European Union, prompting warnings from Jewish civil society groups over structural antisemitism.
  • Financial and populist scandals in the West: In the United Kingdom, Reform UK leader Nigel Farage has found himself embattled by investigations into £5 million (roughly $6.3 million) in undeclared campaign contributions, even as newly appointed Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch pulled ahead of him in public polling. In the United States, far-right agitators recently invaded a community Diwali celebration in Seattle demanding mass deportations, while conservative influencer Laura Loomer faced allegations of taking $5,000 (about 7 million won) per post to seed sponsored political propaganda.

In every instance, the mechanism is strikingly similar: fringe actors find ways to monetize grievance, pull mainstream center-right parties away from institutional norms, and subvert basic democratic legitimacy. Cho’s warning to South Korea’s conservatives is that once a mainstream party surrenders its intellectual core to this digital street-corner mob, regaining public trust becomes nearly impossible.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will determine whether South Korea’s main opposition can shake off its conspiratorial hangover or whether it will continue down a path of self-isolation. Observers should track three critical indicators:

  • The Battle for Candidate Screening: Watch whether PPP leadership heeds Cho’s warning and enacts strict vetting criteria to bar stolen-election conspiracy theorists and extremist livestreamers from official parliamentary nominations.
  • The UK Trip and the Centrist Splinter: The joint visit by Kim Gi-hyeon and Lee Jun-seok could serve as a precursor to informal pacts or a renewed push for conservative consolidation. Monitor whether the Reform Party maintains its distinct identity in southern Gyeonggi or gets subsumed back into the legacy establishment.
  • Parliamentary Scrutiny vs. Street Politics: As President Lee Jae-myung’s administration pushes ahead with its legislative and economic agenda, watch whether the PPP chooses to challenge government policy through rigorous committee work or continues relying on outside rallies and boycotts. A sustained commitment to extra-parliamentary theater will signal that the fringe still calls the shots.
