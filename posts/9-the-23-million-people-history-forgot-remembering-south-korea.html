The 2.3 Million People History Forgot: Remembering South Korea's 1946 October Uprising

September 24, 2026

When you learn the history of modern South Korea in school, the narrative usually starts with the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950, or perhaps a brief, chaotic blur right after liberation from Japanese colonial rule in 1945. It is a neat, sanctioned story that leaves little room for the desperate survival struggles, grassroots resistance, and crushed dreams of ordinary people caught in the sudden geopolitical fault lines of the early Cold War. Eighty years later, listening to elderly survivors speak through online channels about an event long scrubbed from official textbooks brings a heavy, quiet ache—not just for what happened then, but for how thoroughly the state managed to erase it.

Marking the 80th anniversary of the October Uprising—a massive, nationwide wave of popular resistance that began in the southeastern city of Daegu—foreign observers might be struck by how little space this foundational moment occupies in the public consciousness. Even today, the uprising receives virtually no municipal funding or official state backing. Instead, the upcoming commemorative events rely entirely on the voluntary donations and labor of ordinary citizens, a stark reminder of the fragile line between historical truth and state-sponsored amnesia.

The Forgotten Spark: A Nation in Chaos

The period immediately following the end of World War II in August 1945 was a chaotic vacuum on the Korean Peninsula. Before the lingering apparatus of Japanese colonial rule could fully dissolve, the U.S. military administration stepped in to govern the southern half, struggling to manage severe food shortages, economic collapse, and a total breakdown of civil order. According to Chae Young-hee, the inaugural head of the bereaved families' association for the October Uprising, the events of late 1946 were never a simple riot, as conservative authorities later claimed, but rather South Korea's very first major popular uprising.

The crowds that flooded the streets of Daegu were not career politicians or ideological zealots; they were starving citizens furious over soaring rice prices, acute hunger, and the shocking resurgence of former pro-Japanese collaborators into positions of authority under the U.S. military government. While standard history books often wrap this era in the sterile academic language of left-right ideological conflict, the reality on the ground was far more visceral: ordinary people taking to the streets simply because they could no longer feed their families.

Yet this foundational chapter of modern Korean dissent was swiftly met with brutal state violence. In the victors' official records, the uprising was rebranded as a violent communist rebellion, burying the voices of the masses beneath decades of silence.

A Mass Mobilization of 2.3 Million

The sheer scale of the October Uprising is difficult for modern readers to fathom. Park Seok-jun, executive director of the 80th Anniversary Organizing Committee, notes that starting from October 1, 1946, approximately 2.3 million people participated in the uprising nationwide. To put that number in perspective, the entire population of the Korean Peninsula at the time was under 20 million. That means more than 10 percent of the total population rose up in protest.

This was no isolated local disturbance in the Gyeongsang region; it was a full-scale national revolt. Despite the severe repression of the U.S. military administration and newly empowered police forces, millions of people risked their lives because their anger had crossed a critical threshold. Their demand was basic survival and self-determination, forming the true, fiery bedrock of what would later become Korea's modern democratization movement. Yet, compared to the April 19 Revolution of 1960 or the Gwangju Uprising of 1980, the October Uprising has remained relegated to the footnotes of history.

The Scarlet Letter: Living Under the 'Red' Stigma

The human cost of the uprising is etched into the lives of those who survived it, particularly the young. Most of those killed or imprisoned were in their twenties—university students and young workers. Even more heartbreaking were their mothers, many of them in their early twenties themselves, left to mourn husbands executed by the state and children ripped from their arms.

The cruelty of state violence did not end with the killings. Surviving family members were snared by South Korea’s notorious guilt-by-association system (*yeonjwaje*), which effectively locked them out of employment, higher education, and normal social life. For decades, bearing the scarlet letter of being a "communist's family" meant living in a silent, internal prison under constant state surveillance and social ostracization.

Mainstream historical research has long favored grand political narratives and heroic figures, routinely ignoring how state power systematically dismantled the daily lives of ordinary families. Facing this institutional erasure, victims were forced to hide their grief for a lifetime.

Reclaiming the Narrative Through Citizen Action

To break this eight-decade silence, the 80th Anniversary Organizing Committee has organized a series of commemorative events, anchored by a solemn memorial ceremony on October 1 and a cultural festival titled the "Ullim" Concert on October 3. In the local Gyeongsang dialect, the word *ullim* carries a double meaning: it evokes the sharp cry of grief (*ulda*) and the widening echo of a sound traveling across a landscape (*meari*). After eighty years of suppressed mourning, the organizers aim to finally let the families' cries ring out across the country.

Notably, all of this is happening without a single won of municipal or government support. Park Seok-jun notes that the organizing committee has raised about 40 million won (roughly $30,000) through grassroots crowdfunding—an amount that falls woefully short of what a national commemoration requires, but stands as a powerful testament to solidarity.

"Before I die, I want this history to be recorded truthfully so that nothing like this ever happens again." — Chae Young-hee, Head of the Bereaved Families' Association

These words, spoken by Chae Young-hee toward the end of her life, capture the heavy burden carried by the aging relatives of the victims. They are not asking for exorbitant financial compensation or empty honors; they simply want the historical record to accurately reflect why their fathers, husbands, and children died.

Eighty years after the streets of Daegu erupted in protest, the push to bring South Korea's earliest modern uprising out of the shadows rests entirely in the hands of citizens willing to remember.
