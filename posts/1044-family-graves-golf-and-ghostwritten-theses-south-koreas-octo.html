Family Graves, Golf, and Ghostwritten Theses: South Korea's October Parliamentary Audits Degenerate Into Mudslinging

October 13, 2026

Step onto the floor of South Korea's National Assembly during the October parliamentary audits, and you are immediately struck by a heavy sense of exhaustion. This annual ritual—designed to hold the executive branch accountable, review government spending, and investigate state affairs—has curdled into something far more personal and venomous. Instead of probing policy failures or economic data, lawmakers are trading insults over golf games, ghostwritten academic papers, and the resting places of dead relatives. For outside observers trying to make sense of South Korean politics under President Lee Jae-myung, the current spectacle offers a sobering look at how quickly high-stakes governance can give way to petty tribalism.

At the center of this storm is the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's primary progressive party. Facing slumping approval ratings that have recently dipped below those of the conservative main opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), the DPK leadership is struggling to keep its own ranks disciplined while pushing back against relentless conservative attacks. The atmosphere inside the parliament building is not merely tense; it is gridlocked by an escalating cycle of retaliation that leaves little room for actual legislation.

The Anatomy of a Gridlocked Audit Season

To understand why this autumn's parliamentary audit has devolved into such bitter theater, one must look at the immense political pressure bearing down on both sides of the aisle. The PPP, smarting from previous electoral defeats and the ongoing fallout from the conservative scandals of the past, has seized upon the audits as an opportunity to weaken President Lee Jae-myung's administration. Conservative lawmakers have hammered the president over past leisure activities, focusing heavily on a golf controversy, and have even targeted the private family gravesites of the president's parents.

For the DPK leadership, this crosses a sacred red line. Park Byung-do, the DPK floor leader, openly condemned the opposition's focus on family graves as "deranged politics" that has utterly stripped the audit process of its constitutional dignity. When personal grief and familial resting places become ammunition for political point-scoring, the baseline of civic decency in Seoul collapses.

Yet the ruling party is hardly acting as a monolith of restraint. The DPK itself has revived old attack lines, taking direct aim at former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon by dredging up allegations that his daughter's academic papers were ghostwritten. The PPP swiftly retaliated by accusing senior DPK figures of securing unfair legislative favors for their own children—the classic South Korean political weaponization of perceived "parental privilege." As both parties dig through each other's family histories, the public is left watching a political class that appears entirely disconnected from the economic anxieties of ordinary citizens.

Kim Min-seok's Battle to Reclaim the Narrative

Recognizing that this descent into mudslinging is actively alienating voters, DPK leader Kim Min-seok has tried to force his party back onto stable ground. In recent leadership meetings, Kim issued sharp warnings to the party's more radical factions, demanding an end to erratic, emotionally charged attacks against the administration and the opposition alike. He argued that shifting targets and sloppy accusations only dilute the DPK's credibility and fracture its internal unity.

Kim’s anxiety is well-founded. Recent polling shows the DPK's approval numbers slipping to generational lows, with noticeable erosion among core progressive demographics and voters in their forties—traditionally a bedrock of support for the center-left. By publicly reining in party hardliners, Kim is attempting to reassert a coherent governing strategy. He has tried to steer the conversation back toward national security and foreign policy, warning that over-politicizing diplomacy for domestic gain fundamentally damages South Korea's standing abroad.

However, enforcing discipline in a sprawling political party is difficult when the daily news cycle is dominated by viral provocation. While Kim urges his colleagues to focus on systemic issues like cybersecurity and tax collection from high-level delinquents, the gravitational pull of the audit room keeps dragging lawmakers back into the mud.

Beyond the Spectacle: The Fight for Public Trust

Away from the shouting matches in the audit committees, the DPK is desperately trying to prove it can still govern effectively. Recognizing that rural discontent is a major driver behind their declining support, ruling party lawmakers are pushing for a new "Special Rural Act" before the end of the year. The proposed legislation aims to overhaul how agricultural lands are regulated, explicitly excluding inherited farmland from blanket anti-speculation rules that have unfairly penalized generational farmers. By pairing these legislative efforts with a thorough, nationwide land inspection, the party hopes to signal that it takes rural livelihoods seriously.

At the same time, the administration has notched legislative wins in the healthcare sector, successfully passing a bill that establishes clear, legal standards for emergency rooms refusing incoming patients. For years, the South Korean medical system has struggled with institutional buck-passing during critical emergencies. Clearing this legislation through the plenary session represents a rare moment where policy substance outshines partisan noise.

Municipal-level initiatives, such as the DPK's push within the Seoul Metropolitan Council to lower local movie theater ticket prices, reflect a similar attempt to connect with the daily financial burdens of urban residents. These policy moves are the DPK's life raft: an acknowledgment that no amount of rhetorical sparring in parliament will win back voters who simply want to see their cost of living improve.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's parliamentary audit season stumbles toward its conclusion, the coming weeks will test whether the Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling party can permanently pivot away from self-defeating squabbles.

  • Approval Polls and Core Demographics: Watch for upcoming weekly polls to see if the DPK's recent slide among progressive voters and forty-somethings arrests, or if the constant cycle of family-related scandals continues to depress turnout enthusiasm.
  • Legislative Follow-Through on Rural Reform: Track whether the proposed Special Rural Act moves steadily through committee or gets bogged down by partisan horse-trading as the winter legislative session approaches.
  • Internal Party Discipline: Observe whether DPK leader Kim Min-seok successfully silences the party's erratic firebrands, or if internal dissent flares up again the next time the opposition launches a high-profile personal attack.

Ultimately, the events of this audit season highlight the fragile nature of political capital in Seoul. In a hyper-connected democracy where public mood can pivot overnight, parties that mistake theater for governance do so at their own peril.
