Judicial Reform or Political War? The Battle Over Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

October 26, 2026

For many supporters of the current administration, the recent acquittal of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon felt less like a legal verdict and more like a systemic failure. In the eyes of the ruling Democratic Party (DP) and its base, the judiciary—the supposed bastion of fairness—has become a fortress protecting the remnants of the old guard. On October 25, this frustration boiled over into a full-scale political confrontation as the Democratic Party officially moved to launch a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the head of South Korea’s Supreme Court.

The move is a high-stakes gamble. It marks the first time a ruling party has so aggressively targeted the head of the judicial branch with the explicit threat of impeachment. To the Democratic Party, it is a necessary "cleansing" of a biased system. To the opposition People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative party, it is "political stalking" and a blatant attempt to intimidate judges who might rule against the interests of President Lee Jae-myung’s government. This clash is not just about one trial or one judge; it is a battle over the soul of the Korean legal system and the boundaries of executive and legislative power.

The Catalyst: Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s Acquittal and the Crisis of Faith

The immediate trigger for this upheaval was the court's decision to find Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent conservative figure, not guilty of charges related to election law violations. For the Democratic Party, which prides itself on having moved past the era of "political prosecutors" that characterized the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment), the ruling was incomprehensible. It signaled that while the executive and legislative branches had changed hands, the judiciary remained insulated, operating under its own set of rules that many perceive as favoring the established elite.

The public anger following the Oh Se-hoon verdict gave the Democratic Party the political capital it needed to move against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Reformist lawmakers within the party, led by figures like Representative Kim Yong-min, argued that the judiciary’s "self-purification" mechanisms had failed. They pointed to the acquittal and the actions of former Minister of Justice Park Sung-jae as evidence that the courts were no longer reflecting the "common sense" of the people. Under Chief Justice Cho, the DP argues, the Supreme Court has become a bottleneck for justice, plagued by chronic trial delays and a perceived lack of impartiality.

However, the path to reform is fraught with legal hurdles. In South Korea, criticizing a specific verdict is common, but punishing a judge for their legal interpretation is notoriously difficult. The legal standard for "distortion of the law" is vague, making it hard to build a case for impeachment based solely on a ruling one dislikes. This is why the Democratic Party’s current strategy is focused on a "national investigation" (국정조사)—a parliamentary probe—rather than jumping straight to an impeachment motion. It is a process of building a "paper trail" of administrative failures and power abuses to justify more radical action later.

A Strategic Escalation: The Probe as a "Ladder Strategy"

The Democratic Party leadership, including Senior Supreme Councilor Kim Min-seok, has been careful to frame this move as a measured response. Inside the party, there was a significant debate: should they move for immediate impeachment, or should they take a slower, more investigative approach? The risk of immediate impeachment is high. If the Constitutional Court—which has the final say on removing a public official—rejects the motion, it would effectively grant Chief Justice Cho a "certificate of innocence," making him untouchable for the remainder of his term and dealing a massive blow to the DP’s reform credentials.

By choosing a parliamentary investigation first, the Democratic Party is employing what some analysts call a "ladder strategy." The probe aims to expose the internal workings of the Supreme Court, focusing on how Chief Justice Cho uses his administrative powers, how judges are assigned to sensitive cases, and why certain high-profile trials have been delayed for years. The goal is to bring these issues into the public square, turning technical judicial matters into a broad national conversation about accountability. If the probe uncovers specific instances of power abuse or administrative negligence, the "ladder" then leads naturally to impeachment with the weight of public evidence behind it.

"We will move step by step, 'ttabak-ttabak' (resolutely and steadily), to resolve the issues of the Chief Justice’s excessive authority and the stagnation of the courts," party leadership recently stated. This approach seeks to minimize the "backlash" that often accompanies aggressive moves against independent branches of government.

Friction Within the Liberal Camp: The "Golden Time" Argument

While the Democratic Party is moving cautiously, it is facing pressure from its own left flank. Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party and a former Minister of Justice, has been a vocal critic of the DP’s "deliberative" pace. Cho argues that the Democratic Party has already missed the "golden time" for judicial reform by wasting months on internal power struggles and political calculations.

From Cho Kuk's perspective, the public anger following the Oh Se-hoon verdict was a unique window of opportunity that required an immediate, decisive strike. By opting for a time-consuming parliamentary investigation, he argues, the DP is allowing the judiciary time to regroup and giving the opposition room to frame the issue as a "political vendetta." Cho’s critique highlights a growing tension within the liberal coalition: the "establishment" Democrats who want to preserve institutional stability while reforming it, versus the "insurgent" reformers who believe the system is too broken to be fixed through traditional parliamentary procedures.

This internal pressure is a double-edged sword for President Lee Jae-myung’s party. It forces them to act more aggressively to keep their base from drifting toward Cho Kuk’s more radical platform, but it also increases the risk of overstepping and alienating moderate voters who are wary of any action that looks like a direct assault on the independence of the courts.

The Opposition’s Defense: Accusations of "Political Stalking"

The People Power Party (PPP) has wasted no time in characterizing the probe as a "malicious political trick." Jung Jeom-sig, the PPP’s floor leader, has been the primary voice of this counterattack. He argues that the Democratic Party is simply "testing the waters" because they are too afraid of the public backlash an actual impeachment would cause.

The PPP’s strategy is to frame the investigation as a form of "shielding" for President Lee Jae-myung. They argue that the DP is attacking the Chief Justice to intimidate the judges currently presiding over various trials related to the President and his associates. By calling it "political stalking" and "retributory politics," the opposition seeks to shift the narrative from "judicial reform" to "obstructing justice."

  • The "Shield" Narrative: The PPP claims the DP is trying to create a "compliant judiciary" that will protect the current administration from legal scrutiny.
  • The "Inconsistency" Argument: Conservatives point out that the DP often praises the judiciary when rulings go their way, only to label the system "corrupt" when they lose.
  • The "Hostage" Situation: The opposition argues that by targeting the Chief Justice, the DP is effectively holding the entire legal system hostage to its political agenda.

This rhetorical battle is playing out against the backdrop of a paralyzed Supreme Court. There has been a vacancy among the Supreme Court Justices for over seven months. As the two sides fight over the Chief Justice’s fate, the routine business of appointing new justices and holding confirmation hearings has ground to a halt. This vacancy crisis is exacerbating the very "trial delays" that the Democratic Party claims it wants to fix, creating a vicious cycle of dysfunction.

The Broader Agenda: Special Prosecutors and Systematic Reform

The investigation into Chief Justice Cho is only one half of the Democratic Party’s "pincer movement." The other half is the "Special Prosecution Act for Fabricated Indictments." This bill seeks to appoint an independent counsel to investigate allegations that, during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the prosecution and parts of the judiciary conspired to target political opponents through "fabricated" evidence and "targeted" investigations.

By running the Cho Hee-dae probe and the Special Prosecution bill simultaneously, the DP is attempting a total overhaul of the "legal-industrial complex" that they believe has ruled Korea for decades. The goal is to strip the Chief Justice of his concentrated power over personnel—specifically his ability to appoint judges and oversee court administration—and to create a permanent mechanism for holding prosecutors and judges accountable for their actions on the bench.

The stakes are incredibly high. For the Democratic Party, success means finally subordinating the "untouchable" legal elite to democratic oversight. For the opposition, it represents the beginning of the end for the separation of powers in South Korea. As the calendar moves past October 25, the focus will shift to the specifics of the parliamentary investigation.

What to Watch Next

  • The Scope of the Probe: Will the Democratic Party focus on administrative issues (like trial delays) or will they attempt to subpoena judges to testify about specific verdicts? The latter would be a massive escalation.
  • The Supreme Court Vacancy: Watch for whether the DP uses the vacancy as a bargaining chip to force concessions from Chief Justice Cho, or if the PPP uses it to rally public sentiment against "ruling party obstruction."
  • The Constitutional Court: Any move toward impeachment will eventually land here. The current ideological makeup of the Constitutional Court will determine whether the DP’s "ladder" leads to a successful removal or a political dead end.
  • Cho Kuk's Influence: If the parliamentary investigation moves too slowly, Cho Kuk’s party may gain momentum among frustrated liberal voters, potentially forcing the DP into a more radical stance than it initially intended.

In the coming weeks, the halls of the National Assembly will be the site of a profound struggle. Whether this leads to a more transparent judiciary or a deeper politicization of the courts remains to be seen, but one thing is certain: the era of the judiciary being a "quiet" branch of government in South Korea is over.
