Inside the $1.35 Billion Collapse of TMON and WeMakePrice: How Corporate Ambition Destroyed South Korea’s E-Commerce Ecosystem

October 26, 2026

In July 2024, thousands of small business owners across South Korea logged into their vendor portals expecting routine bi-monthly paychecks, only to find their dashboards frozen. Travel agencies canceled booked summer vacations, delivery trucks turned back from warehouses, and payment gateway processors slammed their virtual shutters closed. Within days, two of South Korea’s most recognizable e-commerce platforms—TMON and WeMakePrice—had effectively ceased functioning, trapping roughly 1.8 trillion won (about $1.35 billion) in unpaid vendor funds and stranding over 60,000 merchants.

For international observers, the scandal was initially framed as a routine tech startup failure. But the reality behind the collapse of TMON and WeMakePrice was a textbook case of moral hazard and regulatory neglect. At its core, the disaster was driven by an aggressive, cash-strapped corporate expansion that relied on using small vendors' sales revenues as an interest-free slush fund to chase a Wall Street stock listing. As the dust settles, the tragedy has forced South Korea to rewrite its e-commerce laws while leaving thousands of small businesses holding the bill.

A Nasdaq Ambition Fueled by Vendor Cash

To understand how this crisis developed, one must look at Ku Young-bae, the founder and CEO of Qoo10, a Singapore-based e-commerce conglomerate. Ku was once a legendary figure in South Korean technology circles. In the 2000s, he built Gmarket into South Korea’s dominant online marketplace before orchestrating a high-profile sale to eBay. Seeking to replicate that legendary success on a global scale, Ku established Qoo10 and set his sights on listing its logistics subsidiary, Qxpress, on the U.S. Nasdaq exchange.

Securing a lucrative valuation on Nasdaq required enormous, continuous shipment volumes. Unable to generate that organic growth quickly enough through Qoo10 alone, Ku embarked on an aggressive spree of capital-free mergers and acquisitions. Between late 2022 and early 2024, Qoo10 bought up chronic, cash-hemorrhaging South Korean e-commerce platforms, starting with TMON in September 2022, followed by WeMakePrice in 2023, and later Interpark Commerce.

Because these platforms were already in severe capital deficit, Qoo10 acquired them largely through equity swaps rather than cash injections. Once under Qoo10's control, vendors on these platforms were pressured or incentivized to route their international logistics exclusively through Qxpress, artificially inflating Qxpress's shipping volume to boost its pre-IPO valuation.

The situation escalated dangerously in February 2024, when Qoo10 bought the North American and European shopping platform Wish for $170 million (about 230 billion won) in cash. Lacking liquid capital, Qoo10 diverted funds directly from the sales revenues owed to merchants on TMON and WeMakePrice to fund the deal. In essence, revenue earned by Mom-and-Pop sellers in Seoul was secretly siphoned off to purchase a global e-commerce entity.

To patch the resulting financial deficit, TMON began aggressively dumping pre-paid gift certificates and platform cash ("TMON Cash") at unsustainable discounts exceeding 10%. This provided immediate, short-term cash inflows from bargain-hunting consumers, but created a classic Ponzi-like dynamic: today’s consumer cash was used to settle last month’s vendor debts.

This reckless cash management was enabled by South Korea's outdated regulatory framework. At the time, online shopping platforms were under no legal obligation to hold vendor settlement funds in segregated escrow accounts. Combined with settlement windows that stretched beyond 60 days, platform owners were legally permitted to mix vendor funds with corporate operating capital for months at a time.

The $1.35 Billion Implosion and the 0.8% Payout Farce

By early July 2024, the fragile house of cards collapsed. Whispers of delayed payments spread across vendor forums, prompting major retailers and travel agencies to pull their listings. Panicked payment gateway (PG) firms and major credit card companies immediately froze payment processing and refund approvals for TMON and WeMakePrice. Cut off from fresh consumer cash, the platform's liquidity evaporated overnight.

The human and economic toll was immediate. Over 60,000 merchants found themselves unpaid for inventory shipped months earlier. Small businesses faced immediate insolvency, unable to pay employee wages or supplier costs. In late July 2024, both TMON and WeMakePrice filed for court-supervised debt rehabilitation.

What followed over the next year exposed the harsh limitations of South Korea’s corporate restructuring laws. In mid-2025, Oasis, a South Korean fresh-grocery delivery firm, stepped forward to acquire TMON by injecting 18.1 billion won (about $13.5 million). Although creditors voted against the proposal due to its dismal recovery terms, the court stepped in to grant compulsory approval in June 2025, officially allowing TMON to exit debt rehabilitation by August 2025.

Media headlines announced that TMON had been "saved," but the reality for small business owners was tragic. Because accumulated debts totaled $1.35 billion against a meager acquisition price, the actual recovery rate for unsecured merchant creditors was capped at roughly 0.8%. A small vendor owed 100 million won ($75,000) received just 800,000 won ($600). The court-ordered restructuring legally wiped away the company's debt burden while leaving victims completely devastated.

Meanwhile, sister platforms fared even worse. WeMakePrice failed to attract buyers and was declared bankrupt in November 2025, followed by Interpark Commerce in December 2025. Even TMON’s "rebirth" proved largely illusory: major South Korean credit card networks continued to deny processing integration well into 2026, leaving the platform operating as an empty shell.

Indictments, Systemic Gaps, and the "Anti-T-Me-Price" Laws

As anger mounted, law enforcement moved against the executives behind the collapse. In December 2024, South Korean prosecutors indicted Qoo10 CEO Ku Young-bae and nine top executives on charges of fraud, embezzlement, and breach of trust totaling 1.85 trillion won (about $1.38 billion). Investigators asserted that management was well aware of the impending insolvency when they continued issuing discounted gift vouchers and redirecting settlement capital. Separate criminal proceedings were launched over 26.3 billion won (about $19.5 million) in unpaid employee wages and severance payments. Executive defense teams have maintained that their decisions constituted legitimate "business judgment."

The structural failures that enabled the crisis also sparked intense political debate. Critics pointed out that loose oversight and regulatory leniency allowed during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure had created a dangerous policy blind spot where e-commerce operators functioned like unregulated financial intermediaries. In response, South Korea's National Assembly enacted sweeping legislative reforms widely referred to as the "Anti-T-Me-Price" laws.

Key regulatory changes enacted through amendments to the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and the Large-Scale Distribution Business Act include:

  • Strict Payment Settlement Windows: Mandatory settlement periods for online platforms were slashed from over 60 days to within 20 days of transaction completion (or 35 days for direct buy-and-sell models).
  • Mandatory Custody Segregation: Platforms and payment processors are legally required to hold at least 50% to 100% of vendor settlement funds in separate escrow accounts, trusts, or insurance guarantees with external financial institutions, preventing executives from using vendor money as working capital.
  • Enhanced Capital Requirements: Financial thresholds for payment gateway operators were doubled to prevent under-capitalized entities from handling massive consumer transaction volumes.

What to Watch Next in Korea’s Tech and Retail Sectors

The fallout from the TMON-WeMakePrice scandal has permanently altered South Korea’s retail landscape. As small merchants and consumers abandoned mid-tier, undercapitalized marketplaces, market power consolidated rapidly into deep-pocketed platform giants like Coupang and Naver, deepening the market's duopoly.

Moving forward, international business analysts and investors should monitor three key developments:

First, all eyes remain on the criminal trials of Ku Young-bae and Qoo10 leadership. The court’s verdict will set an important legal precedent for whether corporate officers can be held criminally liable for fraud when using client and vendor balances during high-risk M&A maneuvers.

Second, the full implementation of the mandatory fund segregation rules, set to come into full force by December 17, 2026, will test the financial viability of remaining second-tier e-commerce players in South Korea. Platforms reliant on vendor floating cash to support operating expenses will face severe liquidity stress.

Finally, observers should watch how South Korean regulators enforce oversight over hybrid e-commerce entities that straddle the line between retail brokering and financial processing. The tragedy showed that when tech innovation runs ahead of financial safeguards, it is small business owners who end up paying the price.
