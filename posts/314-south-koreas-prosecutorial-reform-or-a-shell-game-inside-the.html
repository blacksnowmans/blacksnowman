South Korea’s Prosecutorial Reform or a Shell Game? Inside the Plan to Rebrand the Prosecutors’ Office

September 07, 2026

For months, headlines in South Korea have felt like an endless loop of civic frustration. Turn on the evening news, and you are greeted by a relentless stream of accidents, crises, and political maneuvering. It is a modern malaise familiar to many developed democracies, but when an information ecosystem becomes entirely saturated by bad news and institutional failure, a society begins to feel fundamentally out of joint. Yet, beneath the general fatigue of daily headlines, a more calculated drama has been quietly unfolding in the corridors of power—one that targets the very architecture of South Korea’s legal and political system.

The latest maneuver centers on a long-promised cornerstone of South Korea's judicial overhaul: dismantling the powerful Prosecutors’ Office and replacing it with a new entity styled the Prosecution Service agency, or Gongso-cheong, meant strictly to handle indictments rather than investigations. To reformers, the proposal initially sounded like the historic breakthrough they had waited decades for. But upon closer inspection, critics argue the grand structural overhaul looks less like a revolution and more like a high-stakes shell game—a bureaucratic rebranding exercise designed to preserve institutional privilege under the banner of progress.

The Great Rebranding: Same Structure, New Signboard

A close look at the newly released enforcement decrees and organizational restructuring plans reveals a bewildering continuity. Despite the stated mission to strip prosecutors of their sweeping investigative authority, the underlying architecture of the Supreme Prosecutors’ Office, high prosecutors' offices, and district branches remains virtually untouched. The sprawling hierarchies have not been dismantled; they have merely been given fresh paint.

Divisions once tasked with high-level corruption and organized crime, ostensibly slated for abolition, have simply re-emerged under new headings like the "Major Crimes Department." Skeptics naturally ask a blunt question: If a department's mandate is supposedly abolished, why is an equivalent structure immediately spun up in its place? Similarly, specialized units like forensic science departments are ostensibly scaled back, only to be preserved under alternative titles that maintain their original functions. Rather than shedding power to police agencies or dedicated investigative bodies, the restructured agency appears determined to retain its grip.

Critics within the political sphere, including lawmakers who have scrutinized the draft organization plans, point out several glaring contradictions. First, despite the official narrative that prosecutors will no longer lead investigations, staffing levels remain largely unchanged, leaving thousands of investigative personnel nestled safely inside the new framework. Second, the creation of specialized liaison units to coordinate with other investigative bodies suggests prosecutors intend to maintain active oversight and supervisory control over police investigations. When an institution tasked exclusively with handling indictments continues to house vast investigative teams and monitoring units, the core promise of separating investigation from prosecution begins to evaporate.

A Bloated Bureaucracy and the Myth of Downsizing

Beyond the philosophical contradictions lies a staggering bureaucratic reality: the sheer scale of the proposed entity. According to the organizational charts circulating in Seoul, the newly minted prosecution structure will accommodate dozens of high-ranking regional chiefs, dozens of branch heads, and hundreds of internal divisions and subsections. The organizational footprint mirrors the old system down to its very bones.

Consider the staffing math. South Korea’s national police force operates with an immense investigative corps, yet the new prosecution-related agencies are slated to retain thousands of personnel explicitly categorized as investigative support staff—amounting to a double-digit percentage of the entire national police investigative workforce. Maintaining a force of that magnitude requires a purpose, which explains why the agency has engineered justifications to keep them on the payroll. While these personnel may spend their ordinary days reviewing case files transferred from police, the structural capacity to pivot and launch direct investigations remains entirely intact.

This institutional self-preservation has not gone unnoticed by the public or independent commentators, who view the outcome as an inevitable consequence of compromise-driven policymaking. When high-level appointments to newly created investigative oversight bodies are predominantly filled by former prosecutors, cynical observers are left asking how an institution can effectively reform itself when its alumni control the blueprint.

Political Expediency and the Erosion of Rule-Making

To understand why a reform movement with such massive public backing ended up producing a compromised bureaucratic hybrid, one must look at the shifting political dynamics within South Korea’s ruling circles. The trajectory of judicial reform shifted years ago when top political leadership openly voiced deep institutional distrust toward the police, setting off a cascade of policy adjustments. Once the nation’s chief executive signals skepticism toward a specific law enforcement branch, the entire administrative and political machinery quickly adapts to align with that preference.

In this environment, rigorous debate and long-term institutional design take a backseat to political survival and alignment. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal political force, has faced mounting criticism not just for the substance of its judicial policies, but for the mechanics of its internal decision-making. Observers have watched with growing dismay as party leadership repeatedly altered legislative and internal election rules on the fly—adjusting runoff systems and voting structures to fit immediate political calculations rather than enduring democratic principles.

When a political party handles legislative procedures as a tool for short-term advantage, public trust inevitably frays. The promise of reining in powerful unelected institutions gets bogged down in factional maneuvering, leaving voters with an uneasy sense that major structural reforms are little more than internal power-sharing agreements disguised as public service.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves closer to finalizing the legal framework for the new prosecution agency, several concrete milestones will determine whether this overhaul is merely cosmetic or genuinely transformative:

  • Legislative Review of Enforcement Decrees: Watch how opposition lawmakers and legal committees challenge the specific staffing allocations and supervisory powers embedded in the secondary regulations, particularly regarding whether prosecutors retain de facto investigative commands over police.
  • Personnel Appointments: Pay close attention to who is appointed to head the newly created regional offices and specialized liaison divisions. The background of these leaders will signal whether the traditional prosecutorial old guard is retaining operational control.
  • Inter-Agency Friction: Monitor relations between the National Police Agency and the newly structured prosecution service. Any jurisdictional turf wars over major crime investigations will immediately expose whether the separation of powers exists on paper or in practice.
  • Public and Civil Society Pushback: Observe how legal scholars, civic organizations, and grassroots political movements respond as the implementation timeline advances, and whether public dissatisfaction translates into broader electoral accountability.

Ultimately, true judicial reform requires more than changing letterheads and swapping acronyms. Until South Korea's political class summons the political will to dismantle entrenched institutional privileges rather than repackage them, the public will remain right to view grand reform announcements with a heavy dose of skepticism.
