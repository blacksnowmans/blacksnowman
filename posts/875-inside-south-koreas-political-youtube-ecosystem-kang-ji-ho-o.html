Inside South Korea's Political YouTube Ecosystem: Kang Ji-ho on the "Anti-Lee" Frame and the Crisis of Trust

September 21, 2026

To understand modern South Korean politics, you cannot just look at the National Assembly or the presidential office. You have to look at YouTube. Over the past several years, political commentary channels, independent live streams, and digital creator studios have evolved from fringe hobbies into powerful political actors that shape campaigns, mobilize millions of dollars in donations, and make or break political careers. But this ecosystem is currently under intense strain. Ratings are slipping, internal factionalism is tearing through progressive circles, and ordinary citizens who fund these independent media outlets are feeling increasingly alienated.

Recently, the political commentary channel Jeongchi Orakshil (Political Arcade) hosted Kang Ji-ho, a prominent South Korean journalist known for his sharp media analysis, to break down the current political landscape. Moving past personal background stories, the second half of their discussion delivered a remarkably blunt diagnosis of South Korea’s progressive faction, the mechanics of internal party purges, and the quiet betrayal felt by everyday voters. For international observers trying to decipher why South Korea's liberal camp is stumbling despite its massive historical base, this discussion offers a rare, unfiltered look at the machinery of modern Korean political discourse.

The Economics of Independent Media: From Red Superchats to Monthly Memberships

In South Korea’s hyper-digitized political commentary scene, viewer donations—known locally as "Superchats" on YouTube—are the lifeblood of many channels. Yet, in a striking departure from the standard creator playbook, Kang revealed that he and his co-hosts actively discourage viewers from sending large donations. Specifically, they tell their audience not to send Superchats colored red (which YouTube flags for amounts over 100,000 won, or about $75).

The reasoning is rooted in a surprising degree of empathy for the audience. The hosts noticed that many low-income viewers felt deep guilt and anxiety because they wanted to support the channel but simply could not afford to do so. Kang shared a moving email from a regular viewer who apologized profusely, explaining that they loved the news coverage but felt ashamed for watching for free because their daily life was too economically strained to buy a Superchat. While the hosts joked about whether 99,999 won (which triggers a pink alert) was safe, the underlying message was a profound respect for the working-class audience that sustains the channel.

This dynamic highlights a fundamental truth about independent media in South Korea: it is not massive, one-off corporate windfalls or flashy high-tier donations that keep alternative journalism alive. Instead, it is steady, small-dollar support—such as monthly channel memberships costing the price of a cheap meal—that allows independent reporters to plan budgets and conduct investigative work without bowing to corporate or political pressure. When major broadcasting networks are often accused of pandering to massive capital and state power, an independent press survives only through direct, uncorrupted solidarity with its readership.

The "Anti-Lee" Frame: Power Struggles Disguised as Broadening the Coalition

Moving from media economics to party politics, Kang tackled the most toxic internal debate currently consuming the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's main liberal opposition party: the so-called "anti-Lee frame." As the DPK struggles to mount an effective opposition against the conservative government of President Yoon Suk Yeol, a faction within the party has weaponized a narrative aimed at sidelining loyalists close to party leader Lee Jae-myung.

On the surface, the architects of this internal strategy claim they are pursuing "coalition expansion." They argue that the party must moderate its stance, reach out to centrists, and even embrace figures who historically opposed the party's core values to win national elections. But Kang diagnosed this as a transparently self-serving maneuver. In reality, it is an internal purge disguised as broad-tent politics. By labeling long-standing, orthodox party supporters—those who stood by the progressive movement through every historical crisis—as "anti-Lee" or hardliners who must be cleared out, ambitious political operators are carving out paths to internal power.

This creates a bizarre paradox. Figures who sat on the fence or opposed the party during critical moments are suddenly manufacturing ideological purity tests, pushing out the very people who built the party's foundation. Driven by echo chambers and community algorithms, these political operators convince themselves they are pursuing the correct path, even as their actions alienate the core voting base. The result has been a predictable political boomerang: despite endless talk of broadening the coalition, leader Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings have stagnated or declined, precisely because the bedrock supporters who once served as the party's fiercest shield have closed their hearts in disillusionment.

The Erosion of Political Courtesy and the Trauma of Abandoned Voters

Another striking theme of the discussion was the missing element of basic human courtesy and loyalty among elected officials. During South Korea's fiercely contested election seasons, politicians routinely plead for airtime, begging hosts to display their campaign donation accounts on screen. But once they win office or no longer need the platform, their attitude undergoes a swift transformation. Many of these same politicians turn around and attack the very independent media outlets that elevated them, publicly sneering at critical analyses and accusing friendly commentators of harboring hidden agendas against them.

Kang did not mince words, calling this behavior entirely shameless. Many of these politicians would have no political life to speak of if alternative platforms like independent YouTube broadcasts had not given them a megaphone during their darkest hours. Yet, the moment a critical report airs—or when politicians feel they must kowtow to state power—they readily throw their former supporters under the bus.

This cyclical betrayal has inflicted deep psychological scars on the South Korean electorate. Across the country, voters who poured their hearts, time, and money into backing specific politicians or political influencers now suffer from a form of political trauma. Having been repeatedly backstabbed by figures they once trusted, ordinary citizens are increasingly reluctant to invest emotional energy in new political figures. The public's apparent coldness is not a sign of political apathy; it is a defensive reaction to broken trust.

Furthermore, this disconnect is visible in institutional efforts by the party itself. Plans by the DPK to launch its own official broadcasting arms often fall flat because party leadership assumes that simply spinning up another corporate channel will draw viewers. In reality, as Kang bluntly noted, viewers do not tune in to watch politicians deliver stale, scripted talking points. When millions of South Koreans are worrying about skyrocketing housing costs and the grind of daily survival, political elites sit in their insulated bubbles debating abstract legal reforms that ordinary people simply do not care about.

The gap between the political class's internal obsession with procedural power struggles and the harsh economic reality felt by the street-level public remains dangerously wide.

The Value of Ground-Level Reality and the Power of Awakened Citizens

To combat this detachment, Kang emphasized the necessity of radical self-objectivity for journalists and commentators. Sitting in a room staring at internet algorithms and partisan online communities creates a dangerous illusion that the entire world thinks the way you do. True political reality cannot be understood from a computer screen; it requires walking the streets, talking to conservative neighbors, chatting with politically neutral friends, and asking people what they are actually talking about over dinner.

Crucially, Kang rejected the temptation of transitioning from journalism into formal electoral politics, noting that accepting a political badge or an elite title at a young age is a quick way to kill one's soul. When journalists treat proximity to power as a personal badge of success rather than a heavy burden of public service, they lose the critical independence that gives their work value.

Ultimately, the discussion returned to a foundational concept in modern South Korean political history: former President Roh Moo-hyun's famous ideal of "the organized power of an awakened citizenry." When citizens grow frustrated, the correct response is not to abandon the political space out of despair, but to hold ground. Independent media and critical voters must serve as a persistent, unblinking watchdog against opportunistic politicians who try to hijack parties for personal gain.
