Inside South Korea's Political Pressure Cooker: Low Approval Ratings, Fierce Audits, and Foreign Policy Friction

October 10, 2026

Picture the scene in Seoul on a national holiday meant for celebration and rest. While ordinary citizens marked Hangul Day—the anniversary of the invention of the Korean alphabet—by taking a break, the country's political heartland in Yeouido refused to pause. For political observers, laptops clicked open across the district as a relentless wave of news broke all at once: plummeting party ratings, fierce parliamentary clashes, and sudden diplomatic turbulence. Rather than offering a moment of quiet reflection, the holiday underscored a tense political climate where the gap between the nation's leadership and the daily lives of everyday people feels wider than ever.

South Korean politics rarely moves in straight lines, but the events of this past week have captured a distinct moment of vulnerability for the ruling establishment. While the administration attempts to project stability, underlying tremors are shaking both the ruling and opposition camps, transforming routine governance into a high-stakes battle for public trust.

The Paradox of Presidential Stability and Ruling Party Fatigue

The latest political barometer arrived via a closely watched Gallup Korea poll, which quickly became the most debated subject in the capital. According to the survey, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating held steady at 37 percent, matching his previous mark, while his disapproval rating dropped by 5 percentage points to 51 percent. On the surface, the administration managed to hold its ground against ongoing opposition pressure.

However, a closer look beneath the surface reveals a much more alarming picture for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the progressive party currently holding the presidency. The Democratic Party's own approval rating plunged to 35 percent—its lowest mark since the current government took office. Meanwhile, the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, registered at 26 percent.

This divergence tells a telling story. When a president's ratings flatten while the ruling party's support craters, it typically signals that core supporters are either drifting away or moving to the sidelines. Initial post-election optimism has faded, replaced by a lingering fatigue with both major parties. This erosion explains the growing internal friction within the ruling camp, where lawmakers are beginning to openly question the speed and direction of ongoing reforms. Unless the ruling party takes these numbers as a serious warning rather than a temporary dip, defending future policy initiatives will become increasingly difficult.

Capitalizing on this apparent vulnerability, minor and opposition parties have ramped up their rhetoric. The Rebuilding Korea Party voiced sharp criticisms of the Democratic Party, with lawmaker Shin Chang-hyun arguing that the progressive bloc needs to see double-digit support levels before the ruling party will take meaningful notice. At the same time, the conservative People Power Party launched fresh offensives against the Lee administration, leveraging shifting testimonies in past legal cases to keep the pressure high. In a political culture dominated by endless tactical combat, the constant infighting only deepens voter alienation.

Parliamentary Audits Ignite Over Budgets and the Judiciary

Away from party headquarters, the first week of the annual parliamentary inspection of government agencies—known locally as the national audit—burst into motion with a barrage of sharp exchanges. Major committees like the National Policy Committee and the Health and Welfare Committee turned into arenas for aggressive partisan warfare over state budgets and welfare initiatives.

Opposition lawmakers fiercely targeted the administration's newly proposed future-response funds, branding them as a slush fund designed for political distribution. The ruling camp defended the expenditures as vital investments in the nation's long-term preparedness, but largely found themselves on the defensive against the opposition’s framing. Rather than using the audit to address pressing public health concerns or seek practical solutions, lawmakers appeared more interested in scoring political points.

The friction extended directly into the judiciary. Debates over court administration and judicial independence flared up inside audit hearing rooms, prompting quiet expressions of concern from within the courts themselves. Yet, politicians showed little hesitation in treating the judicial branch as another front in their broader power struggle. The system of checks and balances between the executive, legislative, and judicial branches is foundational to South Korean democracy, but the current atmosphere in the National Assembly often resembles a zero-sum game aimed at subduing rivals rather than legislating for the public good.

Diplomatic Friction and the Balancing Act of Trade Policy

Beyond domestic squabbles, foreign policy and international trade presented fresh headaches for the administration. The most disruptive diplomatic chatter centered on a delay in the issuance of a U.S. visa for ruling party lawmaker Jung Chung-rae. The hold-up drew swift and sharp reactions from ruling party members, who argued that such administrative friction with a close security ally lacked basic diplomatic courtesy.

While analysts offered varying interpretations regarding how Washington views Seoul's current foreign policy posture, the incident immediately became political fodder. The conservative opposition seized on the friction to criticize the administration's diplomatic competence, while ruling party officials scrambled to manage the fallout. In a nation where security and foreign alliances are paramount, foreign policy issues rarely escape the pull of partisan domestic politics.

On the economic front, however, the administration made a decisive move that drew a more welcoming response from vulnerable communities. President Lee Jae-myung explicitly directed that South Korea's application to join the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) should not be submitted within the year. The decision stemmed from a recognition that sufficient dialogue and protective measures for local farmers and fishermen must come first.

Every open-market trade policy carries both benefits and hidden costs. While integrating deeper into global economic flows remains essential for an export-driven economy, shielding domestic industries from sudden shocks is a fundamental duty of the state. Striking the right balance between economic pragmatism and social safety nets will remain a critical test for the administration.

Reflecting on Language, Culture, and the Reality of Yeouido

The political noise reached a symbolic peak on Hangul Day, marking 580 years since the publication of the document that introduced the Korean alphabet. Both major parties released formal statements honoring the occasion, but the irony was hard to miss. While the official commentaries praised the historical value of the national language, the actual language used by politicians inside the National Assembly often stood in stark contrast to the spirit of Hangul.

King Sejong created the script to ensure that ordinary people could easily communicate their thoughts, rooting the alphabet in principles of clarity, consideration, and accessibility. Yet, the harsh rhetoric and inflammatory language frequently traded in Yeouido suggest that political elites have drifted far from those ideals. Words spoken by public figures shape national moods and societal standards; when public discourse is dominated by hostility, the loss is cultural as well as political.

At the official Hangul Day commemorative ceremony, Prime Minister Han Sung-sook emphasized the importance of preserving the Korean language's digital assets and expanding its competitiveness in the fast-growing artificial intelligence era. As K-culture continues to captivate global audiences, safeguarding the cultural infrastructure behind the language is a vital national task. Language can either act as a tool for division or a bridge to the future—a distinction that South Korea's political class would do well to remember.

What to Watch Next

As the autumn political calendar moves forward, several concrete developments will determine whether the administration can regain its footing or whether legislative gridlock will deepen:

    The trajectory of parliamentary audits: Watch whether upcoming committee sessions abandon theatrical showdowns to focus on tangible legislative fixes for inflation and household debt, or if partisan bickering completely sidelines the public agenda. Ruling party containment strategies: Observe how the Democratic Party responds to its historic low approval numbers, particularly whether party leadership adjusts its legislative speed or attempts a broader reconciliation within the progressive bloc. Trade and diplomatic follow-throughs: Keep an eye on how the administration handles ongoing friction with Washington and whether the paused CPTPP application leads to genuine, structured consultations with agricultural communities rather than temporary delays.

Ultimately, the sprawling debates consuming the capital serve as a reminder that governance is judged not by the volume of rhetoric produced in legislative halls, but by the tangible security and stability experienced in everyday life. Until South Korea's political actors shift their focus from defeating rivals to serving the public, the disconnect between Yeouido and the rest of the country is likely to persist.
