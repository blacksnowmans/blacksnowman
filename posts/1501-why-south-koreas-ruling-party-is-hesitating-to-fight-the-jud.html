Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Hesitating to Fight the Judiciary

October 26, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape appears, on the surface, to have settled into a new normal. Following the botched December 3 martial law attempt by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former leader was removed from office and now sits behind bars. President Lee Jae-myung heads the government, backed by a strong parliamentary majority held by the liberal Democratic Party (DP). Yet, beneath this surface stability, a bitter battle for the soul of South Korea’s constitutional order is unfolding—not in the halls of the National Assembly, but inside the courtrooms of the Supreme Court.

At the center of this storm is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, head of the Supreme Court of Korea. Progressive critics and civic activists accuse the judiciary of running a double-standard: fast-tracking cases against liberal figures while deliberately stalling trials for conservative figures associated with the failed martial law bid. With public trust in the courts plunging, calls to impeach Chief Justice Cho have moved from fringe legal circles into the mainstream political debate. However, the ruling party finds itself paralyzed, torn between demanding accountability and playing safe political defense.

A Two-Tiered Justice System Under Chief Justice Cho

To understand the growing outrage against Chief Justice Cho, one must look at how South Korean courts treat different political actors. While Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol himself was stripped of his presidency and jailed as a primary figure in the martial law attempt, many of the high-ranking officials, military figures, and bureaucrats who enabled the plot have seen their legal cases grind to a halt. Courts have dragged out proceedings, handed down light sentences, or allowed procedural delays to buy time.

A prime example is the ongoing legal case involving former Deputy Prime Minister Choi Sang-mok. When defense attorneys filed a motion to recuse the presiding judge, Lee Jin-kwan, the court simply sat on the petition for months rather than ruling on it. Legal analysts point out that accepting the recusal would trigger a wave of similar petitions across the country, while rejecting it would force the trial to proceed. By delaying a decision until the judiciary’s routine personnel reshuffle in February, the court effectively buys time to quietly swap out the judge without taking a public stance. Procedural delays have thus become a tool to extend the political lives of conservative figures.

This sluggish pace stands in stark contrast to how the Supreme Court handled cases involving President Lee Jae-myung back when he was an opposition leader. In his past trial for alleged violations of the Public Official Election Act, the Supreme Court’s full bench reviewed tens of thousands of pages of court records and overturned an acquittal in just 34 days—a unprecedented speed run that critics argued was timed to strip him of his eligibility for office right before an election cycle. Two dissenting justices noted at the time that the court’s rush severely undermined judicial neutrality.

This contrast has fueled a widespread perception among progressives that the judiciary under Chief Justice Cho operates as a political actor. While corporate executives and conservative power brokers benefit from technical delays, vulnerable workers and political opponents face rapid, unforgiving rulings. Under Chief Justice Cho, the Supreme Court’s full bench—traditionally a venue for robust constitutional debate among diverse judicial perspectives—is increasingly criticized by legal insiders as an assembly line delivering top-down rulings aligned with conservative interest groups.

The Case for Impeaching the Chief Justice

Article 65 of the Constitution of the Republic of Korea explicitly grants the National Assembly the authority to pass motions of impeachment against judges who violate the Constitution or laws in the performance of their official duties. Proponents of impeachment argue that Chief Justice Cho’s actions—and his inaction—during the December 3 crisis constitute a clear breach of his constitutional duty.

On the night martial law was declared, as military units moved toward key institutions and decree orders threatened to suspend judicial warrants, Chief Justice Cho did not issue an immediate, firm statement declaring the decree unconstitutional. Instead, he adopted a passive "wait and see" posture. Critics contend that failing to defend judicial independence against an unconstitutional martial law plot amounted to tacit cooperation with the insurrection.

Former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook and other vocal reformers argue that impeaching Chief Justice Cho is not merely about removing one man, but about breaking what they describe as a "judicial cartel"—an entrenched establishment of senior judges who view themselves as an aristocratic class exempt from democratic oversight. Proponents argue that bringing Cho to an impeachment trial before the Constitutional Court would force him to testify under oath regarding his directives during the martial law crisis and his handling of politically sensitive cases.

While cautious voices within the political establishment warn that impeaching a Chief Justice could trigger judicial instability or invite a conservative backlash, reform advocates point to recent history. South Korea survived the impeachments of two presidents—Park Geun-hye in 2017 and Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—and emerged with its democratic institutions reinforced. Holding senior judges accountable through constitutional mechanisms, reformers argue, is the only way to restore public trust in the rule of law.

Political Calculations and the Ruling Party’s Paralysis

Despite holding a decisive majority in Parliament, the ruling Democratic Party has taken a surprisingly passive stance toward judicial reform. Rather than introducing an impeachment motion, party leaders have leaned toward soft options like launching parliamentary investigations (국정조사)—a mechanism that lacks the power to compel testimony or enforce criminal penalties on sitting judges.

This hesitation stems from complex internal political arithmetic. A recent turning point came when the courts effectively cleared Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a leading figure in the conservative People Power Party (PPP), of election law charges. While progressives viewed the lenient ruling as another clear example of judicial bias, some leaders within the Democratic Party quietly breathed a sigh of relief. Had Mayor Oh been stripped of his post, it would have triggered a high-stakes Seoul mayoral by-election.

Facing economic headwinds and shifting voter demographics in the capital, DP leadership—including party chief Kim Min-seok—was privately anxious about losing a major election in Seoul early in President Lee’s term. The court’s favorable ruling for Mayor Oh inadvertently removed an immediate political hurdle for the DP leadership. However, relying on judicial rulings to avoid tough political battles has left the party looking weak and opportunistic to its core supporters.

Furthermore, internal party dynamics ahead of future elections play a major role in this paralysis:

  • Nomination Safety: Many DP lawmakers, particularly those representing conservative-leaning or swing districts, fear that leading an aggressive charge against the courts might alienate moderate voters or make them targets for judicial retaliation.
  • Complacency in Safe Seats: Lawmakers in safe strongholds like the Honam region face little competitive threat from opposition parties, focusing instead on securing party nominations rather than pushing for risky institutional reforms.
  • Bureaucratic Avoidance: Rather than exercising their legislative power through direct impeachment votes, party leaders have created numerous parliamentary task forces, substituting symbolic rhetoric for concrete action.

By failing to challenge judicial overreach, the Democratic Party risks handing momentum back to the conservative opposition. Conservative figures like Mayor Oh Se-hoon, independent lawmaker Han Dong-hoon, and Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party are already regrouping, laying the groundwork for the 2028 parliamentary elections and the 2030 presidential campaign. If the ruling party appears too timid to reform an unequal legal system, moderate voters may conclude that the DP lacks the courage to govern effectively.

What to Watch Next

The standoff between South Korea’s legislative majority and its judicial leadership is reaching a critical inflection point. To gauge where the country’s political alignment is heading, keep an eye on three specific developments over the coming months:

  • The February Judicial Reshuffle: Pay close attention to the annual judicial transfers in February. How the Supreme Court reassigns judges handling sensitive martial law cases—specifically whether Judge Lee Jin-kwan is replaced—will reveal whether the judiciary plans to continue stalling trials related to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's allies.
  • Movement on Impeachment Motions: Watch whether backbenchers and reformist factions within the Democratic Party, alongside smaller progressive parties like the Rebuilding Korea Party, can rally enough internal support to force a floor vote on impeaching Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.
  • Polling Trends Among Moderate Voters: Approval ratings for both President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the Democratic Party among voters in their 30s and 40s will dictate the party’s appetite for risk. If polling dips further, DP leadership may be forced to choose between aggressive reform to re-energize its base or deeper retreat into political caution.
