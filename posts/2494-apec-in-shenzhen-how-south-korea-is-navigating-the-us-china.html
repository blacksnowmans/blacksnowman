APEC in Shenzhen: How South Korea is Navigating the U.S.-China-Russia Power Struggle

November 19, 2026

Step onto the tarmac in Shenzhen, and the air feels charged with the kind of high-stakes tension that usually precedes a major shifting of global tectonic plates. Leaders representing the world's most formidable economic and military superpowers have gathered for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, transforming what is ostensibly a trade forum into a crucible for a new world order. For South Korea, caught in the gravitational pull of these competing titans, this year's summit is nothing short of a tightrope walk over an abyss.

Against this backdrop, President Lee Jae-myung—leader of the Democratic Party, South Korea's center-left ruling party—met with Chinese President Xi Jinping for a 34-minute bilateral summit. While brief, the meeting yielded a crucial baseline agreement: that peace and stability on the Korean Peninsula serve the mutual interests of both Seoul and Beijing. But this localized diplomatic win is just a single pixel in a much larger, more volatile picture. Just outside, Xi was seen greeting U.S. President Donald Trump at the airport with a personal embrace—a striking departure from protocol that sent ripples through international diplomatic circles ahead of a historic, unprecedented three-way summit between the U.S., China, and Russia. As anxieties mount over whether these superpowers might carve up regional spheres of influence behind closed doors, South Korea faces an urgent question: How can a middle power preserve its strategic autonomy when the rules of the global game are being rewritten?

Inside the Shenzhen Bilateral: Securing Real Gains While Managing Beijing

The 34-minute meeting between President Lee and President Xi in Shenzhen marked their third encounter, and its primary achievement was anchoring the principle that Korean Peninsula stability is a shared objective. In recent years, as North Korea's nuclear provocations and inter-Korean relations deteriorated, Beijing often turned a blind eye or tacitly enabled Pyongyang. By securing Xi’s verbal support for constructive engagement and the easing of tensions—including a nod toward renewed U.S.-North Korea talks—Seoul has managed to secure a degree of diplomatic breathing room.

From a pragmatic perspective, the Lee administration’s willingness to treat China as a permanent fixture rather than an adversary represents a departure from the confrontational posture of past conservative administrations, most notably the discredited government of former President Park Geun-hye and the chaotic final years of the disgraced Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment. Rather than falling into the lazy trap of treating security strictly as a conservative concern and economics as a progressive one, the current administration operates on the practical premise that regional peace is the ultimate economic driver.

That pragmatic approach bore immediate fruit on the economic front, where negotiators reached a substantial breakthrough on the second-phase negotiations of the South Korea-China Free Trade Agreement (FTA). Eight years in the making, this next phase focuses on market openings in services and investment. Crucially, it secures "most-favored-nation" treatment for South Korean cultural goods, establishing a legal shield and a renewed pathway for K-content—gaming, dramas, and films—to re-enter the lucrative Chinese market. In a global economy increasingly fractured by Washington's supply chain decoupling strategies, Beijing is eager to keep Seoul close, and South Korea is wisely using that leverage to capture market share.

The Red Carpet Embrace: Decoding Xi Jinping’s Overture to Donald Trump

The most arresting visual of the summit occurred on the tarmac at Shenzhen Bao'an International Airport, where Xi Jinping took the unprecedented step of personally greeting Donald Trump with a warm embrace. It was the first time since taking power that Xi has personally gone to an airport to welcome a foreign leader, complete with an honor guard and military band.

This theatrical display of hospitality was much more than simple politeness. It was a calculated move by Beijing to defuse escalating trade tensions and manage the shockwaves of Trump’s America First protectionism. During his CEO summit keynote, Xi subtly rebuked Washington's tariff policies by declaring that "isolation inevitably leads to backwardness," while simultaneously dangling the carrot of vast market access under the banner of "Chinese modernization."

For Beijing, rolling out the crimson carpet is an investment. Xi is attempting to frame the U.S.-China relationship around managed coexistence, buying time and extracting concessions on critical flashpoints like Taiwan and trade policy. But as foreign policy analysts note, such elite-level pageantry is never free. The goodwill purchased at the Shenzhen airport will likely demand a price when the behind-the-door bargaining begins.

The Moscow-Beijing Axis and the Shadow of a New Yalta

Adding another layer of complexity to the APEC stage is Russian President Vladimir Putin, who made his first appearance at an APEC group photo in nine years. Strutting into the venue with visible ease, Putin celebrated his 50th summit meeting with Xi Jinping, loudly proclaiming that Western economic sanctions against Moscow have failed.

The deepening alignment between China and Russia has evolved from a marriage of convenience into a comprehensive partnership spanning military, economic, and security domains, explicitly aimed at dismantling the Western-led unipolar world order. Yet beneath this public display of solidarity lies a subtle undercurrent of friction. Many regional experts suggest Beijing quietly worries about a resurgent Russia becoming too powerful or unpredictable, particularly as the war in Ukraine drags on. China wants Russia as a diplomatic shield against Western pressure, but it does not want Moscow dictating the terms of Eurasian hegemony.

This inherent contradiction presents an opening for middle powers like South Korea. If Seoul plays its cards astutely, it can exploit the gaps in the Sino-Russian partnership to pressure Beijing into exerting a restraining influence on North Korea—especially as Pyongyang deepens its dangerous military exchanges with Moscow.

The tension will peak with the historic three-way summit involving the U.S., China, and Russia. The prospect of these three nuclear-armed giants meeting to carve out spheres of influence has inevitably triggered historical anxieties about a "Second Yalta Conference"—a fear that the destinies of smaller nations will be bargained away to satisfy superpower appetites. The haunting scenario for Seoul is one where Washington, Beijing, and Moscow agree to a messy geopolitical freeze that implicitly legitimizes North Korea's nuclear status in exchange for stability on other fronts.

What to Watch Next

As the Shenzhen APEC summit concludes and the dust settles from the historic U.S.-China-Russia negotiations, several concrete developments will signal whether South Korea has successfully protected its national interests:

  • The Implementation of the FTA Phase Two: Watch for the formal signing of the South Korea-China services and investment agreement. The real test will be whether administrative hurdles for K-dramas, gaming licenses, and K-pop performances in China genuinely vanish or simply shift to new bureaucratic mazes.
  • Beijing's Actions on North Korea: Monitor whether China’s stated support for "constructive dialogue" translates into tangible pressure on Pyongyang to halt provocative missile tests and return to diplomatic channels, or if it remains empty rhetorical cover.
  • The Shape of the U.S.-China-Russia Communiqué: Scrutinize the official readouts from the three-way summit for any oblique references to the Korean Peninsula. Any language implying a shift in denuclearization baselines will require an immediate, forceful diplomatic pushback from Seoul.
  • Trilateral U.S.-Japan-Korea Coordination: Observe how quickly President Lee coordinates with Washington and Tokyo following the APEC meetings to ensure that American concessions to Beijing do not inadvertently compromise allied security architecture in East Asia.

Diplomacy at this level is a relentless game of anticipating moves three steps ahead. President Lee has managed to secure initial economic dividends and essential security assurances in Shenzhen, but the true test of his administration's strategic autonomy will be its ability to ensure that South Korea remains an architect of its own destiny, rather than a footnote in a grand bargain struck by others.
