The Dangerous Gamble of Lee Jae-myung’s "Structural Majority" Strategy

November 01, 2026

Hiking the trails of Mount Gwanak on the southern outskirts of Seoul, one can’t help but reflect on the steep, winding path of South Korean democracy. For decades, these mountains have stood silent witness to the rise and fall of political giants. Today, as the administration of President Lee Jae-myung enters a critical juncture, a sense of unease is permeating the liberal camp. The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), currently the nation’s ruling party, finds itself at a crossroads not because of external pressure from the opposition, but because of a fundamental shift in how the President views his own mandate.

President Lee Jae-myung, who ascended to the Blue House (the traditional seat of the South Korean presidency, now a symbol of executive power) following the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, is currently pursuing a strategy known as the "structural majority." On the surface, it sounds like pragmatic politics: expanding the base to ensure long-term stability. However, as recent analyses from prominent progressive voices like Choe Bae-geun, a well-known economist and political commentator, suggest, this pivot may be a catastrophic miscalculation. By attempting to forge an alliance with the conservative establishment to secure a "60 to 70 percent" approval rating, Lee is systematically dismantling the very "core" support base that protected him during his darkest days of prosecution and political exile.

The Illusion of a Structural Majority and the Erasure of the Base

The history of Democratic administrations in South Korea—from Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun to Moon Jae-in—has always been defined by a symbiotic relationship between the leader and a highly mobilized, reform-minded core support base. These are not mere voters; they are the "shield" that defends the administration against a traditionally conservative media and a powerful prosecutorial service. Yet, President Lee appears to be treating this base as a hindrance rather than a foundation.

The "structural majority" theory posits that the liberal-reformist camp is a "structural minority," capable of winning only 40 to 50 percent of the vote. To break this ceiling and achieve "LDP-style" long-term dominance—referring to Japan’s Liberal Democratic Party, which has ruled almost uninterrupted for decades—Lee believes he must win over the center-right. The logic is that by moving to the center and recruiting conservative figures, the government can create a majority so large that it becomes unassailable.

However, this strategy assumes that the core supporters will stay put regardless of the President’s actions. During a recent press conference at the Chunchugwan, the press center within the presidential complex, Lee remarked that he never promised to be a "president for only one side." He framed the demands of his core supporters for radical reform as "factional selfishness" that prevents national unity. To his supporters, this felt like a betrayal. They argue that they never asked him to ignore the rest of the country; they asked him to fulfill the specific reform promises—such as curbing the power of the state prosecution—that got him elected in the first place. By framing himself as a "President for everyone" to justify ignoring his base, Lee is effectively gaslighting the people who stood in the cold with candles to defend him.

Trading Reform for an Establishment Alliance

The most visible sign of this shift is the President’s recent recruitment drive. Names that were once anathematic to the Democratic Party are now being brought into the fold. This includes figures like Lee Seok-yeon, a former Minister of Government Legislation under conservative administrations; Lee Byung-tae, a staunchly pro-market conservative economist; and former lawmakers Kim Sung-shik and Lee Hye-hoon, who have deep roots in the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition.

President Lee seems to believe that the real power in South Korea lies with what he calls the "trained and organized establishment." By bringing these figures into his orbit, he hopes to neutralize the opposition and manage the state more "efficiently." But this "Big Tent" is beginning to look more like a surrender. For the core supporters, who viewed the Lee administration as a vehicle for dismantling the entrenched privileges of the elite, seeing these figures in positions of influence is a bitter pill to swallow.

"It is a tragedy to watch a leader dismantle his own camp under the illusion of creating a majority. You cannot expand a tree's branches by cutting its roots."

This pivot has led to a noticeable slowdown in key reform agendas. Promises to overhaul the prosecution service—which many liberals view as a "political tool" used by the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—have been watered down or delayed. The administration's focus has shifted from "reform" to "management," a move designed to avoid upsetting the newly recruited conservative allies. The cost, however, is the loss of "political momentum." Without the passionate backing of a base that believes in a cause, the administration becomes a mere technocratic entity, vulnerable to the slightest political breeze.

The Collapse of the Voluntary Defense Shield

One of the unique features of South Korean liberal politics is the "voluntary defense" provided by citizens. During the Moon Jae-in era, when the administration faced distorted media reports or prosecutorial overreach, thousands of citizens would take to social media or the streets to explain the government's stance and correct the narrative. They were a decentralized, unpaid, and highly effective PR machine.

Under President Lee’s current strategy, this shield is evaporating. As the core supporters feel marginalized and dismissed as "radical," they have retreated into a state of "lethargic observation." They are no longer willing to go to bat for a President who describes their demands as a burden. In their place, the administration has come to rely on professional, pro-government YouTubers. But these influencers, often motivated by clicks and financial gain rather than genuine ideological conviction, lack the moral authority and persuasive power of a grassroots movement. They speak to an echo chamber, failing to reach the broader public that the President so desperately wants to court.

The consequences of this isolation were on full display during the President's recent overseas trips. Facing criticism from the media and the opposition, Lee reacted with uncharacteristic vitriol. His comment that "pigs only see pigs"—a reference to a famous Buddhist teaching suggesting that people see the world as a reflection of their own hearts—was seen as a sign of "mental cracking." Without a loyal base to absorb the blows and filter the criticism, the President is taking every hit personally. He is becoming a "lonely commander" in a fortress surrounded by an establishment that is only with him as long as he serves their interests.

What to Watch Next

The gamble for a "structural majority" is a high-stakes play that could define the remainder of Lee Jae-myung’s term and the future of the Democratic Party. If he continues on this path, the risk of a "LDP-style" stagnation is real, where the party maintains power but loses its soul and its ability to enact meaningful change. Here is what to watch for in the coming months:

  • The Approval Rating Ceiling: If the President’s approval rating fails to break into the 60% range despite his centrist pivot, the "structural majority" theory will be proven false, likely leading to an internal revolt within the DPK.
  • The "Yoon Suk-yeol" Factor: While Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is currently in prison, the conservative forces that supported him are regrouping. If Lee alienates his base too much, these forces may find an opening to reassert themselves, framing the current government as having failed its own supporters.
  • Key Legislative Appointments: Watch who the President appoints to senior positions in the coming months. If he continues to tap conservative establishment figures while ignoring reformist voices, the breach with his core supporters may become permanent.
  • The Return of the Street: If the base feels completely unheard, we may see the return of grassroots protests, not against the opposition, but calling for the President to return to his original promises.

In Korean politics, a leader who cuts off his own roots to reach for the sun often finds that the wind blows much harder at the top. President Lee Jae-myung is currently betting that he can build a new foundation while the old one crumbles. It is a gamble that the history of the Blue House suggests is rarely won.
