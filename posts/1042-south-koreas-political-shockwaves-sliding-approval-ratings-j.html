South Korea's Political Shockwaves: Sliding Approval Ratings, Judicial Clashes, and the Battle Over Reform

October 13, 2026

Picture the scene in Seoul: inside the National Assembly, parliamentary committees are roaring with cross-examinations, television screens flash breaking poll results in bold red and blue, and political strategists in both major parties are scrambling to rewrite their playbooks. For an outside observer looking in, South Korean politics often resembles a high-speed bullet train hurtling down a track with the switch constantly being thrown. But beneath the daily theater of accusations and press conferences, a deeper friction is pulling at the fabric of the country's democracy—one where public fatigue with petty partisanship is finally colliding with high-stakes battles over the judiciary, regulatory authority, and national governance.

Recent developments have brought these tensions to a boil. From a stunning reversal in party support numbers to fierce institutional showdowns involving the police, the courts, and e-commerce giants, the political landscape is shifting beneath the feet of South Korea's leaders. Understanding why these events matter requires looking past the noise of the headlines and examining the structural strains currently testing the administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the broader political establishment.

The Warning Lights of Public Opinion and a Shifting Electorate

The most immediate jolt to the political establishment came from a survey by the pollster Realmeter, delivering news that sent ripples of anxiety through the ruling camp. President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating slipped for the second consecutive week down to 35.2%, while support for his party, the liberal Democratic Party, dropped sharply by 7 percentage points in a single week to 35.5%. Crucially, this allowed the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, to edge ahead at 41.3%, marking an inversion within the margin of error.

For a ruling party accustomed to steering the national narrative, two straight weeks of falling numbers cannot be brushed aside as a temporary blip. Observers note that erosion is clearly visible among progressive voters and independents alike, fueled by growing exhaustion over the ruling camp's communication style and the pace of its policy rollouts. Critics within the broader liberal sphere have pointed out that self-congratulatory internal media channels do little to mend fences with a public grappling with economic pressures, arguing that quiet, grass-roots engagement is far more urgent than defensive press statements.

Capitalizing on these openings, the People Power Party has ramped up its offensive, framing the dip as a referendum on the administration's vulnerabilities. Yet, the opposition has faced its own share of criticism for the nature of its attacks. Recent controversies—such as fierce political sparring surrounding the president's schedule—demonstrate how quickly legitimate debate can curdle into personalized mudslinging. When politicians spend their energy trading personal insults rather than debating substantive economic relief, the public’s frustration deepens. The current slide in numbers is less about a single policy failure and more about a cumulative fatigue with perpetual political warfare.

Institutional Flashpoints: The Judiciary, the Police, and the Limits of Reform

While approval numbers capture public mood, institutional friction is where the real power struggles are taking place. The administration's reform agenda has run headlong into deep-seated resistance within South Korea's powerful state apparatus, most notably in the realms of law enforcement and the judiciary.

A recent directive from Minister of Public Administration and Security Yoon Ho-jung ordering an inspection into senior official Im Eun-jung sparked a fierce debate over the boundaries of public service and political neutrality. While the ruling party defended the move as a common-sense measure to enforce administrative discipline, opposition factions labeled it an attempt to purge figures not aligned with the current administration's preferences. This friction sits alongside ongoing controversies over police investigation structures and high-profile appointments, highlighting the enduring dilemma of reforming power agencies without tipping into perceived political bias.

The pressure has extended even higher, reaching the upper echelons of the court system. The Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor opposition party, has pushed forward with plans to pursue an impeachment motion against Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, arguing that his position has lost the confidence of a large segment of the public. This aggressive maneuver has drawn sharp pushback from conservatives who view it as a direct assault on judicial independence and the constitutional separation of powers. When political parties begin treating the judiciary as a target for removal whenever a ruling displeases them, it threatens to erode the ultimate institutional referee designed to moderate social conflict.

Where Sides Agree: Confronting Corporate Power and Digital Safety

Amidst the heavy partisan polarization, rare moments of alignment do occur—and they usually happen when the public interest is blatantly flouted. A striking example emerged when the South Korean legislature united in bipartisan condemnation of Coupang, one of the country's dominant e-commerce and logistics platforms, after it openly resisted an on-site inspection by the Fair Trade Commission. Lawmakers from across the aisle lambasted the company's dismissive attitude toward regulatory oversight, proving that when corporate giants act as if they are above the law, partisan lines temporarily dissolve.

Beyond domestic corporate accountability, South Korean lawmakers are also being forced to reckon with global digital policy trends. As countries like Turkey introduce strict bans on social media usage for minors under 15—joining international efforts led by nations like Australia to combat youth addiction and toxic online content—South Korea faces its own mounting crises regarding deepfake sex crimes, fake news circulation, and youth gambling.

Incidents involving the spread of manipulated digital information and fabricated political claims have forced government warnings of strict legal action. Balancing freedom of expression against the societal imperative to protect citizens from unchecked disinformation is one of the defining challenges of modern governance. Yet, when political actors remain consumed by daily legal battles and partisan posturing, the urgent task of building robust digital and social safety nets for the next generation often falls by the wayside.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly's parliamentary audit progresses and the political calendar moves forward, several concrete indicators will determine whether South Korea's political class can course-correct:

  • The Trajectory of Ruling Party Strategy: Watch to see if the Democratic Party shifts its messaging from defensive posturing to tangible, livelihood-focused legislative achievements to win back disillusioned independent voters.
  • Judicial and Regulatory Committee Showdowns: Monitor upcoming parliamentary hearings regarding police appointments and fair trade enforcement. These will test whether bipartisan anger at corporate non-compliance can translate into actual, enforceable regulatory reform.
  • The Handling of Digital Safety Legislation: Keep an eye on committee discussions addressing deepfakes and online regulation, which will signal whether lawmakers can look past short-term partisan fights to address long-term societal harms.

Ultimately, the current political climate presents a stark choice for South Korea's leaders. The warning signs flashing in opinion polls and institutional clashes are a reminder that citizens are watching closely. Whether the political establishment continues to treat governance as a zero-sum game or pivots toward addressing the real-world concerns of everyday people will define the success of the current administration in the months ahead.
