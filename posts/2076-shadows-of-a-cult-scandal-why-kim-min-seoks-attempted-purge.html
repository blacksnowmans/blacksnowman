Shadows of a Cult Scandal: Why Kim Min-seok's Attempted Purge Is Shaking South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 07, 2026

Behind the closed doors of a Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) general assembly in Seoul, lawmakers formed a curious line. They were waiting in thirty-minute shifts to enter a secure viewing room, expecting to examine what party leader Kim Min-seok had framed as an explosive national security dossier: concrete proof tying his high-profile internal rival, former party leader Jung Chung-rae, to Shincheonji, one of South Korea’s most notorious fringe religious movements. Tensions were running high. Inside the ruling party, allegations of cult ties carry the force of a political death sentence.

Yet as lawmakers emerged from the room one by one, the tension gave way to stunned disbelief and embarrassment. Rather than a smoking gun linking a veteran progressive heavyweight to a clandestine religious organization, the file reportedly contained outdated member rosters from years past, amateurish audio transcripts, and claims so flimsy they bordered on farce. What was pitched as a decisive strike to protect party integrity has instead backfired, setting off fierce calls for Kim’s resignation and raising troubling questions about how low internal party combat can sink in contemporary South Korean politics.

Behind Closed Doors: The Dossier That Fizzled

To grasp why these accusations carry such weight, foreign observers must understand the uniquely toxic role religious sects occupy in South Korea's political landscape. Shincheonji Church of Jesus is a secretive millenarian group that has faced intense public hostility and legal scrutiny, particularly after becoming the primary accelerator of South Korea's first major COVID-19 outbreak in 2020. More recently, anxieties have grown across the political spectrum over coordinated attempts by religious organizations to quietly enroll tens of thousands of their followers as dues-paying political party members in order to influence internal primary votes. A recent internal party survey noted an estimated 14,000 suspected Shincheonji adherents within the DPK’s vast membership rolls.

However, that organizational vulnerability was meant to be treated as an institutional risk, not weaponized as an arbitrary purge mechanism. When Kim Min-seok presented his top-secret materials to DPK lawmakers, the dossiers contained membership registers dating from 2015 to 2021—records from the turbulent years during Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, long before the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung took power. Lawmakers who inspected the binders were reportedly shocked by how little actionable substance they held.

Simply possessing lists of sect members who registered years ago does nothing to establish that a senior lawmaker was colluding with them or coordinating political operations. Instead, lawmakers left the room muttering that circulating such flimsy gossip at an official caucus meeting was an abuse of leadership. The maneuver did not look like an earnest effort to protect the party; it looked like an attempt to leverage legitimate anxieties over religious infiltration to assassinate the character of a prominent intra-party rival ahead of internal leadership contests.

Wardrobe Colors and Municipal Logos: An Absurd Smear Campaign

The materials Kim’s camp put forward ventured from outdated paperwork into unintentional parody. The specifics presented as "evidence" of Jung Chung-rae’s alleged ties to Shincheonji quickly leaked from the assembly, leaving observers incredulous.

One claim targeted Kim Deok-cheon, president of the regional newspaper Honam Ilbo, who had given a lecture at Chosun University in Gwangju. The dossier alleged he was a clandestine Shincheonji operative—primarily on the grounds that he "frequently wears clothes in Shincheonji's signature color." In an ecosystem where South Korean political parties use bright primary colors for identity (the DPK relies heavily on deep blue), deducing that someone belongs to a controversial sect because of their jacket or tie selection struck seasoned politicians as desperate.

Even more bizarre was an attempt to incriminate Jung through the official municipal logo of Mapo District in western Seoul, which forms Jung’s parliamentary constituency. Kim’s dossier suggested that the Mapo District Office logo resembled Shincheonji’s emblem, framing the visual similarity as a hint of coordinated allegiance. But the Mapo logo was created years earlier by former lawmaker Sohn Hye-won, a respected professional brand identity designer who previously revamped the party's own visual branding. Jung had no involvement in the logo’s aesthetic development, nor does the design bear any meaningful resemblance to the sect’s symbols beyond superficial geometry. Fabricating a religious conspiracy out of a renowned designer’s public work for a major district damaged the credibility of Kim’s camp far more than it harmed Jung.

The Whistleblower in the Group Chat: Unraveling the Setup

If the evidence itself was laughable, the mechanism behind its public rollout was far darker. The catalyst for the smear was an appearance on the progressive YouTube news channel New Tamsa by Guk Yong-ho, an elder in Shincheonji. Guk went on air to claim that the sect had mobilized mass registrations specifically inside Jung Chung-rae’s home constituency of Mapo.

Scrutiny into Guk's background, however, revealed that he was hardly a neutral whistleblower. Digital traces placed Guk directly inside a 2,800-member private KakaoTalk group chat established specifically to organize and boost Kim Min-seok’s political support base. Several sitting DPK lawmakers, including Park Sun-won and Lee Un-ju, were active members of the exact same chat group. Guk had reportedly used credentials as a parliamentary correspondent for the Segye Times to move through political circles, facilitating access for figures aligned with factional interests.

To compound the scandal, the same 2,800-member private support network allegedly housed a regional representative from the Unification Church (Tongilgyo), another controversial religious movement. This revelation turned the entire narrative upside down. While Kim’s allies were publicly accusing Jung of secret alliances with religious sects, Kim's own core digital campaign apparatus was actively harboring senior figures from multiple controversial religious organizations to amplify his factional reach. Instead of a bona fide whistleblower coming forward, the episode bore all the hallmarks of an orchestrated hit job: plant an insider from a private campaign chat on an external broadcast, package the broadcast transcript as urgent intelligence, and then force lawmakers to review it behind closed doors.

Legal Jeopardy and the Moral Hazard of Internal Purges

Kim’s gamble has brought considerable legal vulnerability. Under South Korean defamation law and established Supreme Court precedents (notably decisions 2012Do13718 and 2019Do8815), hiding behind opinion or political hyperbole offers little protection when defamatory assertions lack reasonable factual foundations or are made with malicious intent. Under Article 70 of the Information and Communications Network Act and Article 251 of the Public Official Election Act, launching unverified attacks against an electoral rival carries severe penalties.

Beyond statutory violations, the move strikes at the fragile consensus within South Korea's ruling progressives. With President Lee Jae-myung in office after the tumultuous impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol, the Democratic Party was expected to demonstrate steady governance and institutional discipline. Instead, the sight of a party chairman convening a closed-door caucus to circulate unvetted, tabloid-tier opposition research against a colleague feels like a relapse into the dirtiest traditions of factional warfare.

"Fabricating cult allegations based on clothing colors and district emblems does not just demean the target; it degrades the democratic process itself, reducing serious governance to a theater of paranoia."

Rather than consolidating Kim’s authority ahead of internal party reconfigurations, the botched revelation has galvanized support for Jung Chung-rae and triggered widespread demands for Kim to step down. Party insiders are openly questioning whether a leader willing to use such crude tactics can be trusted to run nominations, oversee legislative strategy, or support the Lee administration’s broader reform agenda.

What to Watch Next

The fallout from Kim Min-seok’s viewing-room gambit is now shifting into institutional channels. Observers of Korean politics should watch several concrete flashpoints over the coming weeks:

  • Disciplinary Referrals to the DPK Ethics Tribunal: Lawmakers aligned with Jung Chung-rae are preparing formal complaints regarding the unauthorized use of unverified data during an official caucus meeting. If the tribunal accepts the case, Kim could face formal party censure.
  • Potential Criminal Defamation Filings: Jung and affected third parties—including former lawmaker Sohn Hye-won and media executives named in the dossier—retain the option to file complaints under the Information and Communications Network Act.
  • Scrutiny of Factional Digital Networks: The revelation that figures from both Shincheonji and the Unification Church operated inside a senior lawmaker’s 2,800-member group chat will prompt calls for a sweeping audit of how outside religious interests interface with internal DPK volunteer groups.
  • Leadership Resignation Demands: As public embarrassment deepens, watch whether mainstream backbenchers join the open calls for Kim to surrender his leadership post to prevent further erosion of public trust in the ruling majority.

In modern Korean politics, accusing a colleague of covert cult ties is the nuclear option. But when the launch fails and reveals an amateurish setup manufactured in a partisan chat room, the fallout inevitably engulfs the person who pressed the button.
