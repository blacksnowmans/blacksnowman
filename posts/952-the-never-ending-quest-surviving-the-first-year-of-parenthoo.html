The Never-Ending Quest: Surviving the First Year of Parenthood in South Korea

October 10, 2026

Before the first diaper is even changed, expectant parents in South Korea are inundated with well-meaning but ultimately misleading advice: "It gets easier once they hit 100 days," or "Just wait until they can walk." But for those navigating the reality of raising a child in the modern era, the experience is not one of pain vanishing, but rather of its shape shifting. As one parent candidly put it, "The law of conservation of misery applies; the type of struggle just changes as the baby grows."

The first 12 months are a relentless, grueling series of quests. Just when you think you have mastered one level, a new variable emerges to plunge the household back into chaos. To understand the reality of child-rearing in contemporary Korea, we must look past the sugar-coated milestones and examine the exhaustion, the structural challenges, and the quiet resilience of parents fighting to stay afloat.

The First 100 Days: The Cycle of Exhaustion

The first three months are defined by a repetitive loop of eating, sleeping, and changing diapers. For new parents, the clock is no longer a tool for scheduling meetings, but a master that dictates every waking moment. The “nighttime feeding” is the primary thief of the new parent’s soul. In Korean parenting communities, it is considered a dangerous taboo to brag about a baby sleeping through the night—lest the infant decide to protest immediately that evening.

Scientifically, there is a reason for this madness: newborns do not produce melatonin, the hormone regulating sleep cycles, until they are roughly two to three months old. They are biologically incapable of distinguishing between day and night. Yet, countless parenting books pressure parents to implement "sleep training" during this period, often leaving mothers and fathers plagued by self-doubt. In reality, this is a time for surrender—accepting that the baby's rhythm is the only rhythm that matters.

The obsession with the "100-day miracle"—the myth that life becomes easy once the baby reaches the 100-day mark—often leads to "100-day collapse." Statistically, few babies sleep through the night at this stage. Those who do are the "unicorns" of the parenting world. The burden is compounded by the fact that society often blames parents, particularly mothers, for these natural physiological phases, framing a child’s sleep patterns as a reflection of the parent's "skill" rather than the child's development.

4 to 7 Months: The Risks of Exploration

Once a baby gains the ability to move, the household becomes a minefield. Around the four-month mark, babies enter the "oral stage," where everything—from television remotes to electrical cords—is subjected to a taste test. This is the era of the "Bephanten" ointment (a staple in Korean households for skin irritation) and the constant vigilance required to keep hazardous objects out of reach.

Then comes the "flipping hell." An infant who begins to roll over in their sleep can no longer be left unattended for a second. Parents find themselves glued to their home-security cameras, watching their baby’s breathing while suffering from extreme sleep deprivation. By the sixth or seventh month, as the baby begins to crawl, the risks increase. The frequent trips to the emergency room for minor bumps and bruises become a terrifyingly common ritual, turning physical development into a source of both pride and panic.

This is also the period where "stranger anxiety" kicks in. The baby begins to cling exclusively to the primary caregivers, effectively ending any "couple time" that might have remained. When combined with the pain of teething, the domestic environment can feel like a pressure cooker of helplessness.

8 to 11 Months: Navigating Structural Inequality

By the 8th or 9th month, the baby is a "tiny wild horse." As they learn to pull themselves up on furniture, the focus shifts to child-proofing. Every corner of the house becomes a target for padding, and parents often resort to extreme measures, sometimes even attaching protective foam to the baby’s own clothing.

However, this is also where structural inequality becomes apparent. The cost of child-proofing a home—buying high-quality safety gates, mats, and corner guards—is significant. For young couples living in rental properties (the *Jeonse* or monthly rent system common in Korea), making permanent changes to the home is often impossible. Parents are forced to gamble with their child’s safety because they cannot drill holes into walls to install safety gates or afford premium interior modifications. It is a stark reminder that child safety is often a matter of economic privilege.

As the baby enters the 10-month mark, the dynamic shifts again. They begin to understand simple commands and show early signs of personality. This "Little Einstein" phase brings the first real moments of reciprocal communication, providing a much-needed emotional reward for the months of struggle.

The One-Year Mark: The Cost of Celebration

Reaching the first birthday, or *Dol*, is a monumental achievement. In 2026, a standard celebration for 50 guests can cost between $2,200 and $3,700 (3 to 5 million KRW). While the era of extravagant, bank-breaking parties has faded in favor of "practical" events, the financial strain remains a heavy burden for households already struggling with reduced income due to childcare leave.

The irony is that as the child begins to walk, the "chase" begins. The independence the parents once craved arrives in the form of a toddler who runs headlong into danger, coupled with the "No!" phase, where the child asserts their ego by rejecting every request. Parents find themselves caught in a cycle of frustration and guilt, constantly questioning their own patience and methods.

What to Watch Next

As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung seeks to address the country’s demographic crisis, observers should watch how the government balances rhetoric with policy. While the state offers subsidies, critics argue these are a drop in the ocean compared to the actual costs of child-rearing.

Look for shifts in:

  • Public Childcare Support: Will the government move beyond cash transfers to provide more robust, physical safety infrastructure for young families?
  • Housing Policy: Watch for potential support programs specifically for young parents in rental housing who need, but cannot afford, child-proofing renovations.
  • Parental Mental Health: As the stigma around parenting struggles slowly shifts, look for whether public health initiatives will start treating parental burnout as a legitimate societal issue rather than a private failure.

The reality is that no policy will make the first year "easy." Parenting is, by nature, a grueling, transformative labor. The best that can be hoped for is a society that recognizes the magnitude of this work, shifting the focus from individual perfectionism to a collective effort to support those who are, day by day, building the next generation.
