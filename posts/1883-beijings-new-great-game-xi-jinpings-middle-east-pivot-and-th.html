Beijing’s New Great Game: Xi Jinping’s Middle East Pivot and the Challenge for Seoul

November 03, 2026

Imagine the Great Hall of the People in Beijing, a cavernous space where the air is thick with the scent of high-stakes diplomacy and the rigid protocol of the Chinese Communist Party. On one side of the table sits Xi Jinping, a leader increasingly comfortable in his role as a global architect. On the other, the Sultan of Oman, representing a nation that has long served as a quiet, neutral bridge in the volatile Middle East. This meeting—the first of its kind in nearly five decades—was not just a courtesy call. It was a signal flare to the world that the old order, dominated by Washington’s security umbrellas and petrodollar diplomacy, is being systematically challenged by a new "peace broker" from the East.

For those watching from the outside, China’s recent diplomatic flurry can look like a series of disconnected events: a handshake in Muscat, a cold shoulder to Berlin, and a defiant speech about economic control. But for the Lee Jae-myung administration in Seoul, these moves represent a tectonic shift in the geopolitical landscape. As China moves to secure the energy heartlands of the Middle East while simultaneously drawing a hard line against Western influence, South Korea finds itself at a crossroads. The era of "strategic ambiguity" is being replaced by a much more dangerous era of "strategic competition," where every energy deal in the Gulf and every diplomatic snub in Europe has direct consequences for the Korean economy and the security of the peninsula.

The Oman Pivot: Securing the Energy Heartland

The visit of the Sultan of Oman to Beijing marks a historic milestone—the first such state visit in 48 years of diplomatic relations. While the headlines focused on "strategic partnerships," the substance lies in the geography. Oman sits at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, overseeing the Strait of Hormuz, the world's most important oil transit chokepoint. For China, the world’s largest oil importer, Oman is not just a trade partner; it is a vital node in the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), Beijing's multi-trillion dollar infrastructure project designed to link China to the rest of the world.

During the signing ceremonies, Xi Jinping and the Sultan finalized agreements that move beyond simple oil-for-cash transactions. They are building a framework for long-term energy security and logistics hubs. By strengthening ties with Oman, China is effectively hedging against potential U.S. naval blockades in the Indo-Pacific. If the Malacca Strait were ever closed, a friendly hub in Oman provides China with a secondary route to the energy it needs to keep its massive industrial machine humming.

Furthermore, China is positioning itself as the "adult in the room" regarding Middle Eastern conflicts. Xi’s rhetoric is pointed: he argues that the Middle East should not be a "playground for great powers"—a thinly veiled jab at the decades of U.S. military intervention. By dispatching special envoys to mediate between the U.S. and Iran, China is attempting to claim the moral high ground. It wants to show the "Global South"—a term used for developing nations in Africa, Asia, and Latin America—that while Washington brings sanctions and carrier groups, Beijing brings investment and "non-interference." This "peace broker" frame is highly attractive to regional leaders who are tired of being caught in the crossfire of Western-led regime changes.

The Cold Shoulder: Berlin, Kyiv, and the Western Rift

The warmth shown to the Sultan of Oman stands in stark contrast to the icy reception given to Western leaders. A recent meeting between Xi Jinping and German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier serves as a perfect case study in the current "East-West" disconnect. According to observers, the atmosphere was one of palpable tension. When Steinmeier attempted to press Xi on the war in Ukraine, the Chinese leader’s response was characterized by a "frigid indifference."

The West, and Europe in particular, has been desperate for China to use its "no-limits partnership" with Russia to bring Vladimir Putin to the negotiating table. However, Xi has made it clear that he has little interest in playing the role of a mediator on Western terms. Reports following the meeting suggested that Xi explicitly stated he had "no interest" in discussing a resolution to the Ukraine war with either the U.S. or Russia at this time. To Beijing, the war in Ukraine is a European security problem caused by NATO expansion—a view that aligns closely with Moscow’s narrative.

This refusal to engage is a calculated risk. China knows that Europe is economically dependent on Chinese markets and supply chains. By withholding diplomatic cooperation on Ukraine, Beijing is signaling that Western cooperation on issues like trade and technology is a prerequisite for Chinese help on security. It is a game of "divide and rule" aimed at the transatlantic alliance. Xi is betting that, eventually, the economic pain of the war will force Europe to distance itself from Washington’s hardline stance on China in exchange for a return to "business as usual."

Internal Control: The "Party Leadership" Defense

While Xi projects strength abroad, he is also busy battening down the hatches at home. There is a growing chorus of criticism from Western economists and market analysts who argue that the Communist Party’s tightening grip is "killing the golden goose" of the Chinese economy. They point to the crackdown on tech giants, the debt-ridden property sector, and the exodus of foreign capital as evidence that state control is incompatible with modern growth.

Xi’s response has been a full-throated defense of the "Party’s Leadership." Far from backing down, the recent messaging from Beijing suggests that the Party believes *more* control, not less, is the answer to China’s economic woes. In official documents and state media, there has even been a curious retrospective admission that "Party leadership was once weakened"—a subtle but powerful critique of the more liberal eras of his predecessors. By framing past flexibility as a weakness, Xi is justifying his current 1-person rule as a necessary correction to save the nation.

For foreign investors, this is a chilling signal. It means that political stability and ideological purity will always take precedence over market efficiency. For South Korean companies like Samsung Electronics or SK Hynix, which have massive footprints in China, this translates to increased regulatory risk and a "China-first" industrial policy that could squeeze them out of the market. Xi is effectively saying that if you want to do business in the world's second-largest economy, you must play by the Party’s rules, regardless of how much they defy standard economic logic.

The Strategic Challenge for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

This shifting global dynamic places an immense burden on the current South Korean government. Under the leadership of President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), Seoul is attempting to pivot toward a more pragmatic, "national interest-first" diplomacy. This is a sharp departure from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently imprisoned. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, many critics argued that South Korea's foreign policy became overly tilted toward the U.S. and Japan, leading to a total breakdown in communication with Beijing and a loss of diplomatic leverage.

President Lee Jae-myung’s "Pragmatic Diplomacy" aims to restore that balance. However, as China becomes more assertive in the Middle East, the stakes for Korea's economy—which is heavily dependent on energy imports and manufacturing exports—have never been higher. If China successfully establishes itself as the primary power broker in the Gulf, it could potentially gain significant influence over the price and flow of the oil and gas that power Korean factories.

South Korea cannot afford to be a bystander in this "New Great Game." The challenge is two-fold:

  • Energy Autonomy: Korea must diversify its energy sources and strengthen its own ties with Middle Eastern nations like Oman, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE, ensuring it is not solely dependent on a landscape where Beijing holds the keys.
  • Technological Sovereignty: As China doubles down on state-led economic control, Korea must accelerate its efforts to lead in the "technologies of the future"—semiconductors, EV batteries, and AI—to maintain its bargaining power with both Washington and Beijing.

In the view of many analysts, the "biased diplomacy" of the previous administration left Korea vulnerable. Now, the Lee Jae-myung government must navigate a world where the U.S. and China are no longer just competing for trade, but for the very soul of the international order. It is a high-wire act where the cost of a single misstep could be measured in billions of dollars and years of economic stagnation.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the next quarter, keep a close eye on these concrete developments:

  • Oman-China Infrastructure Deals: Look for specific announcements regarding the Port of Duqm in Oman. If China secures long-term docking or logistics rights there, it marks a significant shift in Gulf security dynamics.
  • The "Global South" Summitry: Watch for Xi Jinping’s upcoming interactions with leaders from the BRICS nations (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa). This will be the primary venue for China to pitch its "alternative" to the Western-led financial system.
  • Seoul’s Trilateral Maneuvers: Monitor the Lee Jae-myung administration’s attempts to revive the Korea-Japan-China trilateral summit. A successful summit would be a major win for Lee’s pragmatic approach, signaling that Seoul can still act as a bridge even in a polarized world.
  • Chinese Domestic Policy Shifts: Pay attention to the "Two Sessions" (China's annual legislative meetings). Any further language strengthening "Party Leadership" over the private sector will be a warning sign for global markets.

The world is no longer a unipolar place where Washington’s word is law. Beijing is building its own web of influence, from the deserts of Oman to the boardrooms of state-owned enterprises. For South Korea, the goal is clear: survive and thrive in the gaps between these giants, using every ounce of its technological and diplomatic prowess to protect the national interest.
