The Midnight Scroller: Why South Korea’s President Is Stuck in an 'Algorithm Prison'

October 14, 2026

Picture the scene: It is 2:00 AM, then 4:00 AM, and finally dawn. Most world leaders are asleep, preparing for the day’s diplomatic cables or economic briefings. But inside the South Korean presidential office, the screen of a smartphone is glowing in the dark. President Lee Jae-myung is not reading intelligence reports; he is scrolling through X (formerly Twitter), blocking critics, and engaging in a relentless, real-time war of words with anonymous accounts. To many observers, this is no longer "direct communication"—it is a sign that the leader of a major economy is trapped in a digital feedback loop that threatens the stability of his administration.

The President’s Midnight Routine: A Window Into Distraction

On the night of October 10 and into the early hours of October 11, President Lee’s social media activity followed a rhythm that looked less like a head of state’s duties and more like that of an obsessive social media user. From 10:22 PM straight through to the next morning, the President was caught in a cycle of posting, responding, and blocking. By the time the sun began to rise at 6:00 AM, the President was still active, continuing his digital housekeeping well into the morning.

This is not merely a matter of a leader being "in touch with the people." Critics, including influential political commentators, have pointed out that this behavior is symptomatic of an "algorithm prison." By obsessively seeking out criticism to refute or block, the President is feeding an algorithm that only serves to alienate him further from mainstream public sentiment. When the highest authority in the land spends his precious hours arguing with commenters rather than overseeing the nation’s complex issues, the question must be asked: Who is actually running the country while the President is fighting in the mention tab?

"At this level of social media usage, it is a genuine addiction. It is impossible for a normal person—let alone a head of state—to function this way." — Political commentary on the President's digital habits.

Communication or Compartmentalization?

The presidential office has long championed these direct social media interactions as a way to dismantle authoritarian barriers and get closer to the public. However, the reality is far more concerning. Rather than fostering genuine dialogue, the practice has become a source of national embarrassment and political friction. When a President responds emotionally to every jab, he loses the gravitas required of his office. Moreover, there is a dangerous side effect: the "coordinate bombing" (a Korean term for organized, collective harassment) of private citizens by the President’s most ardent supporters. When the President highlights a specific critical post, his base often descends upon that individual account with vitriol, turning the leader's digital frustration into a tool for real-world bullying.

The situation is further complicated by the President's use of inflammatory slang—such as "jwa-ah-ha-ge" (a politically charged term implying a specific ideological bias)—which serves to deepen the country's already severe political polarization. Even sensitive, classified internal documents have occasionally been exposed through these hasty social media outbursts, leading to accusations of leaking official secrets.

The Paradox of the "Commoner's President"

President Lee Jae-myung rose to power on the promise of being a champion for the working class, frequently stating, "Lee Jae-myung will always be right in front of you, by your side." Yet, a stark disconnect has emerged between his populist rhetoric and his actions. While he is quick to target ordinary citizens or social media critics with the force of a "tank," he appears remarkably timid when facing the true centers of power, such as large conglomerates or entrenched interest groups.

Observers have noted that the President’s signature administrative force—once seen during his time as Governor of Gyeonggi Province, where he aggressively dismantled illegal structures—seems to have vanished when it comes to major reforms like real estate or tax restructuring. The public is left wondering: why is the government so "tough" on the average person on social media, yet so hesitant to take on the systemic cartels that actually shape the nation's economy?

A Sinking Ship and the 20-30 Voter Exodus

The political consequences of this behavior are becoming impossible to ignore. President Lee’s approval rating has sagged to 35%, with a 61% disapproval rate. Most alarmingly, the critical 20-30 age demographic—a group that traditionally holds diverse political views—has turned against him with near-unanimous intensity. Young voters are not merely disappointed; they are exhausted by a leader who spends his time lecturing them or blaming others on social media rather than providing substantive solutions to their housing and employment struggles.

The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal party) is now staring down the barrel of a crisis reminiscent of the late-term struggles under the Moon Jae-in government, when the party’s parliamentary seat count plummeted. As the Democratic Party loses ground, the Rebuilding Korea Party (a smaller progressive party) is seeing a rise in popularity, suggesting that the liberal bloc is not expanding, but rather tearing itself apart from within.

The Path Forward: What to Watch Next

The internal dynamics of the ruling bloc have become increasingly toxic. Kim Min-seok, the current leader of the Democratic Party, is viewed by many as being locked in a cold war with other key figures like Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party. To Kim, these figures are not partners in progress but "pebbles in his shoe," obstacles on his path to the next presidential election. As long as the ruling party treats potential allies with arrogance and focuses on internal purges rather than national policy, the risk of a political collapse only grows.

What should we watch next? Keep an eye on the upcoming parliamentary sessions and the President’s upcoming public appearances. If the administration continues to prioritize social media skirmishes over legislative reform, the gap between the Blue House and the public will only widen. True communication is not found in 280 characters at 3:00 AM; it is found in the ability to solve the grievances of a nation. It is time for President Lee to put down the phone and return to the work he was elected to do.
