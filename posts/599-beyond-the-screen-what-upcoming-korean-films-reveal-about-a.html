Beyond the Screen: What Upcoming Korean Films Reveal About a Stalled Generation

September 10, 2026

When we talk about cinema, it is easy to get lost in the polish of the production, the box-office projections, and the celebrity interviews. But lately, the South Korean film scene has been shifting its focus away from high-budget spectacle and toward something far more uncomfortable: the raw, messy business of survival. A recent discussion featuring directors and actors has brought this shift into sharp focus, revealing a collective anxiety that mirrors the lives of many young people living in Korea today.

From the streets of Gangnam, where the youth toil without hope of ever putting down roots, to dark comedies that poke at the deep, festering rifts between generations, these filmmakers are using the screen as a mirror. As I watched these creators strip away the promotional gloss, it became clear that they aren't just selling tickets—they are documenting a bitter, modern self-portrait of a society struggling to find its footing.

The Gangnam Outsiders: '97: Drifting'

Set to hit theaters on September 9, 2026, the upcoming film '97: Drifting' (directed by Jeong Ki-hyeok) tackles a subject that resonates deeply with the current zeitgeist. The film follows a group of young people born in 1997—a generation that has come of age during a period of intense economic stagnation. They commute into the heart of Gangnam, Seoul’s most affluent district, every morning, only to retreat to cramped, low-rent housing on the outskirts of the city at night. They are physically present in the center of wealth, yet entirely excluded from it.

Director Jeong notes that the film isn't just a story; it’s an observation of a generation shouldering the crushing weight of their parents' debt and an economy that feels like an impenetrable maze. One of the central characters, Hee-sook (played by Jung Eun-ji), embarks on a frantic, one-day journey from her hometown of Busan to Seoul to collect money. This journey acts as a metaphor for the broader youth experience: a breathless, exhausting scramble to secure basic survival in a city that treats them as disposable labor.

However, the film has sparked a debate about the "generational trap." Renowned director Lee Myung-se, offering a critique during the discussion, suggested that while the film captures the struggle, there is a risk in over-labeling characters by their birth year. "When we stop obsessing over defining a generation, we finally see the human face," he remarked. His point is a vital one: statistics and labels can flatten the human experience, but art should be concerned with the shaking eyes of an individual who doesn't know where to turn.

The Deep-Water Divide: Economic Disillusionment

The conversation inevitably turned to another project, 'Deep Water,' which aims to expose the raw nerves of generational conflict. The film’s creators point to the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis (often called the "IMF Crisis" in Korea) as a symbolic dividing line. Those who lived through it survived by sheer grit, but the younger generation faces a different wall: the sense that the "empty mountain" (an idiom for a land of opportunity) has already been claimed by the generation before them.

This has led to a bitter stalemate. The older generation asks, "Why are you complaining when you have a more prosperous foundation than we ever did?" while the youth retort, "We can't even breathe." The film highlights this through a poignant scene in a children's playground where a security guard scolds a young couple for smoking. The confrontation is trivial on the surface, but it represents a deeper, misplaced anger. The youth are seething at a world they cannot influence, and they are lashing out at targets that are often completely irrelevant to their actual suffering.

The directors behind these works are not offering grand, idealistic solutions. Instead, they are presenting a "micro-empathy"—the small, quiet acknowledgement that says, "I understand why you are hurting." In a society that demands success and constant forward motion, the simple act of acknowledging that the system is broken feels, in its own way, like a radical form of comfort.

The Paranoia of Modern Connection: 'The Drama'

Shifting from social realism to the surreal, the discussion also touched upon Kristoffer Borgli’s upcoming film, 'The Drama.' Following his acclaimed 'Dream Scenario,' which explored the bizarre phenomenon of appearing in others' dreams, Borgli’s new project takes a darker, more psychological turn.

The premise is terrifying: a couple, just one week away from marriage, discovers that one of them harbors a secret, dark past—specifically, they were a conspirator in a horrific terrorist plot. The film explores the resulting paranoia. If the person you love is capable of such a hidden, monstrous depth, what does that mean for the stability of your own life? It serves as a black comedy about the fragility of trust. In the age of digital transparency, we are often forced to realize that the person beside us is essentially a stranger, and our perception of "stability" is merely a house of cards.

What to Watch Next

As these films prepare for their releases, they invite us to look at the "mud" beneath the spotlight. Whether it is the economic drifting of the '97 generation or the psychological terror of hidden secrets, these stories are all asking the same fundamental questions: How do we survive when the ground is constantly shifting? And how can we truly connect with others when we are all busy protecting our own vulnerabilities?

Keep an eye on the box-office trends for '97: Drifting' this September. It will be a clear indicator of whether audiences are ready to embrace a mirror that reflects the unvarnished, often painful reality of life in modern Korea. Beyond the numbers, keep an eye on how the discourse around these films evolves—the conversations they spark in the comments sections and the critics’ circles may tell us more about the future of Korean society than any political poll.
