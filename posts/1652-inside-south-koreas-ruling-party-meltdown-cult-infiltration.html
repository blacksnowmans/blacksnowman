Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party Meltdown: Cult Infiltration Allegations, Factional Warfare, and Economic Discontent

October 29, 2026

On October 2, inside a closed-door meeting room at the Seoul headquarters of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), party leaders sat listening to a sensitive oral briefing. Park Sun-won, head of the DPK’s party membership maintenance task force, was sharing preliminary findings regarding a sensational rumor: that members of Shincheonji—a secretive, controversial fringe religious sect—had systematically infiltrated the party’s ranks. At the time, Park presented no concrete data, no official tallies, and no physical proof. The presentation consisted entirely of fragmented anecdotes already circulated by media outlets.

Yet within days, those unverified whispers spilled into public view, sparking a political firestorm that now threatens to consume President Lee Jae-myung’s ruling party from within. What began as a vague organizational inquiry rapidly transformed into an explosive public feud, complete with media leaks of tens of thousands of covert registrations, public accusations by party leadership, and fierce intra-party warfare. To understand why a ruling party with control of the presidency is tearing itself apart over an unproven cult conspiracy, one must look closely at the bitter factional dynamics and political pressures boiling beneath the surface in Seoul.

A Cult Infiltration Scandal Built on Phantom Evidence

In South Korean politics, allegations of ties to Shincheonji—a fringe religious movement notorious for secret proselytizing and past political controversies—are potent weapons. The current controversy traces back to the DPK’s August 17 national convention, when whispers emerged of a secret tripartite alliance between anti-Lee Jae-myung factions, party divisionists, and Shincheonji members. For weeks, the rumor drifted through political corridors without substantiation.

The situation exploded on October 13, when the conservative daily Maeil Business Newspaper published an exclusive report alleging that at least 30,000 Shincheonji adherents had secretly joined the DPK. Broadcast outlet TV Chosun quickly followed with reports that the influx exceeded 10,000 members, while CBS No Cut News floated rumors that party leadership was planning a full-scale audit of 120,000 members. On October 14, DPK Leader Kim Min-seok poured fuel on the fire during official party broadcasts, declaring publicly that mass enrollment had been confirmed and that testimony was accumulating.

However, a critical logical and legal contradiction lies at the heart of these assertions. To identify Shincheonji members within the DPK’s voter database, party officials would need access to an official, authenticated membership roster from Shincheonji itself. Obtaining such a list through law enforcement would violate Article 24 of South Korea’s Party Act and Article 23 of the Personal Information Protection Act. Furthermore, obtaining it directly from cult leadership would imply an absurd paradox: that the sect’s leadership chose to hand over confidential lists to Kim Min-seok to help him expose their own alleged covert deal with his internal rivals.

Crucially, past official investigations contradict the entire narrative. When a joint police-prosecution task force spent eight months deploying more than 40 investigators to probe illicit links between religious groups and political parties, their official findings were clear: organized, mass infiltration was confirmed only within the conservative People Power Party (PPP), the main opposition party. Investigators found zero evidence of systematic cult enrollment within the Democratic Party.

Internal Witch-Hunts and Weaponized Conspiracy Theories

If the factual foundation for the Shincheonji narrative is nonexistent, why has the ruling party leadership continued to fan the flames? The answer lies in internal power struggles and factional control. Lacking physical evidence, the scandal has shifted from a question of facts to a battle of political belief, used by party leadership to consolidate power and rally hardcore supporters against internal dissenters.

By repeatedly floating the "anti-Lee, factionalist, Shincheonji secret alliance" narrative, party leadership has sought to brand rival factions—most notably lawmakers aligned with Representative Jung Cheong-rae—as illegitimate infiltrators who must be purged. Rather than back down when pressed for proof, leadership figures have retreated behind vague excuses, claiming that releasing raw data publicly would expose the party to law enforcement investigations, and thus they must endure unfair criticism in silence to protect the institution.

This strategy has ignited fierce resistance within the party. Lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae took to social media to push back aggressively, arguing that if a secret alliance truly exists, it must be thoroughly investigated and punished—but if it does not, those spreading false conspiracies to divide the party must face severe accountability. Supreme Council member Han Min-soo further exposed the internal rift during an appearance on the political commentary show Maebul Show, revealing that Task Force head Park Sun-won’s October briefing materials were delivered secretly to Party Leader Kim Min-seok alone, bypassing other council members.

Widening Friction with the Blue House and Judicial Gridlock

The fallout from this self-inflicted crisis has severely damaged relations between party leadership and President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. Speaking on the political commentary channel Park Si-young TV, Representative Kim Young-hwan acknowledged mounting coldness from the presidential office (the Blue House) toward Kim Min-seok’s management of party affairs. Key party appointments have repeatedly bypassed the Senior Secretary for Political Affairs, while major presidential staff appointments have been communicated to party leadership mere hours before public release.

A stark illustration of this rift occurred during a recent law enforcement raid on Sisa Tapa, a pro-ruling party YouTube channel. Immediately following the search and seizure, figures close to President Lee distanced the administration from the outlet in press statements, pointedly directing responsibility toward Kim Min-seok’s camp. The incident highlighted a growing environment of distrust, where key factions reflexively disavow political fallout rather than coordinate strategy.

This internal friction comes at a crucial moment when the ruling party faces intense pressure from the opposition and ongoing battles with the judiciary. The opposition People Power Party has aggressively attacked the administration while shielding its own members; for example, PPP Representative Joo Jin-woo drew public outrage after refusing to apologize for allegations that he secured private golf lessons from professional golfer Lydia Ko, dismissing calls for accountability as "leftist framing."

At the same time, the DPK has struggled to mount a coherent judicial strategy. Rather than pushing forward with the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, floor spokesperson Jeon Eun-soo announced a shift toward a parliamentary investigation in early December, citing risks that an impeachment motion might be rejected by the Constitutional Court. Lawmaker Jung Sung-ho also signaled potential room for compromise if the Chief Justice respects presidential nominations for Supreme Court justices. This tactical retreat coincides with legislative battles over a bill to expand the Supreme Court by four justices annually up to 26 members—a major structural change that would also alter the composition of the National Election Commission (NEC), whose leadership is drawn from Supreme Court justices.

Plunging Approval Ratings, Economic Pain, and What to Watch Next

The ruling party’s preoccupation with internal feuds has extracted a heavy political toll. According to a landmark poll published by the Chosun Ilbo on October 28, 2026, approval for the ruling Democratic Party plummeted to 31.5%, while support for the opposition People Power Party rose to 40.5%. This nearly 9-percentage-point gap marks a historic reversal, leaving Party Leader Kim Min-seok far short of his pledge to boost DPK approval ratings by 10% within his first 100 days in office (a deadline falling on November 24).

While politicians in Yeouido—Seoul’s political district—trade accusations, ordinary citizens are suffering under severe economic strain. Veteran taxi drivers with over 30 years on the road report that street-level business feels worse today than during the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis or the COVID-19 pandemic, with nightlife districts emptying out early in the evening. Data from convenience store chain GS Retail reveals a tragic metric of daily life: between 2023 and June of this year, the largest demographic purchasing convenience store lunch boxes was not young students, but men in their 40s, followed by men in their 50s—working family breadwinners forced to cut meal costs amid relentless inflation.

Voter frustration is already reshaping the progressive political landscape. The rapid rise in support for the Rebuilding Korea Party (Cho Kuk Innovation Party) serves as a direct warning to the DPK that progressive voters are prepared to look for alternatives if the ruling party remains bogged down in internal factional purges.

What to Watch Next:

  • The November 24 Milestone: Watch whether Party Leader Kim Min-seok produces tangible evidence regarding Shincheonji or officially walks back his claims as his 100-day deadline arrives.
  • The December Parliamentary Probe: Monitor the scope and intensity of the National Assembly's December investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and judicial practices, especially during state budget negotiations.
  • Legislative Push on Supreme Court Expansion: Track the progression of the bill to expand Supreme Court justices to 26, which remains a core flashpoint between the ruling party and conservative opposition over control of election oversight.
  • Blue House Reshuffling and Party Relations: Keep a close eye on whether President Lee Jae-myung’s administration takes formal steps to reassert control over party-government coordination or distance itself further from DPK leadership.
