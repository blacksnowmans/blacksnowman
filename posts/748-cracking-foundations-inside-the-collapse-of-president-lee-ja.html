Cracking Foundations: Inside the Collapse of President Lee Jae-myung’s Progressive Base

October 06, 2026

In South Korean politics, approval ratings are never just numbers on a chart. They are the accumulated weight of public expectations, bitter disappointments, and, all too often, a deep sense of betrayal. Right now, that political current is thrashing wildly across local media, independent YouTube political commentary, and public opinion polls. When a cornerstone intellectual voice of the progressive movement publicly pulls their backing, it is not merely a piece of gossip—it is the first visible fracture in what was once an unshakeable coalition.

For international observers watching Seoul, South Korean politics often looks like a high-stakes pendulum swinging sharply between conservative and progressive administrations. But the real drama usually happens within the winning camp itself. When a progressive government comes to power, it brings together a coalition of union workers, reform-minded academics, civic activists, and everyday citizens who believe that electing the right leader will finally purge systemic corruption, reform the prosecutors' office, and level an unequal economy. When those promises stall, the heartbreak is uniquely intense.

The Defection of a Core Intellectual

The turning point came when Choe Bae-geun, a prominent progressive professor who helped rally intellectuals behind South Korea's current leader, Lee Jae-myung, publicly announced he was withdrawing his support. Speaking on a political YouTube broadcast, Choe took direct aim at veteran writer and commentator Rhyu Si-min, declaring that his faith in President Lee had been shattered. After private discussions with political insiders, Choe concluded that the actual content and goals of the administration's promised reforms had completely mutated.

"The president has changed. From my perspective, the president has changed," Choe stated bluntly, adding the painful admission: "So I feel like I was tricked."

The weight of this defection lies in Choe's past positioning. During the election campaigns, he was one of the loudest voices urging voters to trust Lee, famously vowing that if the administration failed to push through its core promises, he would be the very first person to criticize them. Now, watching the golden window for reform close shut without meaningful action, he felt compelled to issue a public apology to the voters he once persuaded. This is not the reflexive opposition of a conservative critic; it is the agonizing public break of an insider who feels the ideals he championed have been abandoned.

If such a critique had been leveled early in the administration's term, it would have drawn immediate, fierce retaliation from loyalist factions. Instead, it was met with a chilling public silence. Among the broader progressive base, a quiet consensus that voters were "tricked" has taken deep root. The sheer accumulation of disappointments has made it impossible to simply write off these defections as irresponsible grumbling.

Grim Numbers in the Polls

Public opinion surveys are ruthless barometers of reality, and recent data shows an administration losing its grip on the electorate. According to a weekly survey conducted by Realmeter in early October 2026, negative evaluations of the government skyrocketed to 59.8%, while positive ratings languished at a dismal 37.4%. Any hope of a temporary rebound has vanished, leaving the administration stuck in a downward slide.

Polls conducted via automated response systems (ARS) and live-interview telephone surveys by the polling agency Johap (Opinion Poll Flower) paint an equally grim picture. Most alarmingly, the bedrock of the Democratic Party's support—voters who select the "strongly approve" option—shrunk to just 18.7%. While this is a slight technical bounce from a historic low in the 15% range, it signals that the psychological tether binding the core base to the leadership has nearly snapped. In party stronghold regions and among traditional progressive demographics, the alarms are no longer blinking—they are blaring.

Party dynamics have shifted in tandem. In traditional telephone interviews, the gap between the ruling Democratic Party and the conservative opposition People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party, historically tied to past industrial and authoritarian legacies) has noticeably narrowed. In certain ARS surveys, the conservative opposition has even managed to edge ahead. Yet, the presidential office and party leadership have largely responded with sluggish diagnostic meetings, seemingly detached from the speed at which their political capital is evaporating.

Echo Chambers and the Limits of Digital Governance

Part of the disconnect stems from how the leadership interacts with the public. President Lee continues to rely heavily on social media, frequently retweeting clips and commentary from loyalist figures like Rhyu Si-min and former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. For ordinary citizens who voted for systemic change, watching the executive branch retreat into a digital echo chamber while the economy and political landscape sour is deeply alienating.

When leadership relies on a few curated tweets to weather a political storm, it communicates arrogance rather than strategy. Voters who once gritted their teeth and argued that "we must protect the party at all costs" are now asking a much darker question: *If this administration compromises everything we fought for, what is left of our movement?*

In online chat rooms and political forums, everyday citizens have begun expressing a bitter sense of fatalism. Warnings from regional strongholds—such as the vital progressive heartland of Honam (the southwestern Jeolla provinces, historically the bedrock of liberal and democratic voting power in South Korea)—suggest that if local sentiment turns permanently against the leadership, the political damage will be absolute. Rather than calling for mass defections, many grassroots voices are instead grappling with a painful dilemma: how to salvage their political identity without falling into despair or blindly enabling policies that have lost their way.

The Approaching Political Gauntlet

The timing could not be worse. South Korea's political calendar is about to enter one of its most punishing phases: the annual parliamentary audit of government operations. During this period, opposition lawmakers will have a magnified platform to dissect every administrative misstep, budgetary failure, and policy reversal.

Yet, there is little sign that the ruling camp has unified its strategy to face the onslaught. Time is no longer on the administration's side. Choe Bae-geun’s public withdrawal of support and the plummeting approval metrics are the final warning shots. If the executive branch continues to lean on institutional inertia and dismisses these fractures as mere noise, the political reckoning that follows will be swift and unforgiving.

Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Prominent progressive intellectual Choe Bae-geun publicly withdrew his support, symbolizing deep internal disillusionment within the ruling camp.
  • Major tracking polls, including Realmeter and Opinion Poll Flower, show negative ratings hitting highs near 60% while positive evaluations slide.
  • An insular digital communication strategy and a failure to deliver on core reforms threaten to fracture the wider progressive coalition ahead of critical parliamentary audits.

Whether this moment marks a catastrophic collapse of South Korea's progressive coalition or simply the agonizing birth pains of a necessary political reckoning remains to be seen. But the illusion of invincibility has officially been broken.
