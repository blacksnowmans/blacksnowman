Factional Feuds, Radical Deregulation Demands, and an Anxious Electorate: Inside South Korea’s Property Policy Battle

November 12, 2026

Imagine living in the Seoul metropolitan area as an average tenant today. Over recent years, the traditional jeonse system—South Korea’s long-standing housing lease arrangement where renters deposit a large refundable lump sum instead of paying monthly rent—has been steadily vanishing. In its place, steep monthly rental charges are eating away at middle-class budgets. Meanwhile, across the capital in the halls of the National Assembly, lawmakers are locking horns over real estate taxation, mortgage caps, and redevelopment penalties.

On November 11, the simmering tension surrounding South Korea's real estate policy exploded into full public view. A single day of intense political maneuvering exposed deep ideological fissures within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), while the primary conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), launched an aggressive counteroffensive demanding sweeping deregulation. With pivotal parliamentary elections on the horizon, the nation's housing debate has escalated from an economic challenge into a high-stakes political battle where housing security risks being overshadowed by election math.

Frictions Within the Ruling Party: Pragmatism Versus Ideology in the Seoul Capital Area

Inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the center-left party aligned with President Lee Jae-myung’s administration—anxiety is mounting among lawmakers representing Seoul and its surrounding suburban districts. A group of 16 DPK metropolitan legislators recently established the "Real Estate Pragmatism Group," spearheaded by Representative Lee Un-ju, seeking to steer the party away from rigid regulatory doctrine.

During a seminar held on November 11, members of this pragmatist group argued that the government must shift its core focus from suppressing home prices to restoring order in the rental market. Pointing to the severity of the capital region's lease crisis, attendees floated proposals such as utilizing housing pensions (reverse mortgages) to expand rental supply. The initiative reflected a growing consensus that traditional regulation-heavy policies have failed to repair the broken housing ladder. However, internal friction was plainly visible: despite 16 lawmakers signing onto the caucus, only four actually attended the seminar—a clear sign that many were hesitant to publicly highlight policy discrepancies with the presidential office and party leadership.

The internal divide within the DPK is particularly sharp regarding proposed revisions to the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax, a national tax levied on high-value property owners. Party members are split over whether to extend a 1.4 billion KRW (approximately $1.05 million) tax exemption threshold to single-home owners who do not actually reside in the property they own. While pragmatic lawmakers argue that tax relief is needed to protect accidental or long-term owners from excessive burdens, party hardliners warn that granting tax breaks to non-resident owners sends a dangerous signal to speculative buyers.

Adding fuel to the debate, a report by the daily newspaper Kyunghyang Shinmun revealed that if tax exemptions for non-resident single-home owners were enacted, roughly 85 percent of the financial benefits would flow to owners in four affluent Seoul districts collectively known as "Gang-Ma-Yong-Seong"—an acronym for Gangnam, Mapo, Yongsan, and Seongdong. Opponents inside the party point out that channeling tax relief primarily to wealthy urban enclaves directly contradicts the Lee Jae-myung administration's central promise to curb asset inequality.

The Opposition Strikes Back: Deregulation Demands and High-Profile Critics

Capitalizing on the ruling party’s internal hesitation, the opposition People Power Party (PPP) launched a coordinated campaign to dismantle the government’s real estate policy framework. The PPP invited Choi Kyoung-hwan, a former Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Economy and Finance from a previous conservative administration—famously remembered for his past "borrow money to buy homes" stimulus stance—to deliver a keynote address at a National Assembly policy seminar.

Choi launched a scathing attack on the administration's real estate agenda, branding current rules "crazy regulations" and declaring that no government can ultimately defeat market forces. Choi focused his critique on strict borrowing limits, specifically highlighting the 40 percent Loan-to-Value (LTV) mortgage ceiling. He used a sharp comparison to make his point, questioning why citizens can buy automobiles on installment credit while being forced to buy homes almost entirely with cash. He urged an immediate loosening of credit rules to allow genuine home-seekers back into the market.

Concurrently, PPP Representative Ahn Cheol-soo targeted the Reconstruction Excess Profit Recapture System, a statutory mechanism that levies clawback fees on financial gains generated by apartment redevelopment projects. Describing the fee as taking "blood money" from property owners, Ahn highlighted what he identified as a core policy contradiction: while government officials warn of potential drops in home prices, the government is simultaneously attempting to collect nearly 2 trillion KRW (approximately $1.5 billion) in clawback fees on anticipated redevelopment profits. Ahn argued that if property values fall as warned, no excess profits will exist, rendering the tax logically unsound and a major hindrance to urban housing supply.

Reinforcing the opposition’s assault, PPP Representative Jeong Jeom-sig dismissed the ruling party’s policy debates as mere political theater. Jeong argued that DPK lawmakers are staging fake calls for reform simply to survive the upcoming general elections, misguiding voters into believing property rules will change while punitive taxation and strict lending constraints remain fully intact.

The Presidential Mandate and the Democratic Party's Balancing Act

Faced with growing opposition attacks and public dissatisfaction over housing stability, President Lee Jae-myung has stepped in to steady the ruling coalition. President Lee hosted a dinner at the presidential office with senior DPK leaders, including Representatives Kim Min-seok and Han Byeong-do, where he explicitly called for unity, stressing that the party and the government must act with "one mind."

The presidential message reflected deep concern that internal mixed signals could be interpreted by the market as policy failure, undermining the administration's overall governing momentum. President Lee reaffirmed that reducing economic polarization and securing stable housing for middle- and lower-income households remain top national priorities, calling for firm adherence to core policy principles. Following the dinner, DPK leadership publicly aligned with the presidential office, emphasizing the necessity of comprehensive measures to address wealth inequality.

To manage the policy balance, the Democratic Research Institute—the DPK’s official policy think tank—has activated a dedicated "Real Estate Task Force." This task force has been tasked with reconciling internal party disagreements over tax policy, including potential fine-tuning of the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax, while drafting practical measures to relieve side effects stemming from recent lease legislation that disrupted the rental market.

Nevertheless, the ruling party faces a persistent political dilemma. Lawmakers running in competitive metropolitan districts feel intense pressure from constituents to lower tax burdens and ease mortgage rules. Conversely, the presidential office and party ideologues remain wary of making concessions that could spark renewed real estate speculation or alienate their core progressive base.

What to Watch Next

With general elections drawing near, South Korea’s real estate policy will serve as a bellwether for voter sentiment. To understand where the market and regulations are headed next, keep an eye on these concrete developments:

  • The DPK Task Force’s Final Tax Compromise: Watch whether the Democratic Research Institute's task force endorses the proposed 1.4 billion KRW ($1.05 million) Comprehensive Real Estate Tax exemption for non-resident single-home owners, or if concerns regarding asset concentration in Seoul's "Gang-Ma-Yong-Seong" districts lead to the proposal being scrapped.
  • Legislative Debates on Mortgage Caps (LTV): Track whether the government or ruling party offers targeted loan-to-value exemptions for first-time buyers and young households in response to the conservative opposition's push to raise the 40 percent LTV limit.
  • Legislative Proposals on Redevelopment Fees: Monitor proceedings in the National Assembly regarding the 2 trillion KRW ($1.5 billion) Reconstruction Excess Profit Recapture System. Any legislative momentum to suspend or modify these fees could immediately impact stalled apartment redevelopment projects across Seoul.
  • Polling Trends in Metropolitan Swing Districts: Pay attention to public opinion shifts in competitive Seoul and Gyeonggi Province constituencies. How suburban renters and property owners respond to the upcoming tax and lease policy adjustments will likely dictate whether the government maintains its regulatory posture or moves toward pragmatic market relief.
