The Trap of Relentless Comparison: Why South Korea Is Turning to Hermann Hesse to Survive Midlife Burnout

October 18, 2026

Picture an office worker in Seoul in their late thirties or early forties, riding the subway home late at night. Their smartphone screen flickers with wedding invitations celebrating spacious new apartments, promotions at major conglomerates, and curated snapshots of effortless luxury. In a hyper-competitive society that demands continuous upward mobility, a sudden setback—a corporate layoff, an investment loss, or a dissolved partnership—does not merely register as a professional hiccup. It lands with the weight of an existential eviction notice.

South Korea’s postwar economic miracle was built on sheer, unyielding acceleration. Yet in that race to the top, society forgot to teach its citizens a crucial survival skill: how to breathe when the ground beneath your feet suddenly gives way. Today, as economic headwinds, volatile asset markets, and rigid social expectations collide, a growing number of Koreans are pausing to ask what remains when the scaffolding of prestige falls away. In doing so, they are increasingly seeking solace not in contemporary self-help manuals promising ten-step formulas to wealth, but in timeless literature—most notably Hermann Hesse’s classic novel Siddhartha.

The Riverbank Dilemma: Aging and the Lost Right to Fail

In Hesse’s masterpiece, the titular seeker tastes the heights of ascetic discipline, the pinnacle of merchant wealth, and the intoxicating pleasures of worldly romance. Yet in the second half of the book, drenched in disillusionment, Siddhartha finds himself standing at the edge of a deep river. He catches his reflection: his youthful vigor has evaporated, his hair has turned half-gray, and every worldly possession has slipped through his fingers. He discovers that he must start over from absolute zero—not as a promising youth, but as a weakened man stripped of everything.

For an adult in South Korea who has crossed thirty or forty, this passage strikes an uncomfortably familiar nerve. In youth, stumbles are tolerated; a scraped knee is viewed as a necessary rite of passage. But once an adult dons the rigid armor of professional titles and societal expectations, Korean society historically revokes what could be called the "right to fail." A single misstep in career trajectory or financial speculation triggers a visceral dread of falling into an irreversible abyss.

The terror of starting over in adulthood is exacerbated by che-myeon, the deep-seated cultural obsession with social face and the gaze of the community. In a culture that expects people to meet rigid milestone targets by specific ages—securing employment at a top firm, acquiring property, raising children—returning to zero can feel like public humiliation. Hesse’s protagonist, however, exposes the fatal flaw in this psychological prison: the realization that adulthood is often an elaborate, exhausting roleplay where fear is manufactured by our own attachment to social illusions.

Dismantling Catastrophe: Finding Freedom at Rock Bottom

What makes Siddhartha’s posture at the riverbank extraordinary is what happens immediately after the shock of ruin. Instead of surrendering to sheer despair, he experiences an unexpected lightness—a peculiar, liberating joy. Having lost every title, every coin, and every intellectual vanity, he is returned to the state of a naked child standing under the sun. He possesses nothing, knows nothing, and can do nothing. Paradoxically, that very vacuum means that the slate is completely clean.

Human panic thrives on ambiguity. When an individual confronts financial or occupational collapse, they tend to bundle their grief into a monolithic catastrophe, believing that their entire life has been obliterated. But when that suffocating dread is dismantled into concrete components, the reality is far more specific: a bank balance has dwindled, or a business card has lost its title. The fundamental realities of existence—the ground underfoot, the sun overhead, and the capacity to breathe and choose—remain intact.

Reaching the genuine bottom of life often yields an odd gift: you can no longer fall. Stripped of pretense, an individual is liberated from the endless performance required by social status. Relearning to see oneself not as a failed master, but as a beginner who has re-entered the world with fresh eyes, transforms an apparent ending into an open horizon.

The Myth of Pure Merit: Dissecting the Anatomy of Success

Much of the anxiety plaguing professionals stems from the toxic mythology of meritocracy. Self-styled lifestyle gurus and corporate cheerleaders preach that success is solely a product of iron discipline, relentless effort, and personal genius. But this narrative is not merely incomplete; it is deeply deceptive.

In reality, the formula for achievement is composed of variables that no single human being can fully orchestrate:

  • Innate talent and temperament: Biological and intellectual predispositions distributed unevenly at birth through the genetic lottery.
  • Effort: Grueling, focused labor, which nonetheless misses the mark if misdirected.
  • External support: Mentors, colleagues, family, and networks whose quiet contributions make individual elevation possible.
  • Timing and macroeconomic fortune: The unpredictable winds of history, demographic shifts, geopolitical shocks, and market fluctuations.

Even the most talented professional can find their career derailed by broad macroeconomic tides, unexpected supply chain disruptions, or global conflicts like the war in Ukraine. Korean market analysts routinely highlight data showing that in volatile entrepreneurial landscapes, only roughly 30 to 40 percent of outcomes stem from controllable inputs; the remaining majority is governed by timing and systemic conditions. Acknowledging that luck and timing play a decisive role is not an excuse for passivity; rather, it is an essential antidote to arrogance in good times and crippling self-blame in hard times.

Shedding the Complex: Authenticity Beyond the Gaze of Others

Those who achieve enduring inner strength share one defining characteristic: they are entirely free from what Koreans call an "inferiority complex" (complex) toward their peers. They do not lose sleep coveting a colleague’s windfall, nor do they rush to minimize another person’s achievement with bitter cynicism.

Envy is an energy black hole. A person who expends mental resources dissecting why someone else got ahead inevitably starves their own life of creative focus. In South Korean cultural history, transformative creators—such as acclaimed filmmaker Lee Chang-dong or legendary actors and civil figures like Moon Sung-keun—left enduring legacies precisely because they refused to tailor their work to commercial mimicry or petty rivalries. They anchored their focus on their own creative questions.

To accept the specific, imperfect hand that life has dealt you without glancing nervously at your neighbor’s cards is the ultimate form of courage.

This dynamic mirrors South Korea's broader socio-political journey. The country's democratic trajectory, rooted in the progressive governance ideals of former presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in, faced severe institutional turbulence during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose term was cut short by impeachment and subsequent imprisonment. Now, under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea is once again navigating the arduous task of democratic consolidation and institutional repair. Societies, like individuals, do not advance along neat, unblemished trajectories; they stumble, regress, and must consciously reset their footing.

What to Watch Next: The Changing Metrics of Midlife in Korea

The growing resonance of philosophical reflections like Hesse's Siddhartha points to an impending cultural recalibration in South Korea. Over the coming years, several structural and social markers will indicate whether this introspection translates into lasting societal shifts:

  • Second-Chance Entrepreneurship: Watch whether the Lee Jae-myung administration and legislative leaders roll out more robust bankruptcy rehabilitation frameworks and credit-reset mechanisms for failed business owners, reducing the punitive social cost of entrepreneurial failure.
  • Alternative Career Trajectories in Demographics: Track labor statistics for workers in their thirties and forties leaving traditional chaebol (family-run conglomerate) paths to pursue localized micro-enterprises or creative fields outside metropolitan Seoul.
  • Cultural Discourses on Comparison: Observe shifts in social media usage and cultural commentary among millennials and Generation X, particularly whether public pushback continues to mount against display-driven consumption and rigid lifestyle benchmarking.

Standing on the riverbank with graying hair is not an admission of total defeat; it is an invitation to shed unearned burdens. The river does not rush to prove itself to the banks it carves, nor does it lament the water that flowed before it. For a society grappling with the exhaustion of ceaseless comparison, learning to hear that steady, indifferent current may be the first real step toward peace.
