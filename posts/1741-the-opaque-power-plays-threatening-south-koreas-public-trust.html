The Opaque Power Plays Threatening South Korea’s Public Trust

October 30, 2026

Imagine a national election where the date is set, but the rules of engagement are kept in a drawer. Imagine a voter bloc of 20,000 people whose identities are known only to the incumbents. This is not the plot of a political thriller; it is the current state of the Korea Football Association (KFA), the governing body for the nation’s most beloved sport. On December 18, the KFA is scheduled to hold an election for its next president, yet the process has been branded a "black-out election" by fans and insiders alike. What should be a moment of renewal for South Korean football is instead being viewed as a calculated maneuver to secure a fourth term for the incumbent chairman, Chung Mong-gyu.

For those outside the peninsula, the drama surrounding a sports association might seem like a niche concern. However, in South Korea, the KFA’s administrative paralysis is a microcosm of a much larger struggle. It reflects a growing frustration with how public systems—from sports to the judiciary—are being "privatized" by entrenched cartels. The lack of transparency in the football world mirrors a broader pattern where those in power rewrite the rules to suit their survival, leaving the public to wonder if the concepts of "fairness" and "common sense" have become mere slogans for the elite.

A Closed-Door Coronation at the Korea Football Association

The KFA’s announcement of the December 18 election date was met with immediate backlash. In any healthy democratic organization, an election is preceded by a clear set of protocols: candidate qualifications are finalized, independent election committees are formed, and the voter registry is made public. The KFA, however, appears to have skipped these essential steps. By rushing the schedule without clarifying the rules, critics argue the association has effectively locked the door against any outside challengers who might have sought to reform the organization.

While the KFA has boasted about expanding the electorate to 20,000 people to give a veneer of inclusivity, the reality is far more opaque. No one outside the inner circle knows who these 20,000 voters are or how they were selected. This has led to accusations of a "hand-picked" election, where the ground has been tilted so steeply in favor of the status quo that a fair contest is impossible. Insiders suggest that while the public remains in the dark, those loyal to Chairman Chung Mong-gyu have been touring the country for months, securing support and consolidating their hold on the electorate.

The frustration is compounded by the KFA’s recent record of leadership. Under the current administration, the national team’s performance and the association’s administrative competence have faced intense scrutiny. Rather than protecting young players from public criticism or taking responsibility for organizational failures, the leadership has often retreated into silence or shifted blame onto individuals. This lack of accountability was on full display during recent parliamentary audits, where Chairman Chung—a member of the influential Hyundai family and head of the HDC Group—consistently failed to appear, showing what many see as a blatant disregard for public oversight.

This culture of impunity is not unique to football. It echoes the corruption recently uncovered within the Korean Sport & Olympic Committee (KSOC), the umbrella organization for all national sports bodies. The government’s Office for Government Policy Coordination recently suspended KSOC President Lee Kee-heung following allegations of illegal hiring practices and embezzlement. Yet, in a bizarre display of "self-review," a commission composed of Lee’s own appointees approved his bid for a third term. This "cartel" structure, where incumbents appoint their own judges to approve their re-elections, is the very rot that football fans fear has consumed the KFA.

The Erosion of Judicial Speed and the Shadow of Selective Justice

If the sports world is struggling with internal cartels, the South Korean judiciary is facing a crisis of confidence of its own. When Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae took office, he promised to solve the chronic problem of "trial delays"—the phenomenon where cases languish for years, effectively denying justice to the parties involved. However, his tenure has seen the Supreme Court’s en banc sessions (where all justices deliberate on major cases) slow to a crawl. In 34 months, only 31 such cases have been decided—less than one per month.

For the average citizen, a delayed trial is a form of judicial violence. For the powerful, however, it can be a convenient "freezer" where sensitive political cases go to be forgotten. Critics argue that the Supreme Court has used this en banc process to avoid making difficult decisions on cases involving high-ranking officials. By dragging out the clock, the judiciary allows political risks to dissipate, effectively granting a "pardon by procrastination." This stands in stark contrast to the swiftness of justice seen in cases involving ordinary citizens or political opponents of the previous administration.

The sense of "selective justice" is further sharpened by recent rulings that seem to defy common legal intuition. A prominent example involves Kim Gi-hyeon, a high-ranking member of the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition. During the party’s leadership race, allegations surfaced regarding the acceptance of a luxury Roger Vivier clutch bag, valued at approximately $1,900 (2.67 million KRW). While the court found his wife guilty and fined her about $3,500 (5 million KRW), it acquitted Kim Gi-hyeon himself, arguing there was no evidence of conspiracy between the husband and wife. To many observers, the idea that a high-profile politician would be unaware of such a gift—and the political influence it bought—strains the limits of belief.

This judicial leniency toward the elite creates a dangerous precedent. It suggests that if you are powerful enough, the law will find a way to distinguish your actions from those of your closest associates, even when the benefits of those actions accrue directly to you. This "asymmetric yardstick" is precisely what the public marched against during the candlelight vigils of the past decade.

Absolving the Unthinkable: The Martial Law Acquittals

Perhaps the most shocking recent development in the South Korean legal landscape is the acquittal of eight military officers involved in the failed martial law attempt on December 3, which occurred during the turbulent final months of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in power. The court’s reasoning for the acquittal has left legal experts and the public in a state of disbelief. The defendants were accused of participating in a plot to subvert the constitutional order, including plans to forcibly detain and interrogate officials from the National Election Commission.

The evidence presented was harrowing. On the day of the attempt, the then-Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun reportedly handed out about $1,400 (2 million KRW) in "activity funds" and told subordinates to "crush everything with tanks." The group later met at a Lotteria fast-food restaurant to finalize their plans, arming themselves with baseball bats, hatchets, cable ties, masks, and ropes. These are not the tools of administrative oversight; they are the tools of an insurrection.

However, the Seoul Central District Court acquitted the officers, ruling that because the appointments were sudden, they might not have fully recognized the "insurrectionary intent" of their mission. The court seemingly accepted the defense that a crowded, noisy fast-food joint was simply a convenient place to meet to avoid eavesdropping, rather than a clandestine meeting spot for a coup. By narrowing the definition of insurrection to such a degree, the court has effectively granted a "get out of jail free" card to those who were ready to use violence against the state’s own institutions.

This ruling is a grim reminder of the fragility of democracy. When the courts refuse to punish those who attempt to dismantle the constitutional order with "axes and ropes," they embolden future actors to try again. It suggests that as long as one can claim a lack of "subjective intent," the objective act of preparing for state-sponsored violence can be overlooked. It is a far cry from the judicial courage that once led to the nighttime assembly ban being declared unconstitutional—a victory for civil rights led by figures like Park Joo-min, a lawyer-turned-politician in the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), who famously fought for the people’s right to protest after dark.

The Entanglement of Local Power and Private Interests

The pattern of opaque administration and judicial leniency extends down to the municipal level, most notably in Seoul. Mayor Oh Se-hoon has faced persistent criticism over his "Han River Bus" project, a high-stakes ferry initiative that critics say is riddled with favoritism toward private cruise operators. The project involves massive public spending to build piers and terminals that will house lucrative commercial spaces like cafes and convenience stores, leading to allegations that public funds are being used to generate private rental income for well-connected interests.

The legal troubles for the Mayor go beyond policy criticism. He is currently under investigation for allegedly making false statements during a City Council meeting regarding the reasons why a state audit was dismissed. Under South Korean election law, making false claims to defend one's record can be a serious offense. Furthermore, the "Myung Tae-kyun Gate"—a scandal involving a political fixer who allegedly manipulated polls—has cast a shadow over the Mayor’s past political funding. While an appeals court recently acquitted him of some charges, the prosecution has appealed to the Supreme Court, arguing that the lower court ignored critical recordings and objective timelines that contradict the Mayor’s defense.

These cases highlight a recurring theme in contemporary South Korean governance: the blurring of lines between public duty and private benefit. Whether it is a football chairman avoiding a parliamentary audit, a military officer carrying a hatchet to a fast-food meeting, or a mayor pushing a controversial ferry project, the common thread is a sense that the rules are negotiable for those within the "cartel."

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will be a stress test for South Korea’s public institutions. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The KFA Election (December 18): Watch for whether any external candidates manage to break through the opaque registration process. If the election proceeds as a "coronation" for Chung Mong-gyu, expect a massive escalation in fan protests and potential legislative intervention to reform the National Sports Promotion Act.
  • The Supreme Court’s Pace: Under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the speed—or lack thereof—of en banc decisions will be a primary indicator of judicial independence. Specifically, watch for the scheduling of cases involving high-ranking officials and the "Martial Law" appeals.
  • The Martial Law Appeals: The prosecution is expected to appeal the Lotteria acquittals. How the higher courts handle the definition of "insurrectionary intent" will determine the legal deterrent against future threats to the constitutional order.
  • Seoul’s "Han River Bus" Audit: As the project nears its launch, keep an eye on the City Council’s investigations and the ongoing police probe into Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s statements. This will be a major flashpoint between the ruling DPK and the opposition PPP.

South Korea’s democracy was built on the principle that no one is above the law and that every contest—on the field or at the ballot box—should be fair. As these "black-out" elections and "selective" court rulings continue to dominate the headlines, the struggle to reclaim that fairness is becoming the defining political battle of the current era.
