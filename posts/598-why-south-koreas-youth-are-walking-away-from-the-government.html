Why South Korea's Youth Are Walking Away From the Government

September 10, 2026

For weeks, political insiders in Seoul have watched a steady, bruising slide in approval ratings that has defied conventional political recovery playbooks. What began as a minor dip has stretched into an eight-week downward spiral, prompting urgent panel discussions across the country's political commentary circuit. Gathered to dissect the slump were figures including Lee Se-mi, a city councilor from Seongnam; Han Ga-son, head of the gender equality committee for the Rebuilding Korea Party; journalist Kim Dae-ho; and editorial writer Kim Seung-hyun. Their conclusion cuts deeper than standard partisan fatigue: the disillusionment is not merely about opposing a political party, but about a fundamental, creeping sense of betrayal among voters who once expected far more from the current administration.

The Erosion of Core Support and the Rise of Political Apathy

The decline in political support starts with a quiet form of detachment. For a long time, progressive administrations in South Korea could rely on a dedicated vanguard of young, digitally active supporters who would watch presidential overseas trips, study policy briefings, and aggressively defend the government online against hostile media coverage. According to editorial writer Kim Seung-hyun, those defenders have largely put down their armor.

When politics bleeds out of the domain of hardcore partisans and into the daily lives of low-engagement voters who mutter, "They promised results, but it's all talk," a massive invisible wall forms between the state and the public. Seongnam Councilor Lee Se-mi frames the core crisis around a simple, painful question: "Is this really the government we hoped for?" As the initial excitement fades, even those who once spent their free time arguing politics online are logging off, exhausted by a widening gap between campaign ideals and governing reality.

Yet for many politically disengaged young adults, grand ideological debates—such as prosecutorial reform or intra-party feuds—have always felt distant. The breaking point, as Rebuilding Korea Party committee head Han Ga-son points out, is far more direct: money. Many young people dove into the stock market using aggressive loans—a phenomenon colloquially known as "gathering every last won"—after watching traditional real estate prices soar beyond reach. When the stock market turned sour, the political calculation evaporated overnight. Staring at plunging portfolio balances and red numbers in their banking apps, voters have little appetite for complex defense mechanisms of ruling party policy.

Policy friction has compounded the frustration. When the labor union at Samsung Electronics clashed over a performance bonus dispute—involving roughly 600 million won (about $440,000)—government ministers stepped in to mediate. Young progressive voters, who had anticipated staunchly pro-labor policies like shorter workweeks or loosened strike restrictions, watched with unease as the state intervened to avert massive corporate losses. When a government seen as an ally of labor appears to side with industrial conglomerates to protect the broader economy, its core supporters perceive a jarring shift in ideological consistency.

The Cruel Arithmetic of Seoul's Rental Market

Beyond abstract economic policies, the brutal realities of urban survival present an even more immediate crisis. On paper, the average monthly rent for a one-room apartment in Seoul hovers around 640,000 to 650,000 won (about $470 to $480). But aggregate city-wide averages conceal severe disparities: rents in upscale districts like Gangnam average 940,000 won ($690), Mapo sits at 880,000 won ($650), and Yongsan at 870,000 won ($640), while even university neighborhoods clear 625,000 won ($460) before utilities.

Compounding this are hidden costs. Landlords frequently lower nominal rents while inflating maintenance fees to 150,000 or 200,000 won ($110 to $150) a month, effectively driving real housing expenses past the million-won mark. State-backed public housing offers little relief. A typical public housing unit requiring a 100 million won ($73,000) deposit alongside a 600,000 won ($440) monthly rent, once loan interest and fees are factored in, frequently demands over 1 million won ($730) a month—leaving it barely cheaper than commercial rentals.

For young adults aged 25 to 29, the median after-tax monthly income sits at approximately 2.59 million won ($1,900). Spending nearly half of that income strictly on housing and utility maintenance breeds a profound sense of paralysis. The dream of starting a newlywed life in a modest, one-room apartment of 40 square meters collides with the reality of paying a million won a month for a cramped six-pyeong (20-square-meter) studio. When housing policies are so restricted in supply and access that winning a lease feels like winning a lottery, they cease to function as a social safety net.

This squeeze does not stop with the younger generation; it places an unbearable burden on their parents. Households in their 40s and 50s are caught in a financial vise, facing average parental medical expenses of 940,000 won ($690) alongside elderly care costs ranging from 1.5 million to 2 million won ($1,100 to $1,470) a month. When these families must also subsidize their children's rent by 600,000 to 1 million won monthly, the financial strain breaks generational support structures.

The Limits of Youth Politics and Generational Divides

Policy proposals meant to ease these burdens often misfire due to a lack of genuine connection. Plans to expand housing supply in areas like Yongsan are frequently met with cynicism among young adults who feel excluded. In informal gatherings of people born in 1987, reactions to new public housing quotas often sound like: "Even if they build it, I won't get to live there. What does it actually do for me?"

This alienation is deepened by the state of youth politics itself. Rather than bridging the gap between grassroots realities and the halls of power, young politicians within major parties are frequently viewed as having adopted the careerist habits of older generations, engaging in factional infighting rather than building practical alternatives. When the political class uses youth as a decorative label rather than an authentic voice, falling approval ratings cease to be mere polling fluctuations and become a warning flare about social cohesion.

As editorial writer Kim Seung-hyun observed, no matter how many policy achievements the administration highlights in morning briefings, the signal rarely reaches the ground floor intact. If the distance between official talking points and the damp reality of semi-basement rentals remains unbridged, opportunities for a sustained rebound in public trust will remain out of reach.

What to Watch Next

As the administration searches for ways to reverse its trajectory, several concrete indicators will determine whether policy shifts are taking root:

  • Adjustments to Real Housing Metrics: Watch whether future housing announcements move beyond aggregate construction targets to directly address hidden maintenance fee inflation and deposit burdens for lower-income brackets.
  • Labor and Corporate Arbitration Stances: Observe how the government handles upcoming industrial disputes to see if it can establish a predictable, transparent framework that satisfies both labor expectations and economic stability without alienating its core base.
  • Low-Engagement Voter Sentiment: Monitor local consumer confidence indices and youth economic participation metrics to see if stock market stabilization or cost-of-living adjustments register in everyday household budgets.

Politics ultimately rests on tangible security. Until governance is judged by its ability to preserve everyday well-being rather than through the lens of political messaging, the downward pressure on public approval is unlikely to lift.
