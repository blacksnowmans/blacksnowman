When a Movie Becomes a Battlefield: The Culture Wars Over "The Assassins" in South Korea

October 06, 2026

If you stand outside the quiet residential home of a well-known South Korean actor and lead a chanting crowd through a megaphone, you are not engaging in political discourse. You are waging a campaign of intimidation. Recently, that reality played out in Seoul as conservative activists targeted actor Yu Hae-jin, not for anything he said or did in his personal life, but simply because he appeared in a film they had already decided to hate. The movie is The Assassins, directed by Hur Jin-ho, and the firestorm surrounding it has quickly transformed from a standard cinematic controversy into a glaring symptom of a deeper political sickness gripping South Korean public life.

To understand why a fictional film has prompted conservative politicians to lead pilgrimages and protestors to picket residential neighborhoods, we have to look past the talking points. What is happening in South Korea around The Assassins is a case study in how modern populist movements weaponize cultural anxieties, construct elaborate fictions, and ultimately threaten the boundaries of a democratic society.

Anatomy of a Manufactured Conspiracy

When director Hur Jin-ho set out to make The Assassins, he was stepping into a sensitive era of modern Korean history. The film touches upon the turbulent political assassinations that reshaped the nation's authoritarian past. Yet, long before the film even premiered, a vocal segment of the South Korean right wing—led by figures within the People Power Party, the country's primary conservative opposition—began pushing a narrative that the film was a malicious, subversive attack on the foundational legitimacy of the South Korean state. Specifically, critics claimed the movie promoted a fringe conspiracy theory alleging that former authoritarian leader Park Chung-hee was directly assassinated by his own wife, First Lady Yuk Young-su—or vice versa, spinning wild historical distortions.

The problem with this narrative is simple: it is entirely untrue. Director Hur explicitly rejected these claims, drawing a firm line and confirming that such absurd conspiracy theories were never part of the script or the production. But in the ecosystem of modern political outrage, factual accuracy is an afterthought. For conservative hardliners, the movie served as a convenient canvas upon which to paint their fears of historical erasure. They did not need to watch the film to condemn it. The mere existence of a cinematic project touching on sensitive history was enough to mobilize a base hungry for grievances.

This reality-denying playbook is increasingly familiar globally, but its manifestation in South Korea has taken a particularly aggressive turn, spilling over from internet forums and press releases directly onto the pavement outside private homes.

The Siege of a Actor's Home and the Rise of Anti-Intellectualism

The campaign against The Assassins quickly escalated from rhetorical condemnation to harassment. Protesters gathered outside the private residence of lead actor Yu Hae-jin, staging noisy demonstrations in a residential neighborhood, calling for boycotts of his commercial endorsements, and hurling personal insults. The invasion of a private neighborhood to pressure an artist over a fictional role crosses a bright red line in any functioning civil society.

While Yu’s management agency quickly announced it would take strict legal action against the harassment, the government also felt compelled to step in. Minister of Culture, Sports and Tourism Choi 휘영 publicly denounced the threats against creators as a direct assault on democracy, calling on law enforcement to ensure the safety of film crews and actors.

This incident reflects a broader global wave of right-wing populism that thrives on what can best be described as institutionalized reality denial. Across many of the world's leading democracies, political factions increasingly rely on confirmation bias, rejecting established facts whenever they conflict with a desired worldview. In South Korea, this tendency has found a comfortable home among politicians looking for short-term mobilization tools at the expense of social cohesion.

Political Opportunism at the Shrine of History

The exploitation of the film reached its peak when People Power Party leader Chang Dong-hyeok and other top party leadership visited the birthplace of former First Lady Yuk Young-su. Standing in a deeply symbolic, revered space, Chang and his colleagues formally denounced The Assassins as an assault on national identity, turning a routine party photo-op into a masterclass in performative outrage.

This was a political production staged for the cameras. By framing a work of cinema as an existential threat to the nation, leadership could project an image of brave defenders guarding the gates of history. It matters little that the threat exists only in their imagination. The political utility of the outrage lies entirely in its ability to manufacture a sense of siege among core supporters.

When mainstream political parties begin treating creative works as targets for political warfare, they degrade the public sphere. Art is reduced to an ideological battlefield, and artists are treated as combatants rather than citizens exercising free expression. It is a sign of a political class increasingly reliant on anti-intellectualism and emotional manipulation rather than policy debate.

Why South Korea’s Democratic Resilience Matters Globally

As populist and far-right movements gain ground globally—with populist or far-right parties now holding power or knocking on the door in a significant share of major advanced economies—South Korea occupies a uniquely strategic position. It stands as a vibrant, fiercely contested democracy in East Asia, acting as a crucial defensive bulwark against the authoritarian temptations washing over other parts of the world.

The administration of President Lee Jae-myung faces the immense responsibility of navigating this global storm. When far-right factions feed on public anxiety, manufacture falsehoods, and justify harassment under the guise of patriotism, a democratic government cannot afford to look away. It must respond with firm, lawful governance that protects public safety and preserves the space for rational debate.

The controversy over The Assassins is a stress test for South Korea's democratic muscles. If the public and its leaders accept that intimidation and baseless conspiracy theories are valid tools of political persuasion, the foundational norms of free expression will slowly erode.

What to Watch Next

As this saga unfolds, several key developments will signal where South Korean political and cultural life is heading:

  • Legal Follow-Through: Watch how police and the courts handle the harassment campaigns outside private residences. Will law enforcement draw a firm line protecting citizens from political intimidation, or will mob tactics be allowed to set a chilling precedent for future cultural projects?
  • Political Strategy Changes: Pay attention to whether the mainstream opposition doubles down on cultural wedge issues as local and national election cycles approach, or if moderate voices within these parties attempt to pivot back toward policy-based debates.
  • Industry Self-Censorship: Monitor upcoming film and television productions in South Korea to see if studios and directors begin practicing preemptive self-censorship to avoid becoming the next target of politically motivated boycotts.

Democracy does not usually collapse overnight in a sudden coup; more often, it frays at the edges when citizens and leaders decide that truth is negotiable and that dissent is a crime. Protecting the right to make, view, and debate art without fear of harassment is not just a cultural issue—it is the baseline requirement of a free society.
