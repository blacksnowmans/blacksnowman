The Assassination Attempt that Never Added Up: Re-examining the Mun Se-gwang Case

October 28, 2026

On August 15, 1974, a gunshot echoed through the National Theater of Korea in Seoul. Mun Se-gwang, a pro-Pyongyang Zainichi Korean (a permanent resident of Japan), stepped forward during a Liberation Day ceremony, allegedly aiming to assassinate then-President Park Chung-hee. The bullets missed their target but claimed the life of First Lady Yuk Young-soo and, tragically, a local high school student named Jang Bong-hwa. For decades, the South Korean government’s official narrative—that Mun was a lone wolf acting under direct orders from North Korea—has served as historical gospel. However, a new film, The Assassins, is peeling back the layers of this narrative, revealing a story of state-sponsored deception, missing ballistics, and a screenplay written by intelligence agencies rather than history.

The Ballistics of a State-Manufactured Myth

For the production team behind The Assassins, the most daunting challenge was reconstructing the shooting itself. When they looked at the official records, the logistics of the bullets simply did not make sense. Eyewitnesses at the scene offered conflicting reports—some claimed the sounds were not gunshots but the noise of objects colliding. Ultimately, the filmmakers were forced to arbitrarily settle on six gunshots for the screen, but the physical evidence remains a glaring void.

Most incriminating is the path of the bullets. One round was found embedded in the ceiling of the theater—a bizarre placement for a would-be assassin targeting the podium. This leads to the "reasonable suspicion" that Mun was not the only one firing. At the time, the Korea Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA, the precursor to today’s National Intelligence Service, or NIS), known for its brutal efficiency in silencing dissent, was desperate to control the narrative. The death of high school student Jang Bong-hwa became a focal point of their manipulation. While the government blamed Mun, contemporary satire—such as the famous Gobau-yeonggam comic strip—ridiculed the official explanation, asking if the bullets had "curved" or if the authorities were playing billiards with the crime scene.

The truth likely rests within the encrypted servers of the National Intelligence Service. The missing bullets—the most vital pieces of evidence—have never been recovered. Their silence is the loudest proof that the events of that day remain under the lock and key of a state that prioritized regime stability over historical integrity.

Internal Inconsistencies and the "Missing" Security Guard

Internal documents from the KCIA suggest that even the agency knew its official script was flimsy. It is widely believed that the true nature of this operation was known to only a handful of high-ranking officials across the agency’s 1st, 2nd, and 6th Bureaus. While initial investigations involved genuine forensic analysis, those who discovered that the evidence did not point to Mun were quickly sidelined, and their reports were destroyed.

Shockingly, archived forensic results indicate that the bullet which killed the high school student was not fired by Mun, but by a bodyguard standing in close proximity to Park Chung-hee. Rather than face the consequences of a botched assassination, the KCIA manipulated the legal process. They treated the bodyguard merely as a witness, never as a suspect, effectively ensuring that the person responsible for the student’s death faced no legal repercussions. For the KCIA, the objective was never the pursuit of justice; it was the completion of a narrative that kept the regime secure. The tragedy is twofold: the victim lost her life, and the state became the primary accomplice in covering up the truth.

The Japan Connection: A Disputed Narrative

While the South Korean government insisted on a North Korean "lone wolf" plot, investigations by Japanese authorities and media painted a different picture. Interviews with Mun’s wife revealed a complex web of collaborators rather than a clean chain of command from Pyongyang. Names like "Mr. Lee of Tokyo," Maeda, and Ikeda surfaced in journalistic reports, suggesting a much broader, organized effort.

Furthermore, the official timeline of the incident contains a catastrophic error. The South Korean government claimed Mun received his orders while aboard the North Korean ship Mangyongbong in Osaka. However, Japanese police records confirmed that on the date in question, the Mangyongbong was not even in the port; it was already en route to North Korea. This amateurish attempt to manufacture a trail to the North resulted in international embarrassment. When a government constructs a lie to cover another lie, the cracks inevitably begin to show.

Even Mun himself was a paradox. He suffered from severe myopia and was physically unfit for an assassination mission. The film highlights this through the actor’s use of thick, magnifying glasses—a visual reminder of how absurd the "deadly sniper" narrative truly was. If the goal was a successful hit, why choose a candidate so poorly equipped? The answer points away from a professional assassination and toward a staged, theatrical event.

Why This Still Matters Today

The international success of The Assassins, which won the Second Runner-up Audience Award at the Toronto International Film Festival, proves that the world is hungry for the truth behind these historical mysteries. In Singapore, even an attempt by the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative opposition) to have the film’s screening cancelled only served to heighten public interest. It was a clear display of the same censorship tactics that the film itself portrays.

As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung continues its term, there is a renewed call for transparency. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which had its previous efforts to investigate this case stifled during the tenure of the now-imprisoned Yoon Seok-youl, must be allowed to resume its work. Under the previous regime, the government was desperate to protect the "state secrets" of its predecessors, often at the expense of the truth.

The history of the Mun Se-gwang case is not just a relic of the 1970s; it is a warning. It reminds us that when power is left unchecked, it uses the state apparatus to manufacture history. As we look toward the future, the work of the Commission and the efforts of investigators to access the NIS servers are not mere administrative tasks—they are a moral imperative. We must continue to question the official records, for as long as we stop asking, we remain the puppets of a fabricated, decades-old script.

What to Watch Next

  • Public Discourse: The ongoing debate between the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition on historical transparency will likely influence how much access independent investigators receive to state archives.
