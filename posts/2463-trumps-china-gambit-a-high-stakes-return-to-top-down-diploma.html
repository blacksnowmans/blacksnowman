Trump’s China Gambit: A High-Stakes Return to Top-Down Diplomacy and the Risk of 'Korea Passing'

November 18, 2026

On November 17, as Donald Trump stepped onto the tarmac in the Philippines, the air was thick with more than just tropical humidity. For the diplomatic corps in Seoul and the security apparatus across Northeast Asia, the former U.S. President—and current frontrunner for the next term—dropped a geopolitical bombshell. Before even beginning his scheduled meetings, Trump signaled a stunning possibility: a surprise summit with North Korean leader Kim Jong-un, potentially hosted on Chinese soil.

This isn't just a repeat of the 2018 Singapore or 2019 Hanoi summits. This time, the board has changed. Trump is operating from a position of domestic political urgency, while South Korea is navigated by the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the current leader from the liberal Democratic Party of Korea. As Trump attempts to force the "pendulum of dialogue" back into motion, he is doing so by leaning heavily on Beijing, effectively handing Chinese President Xi Jinping the keys to the negotiation room. For those watching from outside the peninsula, the move marks a pivot back to a "top-down" diplomatic style that bypasses traditional bureaucratic channels—and potentially, the interests of South Korea itself.

Domestic Desperation as a Catalyst for Foreign Policy

To understand why Trump is suddenly reviving the "Kim Jong-un card," one must look at the recent U.S. midterm elections. Contrary to many predictions, the results were a significant blow to Trump’s political momentum, leaving him in need of a dramatic narrative shift to consolidate his base and reassert his relevance on the world stage. Historically, Trump has viewed North Korea as his "signature" foreign policy arena—a place where his personal brand of "art of the deal" diplomacy can produce the kind of televised spectacles that domestic audiences crave.

By suggesting that "Kim Jong-un would also want to come to China" to meet him, Trump is attempting to manufacture a breakthrough that serves as a distraction from his domestic setbacks. However, this spontaneity carries inherent risks. Unlike the carefully calibrated "Shuttle Diplomacy" often favored by professional diplomats, Trump’s approach relies on individual whims. This raises the immediate specter of "Korea Passing"—a term used in Seoul to describe the nightmare scenario where major powers (the U.S., China, and North Korea) decide the fate of the peninsula while South Korean officials are left waiting in the hallway.

The contrast with the previous era is stark. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was eventually impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence—Seoul’s diplomacy was often criticized for being reactive or overly subservient to Washington’s whims without securing reciprocal guarantees. The Lee Jae-myung administration, by contrast, has sought a more balanced "peaceful coexistence" framework. Trump’s sudden intervention threatens to upend this delicate balance, forcing Seoul to react to a script written in Mar-a-Lago and Beijing.

The Dandong Signal and the North-China-Russia Triangle

While Trump’s comments might seem like mere rhetoric, the movements on the ground suggest a deeper coordination. Reports have surfaced that Kim Jong-un recently visited Dandong, a vital Chinese hub on the North Korean border that handles the vast majority of trade between the two nations. In the cryptic language of North Korean diplomacy, a visit to Dandong by the Supreme Leader is rarely just a site inspection; it is often a precursor to a major diplomatic summit or a shift in strategic alignment.

Kim’s presence at the border suggests that Pyongyang and Beijing have already conducted high-level "pre-coordination." For Kim, a summit in China provides a layer of security and a powerful "protector" in the form of President Xi Jinping. It also allows Kim to play his suitors against one another. Even as North Korea has moved closer to Russia in recent months—bolstered by military cooperation and Vladimir Putin’s own interest in meeting Kim—the Dandong visit signals that China remains the ultimate guarantor of the North Korean regime’s survival.

"The geopolitical math in Northeast Asia is no longer a simple two-body problem between Washington and Pyongyang. It is now a volatile multi-variable equation involving a resurgent China and a Russia that is eager to play spoiler in any U.S.-led initiative."

Russia’s immediate reaction to Trump’s comments—hinting that Putin too "might meet Kim"—underscores this competition. Moscow does not want to be sidelined in the "North Korea business." If Trump and Xi collaborate to host Kim, Russia risks losing its leverage as Pyongyang's newest primary military partner. This competition between the three giants (the U.S., China, and Russia) over who gets to "manage" North Korea places the peninsula in a precarious position, where its denuclearization is often secondary to the broader strategic interests of the global powers.

China’s Strategic Silence and the ASEAN Stage

Beijing’s response to Trump’s invitation has been a masterclass in strategic ambiguity. The Chinese Foreign Ministry’s official stance—that they have "no information" regarding such a summit—is a classic defensive crouch. By not denying the possibility but refusing to confirm it, Xi Jinping is keeping his options open. He knows that Trump has effectively handed him a massive piece of leverage. If the summit happens, Xi is the grand mediator; if it fails, Xi can blame Trump’s impulsiveness.

For China, the "North Korea card" is a valuable tool in its broader trade and technology war with the United States. Xi may well be calculating what he can extract from a future Trump administration in exchange for bringing Kim to the table. Could it be a reduction in high-tech export bans? A softening of tariffs? For the Lee Jae-myung government, this is the core of the concern: that the security of the Korean people might become a "chip" in a larger U.S.-China trade deal.

Meanwhile, President Lee Jae-myung is currently on a tour of ASEAN (the Association of Southeast Asian Nations), attempting to build a multilateral coalition for peace. President Lee’s vision focuses on sustainable, institutionalized dialogue rather than the one-off "shows" favored by his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, or Donald Trump. The challenge for the Lee administration is that the "glamour" of a Trump-Kim-Xi summit in Beijing naturally draws the world’s cameras away from the more substantive, ground-level diplomatic work being done in Jakarta or Manila. Seoul is now in a race to ensure that its "Peaceful Coexistence" initiative remains the primary roadmap, rather than being buried by a "Small Deal" between Trump and Kim.

The Danger of the 'Small Deal' and Seoul’s Strategic Response

The greatest fear among security analysts in Seoul is the "Small Deal." This refers to a scenario where Trump, eager for a quick win, agrees to a partial freeze of North Korea’s long-range missile program in exchange for sanctions relief, while effectively ignoring the North’s existing nuclear stockpile and short-range missiles that target South Korea and Japan. Such a deal would protect the U.S. mainland but leave South Korea under a permanent nuclear shadow.

To prevent this, the Lee Jae-myung administration must navigate three critical tasks:

  • Asserting Agency: Seoul must make it clear to both Washington and Beijing that any agreement regarding the peninsula’s security that does not include South Korean input is illegitimate and unsustainable.
  • Maintaining Sanctions Cohesion: While President Lee supports dialogue, the administration must ensure that the international sanctions regime doesn't crumble prematurely in the excitement of a potential summit, losing the only leverage that brought Pyongyang to the table in the first place.
  • Diversifying Alliances: By strengthening ties with ASEAN and European partners, South Korea can create a "multilateral buffer" that prevents the U.S. and China from treating the peninsula as a private playground.

The legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s "imprisoned diplomacy"—characterized by a lack of strategic depth and a failure to read the room in Washington—serves as a cautionary tale for the current government. The Lee administration cannot afford to be passive. It must be the "architect" of the peace process, not just a spectator in a theater built by Trump and Xi.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will be decisive. Watch for these three concrete indicators to see if this "bomb" Trump dropped will actually explode into reality:

  • Kim Jong-un’s Travel: If the North Korean leader moves beyond Dandong and heads toward Beijing or a neutral site like Shenzhen, a summit is almost certainly in the final stages of planning.
  • Chinese Concessions: Monitor whether the U.S. shifts its tone on trade or the "Taiwan issue" in the lead-up to any North Korean announcement. This will reveal the "price" Xi is charging for his role as host.
  • Seoul’s High-Level Envoys: Look for the Lee Jae-myung administration to dispatch high-level envoys to both Washington and Beijing. Their mission will be to set "red lines" for any deal—specifically ensuring that the removal of the nuclear threat, not just a temporary freeze, remains the goal.

Trump has restarted the clock on North Korean diplomacy. Whether that clock leads to a genuine peace or a dangerous "Korea Passing" remains the most urgent question for the Lee Jae-myung government and the international community at large.
