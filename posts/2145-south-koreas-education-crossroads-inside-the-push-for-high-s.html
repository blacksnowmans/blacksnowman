South Korea’s Education Crossroads: Inside the Push for High School Reform and the Battles over Classroom Staffing

November 09, 2026

Inside a packed municipal auditorium in South Korea, over 300 parents of ninth-graders sat with pens poised, listening intently to admission specialists dissect a looming shift in the country's college entrance system. The atmosphere was charged with an anxiety familiar to South Korean families, but the source of their concern was entirely new. The students currently finishing middle school will be the first cohort to navigate a double transformation in South Korean public education: the full rollout of the High School Credit System (gogyo hakjeomje) alongside a major overhaul of university admission standards set for the 2028 academic year.

For decades, South Korean high school education followed a rigid, standardized curriculum where students sat in identical classrooms taking fixed sets of subjects. Under the incoming credit system—modeled loosely on university course selection—students choose specialized electives tailored to their academic interests and career goals. Combined with the 2028 college entrance exam restructuring, families across the country are scrambling to figure out how high school course selections will impact university applications, internal grade point averages, and the country's grueling standardized entrance exams.

This massive policy shift takes place against a turbulent political backdrop. The current administration of President Lee Jae-myung is working to stabilize and increase public confidence in state education following years of policy confusion and instability during Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure. Yet as education offices and local governments roll out information sessions to reassure families, deep structural fissures are emerging. A fierce battle over teacher staffing, widening regional infrastructure gaps, and the rapid arrival of artificial intelligence are testing whether South Korea's public school system can deliver on its promises.

A System in Transition: High School Credit System Meets College Admission Overhaul

The core promise of the High School Credit System is student autonomy. Rather than forcing every student down an identical path, the policy aims to let teenagers tailor their coursework to match their future majors—whether in computer science, biotechnology, or human rights law. However, for the parents of current ninth-graders, this ideal presents an immediate dilemma: how will university admissions officers actually evaluate these customized transcripts under the reformed 2028 admission framework?

To address this panic, local education offices across the country, including the Gwangju Metropolitan Office of Education in the southwest, have begun holding large-scale briefings for middle school parents. Attendance has surged as families seek clear guidance on subject prerequisites and changes to internal high school assessment methods. The core concern for many parents is whether choosing a challenging elective with a small enrollment might hurt their child's internal relative GPA compared to taking an easier, highly populated course.

The logistical reality of the credit system also exposes severe regional disparities. While wealthy school districts in major metropolitan areas can easily offer a wide range of specialized subjects, smaller regional schools often lack the faculty or facilities to host advanced electives. To bridge this gap, education authorities are relying on "hub schools" (where students travel to a central high school for specific classes) or joint online high school platforms. Yet parents remain skeptical, fearing that reliance on remote or shared classes puts non-metropolitan students at a disadvantage compared to elite suburban schools.

Without clear, accessible guidance from public institutions, private cram schools (known as hagwons) have stepped into the void, utilizing fear-based marketing to push expensive private consulting packages. Education experts warn that unless the public school system establishes a robust, trustworthy counseling infrastructure, the high school credit reform risks driving families even deeper into the arms of the private tutoring market.

The Educator Backlash: "Stopgap Hiring" and the Fight Over Teaching Quality

While administrators attempt to win over parents, a severe crisis is brewing inside the teachers' lounges. Expanding the menu of available high school courses requires significantly more teachers with specialized subject expertise. However, public school faculty quotas have not grown at the same pace as the proposed elective catalogs.

To solve this deficit, the National Education Commission—a presidential advisory body tasked with setting long-term educational strategy—recently raised the possibility of opening teaching positions to external industry specialists and expanding multi-subject certifications for existing teachers. The policy proposal would allow uncertified outside professionals and non-regular contract instructors to teach specialized high school electives.

The backlash from front-line educators was immediate and intense. Teachers' associations in Gyeonggi Province, the nation's most populous region surrounding Seoul, publicly condemned the commission’s discussion, demanding an immediate end to what they termed "stopgap staffing solutions" (ttaemjil-sik inryeok chungwon). Instructors argue that filling classrooms with temporary contract workers or uncertified personnel undermines the pedagogical integrity and professional standards of public education.

The Core Conflict in South Korea's High School Reform:
Education authorities argue that external industry experts are necessary to teach cutting-edge electives like AI and advanced technology, while teachers' unions insist that relying on non-regular contract workers dilutes public education quality and shifts the government's duty onto precarious staff instead of expanding permanent faculty quotas.

The friction underscores a fundamental contradiction in the reform plan. Expanding subject choices without expanding permanent, certified teaching staff forces schools to rely on temporary fixes. Educators argue that the quality of education cannot exceed the quality of its teachers, warning that failing to invest heavily in permanent faculty recruitment will ultimately hurt students who need consistent, professional guidance in the classroom.

Adapting to the AI Era: Beyond Rote Learning to Gaming and Drones

Parallel to structural entrance exam reforms, the content of career education in South Korea is undergoing a quiet revolution driven by rapid technological change. The traditional obsession with safe, prestigious career paths—such as medicine, law, or public administration—is gradually opening up to accommodate digital transformation and emerging tech sectors.

Local authorities are increasingly sponsoring workshops designed to help students adapt to an automated job market. In Gwangju’s Nam-gu district, municipal authorities hosted a specialized career lecture led by prominent English instructor Jeong Seung-ik on "Real Study Methods in the AI Era." The lecture emphasized moving away from traditional rote memorization toward self-directed inquiry and data interpretation. Similarly, Korea National University of Transportation organized a generative AI writing workshop for students at Segwang Middle School, teaching ninth-graders how to utilize artificial intelligence tools to structure logical essays and research papers.

Career exploration programs are also embracing youth culture and technology. In Busan’s Suyeong District, the local government organized a youth career fair centered on esports and indie game development. In South Gyeongsang Province, the Namhae Education Support Office hosted hands-on drone operation training for teachers, equipping them to guide students toward opportunities in aerial technology and content creation.

These initiatives reflect a deliberate strategy to align public education with shifting market realities. As artificial intelligence automates routine administrative and technical tasks, local education boards are attempting to foster creative, self-directed learning models that help students build practical digital skills rather than relying solely on exam preparation.

Democratizing Admissions Counseling and Expanding Inclusive Education

As private consulting fees reach record highs in affluent neighborhoods, local governments and public university networks are stepping up to provide free, high-level counseling services. The goal is to level the playing field for families who cannot afford private admissions agencies.

Municipalities like Suwon and Gwangmyeong have emerged as key hubs for public entrance consulting. Suwon Special City recently hosted its fourth annual specialized career and admission consulting session, pairing high schoolers with top-tier admissions advisors and featured lecturers from EBS (Educational Broadcasting System, South Korea's public educational media network). Gwangmyeong City’s Youth Career and Admission Support Center ran similar seminars breaking down the specific qualitative evaluation criteria top universities will use under the 2028 admission rules.

Public outreach efforts are also expanding into university-school partnerships and inclusive learning initiatives:

  • Higher Education Partnerships: Yeungnam University College in Daegu signed a formal agreement with Dasa High School to share campus laboratory facilities and technical resources for high school career projects.
  • Ecological and Special Education: The Hongseong Education Support Office in South Chungcheong Province launched unified eco-career programs spanning kindergarten to high school, while the Gyeongsan Education Support Office in North Gyeongsang Province organized targeted career-guidance workshops for special education teachers.
  • Global Educational Outreach: Reflecting the international scope of South Korean educational development initiatives, Korean public education models and career counseling sessions were recently extended to over 300 students and workers in An Hiep commune, Vietnam.

By offering institutionalized support for special education, non-metropolitan districts, and low-income families, educational authorities hope to demonstrate that public education can serve as an equalizer rather than an arena reserved for those who can afford elite cram schools.

What to Watch Next

South Korea’s public education system is entering a critical execution phase as the 2028 college admission changes draw closer. Over the coming months, several key indicators will determine whether the High School Credit System succeeds or falters:

  • Teacher Quota Negotiations: Watch how the Lee Jae-myung administration responds to teacher protests over faculty shortages. The outcome of debates between the Ministry of Education, the National Education Commission, and regional teachers' unions over permanent teacher hiring versus contract specialists will dictate classroom stability.
  • Expansion of Public Admissions Databases: Pay attention to whether local municipal consulting programs, such as those in Suwon and Gwangmyeong, expand into a unified, nationwide public admissions database to counteract expensive private consulting services.
  • Implementation of Descriptive and Essay-Based Grading: Monitor how high schools handle internal assessments under the new credit framework, particularly as schools integrate AI tools into student writing evaluations and subjective grading standards.

The transformation of South Korea’s high schools is more than a technical policy update; it is a fundamental stress test of whether the state can modernise its educational apparatus while maintaining equity and professional standards. As thousands of middle school families prepare for the new system, the pressure remains on the government to prove that public education can lead the way.
