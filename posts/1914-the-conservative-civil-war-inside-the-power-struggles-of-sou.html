The Conservative Civil War: Inside the Power Struggles of South Korea’s Main Opposition

November 03, 2026

The headquarters of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, is currently a place of deep unease and calculated maneuvers. To an outsider, the political landscape of South Korea might seem settled: the country is governed by President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party, while the former leader, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, remains behind bars following his landmark impeachment and removal from office. However, within the halls of the PPP, the battle for the future of the conservative movement is reaching a fever pitch. Rather than focusing on a unified front against the governing party, the PPP’s top tier is embroiled in a complex internal power struggle that reveals a party still haunted by its past and desperate to find a new identity.

For those following Korean politics from abroad, the current situation represents more than just standard partisan bickering. It is a fundamental fight over who will lead the conservative wing into the next election cycle. With the presidency held by the liberals and the conservative base still reeling from the disgrace of the previous era, the stakes involve not just party leadership, but the very survival of the conservative ideology in a rapidly changing social landscape. The media, meanwhile, has been accused of fueling this fire by "hyping up" certain figures as saviors, creating a narrative that often diverges sharply from the reality of the public's frustrations with the cost of living and political instability.

Manufacturing Saviors: The Media’s Obsession with Han and Oh

If you were to scan the headlines of major Korean news outlets today, you would see a recurring theme: the supposed "rivalry" or "partnership" between Han Dong-hoon and Oh Se-hoon. Han Dong-hoon, the former Justice Minister who rose to prominence under the previous administration, and Oh Se-hoon, the current Mayor of Seoul, are being framed by the press as the two pillars of the conservative revival. However, critics argue that this media focus is a carefully orchestrated campaign to "elevate" these figures, portraying them as relief pitchers ready to save a failing team.

The reality is far more transactional. This media narrative serves the ambitions of these politicians who seek to control not just their current domains, but the entirety of the party’s leadership. There is a palpable disconnect between the grand political epics written in the newsrooms of Seoul and the lived experience of the average voter. While the media analyzes every handshake and social media post between these "titans," the public is struggling with economic hardship. The suspicion remains that these political figures are less interested in public service and more focused on securing their next "badge" of authority or the party chairmanship.

One of the most prominent examples of this artificial narrative is the so-called "Hun-Hun Alliance"—a play on the names of Han Dong-hoon and Ahn Cheol-soo, a veteran politician and former presidential candidate known for his frequent shifts in political alignment. While pro-Han factions try to market this as a heartwarming political union, the actual relationship is far from cordial. Ahn has recently directed sharp, biting remarks toward Han, suggesting that their political goals and levels of mutual trust are miles apart. Even Lee Jun-seok, the influential former head of the PPP, has criticized Han, noting that the former minister seems to have a talent for making enemies in every corner of the political world. This highlights a recurring problem for the PPP: they are trapped in a cycle of "playing politics" with alliances that have no foundation in shared policy or vision, but are instead temporary marriages of convenience designed to fool the electorate.

Cracks in the Foundation: Jang Dong-hyeok and the Local Power Balance

While the media focuses on the flashy names in Seoul, a quieter but equally significant challenge is brewing from within the party’s legislative ranks. Jang Dong-hyeok, a prominent lawmaker within the PPP, has recently signaled his intent to run for the party leadership. His move is a direct challenge to the Han-Oh duopoly that the media has tried to cement. Jang’s strategy involves more than just a leadership bid; he has even gone so far as to suggest a re-election for the Seoul mayoralty, a move clearly intended to destabilize Oh Se-hoon’s position and shake the existing power structure.

This internal friction is a symptom of a larger rot within the conservative movement. The party is divided between those who want to maintain the current status quo and those who feel marginalized by the current leadership. When a party is this divided, it becomes a ticking time bomb. The "innovation" and "change" promised by various PPP leaders often turn out to be nothing more than cover for old-fashioned turf wars. Jang’s move suggests that the party’s internal fractures are deeper than they appear on the surface, and his bid could either be a catalyst for a much-needed reorganization or the trigger for a total collapse into infighting.

Furthermore, the political landscape at the local government level is becoming increasingly unstable. In places like Seoul’s Olympic Park, events are still being marred by the presence of far-right elements claiming that past elections were fraudulent. These "stolen election" conspiracy theories are a cancer for a party trying to appeal to moderate, rational voters. The failure of the PPP leadership—including those holding major mayoral offices—to decisively distance themselves from these radical elements shows a cowardice that prevents the party from modernizing. By catering to, or at least refusing to condemn, these fringe groups for the sake of political expediency, the PPP remains tethered to an irrational past that the rest of the country has moved beyond.

The "Fairness" Trap: Allegations Against Han Dong-hoon’s Family

Central to the PPP’s struggle for moral authority is the recurring question of integrity, particularly concerning Han Dong-hoon. During his time as Justice Minister, Han was a vocal proponent of "law and order" and "fairness," often using these themes to criticize his political opponents. However, he now finds himself on the defensive. Lawmaker Kang Deuk-gu of the governing Democratic Party has recently filed a formal complaint against Han and his wife, alleging the falsification of their daughter’s volunteer records for use in academic credentials.

The allegations center on whether Han’s daughter actually developed a mobile application for volunteer work that was subsequently released on the Apple App Store, or if she received significant outside help—or even had it ghostwritten. For many Koreans, this carries a heavy sense of déjà vu. The "integrity" of children’s academic resumes is a explosive topic in South Korea, where the pressure for elite education is immense. If these allegations prove true, it would expose a deep hypocrisy: a man who rose to power by prosecuting others for similar academic irregularities would be seen as having used his influence to build a "spec-heavy" resume for his own child.

"Politics is about more than just words; it is about the moral weight of one's actions. When those who preach fairness are caught in a web of their own making, the damage to the public trust is often irreparable."

The PPP’s response to these allegations has been largely evasive, often relying on media play to distract from the core issues. However, the shadow of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment—a historic tragedy for the country—should have been a wake-up call for the conservatives to embrace transparency. Instead, by falling into the same traps of alleged nepotism and elite privilege, figures like Han Dong-hoon risk rendering the party’s platform of "fairness" entirely hollow. For an international audience, this underscores the "naeronambul" (a Korean acronym meaning "my romance, your adultery," or blatant hypocrisy) that continues to plague the upper echelons of the country's political class.

The Shadow of Impeachment and the Persistence of Extremism

Perhaps the most significant obstacle to the PPP’s recovery is its inability to fully sever ties with the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Despite his removal and current imprisonment, a vocal minority within the party and its base continues to support the disgraced former leader, even participating in protests against his impeachment. These figures, who stood on the streets waving flags and denying the constitutional process that removed Mr. Yoon, still hold positions of influence within the party today. This is not just a matter of political loyalty; it is a fundamental disagreement with the democratic norms of the Republic of Korea.

As long as the PPP allows itself to be influenced by those who reject the legitimacy of the impeachment, it will remain a "zombie party," unable to move forward because it is still clinging to a dead past. The data reflects this: public approval ratings for the PPP have remained stagnant as voters see a party more interested in protecting its own than in acknowledging the gravity of the constitutional violations that led to the previous administration's downfall. The internal reputation of the party is defined not by fresh policies, but by the same old faces engaging in the same old ideological battles.

The future of the People Power Party depends on a total transformation that it seems unwilling to undergo. To truly become a viable alternative to President Lee Jae-myung’s government, the PPP needs to do more than just swap leaders or rebrand its image. It needs to:

  • Decisively break from the "pro-Yoon" elements that still harbor anti-democratic sentiments.
  • Address the integrity concerns surrounding its top figures with total transparency rather than media spin.
  • Stop using local government positions as mere stepping stones for higher office and focus on actual governance.
Without these changes, the conservative civil war will continue to be a spectacle of self-destruction, leaving the South Korean public with an opposition that is loud, divided, and ultimately irrelevant to the pressing needs of the nation.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the formal proceedings regarding the complaints against Han Dong-hoon’s family. If the investigation gains traction, it could effectively end his aspirations for the party leadership. Additionally, the upcoming internal party elections will serve as a litmus test: will the members choose a path of moderate reform, or will they double down on the combative, grievance-based politics of the past?
