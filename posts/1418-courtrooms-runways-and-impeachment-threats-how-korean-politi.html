Courtrooms, Runways, and Impeachment Threats: How Korean Politics Revolved Around Lawfare This Week

October 24, 2026

The cameras packed tightly outside the Seoul High Court in Seocho-dong told the entire story before a single gavel dropped. On October 23, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon walked into court for his second-instance appellate verdict over allegations that a third party improperly covered the costs of political polling on his behalf. The proceedings were broadcast live nationwide—a level of scrutiny typically reserved for historic political transitions or former heads of state facing down systemic corruption trials.

For observers outside South Korea, watching the mayor of a metropolis of nearly ten million people fight for his political life in a courtroom may feel like an isolated legal drama. But in Yeouido, Seoul's legislative nerve center, the hearing was merely the center ring of a much broader circus. South Korean politics has entered a phase where virtually every major policy dispute, institutional friction, and personal feud is immediately translated into the language of the criminal code and constitutional impeachment.

The Mayor on the Brink and Conservatism's Internal Fissures

Oh Se-hoon is not simply an administrator managing buses and urban development; he is the most prominent conservative figure holding significant executive office today. Within the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition, Oh represents one of the few plausible, battle-tested contenders for the next presidential cycle. A guilty verdict serious enough to strip him of his mayoral seat would not only plunge Seoul City Hall into an administrative vacuum and trigger a high-stakes by-election, but it would also hollow out the opposition's national leadership bench.

The People Power Party publicly closed ranks, insisting that Oh is innocent and that municipal governance will remain stable. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea, conversely, pressed for strict legal accountability, hoping that the appellate court would uphold the initial ruling and permanently sideline an influential rival.

Yet beneath the public solidarity within conservative circles lies deep personal and ideological rancor. Representative Lee Jun-seok of the splinter Reform Party—himself a former conservative party chief who was forced out during past factional infighting—voiced support for Oh's innocence while simultaneously slamming the broader conservative apparatus. Lee openly poured cold water on rumors of a political alliance between Oh Se-hoon and Han Dong-hoon, the former interim head of the PPP, dismissing Han as a "vulgar legal technician."

Lee's sharp jab touches on a systemic critique that resonates across ideological divides: the frustration with an era in which politics is treated as a subsidiary of the prosecutor's office. Many conservatives still grapple with the hangover from the administration of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was removed from office through impeachment and currently sits in prison—where governance was frequently reduced to indictments, court motions, and retaliatory investigations. When the primary method of political survival is seeking an opponent's incarceration, long-term policy vision inevitably vanishes.

The National Assembly Audit: A Cascade of Impeachment Threats

While Mayor Oh navigated the appellate docket, eight standing committees in the National Assembly were concluding South Korea's annual government audit (gukgam). Designed as a sweeping legislative check on the executive branch, this year's sessions frequently degenerated into mutual recriminations over who should be impeached next.

The primary clash erupted over the newly established Major Crimes Investigation Office. Opposition PPP lawmakers, led by Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik, fiercely protested against the agency's director attending Cabinet meetings chaired by President Lee Jae-myung. The opposition claimed that bringing an investigative agency head into cabinet deliberations amounts to direct executive interference in independent investigations—a violation so grave, they argued, that it constitutes grounds for presidential impeachment.

The ruling Democratic Party pushed back vigorously. Democratic lawmakers argued that open attendance at Cabinet meetings represents legitimate, transparent administrative oversight. They contrasted this with the backchannel direct phone calls and closed-door coordination between the presidential office and state prosecutors that characterized Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. Democratic Party lawmaker Yang Bu-nam noted caustically that keeping the Prosecutor General out of cabinet meetings in the past had hardly guaranteed neutrality, urging a methodical fact-finding inquiry rather than reflexively brandishing the impeachment weapon.

Simultaneously, the judiciary itself came under legislative fire. Some voices within the Democratic Party floated the possibility of impeaching Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over grievances regarding judicial independence and controversial rulings. While figures like Yang Bu-nam urged caution—emphasizing that strict constitutional thresholds and formal investigations must precede any impeachment push—the mere suggestion provoked outrage from the conservative opposition, who decried it as an assault on the separation of powers.

Even Yoon Suk-yeol reportedly weighed in from behind bars, questioning via his legal counsel whether hauling the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court to testify before lawmakers could still be described as democracy. The resulting atmosphere was one of profound institutional exhaustion: whichever party holds executive power defends ministerial control, while the out-of-power party accuses the other of dismantling constitutional order.

Runway Grids and Labor Walkouts: When Lawfare Meets Real Lives

The friction between Yeouido's partisan rhetoric and everyday civic life spilled into public view at Jeju International Airport. During hearings in the Land, Infrastructure, and Transport Committee, People Power Party lawmaker Kim Eun-hye seized on widespread flight delays and cancellations on the resort island, sarcastically asking whether the Korea Airports Corporation had been demoted to the "President's Departure Protocol Office."

The opposition alleged that airspace controls and protocol surrounding President Lee Jae-myung's official plane had cascaded into ground stops, leaving thousands of ordinary domestic travelers stranded on tarmacs. The ruling party fired back, stating that adverse weather and existing operational bottlenecks had already disrupted the flight schedule, accusing the opposition of fabricating a malicious political frame around standard, non-negotiable security protocols for the head of state. As both sides traded insults, substantive scrutiny of regional air traffic management quietly evaporated.

A more consequential policy clash took place just outside the National Assembly gates. South Korea's two largest umbrella labor organizations—the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU) and the Federation of Korean Trade Unions (FKTU)—held joint press conferences demanding the withdrawal of the Democratic Party's proposed "Mega-Regional Strategic Industrial Special Zone Act."

The government and ruling party promote the legislation as an essential framework to build regional tech clusters, bolster semiconductor manufacturing, and address the severe economic imbalance between Seoul and the rest of the country. Organized labor, however, denounced the bill as a giveaway to conglomerates that rolls back labor protections under the guise of industrial competitiveness. For a progressive-leaning governing party, facing fierce pushback from its traditional labor base illustrates the tightrope the administration must walk as it attempts to stimulate sluggish growth while protecting core social constituencies.

President Lee's 'War on Crime' and Public Fatigue

Seeking to regain the initiative, President Lee Jae-myung convened a high-level government meeting to proclaim an all-out "war on violent crime," framing it as a transition to a "hyper-safe society." Emphasizing prevention over post-incident punishment, the president directed state agencies to establish sweeping safety nets against unpredictable, indiscriminate offenses that have shaken public confidence.

Yet even this law-and-order initiative met skepticism regarding administrative coherence. Lawmakers across the aisle pointed out the irony of pledging ambitious security initiatives while the National Assembly was simultaneously wrangling over inadequate budget allocations and support for frontline firefighters and emergency personnel.

Meanwhile, the Democratic Party's Floor Leader Kim Min-seok pledged to restore the office of the Special Inspector—a watchdog post designed to monitor corruption among the president's relatives and senior aides that has sat vacant for a decade. While meant to signal institutional transparency, party discipline was promptly tested when Democratic lawmaker Park Sun-won made unauthorized statements during a Defense Committee hearing regarding an investigation into the Shincheonji religious group, leaving floor leadership scrambling to contain the fallout.

Despite the constant legislative warfare, public opinion appears remarkably stable—if deeply polarized:

  • Presidential Approval: A Gallup Korea poll released for the fourth week of October placed President Lee's approval rating at 40%, with negative evaluations standing at 50%.
  • Party Support: The ruling Democratic Party polled at 37%, maintaining a comfortable lead outside the margin of error over the People Power Party at 28%.
  • The Independent Bloc: Non-aligned voters stood at 27%, matching the size of the main opposition party.

What to Watch Next

The high proportion of undecided and independent voters underscores the core structural problem of current South Korean politics: both major blocs remain trapped in a zero-sum legal war, while a massive segment of the electorate feels entirely unrepresented by either.

Over the coming weeks, keep an eye on three concrete developments:

  • The Legal Aftermath for Mayor Oh: If the appellate ruling hands Oh Se-hoon a disqualifying sentence, expect immediate jockeying within the People Power Party to fill the void, alongside an aggressive push by the Democratic Party to prepare for an early Seoul mayoral race.
  • The Special Inspector Nomination: Whether the ruling party and the National Assembly actually finalize the appointment of a permanent Special Inspector will serve as a genuine test of whether the administration is willing to submit to independent executive oversight.
  • The Fate of the Industrial Zone Bill: Watch whether the Democratic Party modifies the Mega-Regional Strategic Industrial Special Zone Act to placate the two major labor federations, or whether it pushes the pro-business legislation through despite union resistance.
