Why the Lee Jae-myung Government is Repeating South Korea’s Oldest Political Mistake on Prosecution Reform

September 13, 2026

For decades, the central battleground of South Korean politics has not been the economy, nor housing, nor foreign policy, but the prosecutor's office. The country's prosecutors have long wielded a terrifying concentration of power—combining both the authority to investigate crimes and the exclusive power to indict suspects. To generations of reformers, the solution has always been simple in theory: separate investigation from prosecution, stripping the country's elite prosecutors of their unchecked monopoly on justice. Yet watching the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung grapple with this issue reveals a sinking sense of déjà vu. What was supposed to be a historic dismantling of prosecutorial overreach has instead curdled into compromise, quiet appeasement, and self-sabotage.

When veteran investigative journalist Chang In-soo publicly floated the idea of dropping certain contentious indictments—a move framed as a backroom fix—it opened a window into the darker, more transactional mechanics of modern Korean governance. Far from breaking the institutional power of the prosecutors, Lee’s administration appears trapped in a paradox: trying to tame a monster by feeding it, hoping that a soft touch and cozy personnel appointments will secure peace. It is a strategy born of deep-seated institutional trauma, and it is failing.

The Paradox of the Pro-Prosecutor Justice Ministry

Before taking office, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party, made grand promises to completely separate investigative and prosecutorial powers. But reality has moved in the exact opposite direction. The administration has largely embraced the very people it vowed to curb, filling key positions in the Ministry of Justice with career prosecutors at rates that rival, and in some cases exceed, those of the previous conservative administration under Yoon Suk-yeol. The systematic de-prosecuturization of the Ministry—a prized goal of earlier progressive administrations—has been quietly abandoned.

Top-tier figures with deep ties to the prosecutorial establishment have been welcomed into influential advisory roles, while successive justice ministers have consistently defended the institution from the podium. Critical administrative posts that were once reserved for civilian experts have been handed back to prosecutors. The structural result is clear: instead of dismantling the internal cartel, the administration has institutionalized it. The power to appoint officials, which should serve as an instrument of democratic accountability, has been reduced to a peace-offering mechanism designed to keep the prosecutors from biting.

Statistics tell a stark story. Under previous administrations, the proportion of non-prosecutor appointments in the Ministry peaked before sliding backward. Now, any attempt to bring in outside legal experts is routinely dismissed under partisan frames, and policy has retreated to relying on individuals rather than reforming systems. It is a recurring lesson in Korean politics: relying on the goodwill of prosecutors instead of rewriting the laws that empower them always ends in capitulation.

A New Investigative Agency in Name Only

Nowhere is this half-measured approach more visible than in the creation of the proposed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, pitched as South Korea's answer to the Federal Bureau of Investigation—an independent body meant to strip regular prosecutors of their primary investigative duties. Yet a close look at the agency’s planning and leadership committees reveals a troubling familiar blueprint. Many of the figures tasked with shaping the new body are veterans of the old prosecutorial establishment.

Under the current plans, the new agency's jurisdiction will cover a sprawling array of offenses—corruption, economic crimes, public official misconduct, elections, defense acquisitions, and major disasters. These are precisely the high-profile domains that prosecutors historically used to position themselves as the ultimate arbiters of the state. Merely repackaging the traditional prosecutor-investigator hierarchy into "judicial investigation officers" and "expert investigators" does not change the underlying architecture. Without an absolute structural break, any new sign hung on a government building is simply a second prosecutor's office.

The Trap of the "Indictment Drop" Controversy

The political friction intensified recently when investigative journalist Chang In-soo discussed the possibility of the government quietly dropping certain politically charged indictments. The commentary sparked intense backlash, but the true danger lies not in the gossip of backroom deals, but in the legal exposure it creates. According to legal experts consulted on the matter, overt government pressure on prosecutors to drop specific cases provides the prosecution with a golden legal pretext: abuse of authority.

Rather than gaining momentum for reform, the administration’s clumsy signals have handed prosecutors valuable evidence to build counter-offensive strategies. Behind closed doors, prosecutors appear to be logging these executive interventions, treating them as leverage for future standoffs. When a reform-minded government tries to cut corners and negotiate with an entrenched bureaucracy instead of mobilizing public pressure, it surrenders the initiative. What was meant to look like pragmatism reads instead as amateurism.

The ensuing media circus—focusing on whether presidential aides were distancing themselves from Chang—only served to drown out the core issue. The press spent days chewing over personal gossip while ignoring the substantive warning: that the executive branch's casual approach to legal intervention is actively legitimizing the very prosecutorial overreach it claims to oppose.

The Ghost of Past Presidencies and the Fear of Retaliation

To understand why the Lee administration is pulling its punches, one must look at South Korea's most punishing political rixue: the inescapable curse of the post-presidency. From conservatives to liberals, nearly every former president in modern Korean history has faced aggressive, politically motivated investigations after leaving office. This recurring trauma leaves an indelible mark on every sitting leader.

The fear of retributive prosecution hangs over the Blue House like a heavy cloud. Presidents worry about their post-term security, and that anxiety routinely subverts long-term reform agendas. When fear dictates policy, leaders hesitate to pick fights with the country's elite legal class. As critics within political circles have noted, the visible preoccupation with life after the presidency drains the administration's reforming zeal.

History offers a stark warning here. During the administration of the late progressive President Moon Jae-in, hesitation born of institutional anxiety ultimately allowed the prosecution to regroup and launch relentless investigations that crippled the government's later years. Compromising with prosecutors does not buy safety; it simply fattens the predator. Unless an administration is willing to spend its political capital on systemic, irreversible legal changes, it will eventually fall victim to the very institution it coddled.

What to Watch Next

As this political drama unfolds, observers outside Korea should look for a few concrete indicators to see whether the administration will course-correct or slide further into compromise:

  • Legislative Language on Investigative Powers: Watch the final draft bills regarding the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. If prosecutors retain residual powers to direct or supplement investigations, the reform has failed.
  • Personnel Shuffles at the Justice Ministry: Pay attention to whether future appointments lean further toward career prosecutors or return to the goal of civilian-led administrative oversight.
  • Handling of High-Profile Cases: Monitor how the administration responds when prosecutors target political allies. Concessions made under the table will quickly manifest as visible retreats in high-stakes legal battles.

Real reform requires an unwavering mandate backed by public support, not quiet deals struck in bureaucratic hallways. If the Lee administration continues down the path of least resistance, it will find that the prosecutors it sought to appease were only sharpening their knives for the day the current lease on power expires.
