The Warning Signs: Why Public Sentiment is Turning Against the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 12, 2026

If you have been scrolling through the South Korean news cycle lately, it is impossible to ignore the mounting anxiety surrounding President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings. For two consecutive weeks, the numbers have been trending downward, and the gap between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the liberal party currently holding the presidency—and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), has narrowed to a statistical dead heat. To the casual observer, these might look like the typical oscillations of a high-pressure political environment. However, beneath these figures lies a brewing frustration that suggests something more profound is at stake.

Despite significant national successes, such as the recent fifth launch of the Nuri space rocket, public sentiment remains surprisingly cold. Why are these major achievements failing to lift the government’s standing? The answer lies in the disconnect between grand national projects and the daily struggles of the average voter. To understand the current climate, we must look past the headlines and examine the friction between the government’s reform agenda and the reality on the ground.

A Shifting Political Landscape: The Disconnect Between Reform and Reality

The latest polling data paints a sobering picture for the Lee Jae-myung administration. With disapproval ratings climbing to 56%, the public is signaling a clear warning. The DPK’s approval has dipped by roughly 7 percentage points to 35.5%, while the conservative PPP has seen a modest rise. For the ruling party, this is a moment of reckoning. It is a mistake to view these shifts as mere noise; they are a direct reaction to a perception of administrative fatigue.

The irony is stark: the government has secured major wins in science and technology, but these victories are being swallowed by domestic grievances. The primary culprits for this dip are internal disputes regarding the appointment of candidates for the newly proposed Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA) and, more pressingly, the relentless surge in real estate prices. Reports indicate that apartment prices in Seoul have risen for 87 consecutive weeks. For a family struggling to secure housing or manage living costs, the success of a space rocket feels like a distant, hollow echo compared to the crushing weight of their bank statements.

Furthermore, internal party friction over the MCIA nominations has fueled a sense of disillusionment among the DPK’s core supporters. When a government runs on a mandate of reform but appears preoccupied with internal squabbling, the base begins to lose faith. If the DPK hopes to regain its footing, it must stop blaming external forces and instead conduct a rigorous internal audit of its priorities.

The Rise of the Cho Kuk Innovation Party

While the DPK stumbles, the Cho Kuk Innovation Party—a smaller, more ideologically aggressive liberal party—has hit an approval milestone, breaking the 8% mark for the first time. Some analysts see this as a sign that the DPK has become too institutionalized and cautious, leaving a vacuum for a more "sharp" and confrontational reformist force.

While the current numbers are not enough to drastically alter the legislative balance, the trajectory is clear: voters are searching for a more decisive voice. Whether the Cho Kuk Innovation Party can sustain this momentum and break into the double digits will be the true test. For the DPK, this represents a unique challenge: they must manage this new competitor while simultaneously proving that they can deliver on their promises of reform, or risk losing their role as the primary standard-bearer for the liberal movement.

Seoul's Cold Warning and the Burden of Policy Failure

At the center of this political storm is Seoul, the barometer for national sentiment. Public anger is concentrated in three areas: real estate policy (cited by 18% of those disapproving of the government), national security and North Korea-related issues (which saw a 12 percentage point surge in negative sentiment), and the ongoing controversies surrounding prosecutorial reform.

The Seoul electorate is particularly sensitive to economic instability. With the capital’s housing market showing no signs of cooling, the government’s inability to tame prices has become the single largest fuse for public discontent. When combined with perceived amateurism in the execution of judicial reforms, the result is a massive decline in public trust. If the government continues to ignore the "icy" sentiment in the capital, they risk a total collapse of their support base in the next election cycle, regardless of what the party leadership expects.

The Paradox of Justice: The Yoon Suk-yeol Verdict

Perhaps the most shocking data point in recent surveys concerns the public perception of the imprisoned former president, Yoon Suk-yeol. Despite his impeachment and subsequent incarceration for his role in the collapse of his administration, a recent poll found that 50.6% of respondents believe a "not guilty" verdict would have been appropriate.

This is a jarring figure. It suggests that, regardless of the legal reality, a significant portion of the public remains either sympathetic to the former conservative regime or deeply skeptical of the current judicial process. This gap between the historical truth—the systemic abuse of power that led to his downfall—and current public opinion is a dangerous symptom of social polarization. It indicates that the work of "historical reckoning" is far from complete. When history can be rewritten or forgotten so easily, the foundation of a democratic society becomes dangerously fragile.

The Golden Time for Judicial Reform

The public’s frustration is further stoked by the potential release of Kim Keon-hee, the former first lady, as her detention period nears expiration. With 56.7% of the public opposing her release, there is a clear demand for accountability. Simultaneously, 50.8% of respondents support the impeachment of the current Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, citing a profound lack of trust in the judiciary.

These numbers indicate that the public views the current judicial system as a relic of a past that protects the privileged rather than serving justice. The call for the Chief Justice's impeachment is not just a political tactic; it is a desperate plea for a complete overhaul of the institutions that are supposed to be the "last bastion" of democracy.

What to Watch Next

  • The MCIA Appointments: Watch to see if the DPK can resolve the internal conflict regarding the MCIA nominations without further damaging their reputation.
  • Real Estate Measures: Any concrete, actionable plan to curb Seoul’s housing prices will be the primary indicator of whether the government is truly listening to the public.
  • Judicial Accountability: The handling of the former first lady’s detention and the ongoing debate over the Chief Justice’s tenure will determine whether the administration can maintain its moral authority or whether it will succumb to the perception that the political elite are beyond the reach of the law.

The government is currently at a crossroads. It can choose to ignore these warnings and continue on its current path, or it can acknowledge the fatigue and frustration of the people and pivot toward a more pragmatic, transparent, and responsive style of governance. Time is running out, and the next few weeks will likely define the remainder of the current administration’s tenure.
