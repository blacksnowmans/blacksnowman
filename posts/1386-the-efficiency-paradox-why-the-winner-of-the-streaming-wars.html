The Efficiency Paradox: Why the "Winner" of the Streaming Wars is Cutting Thousands of Jobs

October 22, 2026

Imagine a company that dominates its global market, pulls in nearly $20 billion in annual operating profit, and produces the most-watched television on the planet. By every traditional metric of the "Streaming Wars," this company—Netflix—has already won. Yet, inside the company’s headquarters, the mood is far from celebratory. Despite the massive success of Korean hits like Squid Game and a dominant lead over rivals like Disney+ and Amazon Prime Video, the industry giant recently executed its largest layoff in history, slashing 6% of its workforce in a single night.

To the casual observer, Netflix looks invincible. But if you look closer at the structural shifts in how people consume media, a different story emerges. The "winner" of the streaming era is discovering that its greatest enemy isn't another Hollywood studio, but a fundamental change in the human attention span and a disastrously expensive pivot into gaming. For those watching the Korean market—which has become Netflix's most vital content factory—these tremors in Silicon Valley have massive implications for the future of the Hallyu (Korean Wave) export machine.

Record Profits Meet the Great Layoff

The financial disconnect at Netflix is staggering. In the second quarter of 2026, the company reported an operating profit of $4.19 billion, with a healthy operating margin of 33.4%. On paper, the company is a cash cow, pacing toward an annual operating profit of approximately $15 billion to $22 billion (20 to 30 trillion won). Yet, its stock price has plummeted more than 40% over the past year.

On October 14, 2026, the axe fell. Netflix terminated approximately 1,000 employees, followed immediately by a shake-up of its C-suite executives, including the appointment of Peter Rice as a new content lead. While some analysts attribute this to a temporary lack of "mega-hits," the reality is a deeper structural crisis. The very business model of subscription-based video on demand (SVOD) is being questioned.

The industry is currently grappling with "Streamflation." In Korea and beyond, the total cost of maintaining various streaming subscriptions now often exceeds $60 (about 80,000 won) per month. This has led to "subscription dieting," where users no longer keep their accounts active year-round. Instead, they act like "nomads," subscribing for a single month to binge a specific show and then immediately canceling. Netflix has attempted to curb this with "dark patterns"—complex user interfaces designed to make canceling as difficult as possible—but these tactics have only fueled consumer resentment and increased calls for regulatory oversight from agencies like the Korea Fair Trade Commission.

The data tells a sobering story: while Netflix’s revenue grew by 14.7% in the first half of 2026, actual watch time increased by a meager 2.0%. People are paying more, but they are watching less. The platform is losing its "stickiness," and the market has noticed.

The Korean Engine and the AI Pivot

To maintain its lead, Netflix has leaned heavily on South Korea. Under the current political climate in Seoul, where the government of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea have emphasized the "creative economy" as a pillar of national growth, Netflix has found a willing partner. The company previously announced a $2.5 billion (about 3.3 trillion won) investment plan for Korean content, and that bet has paid off spectacularly in terms of raw viewership.

In July 2025, Squid Game Season 3 became a global phenomenon. In the United States alone, it racked up 5.4 billion minutes of watch time, making it the #1 program across all of broadcast, cable, and streaming. Another Korean production, K-Pop Demon Hunters, also broke into the Top 10 with 3.51 billion minutes. For a brief moment, Korean stories weren't just part of the menu; they were the menu.

However, even the "Squid Game" effect isn't enough to offset rising production costs. To combat this, Netflix is aggressively pivoting toward Generative AI. The sci-fi series The Eternaut, produced in Argentina, served as a test case. By using AI for visual effects (VFX) and massive scale invasion scenes, Netflix reportedly increased production speed by tenfold while drastically cutting costs.

This is the new Netflix strategy: use high-prestige, high-cost "tentpole" series like Stranger Things (which costs upwards of $60 million per episode) to draw people in, while using AI to lower the floor for everything else. By 2025, the ratio of content amortization costs to revenue dropped to 36.3%, down from 42.1% in 2023. The company is becoming more "efficient," but critics argue this is leading to "AI Slop"—content that fills the library but lacks the soul that made the platform a cultural touchstone.

The Losing Battle for the Living Room

The most existential threat to Netflix isn't Disney or HBO; it's YouTube. The battle for the "living room screen" is being won by creators who produce content for a fraction of a percent of a Netflix budget.

The structural advantage of YouTube is devastating. Netflix must front hundreds of millions of dollars in production costs and take 100% of the risk. If a show fails, Netflix loses the money. On the other hand, YouTube takes zero risk. Its creators fund their own videos, and YouTube only shares revenue if the content is successful. As one industry insider put it, Netflix is fighting a war with heavy tanks, while YouTube is an endless swarm of drones.

According to Nielsen’s "The Gauge" data, the gap between the two is widening. In January 2025, YouTube held a 10.0% share of total TV viewing time in the U.S., while Netflix held 8.6%. by July 2026, YouTube had surged to 14.2%, while Netflix had actually dropped to 7.8%. Even though Netflix remains the leader among paid streaming services, it is losing the war for "attention."

Modern audiences, particularly younger demographics, are increasingly choosing the "dopamine hit" of short-form videos or the authentic connection of a YouTuber’s vlog over a scripted 10-episode drama. When a person sits on their couch today, they aren't just choosing between a movie and a TV show; they are choosing between a $100 million production and a 15-minute video about a cooking challenge. Increasingly, they are choosing the latter.

The Ten-Billion-Dollar Gaming Blunder

Recognizing that movies and TV shows might not be enough to keep subscribers from canceling, Netflix attempted a massive "hail mary": video games. The company reportedly funneled more than $1.1 billion (1.5 trillion won) into acquiring gaming studios and talent, with some estimates suggesting the total investment and IP licensing costs reached $7.5 billion (10 trillion won).

The company acquired massive titles like Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas and Red Dead Redemption, hoping to transform its app into an all-in-one entertainment hub. On the surface, the numbers looked okay—GTA recorded 40 million downloads. But the engagement figures were a disaster. Data from Apptopia and Omdia revealed that less than 1% of Netflix's daily active users actually played the games.

The technical hurdles were too high. Most Netflix users access the service on smart TVs or mobile devices not optimized for high-end gaming. The graphics were often downgraded, and the touch-screen controls were clunky. Attempting to run these games via cloud streaming also failed to gain traction.

The fallout has been swift. Since late 2024, Netflix has been shuttering its major game development teams, including the recent closures of studios like Night School and Moonloot. This failed venture serves as a cautionary tale: simply having a large pile of cash and a famous brand name does not guarantee success in a different medium. Netflix tried to buy its way into the gaming world, but it forgot that gaming requires a completely different ecosystem of hardware and user habits.

What to Watch Next

The next twelve months will determine if Netflix can stabilize its "castle" or if the cracks will continue to widen. Investors and fans should keep a close eye on three specific developments:

  • The AI Content Backlash: As Netflix integrates more Generative AI into its Korean and global productions to save money, watch for the audience's reaction. If the quality of "mid-tier" shows drops significantly, the subscription churn will likely accelerate.
  • The YouTube Integration: Watch for whether Netflix attempts to mimic YouTube's social features or moves more aggressively into "unscripted" and "creator-style" content to reclaim watch time.
  • Regulatory Pressure in Seoul: With the Democratic Party of Korea holding a strong majority, there is increasing talk of "fair compensation" laws that would require global streamers to share more profits with local creators and pay "network usage fees" to Korean internet service providers (ISPs) like SK Broadband. These costs could further squeeze Netflix’s margins.

Netflix is currently a "profitable giant in retreat." It has more money than ever, but less of our time. In the world of modern media, time is the only currency that truly matters.
