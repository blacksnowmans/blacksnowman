The $30 Million Bill and a Broken Defense: How Yoon Suk-yeol’s Election Law Verdict Threatens South Korea’s Conservatives

November 04, 2026

When the presiding judge at the Seoul High Court delivered his verdict on November 4, 2026, it marked an unprecedented legal and financial reckoning for South Korea’s conservative establishment. Yoon Suk-yeol, already stripped of power following his disastrous declaration of emergency martial law and currently held behind bars, was seeking to overturn a lower court conviction for violating the Public Official Election Act. Instead, Judge Baek Seung-yeop dismissed the appeal entirely, confirming an 18-month prison sentence suspended for three years.

In South Korea’s strict legal system, any criminal fine over 1 million won (about $750) or any suspended prison sentence under election law automatically invalidates an elected official’s victory. While Mr. Yoon had already been removed from the presidency through impeachment, the confirmation of a victory-invalidating sentence carried a brutal sting in its tail: the National Election Commission (NEC) is now mandated to claw back the entire 39.7 billion won (roughly $29.5 million) in public campaign subsidies awarded to his campaign in 2022. For the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), this ruling does not merely close the book on a disgraced former leader; it lands the party with an existential financial burden and shatters what remained of its political cohesion.

The Shaman Controversy and the $30 Million Clawback

The core of the criminal conviction against Mr. Yoon stems from calculated falsehoods during the heated 2022 presidential campaign. At the time, public attention was transfixed by allegations surrounding Mr. Yoon’s ties to folk shamans—a scandal ignited when he appeared at a televised debate with the Chinese character for "King" (王) scrawled across his palm. Facing intense scrutiny over his relationship with a spiritual mentor named Jeon Seong-bae (widely known as "Geonjin Beopsa"), Mr. Yoon repeatedly insisted that he had only crossed paths with Jeon briefly through party intermediaries and that his wife, Kim Keon-hee, had never met him.

The appellate court found that this was a deliberate lie. Evidence presented during the trial revealed that Kim Keon-hee had maintained close personal ties with Jeon dating back to March 2013. Furthermore, Mr. Yoon had been introduced to the shaman by his wife, visited Jeon’s shrine to perform traditional deep bows, and hosted the shaman at their private residence. The court concluded that these were not casual misstatements, but deliberate, fraudulent misrepresentations designed to deceive the electorate and salvage poll numbers in a razor-thin presidential race.

With the invalidation of the election victory finalized, the legal consequences fall squarely on the People Power Party. Under South Korean law, the party must repay the campaign subsidies within three to four months. A 39.7 billion won (about $29.5 million) penalty would bankrupt most political organizations, yet the PPP’s leadership has displayed a curious calm. That confidence rests on substantial real estate and financial reserves:

  • Total Claimed Obligation: 39.797 billion won (approximately $29.5 million) to the National Election Commission.
  • Available Liquid Cash: Roughly 19 billion won (about $14 million) sitting in the central party’s accounts as of the second quarter of 2026.
  • Party Headquarters Asset: The central party building in Seoul’s political hub of Yeouido, originally purchased for about 45 billion won, is currently estimated to have a market value between 100 billion and 150 billion won ($74 million to $110 million), with a book value of 53.9 billion won (about $40 million).
  • Total Party Assets: Estimated between 150 billion and 200 billion won ($110 million to $150 million).

Rather than liquidating its assets immediately, the PPP appears intent on playing a cynical political game. Internal strategies include taking out commercial bank loans against the Yeouido headquarters, asking sitting conservative lawmakers to contribute 100 million won (around $74,000) each, and staging public fundraising drives. By framing the court-ordered clawback as "political persecution" by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung, the PPP hopes to drag out the payment process until early next year to whip its base into a frenzy and solicit sympathetic donations.

"Too Drunk to Conspire": Yoon’s Desperate Legal Defenses

While the election law verdict strips away the legitimacy of Mr. Yoon's 2022 victory, his legal peril runs far deeper. In separate proceedings before Criminal Division 12-1 of the Seoul High Court, Mr. Yoon is on trial for insurrection related to his unconstitutional attempt to impose emergency martial law. The defense arguments mounted by his legal team have baffled both legal analysts and the general public.

Addressing a crucial November 9, 2024 dinner meeting at the defense minister's residence where the deployment of troops was allegedly planned, Mr. Yoon’s defense team argued that criminal conspiracy was impossible because the attendees were "blind drunk." According to his lawyers, key commanders—including Kwak Jong-geun, then head of the Army Special Warfare Command, and Yeo In-hyung, former Defense Counterintelligence Commander—were so severely intoxicated that coherent communication was out of the question.

"The argument that the commander-in-chief and his top generals were too heavily intoxicated to remember planning martial law is not a defense; it is a confession of grotesque negligence and total unfitness to hold state power."

Mr. Yoon’s defense has piled absurdity upon absurdity, arguing that because the martial law decree was announced on live television, it could not possibly have been a real coup d'état, and disavowing any connection to operational notebooks that detailed advance planning. For the South Korean public, watching a former leader attempt to dodge treason charges by pleading a drunken blackout has only deepened the national outrage.

Meanwhile, the political figures who once formed the core of Mr. Yoon’s inner circle are falling one by one. Representative Kweon Seong-dong, long regarded as the chief operative of the pro-Yoon faction, was formally stripped of his parliamentary seat after the Supreme Court upheld a two-year prison sentence and a 100 million won ($74,000) forfeiture for political fund violations. Notably, Kweon’s appeal was heard by the exact same appellate panel that rejected Mr. Yoon’s election law challenge. His downfall ends his long-standing dominion over his district in Gangneung, Gangwon Province, triggering a high-stakes by-election and gutting the conservative machine in the region.

Ethics Scandals, Lawmaker Extremism, and a Strained Judiciary

The disintegration of the conservative camp is compounded by widening scandals around its remaining standard-bearers. Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has been formally referred to the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) by whistleblowers who allege that he actively quashed a police investigation into kickbacks surrounding his signature Han River bus project, reportedly telling aides that there was no need to "spoon-feed the police."

Concurrently, Representative Han Dong-hoon and his family face police inquiries over allegations that his daughter’s academic portfolio was padded using a mobile app ("Sharey") built by her US-based aunt to win awards from public institutions—an ironic twist for a former justice minister who built his political brand on prosecuting the nepotistic admissions practices of his rivals.

Desperation has also fueled outright extremism within the PPP. Representative Joo Jin-woo, a former prosecutor and key ally from Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, sparked widespread condemnation by filming a promotional video praising Colonel Kim Hyun-tae, the former commander of the 707th Special Mission Group who received a 12-year sentence for leading the armed assault on the National Assembly during the martial law crisis. Joo hailed the convicted insurrectionist as a hero fighting to "protect free South Korea," underscoring the deep embrace of reactionary radicalism among embattled conservatives.

Yet the judiciary itself is struggling under an erosion of public trust. Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae faces mounting scrutiny over revelations that top justices routinely collected millions of won in under-the-table "competency development" cash allowances from court budgets, receiving roughly four times the allowance permitted for regular court staff. Chief Justice Jo has also been implicated in doctoring official dining expense reports to circumvent the Kim Young-ran Act (the Anti-Graft Act), artificially inflating attendee headcounts on receipts for high-end meals with journalists and academics to stay under the statutory spending caps.

What to Watch Next

As the legal fallout ripples across Yeouido, several concrete flashpoints will determine how South Korea's political landscape reshapes itself over the coming months:

  • The NEC Repayment Timeline: Watch whether the National Election Commission issues an official demand for the 39.7 billion won campaign refund and whether the PPP files injunctions to delay payments past early next year. Any attempt to stall will trigger a fierce legislative battle with the ruling Democratic Party.
  • The Gangneung By-Election: The race to fill Kweon Seong-dong’s vacant seat will serve as a bellwether for the conservative brand. If the Democratic Party captures this traditional conservative stronghold, it will signal the structural collapse of the old pro-Yoon voting bloc.
  • Verdict in the Insurrection Trial: The High Court’s ruling on Mr. Yoon’s martial law charges will provide the definitive legal judgment on the December 2024 crisis, likely dealing a fatal blow to the "drunken dinner" defense and setting a constitutional precedent for accountability at the pinnacle of power.
