South Korea’s Education Minister Resigns Over Exam Chaos as Political and Reform Debates Grow

November 23, 2026

On the evening of November 22, South Korea witnessed a remarkably swift ministerial exit. Within roughly seven hours, Education Minister Choi Gyo-jin moved from tendering his resignation to having his formal dismissal signed off by President Lee Jae-myung. According to Korean broadcasters MBC News and Yonhap News, the speed of the presidential response amounted to a de facto firing. The catalyst was not a financial scandal or a diplomatic misstep, but mounting public anger over preparation failures and administrative confusion surrounding the Suneung, South Korea’s annual national college entrance exam.

For readers outside the country, the notion that confusion over a standardized test could topple a cabinet minister in a single afternoon may seem striking. Yet in South Korea, the Suneung functions as one of the most consequential and closely scrutinized institutions in public life—a high-stakes milestone for students and families where any perceived lapse in fairness or execution quickly becomes a national political crisis. Following Choi’s departure, reporting from outlets including KBS News, SBS News, and the Hankook Ilbo highlights a widening rift among the ruling Democratic Party, the main conservative opposition People Power Party, student advocates, and educators over who bears ultimate responsibility and whether the entrance exam system itself needs fundamental reform.

How the Exam Turmoil Triggered a Rapid Dismissal

According to DTNEWS24, Kukmin Ilbo, and several other domestic outlets, Choi’s resignation was directly prompted by criticism over mismanagement and preparation shortcomings during this year’s exam cycle, a situation multiple Korean news organizations described as "Suneung chaos." Reporting from KBS News, Busan Ilbo, and Jayu Ilbo noted that Choi stepped down stating he felt deep responsibility for the exam turmoil, while Yonhap News TV reported that he acknowledged criticism over inadequate preparation.

Behind that broad acknowledgment were specific grievances that had eroded confidence in his leadership. News1 reported that Choi’s position was undermined by controversies surrounding the government’s artificial intelligence response measures for the exam, which compounded earlier disputes. YTN pointed to past controversial remarks by the minister—including a widely criticized statement translated as "I can't do it"—and questioned his perceived absence during the critical exam period. Jayu Ilbo further reported that discontent had reached the point where even students who had taken the exam were pressuring the minister to resign.

Once Choi offered to step down on November 22, the presidential office moved without hesitation. MBC News reported that President Lee accepted the resignation roughly 50 minutes after it was submitted, a pace that both MBC News and Munhwa Ilbo characterized as a de facto dismissal. By around 7:22 p.m. the same evening, Newsis, Hankyoreh, and YTN reported that President Lee had formally approved the dismissal order—seven hours after accepting the resignation, according to Chosunbiz. Completing the entire administrative process within a single day signaled how sensitive public sentiment among test-takers and parents had become.

In approving the dismissal, President Lee framed the decision around a core national standard. According to Newsis and Hankyoreh, the president stated that test fairness is a "non-negotiable principle." That language underscored that the government viewed the crisis not merely as routine administrative underperformance, but as a breach of the exam’s foundational integrity. Still, as MBC News noted in its evening coverage, removing the minister raised an immediate follow-up question: how the government intends to rebuild public trust in an evaluation system whose credibility has been shaken. Yonhap News placed the exit in a broader historical pattern, describing a recurring "cruel history" in South Korea in which Suneung controversies repeatedly force both the head of the national testing institute and the education minister out of office in succession.

A Sharp Political Divide Over Accountability and Succession

With the Education Ministry suddenly leaderless, South Korea’s major political parties offered starkly different interpretations of what Choi’s departure meant. The People Power Party, the country’s main conservative opposition, immediately sought to keep pressure on the administration rather than treating the resignation as the end of the story. According to SBS News and the Chosun Ilbo, the opposition characterized the turmoil as proof of "a country where you cannot even trust the Suneung," taking aim at the government over instability in an institution widely seen as a baseline guarantee of social fairness.

Opposition lawmakers insisted that a single resignation could not contain the political fallout. SBS News, Chosun Ilbo, KBS News, and Munhwa Ilbo all reported the People Power Party’s stance that the controversy "cannot end with Minister Choi's resignation." Munhwa Ilbo added that the opposition demanded a thorough investigation into why the poor response occurred in the first place. Crucially, the People Power Party directed its criticism upward toward the presidential office. According to KBS News, MBC News, and The Fact, opposition figures raised the issue of presidential responsibility and demanded that President Lee speak out directly on the exam chaos rather than letting his statement on test fairness serve as the final word.

The ruling Democratic Party, by contrast, framed the resignation as a turning point for administrative renewal and policy adjustment. According to The Fact, the ruling party emphasized "educational change" in responding to Choi’s exit, seeking to shift focus from retrospective blame toward future governance. In statements reported by KBS News, MBC News, and Sisaon, the Democratic Party stressed the need to appoint a successor with strong expertise to guide the ministry through a period of rapid educational transformation, including the arrival of the AI era and shifting university admissions conditions.

Even so, the question of who will actually take over the ministry remains an open political debate. While the ruling party publicly called for a qualified specialist, YTN noted lingering questions over whether President Lee’s eventual nominee will be an academic or policy expert, or a political figure tapped to stabilize the ministry under intense public scrutiny.

Students Demand Remedies While Educators Question the System

While lawmakers debated ministerial succession and presidential accountability, students directly affected by the disruption pressed for practical solutions. According to MBC News, a student-led advocacy group known as the Suneung Countermeasure Coalition issued a statement on November 22 insisting that the matter "must not end with the minister's resignation" and urging concrete relief measures for harmed test-takers. With score processing and university application schedules approaching, observers noted that student frustration could persist unless education and testing authorities present tangible remedies rather than personnel changes alone.

Across the broader education sector, the crisis also revived longstanding doubts about South Korea’s heavy reliance on the Suneung itself. DTNEWS24 reported growing calls within educational circles to "change the college admissions system," reflecting the view that recurring exam disruptions stem from structural flaws rather than an individual official’s missteps. Speaking to the Hankook Ilbo, Education Superintendent Ahn Min-seok called for a clean break from the current testing model:

"Goodbye Suneung! It is time to break away from memorization and rote-learning evaluation."

That skepticism was echoed in commentary from the education outlet Education Plus, which questioned what simply replacing the minister could actually accomplish. For critics of the status quo, breaking the cycle in which testing agency heads and education ministers repeatedly resign over exam mishaps requires addressing both the operational causes of this year’s chaos and the underlying structure of South Korea’s evaluation system.

What to Watch Next

As the immediate shock of November 22 settles, several concrete developments will determine whether this episode leads to lasting change or follows South Korea’s familiar pattern of crisis management:

  • The Choice of Successor: Watch whom President Lee nominates to lead the Education Ministry—specifically whether the administration selects a career educator or policy specialist, as the Democratic Party has urged, or turns to a political appointee to manage the fallout.
  • Relief Measures for Students: With university admissions timelines moving forward, watch for how the Education Ministry and testing authorities respond to the Suneung Countermeasure Coalition’s demands for tangible remedies for affected test-takers.
  • Investigation and Presidential Response: Monitor whether the government launches a formal inquiry into the causes of the preparation failures, and whether President Lee issues a fuller public explanation in response to People Power Party demands.
  • Traction for Admissions Reform: Watch whether calls from figures such as Education Superintendent Ahn Min-seok to move away from rote-based standardized testing gain broader political momentum, or fade once this year’s exam cycle concludes.
