The "Prosecution Service" Rebranding: A Reform or a Mirage?

September 10, 2026

"I stand here today with a heavy heart, for something truly unbelievable has occurred." These were the opening words of Shin Jang-sik, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party—a progressive, reform-minded splinter party—during his recent address to the National Assembly. His tone reflected the growing sentiment among reformers and activists who believe the years of effort poured into curbing the power of South Korea’s formidable prosecution service are being dismantled.

The controversy centers on the Ministry of Justice’s sudden unveiling of a legislative proposal to reorganize the prosecution structure. While the ostensible goal of recent reform movements has been to separate investigative powers from prosecutorial ones—a move intended to check the unchecked authority of the state—critics argue that the new plan is merely a shell game. It promises reform but keeps the organizational bloatedness intact, leading many to ask: is this a genuine transformation, or just a new nameplate on an old house?

The Structural Illusion: Why the Prosecution Remains Intact

The Ministry of Justice’s proposal for the new "Prosecution Service" (an agency meant to handle prosecutions once investigative powers are stripped away) involves a total workforce of over 8,400 people. While the agency’s exterior looks slightly trimmed, the core number of prosecutors remains unchanged. The Ministry justifies this by citing a long-standing freeze on personnel since 2014 and an increased workload for trial support.

However, reformers see this as a convenient excuse to preserve a bureaucratic machine that contradicts the very goal of separating investigation from prosecution. Shin Jang-sik did not mince words, calling the plan "a facelift, not a reform." The critique is supported by figures like Choo Mi-ae, a former Justice Minister and long-time proponent of dismantling the prosecution's monopoly on investigative power. The argument is simple: if the agency’s investigative remit is removed, the number of prosecutors should logically shrink. Instead, the government is building an organization first and filling it with people later, wasting a critical "golden time" for systemic change.

Furthermore, the Ministry’s plan to establish a "Judicial Control Department" within local branches—ostensibly to oversee cases where police decide not to move forward—has raised red flags. Critics fear this is just another way for the prosecution to maintain its grip on police investigations under a different name. Instead of creating new layers of oversight, they argue, the government should be slimming down the agency and reassigning expert investigators to independent bodies like the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency.

A Political Shift to the Right

The frustration is directed not just at the conservative government, but at the main opposition, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s largest liberal party. Many activists are asking why the DPK—the "big house" of the liberal bloc—has remained so quiet while these reforms were watered down.

South Korean politics appears to be drifting toward the right. The DPK’s recent hesitance on progressive tax reforms and its tendency to prioritize the complaints of residents in wealthy, high-cost housing districts over the struggles of average citizens have led to accusations that it is losing its identity. The party is seemingly obsessed with the idea that by "moderating" its stance, it can capture conservative voters and win future elections. Yet, this tactical calculation is proving to be a double-edged sword, alienating the party's core base of young, non-homeowning voters who are struggling to pay monthly rents while the political elite argues over tax cuts for the wealthy.

The Rebuilding Korea Party has signaled that it may reconsider its relationship with the DPK. If the Democrats continue to act as a "second team" to conservative interests rather than a force for structural change, the entire liberal movement risks being sidelined. The consensus among the grassroots is that if the party fails to address the growing economic inequality, its future electoral prospects—including the 2028 general election—will be built on sand.

The Great Economic Disconnect

The government points to macroeconomic indicators to highlight its "success." In August alone, exports hit about $98 billion (approx. 130 trillion won), a 68.7% jump from the previous year. Projections for 2026 suggest a 3.3% GDP growth rate, the highest in five years. On paper, South Korea is a high-flying economy.

But as Shin Jang-sik aptly put it, "A high-altitude altimeter doesn't show you who is inside the plane, or how those left on the ground are living." While the export-heavy conglomerates celebrate record numbers, the domestic economy is in tatters. Youth unemployment has hit a five-year high of 6.8%, and the survival rate for small business owners after five years is a dismal 25%.

The human cost of this inequality is staggering. South Korea’s elderly poverty rate remains the highest among OECD nations at nearly 40%. The suicide rate, consistently the highest in the OECD for two decades, stands at 29.1 per 100,000 people—a grim testament to societal despair. Meanwhile, the country’s birth rate has plummeted to 0.8, a figure that economists and sociologists alike view as a desperate scream from a generation that sees no viable future.

What to Watch Next

The Rebuilding Korea Party has declared its intent to pivot from purely institutional reform to the fight against inequality. They are calling for the establishment of a "Special Committee on Inequality" in the National Assembly to tackle the interconnected issues of housing, labor, education, and youth poverty.

Key developments to track include:

  • The "Workers' Basic Law": Look for legislative pushes to grant protections to the 8 million "platform workers" and freelancers who currently fall outside the protections of the 70-year-old Labor Standards Act.
  • Policy Re-evaluation: Whether the Democratic Party will respond to the grassroots pressure to return to a more reform-oriented platform or continue its current moderate strategy.
  • Personnel Appointments: Watch who is tapped to lead the new prosecution-related agencies. If they are packed with individuals from the former Prosecutor General-turned-President’s inner circle, it will confirm the fears of the reformist bloc.

The fundamental question for South Korea is no longer just about the scale of the economy, but the nature of its social contract. If the government continues to ignore the widening chasm between the winners of the export boom and the millions struggling on the margins, no amount of organizational restructuring will save it from a deepening crisis of legitimacy.
