Nvidia's $110 Billion Quarter, The Rejection of Jensen Huang, and the New Era of AI Geopolitics

November 19, 2026

When financial markets woke up on November 18, they were braced for a reckoning. For months, Wall Street and global tech hubs had wrestled with a nervous question: What happens to the world's most critical artificial intelligence engine when its visionary architect walks away? The sudden resignation of Jensen Huang had previously sent shockwaves deep enough to fracture South Korea’s benchmark Kospi index. Investors feared that without his charismatic gravity, orders for high-end AI infrastructure would stall and the grand narrative of endless tech expansion would begin to fray.

Instead, the third-quarter numbers dropped like a thunderclap. Nvidia posted a staggering 155 trillion won (about $110 billion) in revenue, alongside a powerful double-growth trajectory in both sales and operating profit that easily cleared market expectations. Yet, the story of November 18 was far bigger than a simple earnings beat. It was a chaotic nexus of corporate power struggles, sudden geopolitical drawbridges, and shifting supply chains that stretched from Silicon Valley boardrooms down to Seoul, Taipei, and Beijing. As Nvidia decisively slammed the door on Huang's attempted return, it triggered a cascading series of events—from renewed rallies in global tech stocks and localized supply chain realignments to sudden regulatory crackdowns by Washingtonwatchdogs.

Defying the Prophet: Why Nvidia Rejected Jensen Huang

The most arresting headline of the day was not financial, but profoundly structural. Alongside its blowout earnings report, Jensen Huang officially signaled his intention to return as Chief Executive Officer. Nvidia's board of directors, however, issued an immediate and uncompromising rejection, stating plainly that there are no plans to reinstate him.

For an industry accustomed to treating founder-CEOs as indispensable deities, this was an extraordinary public rebuff. Just weeks earlier, Huang’s departure had thrown global markets into a tailspin. Yet, the board chose to read the stellar third-quarter performance as proof of institutional independence. By delivering massive vertical growth without Huang at the helm, the company’s current executives and board successfully seized autonomous decision-making power, effectively closing the book on the old era of personalized leadership.

This rejection forces a sudden pivot in how the market views the AI ecosystem. With the era of unquestioned, founder-driven dominance drawing to a close, a fierce internal debate has erupted within Nvidia over whether to recruit an external leader or groom an internal successor. This leadership vacuum has rippled outward across the competitive landscape. As Nvidia recalibrates its leadership structure, rivals like Broadcom smell blood in the water, while major enterprise partners like OpenAI and Anthropic watch closely to see how a more corporate, committee-driven Nvidia will manage its dominant market share.

The Ripple Effect: Kospi's Recovery and the K-Semiconductor Supply Chain

Half a world away in Seoul, the earnings report acted as an immediate financial shock absorber. Fueled by the unexpected strength in Nvidia’s numbers, the Kospi—South Korea's primary stock market index, which had previously breached key psychological floors following Huang's exit—climbed back above the 5,000-point threshold. Major domestic heavyweights like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix saw their share prices rebound sharply as fears of an imminent freeze in AI capital expenditures evaporated.

For South Korea's chipmakers, the numbers offered profound relief. The relentless demand for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—the critical memory architecture powering advanced AI accelerators—remains robust. However, beneath the celebratory market rebound, domestic industry analysts offered a more cautious diagnosis, noting that Nvidia's post-Huang trajectory is a double-edged sword for South Korean suppliers.

On the hardware front, Nvidia moved quickly to lock in physical production capacity. On the very same day as the earnings release, Nvidia restructured its substrate supply contract with Samsung Electro-Mechanics, deepening its manufacturing ties to handle next-generation AI infrastructure. Meanwhile, academic and technical cooperation also accelerated: Yonsei University and Nvidia partnered to open-source a staggering 1.049 billion pieces of radio frequency data, aiming to jumpstart research into 6G wireless foundational models and AI-RAN (Radio Access Network) technology.

The Closing Gates: Export Bans, SEC Inquiries, and Old Shadows

Even as hardware partnerships expanded, Nvidia’s regulatory and geopolitical horizons darkened considerably. Most notably, the company announced it was halting all AI chip supplies to mainland China, despite having secured prior export approval from Washington. While Nvidia had previously maintained that it could weather a decoupling from the Chinese market, this move marks a shift from rhetorical posturing to an aggressive, self-imposed operational cutoff within the world's second-largest economy.

Simultaneously, regulatory pressure inside the United States intensified. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) launched an official inquiry into Nvidia, probing whether the company had misled investors in its corporate disclosures. While a $110 billion quarterly revenue figure demonstrates immense commercial muscle, navigating a federal regulatory investigation introduces an entirely different category of risk.

To compound matters, old shadows re-emerged. Investigative reports surfaced detailing a 2002 arrangement in which Nvidia allegedly acted as a corporate matchmaker, introducing Alice Huang to Alibaba to help secure the massive Chinese e-commerce giant as a marquee client. Bringing these historical networking ties back into the light during a period of intense U.S. regulatory scrutiny and sudden supply blackouts complicates Nvidia’s defensive posture, forcing the firm to answer for past commercial entanglements just as it attempts to forge a new corporate identity.

What to Watch Next

Nvidia’s November 18 watershed moment proves that a company can post astronomical financial results and still find itself standing at a dangerous crossroads. The immediate market euphoria—marked by the recovery of the Kospi and a resurgence in global tech equities—masks deep underlying transitions.

As investors and market observers look toward the months ahead, three critical variables will determine whether Nvidia can maintain its throne:

  • The CEO Succession Battle: Watch closely for whether Nvidia's board opts for an outsider to shake up operations or an internal successor to maintain stability, and how competitors like Broadcom respond to any executive turbulence.
  • The Real-World Impact of China Decoupling: Monitor how Chinese tech giants pivot in the face of Nvidia's total supply freeze, and whether strong non-China enterprise demand can fully offset the lost revenue.
  • Regulatory and Legal Fallout: Pay strict attention to the SEC's investigation and any further developments regarding historical client introductions, which could introduce severe valuation volatility regardless of quarterly earnings reports.

Nvidia has proven beyond a doubt that its microchips can mint money without their legendary founder at the desk. But as regulatory walls close in around Beijing and Washington, and as internal governance undergoes a painful structural overhaul, the real test of Nvidia's resilience has only just begun.
