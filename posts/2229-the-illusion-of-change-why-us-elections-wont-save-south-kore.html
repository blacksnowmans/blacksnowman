The Illusion of Change: Why U.S. Elections Won't Save South Korea

November 11, 2026

As the world watches the U.S. presidential election cycle, a familiar fever grips the Korean political scene. In Seoul, pundits and citizens alike often treat the shifting winds of Washington as a harbinger of destiny for the Korean Peninsula. There is a persistent, if misguided, hope that a victory for one party or the other will fundamentally alter the trajectory of the U.S.-Korea alliance. To look at the situation with clear eyes, however, is to realize that this hope is a fantasy. Whether the Democratic Party—the center-left party currently associated with traditional liberal values in the U.S.—or the Republican Party takes the helm, the core of American policy toward the Korean Peninsula has remained virtually unchanged since the 1953 Armistice Agreement.

For South Korea, this is a moment to confront a cold reality: the United States views the peninsula primarily as a "strategic asset" in its grand design to contain China and maintain global hegemony. Despite the billions of dollars South Korea contributes annually—the 2024 defense cost-sharing contribution alone reached about $1.1 billion (1.52 trillion won)—the fundamental power dynamic remains skewed. The U.S. military has maintained a continuous presence on the peninsula since the Korean War, and regardless of who resides in the White House, their goal remains the same: ensuring that South Korea serves as the frontline bulwark in their geopolitical chessboard.

The Illusion of Policy Divergence

It is a common misconception that the Democratic and Republican parties hold vastly different philosophies regarding Korea. While the rhetoric differs—Democrats often speak of "value-based alliances" while Republicans may demand more overt financial transactions—the strategic outcome remains identical. Under both parties, the U.S. has consistently pushed to integrate South Korea more deeply into its military and economic sub-structure. Even during Democratic administrations, sanctions against North Korea have been reinforced, and the American military footprint in the region has only expanded.

The Democratic Party’s approach is often more subtle, using the language of "alliances" to effectively "gaslight" Seoul into deeper compliance. When they speak of democratic values, they are often simultaneously calculating how to maximize Korea's military contribution. For Washington, South Korea is not an equal partner, but a tool to be managed. The danger for Seoul lies in the belief that a Democratic victory is necessarily a "win" for Korean interests. In truth, both parties view the Korean Peninsula as a controlled environment to be kept in a state of manageable tension, preventing any move toward true, independent sovereignty that might clash with U.S. interests.

The Trap of the Tripartite Alliance

The geopolitical reality has grown even more complex, particularly regarding the U.S.-led push for a trilateral military framework involving Japan. Traditionally, Democratic administrations have favored a stronger, more central role for Tokyo in Northeast Asian security. In this design, Japan is cast as the regional manager, while South Korea is expected to be the frontline actor that bears the physical and political cost.

This "Japan-first" leaning of the U.S. Democratic establishment threatens to institutionalize an asymmetric security order. During the administration of the now-imprisoned former president, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the push for humiliating concessions in diplomatic relations with Japan was largely fueled by quiet, but firm, pressure from Washington. A sweep by the U.S. Democratic Party would likely accelerate this, forcing South Korea further into a regional order where its own national sentiment and historical justice are treated as secondary to the stability of a U.S.-Japan-Korea military bloc. For the current government of President Lee Jae-myung, navigating this bureaucracy is far more difficult than dealing with the erratic, deal-focused nature of a Trump-style presidency, which occasionally opened room for unconventional negotiation.

A Call for Sovereign Action

If South Korea is to be a truly sovereign nation, it must move beyond the cycle of waiting for U.S. election results to define its security. The status quo—a nation that does not even possess the right to command its own troops in wartime—is an abnormality that must be rectified. The retrieval of Wartime Operational Control (OPCON) is not a matter for negotiation with Washington; it is a fundamental right that a state with the world’s fifth-largest military should have asserted long ago.

The path forward for the Lee Jae-myung administration must involve a bold, pragmatic recalibration of national interests:

  • Restoring Military Sovereignty: The immediate and unilateral assertion of OPCON is essential. We must stop relying on foreign command for our survival.
  • Reviewing Overseas Deployments: Deployments such as the Cheonghae Unit in the Strait of Hormuz, which serve American Middle East strategy rather than Korean national interest, must be re-evaluated and withdrawn.
  • Economic Autonomy: The pressure on major Korean conglomerates to invest billions in the U.S., often under the guise of the Inflation Reduction Act or semiconductor legislation, must be scrutinized. Korea must diversify its markets and prioritize its own industrial base over the demands of Washington’s industrial policy.
  • Ending Diplomatic Subservience: When U.S. demands threaten our sovereign interests, the government must be prepared to say "No." True diplomacy is not the art of pleasing a hegemon, but the art of defending one's own people.

The Shadow of the Ukrainian Conflict

The current international landscape, particularly the war in Ukraine, serves as a grim warning for Korea. In the eyes of many analysts, the conflict in Eastern Europe is being managed not by the parties on the ground, but by the geopolitical calculations of Washington and Moscow. There is a high probability that the war in Ukraine will end in a "Korean-style" stalemate—a division along a fixed frontline without a decisive peace, leaving the region in a state of permanent insecurity.

This is the "Korean scenario" playing out in Europe, orchestrated by the same global powers. If South Korea continues to leave its fate in the hands of foreign officials 10,000 kilometers away, it risks being trapped in this cycle of proxy warfare indefinitely. The U.S. military presence in Korea, while vast and costly, is ultimately designed for American strategic goals. We must recognize that relying on the benevolence of a foreign power is a strategy of despair, not a strategy for survival.

What to Watch Next

As the U.S. election unfolds, the most important metric is not who wins, but how the Lee Jae-myung administration asserts Korea's independent voice in the months following. Watch for moves to amend the unequal Status of Forces Agreement (SOFA), concrete steps toward the transfer of OPCON, and a shift toward a more multi-polar diplomatic stance that prioritizes Korean economic stability over forced investment in the U.S. The goal of the next phase of Korean foreign policy must be to ensure that 50 million Koreans are no longer held hostage to the electoral politics of a country with 300 million people. It is time to leave the shadows of client-state diplomacy behind and walk toward a truly independent future.
