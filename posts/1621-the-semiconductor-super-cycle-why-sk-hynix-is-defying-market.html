The Semiconductor Super-Cycle: Why SK Hynix is Defying Market Skeptics

October 28, 2026

Walking through the bustling corridors of Seoul’s Yeouido financial district, you can feel the tension in the air. Investors are locked in a fierce debate over SK Hynix, South Korea’s leading memory chipmaker and the world’s primary supplier of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) for AI. Some argue the stock’s meteoric rise has no ceiling, while others are nervously scanning the horizon for signs of a bubble burst. Yet, despite a strong Korean won—which typically acts as a headwind for export-heavy semiconductor firms—the company’s recent performance has shattered expectations, forcing analysts to rewrite their outlooks on the entire industry.

Defying Currency Pressures and Market Doubts

The market had been bracing for impact. Analysts previously projected an operating profit of around 78 trillion won (approximately $56 billion), fearing that the strengthening won would erode margins. Instead, SK Hynix reported an operating profit exceeding 83 trillion won (approximately $60 billion). This result was not just a small beat; it was a firm rebuke to the skeptics.

While D-RAM shipment guidance met expectations, the real story lies in pricing power. The average selling price (ASP) for D-RAM jumped 29%, and NAND flash memory prices climbed by 25%. This aggressive price growth, even against the headwind of a strong currency, proves that the company’s structural health has reached a new peak. With an operating profit margin reaching 79.7%, SK Hynix is effectively turning nearly 80% of every dollar of revenue into profit. This level of efficiency suggests that this is not merely a temporary boom, but a fundamental shift in the company’s ability to dominate the high-end memory market.

The Path to 2030: Why AI Infrastructure Demand Holds Strong

A common critique in the financial sector is that AI infrastructure spending is an unsustainable bubble. Skeptics point to the B2C (business-to-consumer) market, where tangible AI applications for the average user remain limited, and to enterprises that are struggling to turn massive AI investment into immediate bottom-line revenue. However, viewing these concerns as a "crash in waiting" ignores the reality on the ground.

The GPU rental market tells a different story. Amazon’s leadership has confirmed that their capacity is fully booked for the next two years. Meanwhile, Google—despite its own significant internal AI resources—is increasingly turning to external infrastructure to meet demand. This is not a situation of companies over-investing in a vacuum; it is a race to secure resources that remain in chronic short supply.

Most notably, the industry outlook has shifted. While many previously believed the peak of AI infrastructure investment would arrive by 2028, current data suggests a much longer runway. The paradigm is shifting from "training" AI models to "inference"—the process of AI actually performing tasks. This shift requires a massive, exponential increase in memory processing, creating a structural demand that will likely keep the industry in a growth phase through 2030. It isn't just about HBM; as token processing grows, the need for high-performance standard D-RAM and enterprise-grade SSDs is expanding in lockstep.

Separating Supply Chain Myths from Reality

Conspiracy theories about "artificial supply cuts" have circulated in some corners of the market, suggesting that chipmakers were intentionally limiting production to inflate prices. However, data from key suppliers, such as Japan’s Shin-Etsu and SUMCO, indicates that inventory levels for memory chips have been trending downward since early 2026. This isn't due to production cuts, but to a surge in wafer consumption by memory producers.

Furthermore, electronic disclosures show that both SK Hynix and its domestic rival, Samsung Electronics, are running their manufacturing lines at 100% capacity. Any perceived "lag" in production is merely the physical reality of transitioning lines to produce more complex, high-value HBM chips. Given the complexity of modern semiconductor equipment—where a single EUV machine from the Dutch supplier ASML requires tens of thousands of parts to assemble—bottlenecks are inevitable. The industry is not restricting supply; it is operating at the absolute physical limit of its current infrastructure.

Looking Ahead: The "Virtuous Cycle" of AI

The integration of AI into the global economy is entering a more mature phase, led by companies like Anthropic and the massive investment by tech giants like Google in their own Tensor Processing Units (TPUs). By bypassing traditional, expensive chip architectures, these companies are driving down the "cost per watt" for AI operations. This creates a virtuous cycle: as infrastructure becomes more efficient, the cost of running AI services drops, which encourages further adoption, which in turn fuels further demand for high-end memory components.

For investors, the takeaway is clear: while the current political environment under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration focuses on stabilizing domestic markets, the global semiconductor narrative remains driven by concrete data. With the era of the former president—who is currently serving time in prison—firmly behind us, the industrial focus has shifted toward securing long-term technological dominance.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the Q4 earnings reports. If the current trajectory holds, the possibility of SK Hynix hitting 100 trillion won in annual operating profit is no longer a fringe theory—it is a distinct possibility. Furthermore, monitor the rollout of HBM4; as this next-generation product becomes a larger part of the company's product mix, it will be the ultimate litmus test for whether SK Hynix can maintain its current profit margins against the inevitable entry of new competitors.
