South Korea's Judiciary Under Fire as Legal Fallouts Deepen Over Yoon's Martial Law and Kim Keon-hee's Detention

October 28, 2026

The aftermath of South Korea's short-lived martial law attempt and the legal reckoning facing the country's former political elite have exposed deep fractures in the nation's democratic foundations. For observers outside the country, South Korea often appears to be a high-tech powerhouse humming along with seamless institutional efficiency. Yet beneath that surface lies a fierce struggle over the rule of law, pitting public expectations of accountability against an establishment judiciary that critics accuse of dragging its feet and shielding the powerful.

Recent developments—ranging from controversial rulings in martial law trials to the detention of Kim Keon-hee, wife of the impeached former leader—have brought these tensions to a boil. At the center of the storm is a profound question: Can South Korea's judicial system deliver fair justice when the crimes trace back to the highest levels of recent state power?

The Slippery Slope of Martial Law Rulings

To understand why public anger is mounting, one must look at how the courts are handling trials related to the martial law decree issued by former leader Yoon Suk Yeol, who was subsequently impeached, stripped of power, and imprisoned. Recent rulings by judges like Lee Jin-gwan have raised serious eyebrows by introducing legal interpretations that critics argue strain credulity.

In one prominent case, legal logic applied in a favorable ruling for Yoon regarding opinion poll controversies has sparked concern that similar arguments could benefit other conservative figures, such as Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon. More alarmingly, judgments stemming from the martial law crisis have begun to excuse mid-level public servants—including police and military personnel who followed orders on the night of the decree. By ruling that standing by for emergency duty did not explicitly infringe on constitutional rights, courts have threatened to hollow out charges like abuse of authority. Under this logic, key documents attempting to justify the martial law might effectively escape criminal liability.

The double standard appears even sharper when examining how prosecutors and justice ministry officials are treated. While lower-level correctional and immigration officials have seen their convictions upheld, charges against dispatched prosecutors have been systematically overturned. Courts have described their actions as mere "principled instructions," creating the impression of a judicial cartel shielding its own from accountability. When former Justice Minister Park Sung-jae saw his prison sentence slashed dramatically on appeal from 25 years down to 9 years, legal analysts pointed out that the true danger is not merely the reduced number, but the methods and rationale used to dismantle the original conviction.

Kim Keon-hee's Detention and the Myth of Integrity

While the courts show signs of hesitation in martial law cases, the legal dragnet has tightened around Kim Keon-hee. Following a multi-hour interrogation, a court issued a warrant for her arrest on grounds of destroying evidence and flight risk, extending her detention period initially by two months—with a potential extension up to eight months through the Supreme Court appeals process.

This development cut through rumors that Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae's administration might orchestrate her release. True to form, Kim's defense has relied on sweeping denials, reportedly asking how a man as "clean and principled" as Yoon Suk Yeol could have ever entrusted her with personnel appointments—a defense of influence-peddling that critics find deeply out of touch with the documented facts of institutional capture during their time in power.

Meanwhile, Yoon himself continues to offer defiant explanations for his martial law decree, arguing in public remarks that he never intended to establish a dictatorship and that the harsh measures were merely intended to manage crowds gathering outside the National Assembly. To the public and to victims of the political turmoil, such justifications sound less like defense and more like an evasion of the violent reality of armed troops moving against the legislature.

A New Shift in Executive Oversight Under the Lee Jae-myung Government

As the judiciary navigates these political minefields, the executive branch under President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the progressive Democratic Party, South Korea's main center-left party—has set a distinctly different administrative tone. The Lee administration has moved to integrate oversight agencies more directly into regular governance structures, sparking debates over institutional independence.

President Lee defended the inclusion of the head of the National Office of Investigation in cabinet meetings, arguing that all ministries ultimately operate under the command of the president and prime minister according to the constitution. Rather than treating this as a threat to political neutrality, the administration frames it as a way to ensure policy alignment and administrative efficiency without sacrificing investigative independence.

Lee's approach emphasizes clear accountability, captured in his remark that a president's decisions carry the collective weight of the nation's 52 million citizens. This philosophy stands in stark contrast to the closed-door power dynamics of the previous administration, signaling an executive branch eager to draw a hard line against the perceived abuses of the past.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves forward, several critical fronts will determine whether the country can successfully clean the slate or whether institutional impunity will prevail:

    Appeals and Sentences: Watch how higher courts handle the reduced sentences of former ministers and officials tied to the martial law attempt. If systematic acquittals follow, public pressure for sweeping judicial reform will likely reach a boiling point. The Legal Timeline for Kim Keon-hee: Monitor the Supreme Court proceedings regarding Kim's extended detention and corruption charges, which will serve as a litmus test for the judiciary's willingness to hold elite figures accountable. Legislative and Executive Reform: Keep an eye on how the Democratic Party-led legislature uses parliamentary investigations and institutional reforms to restructure oversight agencies and curb potential executive overreach.

South Korea's democratic institutions are enduring a severe stress test. How the courts rule in the coming months will write a precedent not just for current defendants, but for the integrity of the nation's constitutional order for decades to come.
