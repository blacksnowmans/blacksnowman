The Disciplinary Trap Inside South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

November 27, 2026

Cameras outside a closed-door meeting room at the National Assembly caught Representative Kim Min-seok clutching a printed booklet of party rules. For observers of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party, the image was telling. The party leadership was preparing to navigate a high-stakes internal disciplinary proceeding against Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee, and Kim’s sudden, public focus on procedure raised immediate eyebrows. To critics, brandishing the rulebook at the eleventh hour looked less like a commitment to due process and more like political theater intended to shield the leadership from accusations of selective justice.

The incident reflects a deepening fracture inside the Democratic Party, which currently controls both the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung and the legislative majority. At the heart of the dispute is a bitter fallout from the party’s August 17 National Convention, where unproven accusations of factional conspiracies were used as campaign weapons. Now, as internal ethics bodies weigh punishment for leaked comments made by top officials, the party finds itself trapped in a political double standard—one that threatens internal cohesion and raises fundamental questions about party democracy in South Korea.

A Smear Campaign Without Evidence: The August Convention Fallout

To understand the current crisis inside the ruling party, one must look back to the contentious national convention held this past summer. During the leadership race, Representative Kim Min-seok leveled a grave charge against his internal rivals, claiming the party needed to smash a "secret three-way alliance" consisting of anti-Lee Jae-myung forces, factional instigators, and Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious movement frequently deployed as a political lightning rod in South Korean public discourse.

The accusation was potent, casting Kim’s opponents as covert orchestrators of a shadow alliance aimed at sabotaging party unity. However, four months after winning his post, Kim acknowledged that no concrete evidence existed to support the claim. That admission transformed a standard political spat into a serious legal and ethical problem for the party apparatus.

Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae publicly challenged the leadership over the issue, citing Article 52 of South Korea's Political Parties Act, which strictly penalizes the dissemination of false information during party leadership elections. Choo argued that before the party moves to discipline lower-level officials for casual remarks, the Democratic Party Ethics Tribunal—the internal body tasked with enforcing member discipline—must strictly evaluate the deliberate spread of baseless rumors that distorted the outcome of a national convention.

Representative Jung Chung-rae, one of the primary targets of the convention-era allegations, issued a detailed statement refusing to accept a casual reconciliation. Jung pointed out that while Kim’s camp offered vague expressions of regret, the gesture lacked three essential components of a genuine apology:

  • Specific admission of wrongdoing: Failing to explicitly state what false claims were made.
  • Direct apology to the victim: Neglecting to apologize directly to the individuals targeted by the rumor.
  • Preventive guarantees: Offering zero structural safeguards to prevent similar character assassination in future elections.

Jung warned that allowing such tactics to be swept under the rug would establish a dangerous precedent: candidates could launch malicious, unverified attacks to win key internal elections, knowing they could simply offer a superficial apology after securing power.

Choi Min-hee’s Ethics Hearing and the Factional Dilemma

The conflict erupted into open warfare when Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee was summoned before the Ethics Tribunal over remarks made during a private meeting with local party members in her district. Reports indicated that Choi was recorded telling attendees that President Lee Jae-myung, during his tenure as party leader, had initially opposed the impeachment of former President Yoon Suk-yeol (who was later removed from office and remains incarcerated).

When excerpts of the audio recording circulated in mid-October, Kim Min-seok ordered an internal ethics inspection on October 12, referring the matter to the Ethics Tribunal three days later. Choi agreed to appear before the panel to clarify her comments, but she and her allies within the leadership questioned the sudden speed and fairness of the proceeding.

During a Supreme Council meeting at the National Assembly, pro-Jung lawmakers drew a sharp contrast between Choi’s swift referral and the handling of complaints against Kim. Representative Lee Sung-yoon questioned why party leaders held onto the recording for nearly a month before ordering an emergency inspection. Representative Han Min-soo pointed out that formal petitions requesting discipline against Kim Min-seok for his unverified Shincheonji allegations were already submitted to the Ethics Tribunal, demanding that both cases be judged by the exact same standard.

Political analysts view this dynamic as a classic political "zugzwang"—a situation where any move made by party leadership carries severe costs:

  • If Choi is heavily disciplined: Stripping Choi of her leadership post will trigger an explosive backlash from rank-and-file members and intensify calls to discipline Kim Min-seok for spreading false convention rumors, threatening leadership stability.
  • If Choi is cleared or spared: Suspending her punishment risks alienating influential pro-Lee political commentators and media voices who view her remarks as an unforgivable distortion of the president’s impeachment record.

Institutional Integrity, Judicial Bounds, and Police Raids

The dispute has exposed systemic vulnerabilities within South Korean party governance. Questions have been raised regarding the impartiality of the Democratic Party’s Ethics Tribunal itself. Critics have pointed out that Moon Jin-seok, who serves as the tribunal's deputy head, previously faced controversy over job solicitation practices without incurring party sanctions, weakening the moral authority of the panel when evaluating other members.

This challenge is not unique to the ruling party. The main opposition conservative People Power Party (PPP) experienced a similar battle when its ethics committee voted to expel former Supreme Council member Kim Jong-hyuk over critical media comments. However, the Seoul Southern District Court granted an injunction suspending the punishment, ruling that the party had exceeded its authority. While PPP leadership criticized the court’s decision as an unconstitutional intervention in party autonomy, legal experts note that when internal ethics bodies fail to maintain objective standards, internal political disputes inevitably spill into the state judicial system.

The post-convention fallout has already breached the boundaries of internal party discipline. On November 20, detectives from the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency raided the office and residence of Lee Jong-won, operator of the political YouTube channel Sisa Tapa TV. Police suspect that during the August 14–15 convention polling period, Lee used live broadcasts to instruct viewers to forge their demographic details—such as age and geographic region—to bypass survey quotas and skew opinion polls favoring specific leadership candidates.

The Democratic Party itself filed the criminal complaint that triggered the police raid. However, legal representatives for the channel argued that selective state enforcement—raiding pro-party media while ignoring criminal complaints filed against senior lawmakers for convention disinformation—demonstrates how party disputes are distorting law enforcement priorities.

What to Watch Next

As internal tensions peak, the battle lines are extending beyond the halls of the National Assembly. Tensions are spilling onto the streets, with factional groups announcing public rallies outside Exits 4 and 5 of the National Assembly Station in 여의도 (Yeouido), demanding Representative Kim’s resignation and protesting the disciplinary proceedings against Choi Min-hee.

This internal friction occurs alongside escalating legislative pressure on the judiciary. In recent committee hearings, Democratic Party lawmakers issued explicit warnings to Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, hinting at impeachment proceedings if the high court delivers unfavorable rulings regarding former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo's trial involving insurrection charges. Critics argue that threatening the judiciary while failing to maintain internal procedural justice undermines basic principles of constitutional separation of powers.

Over the coming weeks, political observers should monitor several key developments:

  • The Ethics Tribunal's Ruling on Choi Min-hee: The severity of any penalty imposed on Choi will serve as an immediate test of leadership control and determine whether internal critics initiate formal legal challenges in court.
  • Action on Pending Petitions Against Kim Min-seok: How the party handles formal complaints regarding unproven election claims will demonstrate whether the ruling party can apply uniform disciplinary standards to high-ranking leaders.
  • Police Investigation into Polling Fraud: The ongoing police probe into Sisa Tapa TV could reveal broader operational ties between digital media channels and campaign strategies during the August convention.
  • Senior Lawmaker Interventions: A group of senior Democratic Party parliamentarians recently convened to attempt mediation. Whether senior figures can broker a compromise will show whether traditional party diplomacy can still function under intense factional strain.
