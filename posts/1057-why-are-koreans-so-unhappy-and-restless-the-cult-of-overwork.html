Why Are Koreans So Unhappy and Restless? The Cult of Overwork and the Crisis of Self

October 13, 2026

Step onto the streets of Seoul on any given night, and you will see a society running at a relentless, exhausting pace. Neon lights buzz over offices where employees burn the midnight oil, long after the work day has officially ended. Yet, beneath South Korea's staggering technological advancement and global cultural footprint lies a quiet, pervasive despair. National surveys consistently place South Korea near the bottom of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) member states for life satisfaction. Why is a nation of hyper-achievers so deeply unhappy? According to cultural psychologist and author Dr. Kim Jung-woon, the answer is startlingly simple: South Koreans have forgotten how to ask their own questions.

For generations, the path laid out for South Koreans has been rigid and uniform. From cram schools known as hagwons to grueling preparation for the state university entrance exam, life has been a relentless exercise in answering questions posed by others—parents, teachers, employers, and society at large. In this environment, true rest is viewed as a luxury or, worse, a form of laziness. Recent national statistics show that an overwhelming 92.1% of South Koreans' leisure time is spent on passive activities like watching television or sleeping, while a mere 4.2% is devoted to cultural or artistic participation. Koreans are remarkably good at collapsing from exhaustion, but they have completely forgotten how to rest, reflect, or create.

The Trap of Economic Determinism and the Loss of Context

When asked why life is so difficult, South Koreans almost invariably point to money. The cultural narrative dictates that financial security is the ultimate panacea for all human suffering, leading to a relentless pursuit of wealth—symbolized by the ubiquitous obsession with becoming a geonmulju, or building owner. But Dr. Kim argues that this economic determinism is a dangerous delusion. A person who has forgotten how to think critically will find no happiness, even if they strike it rich.

The tragedy of modern Korean life lies in the unyielding pressure to conform to a predetermined script. Society forces individuals to constantly react to external metrics: How many dates did you go on last month? What is your salary? Have you bought a home? Without stepping back to question the context of these questions, life becomes a hollow routine. When people are conditioned to accept whatever premise is handed to them, their sensitivity to new experiences dulls. The relentless repetition of meaningless work breeds a chronic emotional state that swings endlessly between suffocating boredom and paralyzing anxiety.

True intellectual and emotional survival requires what experts call "editing" one's life—taking apart the fragments of daily existence and reassembling them through a subjective lens. Without this capacity for meta-cognition, or thinking about one's own thinking, wealth is merely a gilded cage.

Physical Dislocation as a Catalyst for Self-Discovery

Breaking free from this cycle requires more than willpower; it often requires a radical change in physical space. Familiar environments—cramped offices, rigid living rooms, and routine commutes—lock the human mind into fixed patterns of despair. Dr. Kim recounts his own experience of stepping away from the public eye in South Korea and disappearing into a quiet, unfamiliar rural village in Japan where nobody knew him. Stripped of his social identity, he was forced to confront an existential void that ultimately sparked profound self-reflection.

South Korea’s hyper-competitive educational system and corporate culture fundamentally crush this kind of existential awakening out of fear that stepping off the beaten path means total social ruin. Overcoming this fear requires what psychologists call visual thinking and spatial disruption. By removing oneself from the constant gaze of a judgmental society, an individual can finally begin to ask the most fundamental, terrifying question: "What do I actually enjoy?"

Rest is not merely physical inactivity; the very Chinese character for rest (休息) depicts a person leaning against a tree to examine their own heart. Koreans have mastered the art of working until they drop, but they have entirely lost the art of introspection.

Borrowing from the Bauhaus: The Intersection of Technology and Art

This same principle of creative editing applies not just to psychology, but to knowledge creation and innovation. Reflecting on the evolution of modern design, Dr. Kim draws a direct line from the historic Bauhaus movement of 1923 to Steve Jobs and Apple. When Apple revolutionized personal computing, it did not do so by copying its corporate rivals function for function. Instead, it embraced the Bauhaus philosophy of merging rigorous technology with humanistic art.

Apple’s adoption of sans-serif typography on the early Macintosh was a watershed moment in design history, creating an entirely new aesthetic experience for users. It was an exercise in what can be called "cross-editing sensory domains"—taking elements from disparate fields and weaving them into a cohesive, emotionally resonant narrative. In an era dominated by artificial intelligence, this ability to synthesize disparate threads of knowledge is more valuable than ever.

With information readily accessible through digital tools and AI models like ChatGPT, mere data collection is worthless. As the German "slip-box" note-taking method demonstrates, true study is not about highlighting text or summarizing a book; it is about recording one's own thoughts in a meta-language. Software tools like Obsidian allow modern thinkers to visualize networks of ideas rather than storing static facts in a warehouse. Information without personal narrative is just noise.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates a rapidly shifting political and economic landscape under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—following the tumultuous impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Suk-yeol—the societal pressure to find stability remains intense. Yet, the systemic anxiety gripping the country cannot be legislated away by government policy alone.

To watch moving forward, keep an eye on how younger generations in South Korea begin to reject traditional corporate paths in favor of independent content creation, decentralized digital communities, and alternative lifestyles. As more individuals adopt personal knowledge management systems and break away from rigid societal expectations, the definition of success in South Korea is slowly shifting.

The ultimate challenge for anyone seeking a more fulfilling life is to stop answering the questions the world demands you answer. Put down the script, pick up a notebook, and start building a world defined not by anxiety, but by your own relentless curiosity.
