South Korea's Judiciary at War with the Constitution: The Cho Kuk Trial and the Battle Over the Supreme Court

October 12, 2026

Walk into a coffee shop in Seoul right now, and the conversation among the regulars is rarely about inflation or housing prices alone. There is a heavier, quieter tension hanging in the air—the kind that usually precedes a constitutional crisis. On the surface, the nightly news is a blur of petty political bickering over expense accounts and procedural fights in the National Assembly. But beneath those ripples lies a much darker current: a direct, unblinking standoff between South Korea's elected lawmakers and its unelected judiciary.

At the center of this storm is Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive minor party that carved out a potent anti-prosecgement and reformist platform. Yet, as Cho fights his own legal battles, the real tremor shaking the country's political foundations is not coming from the legislature or the executive branch led by President Lee Jae-myung. It is coming from the Supreme Court, where Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is accused by critics of transforming the nation’s highest court from a temple of neutral justice into a political command center.

The Fall from Grace: Cho Kuk, Public Opinion, and the Youth Vote

To understand why these legal battles carry such immense weight, one must first look at the man who started the Rebuilding Korea Party. Cho Kuk is a polarizing figure in South Korean politics, a former law professor and cabinet minister whose personal history has become a proxy war for the soul of the progressive movement. Recently, polls have shown that over 54% of the public believes Cho should face criminal punishment regarding ongoing controversies surrounding official expense accounts and discretionary spending.

What stings the Rebuilding Korea Party most is not merely that conservative media outlets have weaponized these figures, but where that skepticism is coming from. Analysts note that hostility toward Cho’s spending controversies is remarkably high among South Korea's 20-30 demographic. For voters in their twenties and thirties—a generation intensely sensitive to the rhetoric of fairness and meritocracy—Cho’s past academic privileges and elite background remain a sore spot. They are less forgiving of perceived hypocrisy, regardless of political affiliation.

This dynamic has handed the conservative People Power Party—South Korea's main conservative opposition—a blunt instrument to bludgeon the party's moral authority. As lawmaker Kim Ki-pyo of the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party, has pointed out, several allegations surrounding Cho and his party's inner workings have moved past the realm of mere political smears into documented facts. Politics in Seoul operates on currency; without moral legitimacy, even the most coherent policy platform turns to ash. Whether the Rebuilding Korea Party can survive this erosion of trust remains one of the most volatile subplots in the National Assembly.

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the Spectre of Judicial Overreach

Yet, if Cho Kuk represents the ongoing culture wars of South Korea's political class, the actions of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae represent something far more dangerous: an institutional coup from the bench.

During recent parliamentary audits, Chief Justice Cho’s demeanor shocked even seasoned political reporters. Rather than answering lawmakers with the measured neutrality expected of South Korea's top jurist, he appeared to read from prepared scripts with a dismissive arrogance, treating the legislature not as a co-equal branch of government, but as an annoying obstacle. When questioned about the Judicial Recommendation Committee, he flatly dismissed the National Assembly's oversight authority, declaring that parliament had no business interfering.

Critics argue this goes far beyond a simple lack of diplomatic tact. It points to a profound misunderstanding—or deliberate defiance—of constitutional norms. The Supreme Court is meant to guard the constitution, not rule above it. Yet, inside the hallowed halls of the judiciary, rumors have swirled that the Chief Justice views current national politics as being "in the palm of his hand." Lawmakers like Kim Ki-pyo have voiced a chilling suspicion: that the judiciary under Cho Hee-dae no longer sees itself as an arbiter of legal disputes, but as a superior political authority capable of steering the country’s future by manipulating dockets and reviving stalled trials.

The Threat of Resumed Trials and the Deconstruction of the Separation of Powers

The most alarming scenario currently making the rounds among constitutional scholars in Seoul is the prospect that the Supreme Court will soon move to resume criminal proceedings against President Lee Jae-myung. To justify such a move, the judiciary is reportedly preparing a paradoxical argument: that the executive branch, under President Lee, has so severely damaged the judicial branch that the court has no choice but to intervene to "restore the separation of powers."

Legal experts view this as a breathtaking piece of judicial gymnastics. Under Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution, a sitting president enjoys immunity from criminal prosecution while in office. To bypass this, judicial insiders are reportedly floating the sophistry that because a past indictment already existed prior to taking office, resuming the trial does not technically violate the ban on new indictments.

This is not legal interpretation; it is a political weapon. Utilizing the machinery of the courts to hobble a democratically elected president strikes at the very heart of South Korea's constitutional order. It echoes the darkest periods of the country's authoritarian past, where the robe was used as a guillotine for political opponents. Whispers from within the court system suggest that certain specific panels, such as the Criminal Division 7, are already being eyed to operationalize these political ambitions.

The Path Forward: Why Judicial Reform Is No Longer Optional

For a country that bled to establish its democracy—overthrowing dictators and sending disgraced executives like the imprisoned former leader Yoon Seok-yeol to jail through fierce civic resistance—the sight of an out-of-control judiciary is deeply unsettling. The notion of impeaching a Chief Justice or freezing the duties of rogue judges once sounded like a radical, nuclear option. Today, constitutional scholars and ruling party lawmakers are arguing that it is the minimum defense required to keep the Republic from unraveling.

The system's internal self-correction mechanisms have clearly failed. When judges begin treating courtroom assignments as personal political collateral, the public can no longer afford to treat the judiciary as a sacred cow. Real reform will require the National Assembly to exercise rigorous legislative oversight over judicial powers, and it will require civil society to shed its complacency.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the parliamentary budget sessions and judiciary committee hearings in the coming weeks. Watch whether the Democratic Party moves from rhetorical warnings to formal institutional motions, such as drafting bills for judicial suspension or impeachment. More importantly, monitor how the lower-tier judges react to the Chief Justice's power plays—whether the rank-and-file magistracy chooses to side with institutional integrity or political patronage will determine whether South Korea's democracy weeps or stands tall.
