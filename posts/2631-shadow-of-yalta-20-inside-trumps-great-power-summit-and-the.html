Shadow of Yalta 2.0: Inside Trump’s Great-Power Summit and the Collapse of North Korea Talks

November 23, 2026

Imagine scrolling through social media after a high-stakes gathering of world leaders and seeing a striking side-by-side comparison. On one side sits the iconic 1945 photograph of Winston Churchill, Franklin D. Roosevelt, and Joseph Stalin at the Yalta Conference, carving out the post–World War II global order. On the other sits a fresh photograph featuring U.S. President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin gathered at the same table. Accompanying the post were words from the American president labeling the trio as "friends" and "great powers."

This was not a historical retrospective. It was a deliberate diplomatic statement posted following an unprecedented 90-minute trilateral summit between the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia on November 22. For foreign observers watching Northeast Asia, the imagery was as chilling as it was calculated. Eight decades after superpowers decided the partition of the Korean Peninsula without consulting a single Korean, the ghost of great-power sphere-of-influence diplomacy has returned to center stage.

Behind the theatrical optics lies a rapidly shifting international environment. From Washington's desperate, last-minute attempt to pull off a summit with North Korea to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky accepting a proposal for three-way peace talks before Christmas, the global security architecture is under intense strain. For South Korea, these developments represent a profound challenge to its national security strategy, raising uncomfortable questions about whether major powers are once again preparing to decide the fate of the region over Seoul's head.

Great-Power Politics Reborn and the Vanishing Mandate for Denuclearization

The 90-minute meeting between Trump, Xi, and Putin marked a dramatic pivot toward explicit great-power dealmaking. President Xi strengthened China's diplomatic footing, while President Putin effectively broke through Western isolation tactics, gaining critical time and prestige. Although President Zelensky dismissed the summit as a "failure for Russia" and proof that Putin's "Yalta 2.0" ambition failed to fully materialize, the message conveyed by Washington’s leadership was clear: the world is increasingly being managed by great powers pursuing their own strategic spheres of interest.

The most consequential casualty of this summit, however, was hidden in what was left unsaid. While President Trump confirmed that the three leaders discussed managing risks on the Korean Peninsula, the final outputs contained only high-level, routine language emphasizing peace and stability through dialogue.

Crucially, the phrase "denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula"—which has served as the foundational bedrock of international diplomacy toward Pyongyang for decades—was entirely absent from the official outcomes of the meeting. With North Korea sending troops to support Russia's war effort in Europe and drawing closer to both Moscow and Beijing, the missing commitment to denuclearization signals a troubling shift. Rather than demanding the total dismantling of Pyongyang's nuclear program, the superpowers appear inclined toward tacitly managing North Korea as a de facto nuclear weapons state. For South Korea, watching the principle of denuclearization vanish from superpower rhetoric is a stark warning that its core security interests are being downgraded to a mere geopolitical side note.

Letters Thrown Unopened: Inside the Collapse of Trump’s North Korea Bid

While the trilateral summit unfolded, another diplomatic drama was occurring behind closed doors. Reports from major foreign media, including The Wall Street Journal, revealed that the Trump administration had been aggressively pushing up to the last minute to arrange a surprise summit between Donald Trump and North Korean leader Kim Jong Un during Trump's tour of Asia.

President Trump went so far as to send a personal letter to Kim to salvage the meeting. However, North Korean diplomats reacted with extraordinary hostility, throwing the envelope back at the U.S. delegation without even opening it. Pyongyang demanded conditions that Washington could not possibly accept, including drastic relief from international economic sanctions and the complete, permanent termination of joint U.S.–South Korea military exercises. President Trump ultimately admitted that a meeting could not be arranged, bringing the high-stakes gamble to an abrupt halt.

What is most unsettling for policymakers in Seoul is what Washington was reportedly willing to concede just to get Kim Jong Un to the table. Internal discussions within the U.S. administration included extraordinary proposals: offering a formal declaration ending the 1950–1953 Korean War and refraining from immediately demanding that North Korea abandon its nuclear arsenal.

This willingness to entertain a temporary freeze or de facto recognition of North Korea's nuclear capabilities highlights a dangerous impatience in Washington. While Pyongyang's refusal prevented a potentially disastrous accord, the fact that the U.S. executive branch considered dropping the demand for immediate nuclear dismantling severely undermines the baseline for all future negotiations. It signals to Pyongyang that holding out against American overtures while backed by Beijing and Moscow will eventually force Washington to compromise on core security principles.

A Christmas Deadline in Ukraine and Putin’s Strategic Calculus

The great-power maneuvering is not confined to East Asia; it is directly intertwined with the conflict in Eastern Europe. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky confirmed that he has agreed to a U.S. proposal to hold three-way peace talks involving the United States, Russia, and Ukraine before Christmas. Potential locations under consideration for these landmark negotiations include Miami or the sidelines of the upcoming G20 Summit.

Zelensky’s acceptance of a Christmas deadline reflects mounting pressure from Washington alongside deep domestic fatigue after years of brutal warfare. However, the ultimate fate of this initiative rests entirely on Russian President Vladimir Putin. Moscow enters potential talks from a position of relative strength, emboldened by its tactical military position and its diplomatic engagement with Washington and Beijing.

Putin’s calculus remains complex. Having demonstrated Russia's status as an indispensable global player during the tri-summit, he may view the proposed three-way talks as an opportunity to cement territorial gains and permanently block Ukraine's accession to NATO. Alternatively, if Western terms do not meet Russian demands, Moscow could simply delay negotiations to extract further concessions on the battlefield. How this diplomatic push unfolds in December will reshape European security and send immediate ripples across East Asia.

'Korea Passing' and Seoul’s Drive for Strategic Autonomy

For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—led by the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s ruling party—these rapid global developments represent a severe strategic test. South Korean foreign policy experts have long warned of "Korea Passing," a term describing the nightmare scenario in which external superpowers negotiate decisions impacting the Korean Peninsula without Seoul's input or consent.

As major powers pivot toward transactional sphere-of-influence diplomacy and show a willingness to compromise on denuclearization, reliance on traditional diplomatic umbrellas is proving increasingly precarious. In response, voices within South Korea's policy establishment are demanding a decisive pivot toward self-reliant defense capabilities to ensure that Seoul remains an active player rather than a bystander in regional security.

Two major policy imperatives have emerged from this crisis:

  • Completing the Transfer of Wartime Operational Control (OPCON): South Korea currently holds peacetime operational control of its military, but command reverts to a U.S. general during wartime. Accelerating the long-delayed transfer of wartime command is increasingly viewed as an essential step for South Korea to exercise full strategic leadership over its own defense posture.
  • Acquiring Independent Strategic Deterrence: To counter North Korea's advancing nuclear and missile capabilities without relying solely on volatile foreign political cycles, discussions regarding the acquisition of conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines and enhanced independent defense systems are gaining significant traction.

The lesson from the November diplomatic flurry is unequivocal: in a world governed by great-power realpolitik, diplomatic leverage belongs only to those who possess the autonomous power to defend themselves.

What to Watch Next

As the international community moves into the final weeks of the year, several critical indicators will reveal whether this era of great-power bargaining expands or hits a wall:

  • Putin’s Response to the Christmas Peace Talks: Watch whether Moscow formally accepts the U.S. proposal for three-way talks with Kyiv before Christmas, and whether Miami or the G20 venue is selected. Any preconditions set by the Kremlin will indicate whether Russia seeks a genuine armistice or further military leverage.
  • President Lee Jae-myung’s Multilateral Strategy: Keep a close eye on President Lee's diplomatic engagements at upcoming multilateral forums, including the G20. The South Korean government will likely attempt to rally international support for explicit denuclearization language and counter any efforts by Washington, Beijing, or Moscow to sideline Seoul.
  • South Korean Defense Infrastructure Debates: Track legislative and executive moves in Seoul regarding defense spending, specifically efforts to fast-track the OPCON transfer process and fund advanced strategic assets like nuclear-powered submarines.
