The De-Nvidia Shift: How TSMC’s Record Revenue and Jensen Huang’s Washington Troubles Are Reshaping Korean Tech

November 11, 2026

For more than two years, the global artificial intelligence boom has operated under a single, unchallenged gospel: Nvidia designs the chips, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC) fabricates them, and South Korea’s memory giants supply the high-bandwidth hardware that makes the entire apparatus run. But on November 10, that tightly coupled axis registered its first major structural tremors.

In Seoul, chip executives and market analysts woke up to a stark contrast in headlines. On one side of the Formosa Strait, TSMC smashed all historical records by posting October revenue of 586.9 billion New Taiwan dollars (roughly 25 trillion Korean won, or about $18 billion), representing a staggering 59.7% jump year-over-year. On the other side of the Pacific, Nvidia Chief Executive Jensen Huang found his company trapped in a gathering storm in Washington over illicit chip shipments to China and mounting congressional pressure over regulatory compliance.

For South Korea—home to Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the two memory makers that produce the vast majority of the world’s High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—this shift is far more than routine tech news. It marks the opening chapter of a supply chain realignment. As Big Tech players like Google accelerate their efforts to bypass Nvidia, and as TSMC asserts greater technical dominance over memory integration, South Korea’s semiconductor industry is being forced to recalculate its survival strategy between American regulatory crackdowns and shifting silicon architectures.

TSMC’s Record Revenue and the Monopoly Trap

TSMC’s October revenue report delivered definitive proof that enterprise demand for AI hardware remains insatiable. The Taiwanese foundry giant extended its winning streak to 34 consecutive months of year-over-year revenue growth, fueled by maxed-out capacity utilization across its cutting-edge 3-nanometer and 5-nanometer production lines. With fourth-quarter revenue projections revised upward to as much as $54.3 billion, TSMC has solidified its position as the indispensable tollbooth of the global AI economy.

To lock in this advantage, TSMC is planning aggressive capital expenditure expansions spanning from 2026 through 2028. Beyond physical fabs, the foundry leader recently approved the creation of a $500 million strategic investment entity designed to acquire equity stakes in AI startups and broaden its voting influence across the broader technology ecosystem. By pairing unmatched manufacturing yield with financial leverage, TSMC is attempting to build an unassailable ecosystem that controls chip fabrication from initial design to final advanced packaging.

This aggressive posture has reignited industry speculation regarding TSMC’s broader ambitions, including rumors that the company might venture directly into 3D DRAM or proprietary memory solutions. While industry observers consider a direct entry into memory manufacturing unlikely in the short term due to high technical risks and different yield economics, TSMC’s growing influence over advanced packaging tells a different story.

Rather than building memory fabs itself, TSMC is asserting structural dominance by dictating the packaging standards to which memory suppliers must adhere. As standard HBM integration gives way to custom base-die designs, memory manufacturers are increasingly obliged to follow TSMC's design guidelines. For Korean suppliers, this dynamic threatens to reduce memory makers from strategic co-architects into standard component vendors whose profitability remains subject to TSMC’s architectural terms.

Jensen Huang’s Washington Headwinds and the China Smuggling Fallout

While TSMC demonstrated financial invincibility, Nvidia found itself confronting severe governance and regulatory headwinds. Reports that prohibited, latest-generation Nvidia "Blackwell" processors worth approximately 86.8 billion Korean won (around $65 million) had surfaced inside China sent shockwaves through Washington. The revelation triggered fierce bipartisan criticism in the U.S. Congress, with lawmakers demanding tighter export controls and scrutinizing the U.S. Commerce Department over stalled investigations into potential smuggling routes.

The scandal has put Jensen Huang’s corporate strategy under uncomfortable scrutiny. Critics argue that Nvidia’s attempt to walk a fine line—complying nominally with U.S. export restrictions while seeking to preserve its lucrative footprint in the Chinese market—has reached its structural limits. With congressional leaders floating reinforced legislative frameworks to block third-party smuggling networks and equipment diversions, Nvidia’s "owner risk" has suddenly emerged as a primary variable in global chip markets.

For South Korea’s semiconductor sector, Nvidia’s regulatory entanglement represents a double-edged sword:

  • Short-term market volatility: Pullbacks in Nvidia’s valuation create immediate drag on Korean semiconductor indices and memory suppliers heavily linked to Nvidia’s order volume.
  • Geopolitical exposure: Stricter U.S. enforcement increases operational scrutiny on Korean chipmakers maintaining production facilities or significant commercial ties in China.
  • Bargaining leverage: Prolonged political pressure on Nvidia slows its ability to dictat unilaterally to supply chain partners, offering Korean memory makers room to diversify their customer bases.

Rather than severing South Korea’s ties with Nvidia, industry analysts suggest the controversy may temporarily weaken Nvidia’s ability to treat Korean suppliers as mere captive suppliers. As Washington’s regulatory radar intensifies, the premium on trusted, compliant manufacturing partners like Samsung and SK Hynix only increases.

Google and AMD Mount the ‘De-Nvidia’ Offensive

Capitalizing on Nvidia’s regulatory distractions and persistent supply shortages, rival tech giants are moving aggressively to break the GPU monopoly. Google has positioned itself at the forefront of this shift, rolling out four custom AI chips this year alone, headlined by its 8th-generation Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) systems. Designed specifically to run inside Google’s proprietary cloud infrastructure, these chips aim to offer hyper-efficient compute alternatives to expensive, hard-to-source Nvidia graphics hardware.

At the same time, AMD is ramping up commercial deployments of its "Helios" server systems, taking direct aim at enterprise data center budgets. Crucially, these non-Nvidia hardware coalitions are focusing heavily on expanding open-source software ecosystems to undermine Nvidia’s proprietary CUDA software layer—the walled garden that has historically locked developers into Nvidia’s hardware orbit.

This "de-Nvidia" momentum fundamentally alters the strategic landscape for South Korean memory vendors. Historically, SK Hynix and Samsung focused the bulk of their HBM capacities on meeting Nvidia’s rigorous qualifications. The rise of competitive custom silicon from cloud service providers (CSPs) like Google and hardware rivals like AMD opens up a far more diversified, custom-tailored HBM market.

Rather than selling standardized HBM modules to a single dominant buyer, Korean suppliers are now negotiating custom memory architectures directly with hyperscalers. This shift benefits Samsung in particular, which has been championing a "one-stop shop" strategy that combines memory production, logic fabrication, and advanced packaging under a single corporate roof.

Architectural Power Struggles: NVHBM and the Race for HBM4

The tension between chip designers, foundries, and memory makers is culminating in next-generation hardware designs. Nvidia recently unveiled its "NVHBM" concept, an architectural shift that proposes moving the HBM memory controller off the memory die and directly onto the central logic base die. While this reconfiguration offers significant improvements in power efficiency and interconnect latency, it also transfers key control over memory performance to the logic designer—in this case, Nvidia.

This architectural shift presents Korean chipmakers with a delicate technical tightrope:

If memory makers accept logic-side control of the HBM base die, they risk ceding proprietary design IP and becoming downstream sub-assemblers. If they reject it, they risk exclusion from top-tier AI accelerator platforms.

In response, Korean memory makers are deploying divergent strategies ahead of the HBM4 generation:

  • Samsung Electronics is leveraging its internal foundry division to build integrated HBM4 solutions, offering customers a turnkey package where memory cells, logic base dies, and foundry fabrication are handled in-house.
  • SK Hynix is deepening its technical alliance with TSMC, pairing its world-leading HBM stacking yields with TSMC’s advanced logic foundry nodes to deliver customized base dies that satisfy Nvidia and non-Nvidia clients alike.

As the boundary between memory and foundry logic blurs, success in the semiconductor industry is no longer defined simply by density or yield, but by how effectively a company navigates complex ecosystem alliances.

What to Watch Next

As the global semiconductor market digests the fallout from TSMC’s surge and Nvidia’s political troubles, several concrete metrics will determine how the next phase of supply chain restructuring plays out:

  • U.S. Congressional Action: Watch for formal committee hearings or new legislative proposals targeting third-country diversion of advanced GPUs like Nvidia's Blackwell series, which could impose stricter compliance mandates on East Asian supply chains.
  • Google TPU Adoption Rates: Monitor commercial cloud benchmarks and deployment scale for Google’s 8th-generation TPUs as enterprise customers seek cost-effective alternatives to Nvidia clusters.
  • TSMC’s Q4 Revenue Execution: Track whether TSMC hits the upper bound of its $54.3 billion Q4 guidance, serving as a barometer for whether AI hardware investments are maintaining momentum into 2026.
  • HBM4 Base-Die Standards: Pay close attention to engineering updates surrounding HBM4 development from Samsung and SK Hynix, specifically whether customer specifications lean toward TSMC-led foundry packaging or Samsung’s integrated turnkey model.
