The Pilgrim's Paradox: Why South Korea's Love Affair with the Camino de Santiago is Breeding Conflict

September 07, 2026

For many modern travelers burnt out by the relentless pace of urban life, the Camino de Santiago—the ancient network of pilgrimage routes leading to the shrine of Saint James in northwestern Spain—has transformed from a religious journey into a secular sanctuary. It is a place to press pause, reflect, and walk off the existential fatigue of the twenty-first century. South Koreans have embraced this movement with characteristic intensity. By 2024, nearly 7,910 South Korean pilgrims completed the journey, placing the country tenth in overall national rankings and cementing its status as an outsized demographic from Asia. Packing light, flying halfway across the globe, and willingly walking 800 kilometers (nearly 500 miles) is no small feat of endurance.

Yet, when national enthusiasm scales to such heights, it inevitably spills over into friction. Lately, even local Spanish residents have begun expressing bewilderment at the sheer volume of Korean travelers, and not simply because of the numbers. A deeper unease has taken root, tied to a loss of basic trail etiquette and mutual respect. The Camino is fundamentally a space for honoring other lives, not a territory to be colonized by one's own cultural habits or personal standards. By examining the most common complaints and "tiresome archetypes" reported along the route, it becomes possible to confront how traveling abroad with a collective mindset can inadvertently turn a spiritual journey into a social minefield.

The Impact of Group Travel on Communal Spaces

As pilgrims approach the final 100 kilometers (about 62 miles) of the trek into Santiago de Compostela, the presence of South Korean organized tour groups becomes starkly visible. The core issue lies in how these groups travel compared to independent backpackers. While individual pilgrims trudge wearily from town to town searching for a bed, organized groups often move with pre-booked accommodations and luggage-transfer services that spare them the physical toll of carrying their packs.

This logistical efficiency creates friction. By occupying limited beds in bulk, these groups frequently leave independent travelers stranded with nowhere to sleep. In Portomarin, for instance, independent walkers struggling to find lodging on a crowded night have found hostels completely overrun by Korean tour groups. Bringing along rice cookers and demanding kitchenware from local innkeepers might offer domestic comfort to the group, but to locals and fellow pilgrims, it feels like an intrusion that shatters the unwritten peace of the trail.

This excess energy often manifests as disruptive noise. Tour groups, having walked only short distances before spending their afternoons at leisure, frequently fill cafes and public squares with loud conversations and singing. The stark contrast between this boisterous socializing and the quiet, contemplative atmosphere of the trail has pushed some local business owners to the breaking point, with some venting openly about their frustration with large, loud tour parties. When travelers clump together in groups that ignore local norms, they risk branding entire nationalities as disruptive outsiders.

Unsolicited Advice and Cultural Blind Spots

Beyond logistics, interpersonal dynamics on the trail often mirror the rigid social hierarchies and pressures left behind in Korea. It is not uncommon for older travelers to corner strangers with unsolicited boasts about their children—recounting sons working as doctors in the United States or corporate climbers at Samsung. For a young traveler who has quit a high-pressure job or is walking the trail to process a major life crisis, being cornered by unprompted status updates feels less like conversation and more like an emotional imposition.

A similar boundary violation occurs with aggressive proselytizing. Some Christian travelers view the Camino not as a shared historical route, but as a literal mission field. Attributing other people's journeys entirely to divine design—or worse, explaining away personal misfortunes or injuries as a lack of faith—violates the unspoken secular and interfaith courtesy of the trail. The Camino belongs to everyone, but no one has the right to weaponize personal beliefs to judge the struggles of strangers.

The Micro-Etiquette of the Albergue

Living in close quarters requires a high degree of self-awareness, which sometimes evaporates in communal hostels known as *albergues*. South Korean travelers are frequently noted by peers for leaving chat notification sounds blaring on their phones well into the night, ignoring the time-zone differences back home while disturbing the sleep of a dozen exhausted roommates. Similarly, travelers pushing through severe colds often refuse to isolate themselves, coughing through the night in crowded dormitories out of personal convenience rather than public consideration.

This individualistic streak also resurrects domestic age-based hierarchies. Older travelers sometimes fall into the trap of assuming authority over younger companions, delegating chores like cooking and dishwashing simply because of generational seniority. When senior walkers treat younger peers as assistants rather than equals, it drains the egalitarian spirit right out of the journey.

What to Watch Next

As international travel continues its post-pandemic surge—with South Korean visitors to Spain approaching pre-pandemic highs and spending an estimated 550,000 won (about $400) per person daily—the economic footprint of Korean tourists is undeniable. Yet the currency that matters most on the trail is not financial; it is behavioral.

For those planning to walk the yellow arrows of the Camino, the coming seasons will test whether the conversation shifts from critique to self-correction. Watch for how local municipalities and hostel operators begin implementing stricter rules regarding group bookings and quiet hours. Pay attention to whether online travel communities in Korea shift their advice away from luxury shortcuts and toward responsible integration into local trail culture.

Ultimately, the Camino de Santiago remains vast enough to absorb human friction. Walkers can simply step past difficult companions, adjust their pace, and find solitude. But the deeper test is internal: whether travelers can leave their national speed, status anxieties, and domestic habits behind at the starting line. The true measure of finishing the pilgrimage is not the certificate at the cathedral in Santiago, but whether one leaves the path a little quieter, kinder, and more considerate than when they began.
