The Erosion of Hope: Why the Lee Jae-myung Administration Faces a Crisis of Legitimacy

October 06, 2026

Walking through the streets of Seoul today, one is struck by a disorienting paradox. Political factions that were rightfully relegated to the pages of history following the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Seok-youl are suddenly reappearing, emboldened and vocal. Just over a year into the term of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea’s political landscape is suffering from a vacuum of leadership that defies common sense. The sentiment is captured sharply by Professor Choi Bae-keun, a prominent economic commentator, who warns that the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the liberal party that swept into power promising reform—is now staring down the barrel of a catastrophic defeat in upcoming elections, even in its traditional strongholds.

The disillusionment is not merely a matter of polling numbers; it is a profound sense of betrayal felt by the base that once championed the Lee administration. As we assess the state of the nation, the question arises: how did the high hopes of the last presidential election cycle evaporate so quickly, leaving behind only a bitter sense of powerlessness?

One Year In: The Failure to Improve Daily Life

Throughout the history of Democratic-led governments—from Kim Dae-jung to Moon Jae-in and now Lee Jae-myung—the same heavy question has haunted leaders: "What has the party actually changed for the working class?" Professor Choi admits that this is a question the current administration cannot answer. While the government has consistently rolled out optimistic policy blueprints, the reality for the average citizen remains unchanged.

Despite rhetoric regarding "inclusive growth" and "income-led growth," the tangible effects have been glacial. Efforts to strengthen the social safety net and adjust labor conditions—such as raising the minimum wage or mandating a 52-hour workweek—have often failed to translate into higher real income for those at the bottom of the economic ladder. In many cases, the most vulnerable have seen their purchasing power decline. The administration has relied on grand discourse, but the average person is concerned with the price of groceries and the balance in their bank account. By missing the "golden time" for genuine reform, the DPK has effectively abandoned the people they promised to protect.

This dissonance became painfully clear following the President’s recent press conference. While his rhetoric regarding a "society where everyone prospers" was emotionally charged, it was followed by policies that amounted to little more than temporary, performative measures—such as increasing "holiday discount" subsidies—rather than the structural overhauls needed to narrow the wealth gap between conglomerates and small businesses.

The Paradox of "Pragmatism" and the Resurgence of the Right

There is a growing concern that President Lee’s brand of "pragmatism" is beginning to mirror the market-centric, pro-business approach of the conservative regimes that preceded him. When a self-proclaimed progressive government prioritizes appeasing the establishment over implementing systemic changes, it signals a drift toward self-preservation. Statistics from the third quarter show that job growth in small and medium-sized enterprises—outside of the semiconductor sector—has slowed significantly compared to previous years.

This vacuum of reform has created the perfect conditions for the opposition—the People Power Party (the main conservative party)—to mount a comeback. Despite being the party associated with the disgraced former leader Yoon Seok-youl and facing severe legal challenges, the People Power Party has taken to the streets, holding rallies and criticizing the government’s failure to address livelihood issues. That they can do so with such confidence is a damning indictment of the DPK’s performance. By failing to solidify the reform agenda and allowing the opposition to frame the narrative on "livelihood recovery," the administration has effectively handed the keys of political momentum back to the very forces it was meant to displace.

The "Zombie" State of the Majority Party

The DPK, which holds a massive majority of over 170 seats in the National Assembly, has largely failed to utilize its mandate. Under the current leadership of party head Kim Min-seok, the party appears paralyzed, behaving less like a reform engine and more like a "zombie" organization. There is a palpable frustration among supporters that the party leadership is more concerned with internal power struggles and positioning for the next presidential race than with passing the legislation that the public was promised.

This inaction has emboldened the bureaucracy, most notably within the prosecution. Reports of internal dissent and procedural irregularities—such as the recent disclosures from the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO)—suggest that state institutions are sensing the government’s weakness and preparing to push back. Without firm, decisive leadership to clean up these systemic remnants of the old regime, the administration is allowing the very structures it promised to reform to regain their footing.

A Call to Action: Beyond Empty Apologies

Professor Choi’s decision to publicly withdraw his support for the Lee administration was not a trivial act; it was a desperate plea for the government to wake up. When even the intellectuals who once campaigned for a leader lose their faith, the administration is in deep trouble. The public is tired of being treated as recipients of "benevolence" via temporary subsidies rather than as sovereign citizens entitled to structural rights.

To reverse this trend, the government must move beyond performative apologies and pivot toward genuine systemic change. It needs to stop fearing the backlash of the status quo and start dismantling the obstacles to equality. If the Lee administration continues to rely on inertia and political maneuvering, it will inevitably end up as another footnote of failure in South Korean history.

What to watch next:

  • Cabinet Reshuffle: Keep an eye on whether President Lee replaces key economic advisors with figures who prioritize structural reform over short-term market appeasement.
  • Legislative Deadlocks: Watch for whether the DPK leadership forces a vote on major reform bills currently stalled in the National Assembly to prove they still control the political agenda.
  • Prosecution Moves: Monitor further leaks or public confrontations between the government and the prosecution, which will indicate how much control the administration actually maintains over the state machinery.
