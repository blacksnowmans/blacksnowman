Has the Korean Stock Market Found Its Floor? The Numbers Behind the Rebound Case

October 19, 2026

For months, opening a brokerage app in South Korea has felt like an exercise in self-punishment. The benchmark Korea Composite Stock Price Index (KOSPI) has endured brutal volatility, leaving retail investors battered by persistent inflation fears, currency instability, and geopolitical anxiety. But underneath the daily panic, a growing body of macroeconomic and corporate data suggests that South Korean equities have passed peak oversold territory and are quietly carving out a bottom.

To foreign observers, the Seoul market often looks like an enigma: a high-tech export powerhouse whose equities perennially trade at steep discounts relative to their global peers—a phenomenon long known as the "Korea Discount." Yet current technical levels, turning tides in real interest rates, and an undeniable wave of artificial intelligence hardware demand suggest the market’s sharp pullback is giving way to a structured recovery.

Real Interest Rates Turn Positive in Seoul and Washington

The primary dark cloud hanging over the KOSPI since mid-summer has been restrictive monetary policy. When consumer inflation outpaces benchmark interest rates, central banks are forced to remain hawkish, draining liquidity from risk assets. However, recent data releases indicate that this dynamic has reached a pivotal inflection point.

In South Korea, the Bank of Korea—the nation's central bank—currently holds its benchmark policy rate at 3.0 percent. In September, headline consumer price inflation cooled to 2.9 percent year-on-year, marking the first time in this cycle that domestic inflation has dipped below the central bank's policy rate. Historically, asset markets establish durable floors once real policy rates shift into positive territory. When borrowing benchmarks exceed the pace of price increases, monetary authorities gain breathing room, easing downward pressure on equity multiples.

A similar stabilization is visible in the United States, which exercises an outsized influence on global liquidity and emerging-market risk appetite. With the Federal Reserve holding its federal funds rate at 4 percent, the U.S. core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index rose 3.4 percent year-on-year in August. That marked three consecutive months of positive real interest rates in the world's largest economy. Even as sticky U.S. inflation data occasionally triggers brief market tremors, historical cycles show that once real rates turn positive, equity markets tend to halt structural declines and attempt sustained rebounds.

A Technical Floor and the Squeeze on Leverage

Beyond macroeconomic indicators, technical chart patterns offer concrete reasons to look for stability. The critical reference point for Seoul traders is the market trough established on July 29, when the index held near the 5,200 level. Despite intense volatility and sharp subsequent pullbacks, that July 29 low has remained intact.

Much of the sharp sell-off in recent weeks—which saw the index drop rapidly between benchmark levels—was driven not by deteriorating corporate balance sheets, but by aggressive forced liquidations. Single-stock leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs) tracking bellwethers like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix saw unprecedented speculative unwinding around derivatives expiration dates. At the end of July, leveraged derivative products accounted for an astounding 32.5 percent of total KOSPI trading turnover. Recently, that share collapsed to just 3.4 percent.

This rapid deleveraging created a mechanical wave of selling that dragged down spot share prices regardless of underlying earnings. As debt-fueled speculative positions were flushed out, foreign institutional investors began quietly stepping in on the buy side, soaking up shares at valuations rarely seen in modern market history.

The Valuation Disconnect: Following the sell-off, the KOSPI’s 12-month forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio fell to 5.12 times. This valuation is notably cheaper than the 6.27 times recorded during the depths of the 2008 global financial crisis and the 7.53 times seen at the pandemic trough in 2020. Concurrently, 12-month forward earnings per share (EPS) estimates have climbed 184 percent since the start of the year, expanding for 12 straight months. While market prices reflected extreme systemic dread, corporate earnings power was moving toward record territory.

TSMC’s Blowout Quarter Shatters the "Peak Chip" Thesis

Because semiconductors account for roughly 30 percent of the KOSPI's total market capitalization, the index cannot mount a recovery without solid footing in chipmaking. For months, short sellers and skeptics warned of a "peak-out" in semiconductor demand, predicting that the artificial intelligence infrastructure boom would soon run out of funding or power capacity. Those doubts were emphatically dispelled by third-quarter financial results from Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), the world’s leading contract chip foundry.

TSMC reported a net profit surge of 68.7 percent year-on-year to 763.06 billion New Taiwan dollars, marking six consecutive quarters of record-breaking profits and beating consensus estimates. Its operating margin reached 58.7 percent, topping the company's own high-end guidance. Surging demand for advanced 3-nanometer and 5-nanometer process nodes utilized in AI servers and data centers completely overwhelmed any ongoing sluggishness in legacy consumer electronics.

Looking ahead, TSMC projected fourth-quarter revenue to rise approximately 14.1 percent quarter-on-quarter to between $52.3 billion and $54.3 billion—representing more than 50 percent year-on-year growth. Addressing concerns over long-term capital intensity, the foundry confirmed its 2024 capital expenditure (CapEx) budget will remain at an enormous $60 billion to $64 billion, with CapEx between 2026 and 2028 slated to surpass the previous three-year average. As advanced lithography systems from vendors like ASML continue shipping to support 3nm expansions and upcoming 1.4nm (A14) nodes, the foundational demand underpinning the AI hardware supply chain remains structurally intact.

Foreign Capital Returns to Korean Memory Giants

This resilience in global semiconductor hardware directly impacts Korea's two industrial giants: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. While TSMC dominates pure-play foundry fabrication, AI accelerators cannot function without high-bandwidth memory (HBM), a market dominated by the two Korean manufacturers.

Throughout the recent correction, spot prices for standard DRAM remained firm, while demand for advanced DDR5 and HBM continued to outstrip industry production capabilities. Long-term supply agreements spanning up to five years, signed with major global cloud hyperscalers and specialized infrastructure providers like CoreWeave, have significantly cushioned the traditional boom-and-bust cyclicality of Korean memory earnings. Furthermore, accelerating production schedules for 12-layer HBM4 chips are creating expanding opportunities across both Samsung and SK Hynix.

The turning point for Seoul has arrived via the order books. For weeks, institutional corporate buybacks provided the main bids supporting shares; Samsung Electronics wrapped up its share repurchase program on the 6th, with SK Hynix concluding its own shortly after. Typically, the expiration of corporate buyback programs leaves stocks vulnerable to immediate selling. Instead, foreign institutions turned into net buyers of KOSPI spot shares, stepping into the void left by company treasuries.

What to Watch Next

A recovery rarely moves in a straight line, but the structural foundations for a year-end rebound are taking shape. Investors monitoring South Korean equities should watch three key dynamics over the coming weeks:

  • The Durability of the July 29 Benchmark Low: Technicians will be watching whether the KOSPI can keep its distance above the 5,200 support zone. Continued consolidation above this baseline confirms that the forced deleveraging of late summer has fully run its course.
  • Foreign Net Inflows in Tech Bellwethers: Sustained foreign institutional buying in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix will serve as the primary engine for any broader market re-rating, particularly as the domestic buyback cushions wind down and memory supply tightness translates into quarterly earnings reports.
  • U.S. Equity Resilience and Midterm Seasonality: Historically, S&P 500 performance in the third year of a U.S. presidential administration—and following midterm election cycles—shows pronounced upward momentum as policy uncertainty clears. If Wall Street maintains its upward trajectory, the relief is likely to ripple outward, unlocking value in heavily discounted export hubs like South Korea.
