The Cruel Reality of Month Three and the Myth of Expensive Courses

When someone dives into short-form content platforms like YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, and TikTok to funnel buyers toward Coupang—South Korea’s dominant e-commerce giant, often described as the country’s Amazon—they usually start out of financial desperation. Facing mounting debts or a failed small business, the appeal is obvious: zero upfront capital, zero inventory risk, and total anonymity. But idealism quickly collides with the algorithm.

Month three is where most hopefuls hit a brick wall. This is the valley of despair where creators spend two to three hours cutting a single video, only for it to flatline at fewer than a thousand views. Bank accounts show daily earnings of less than 10,000 won (about $7), triggering a wave of psychological doubt. More than 90% of beginners quit here, convinced the system is broken. In truth, this period is a necessary algorithmic vetting process, but the lack of immediate feedback breaks most people's resolve.

Capitalizing on this desperation is a cottage industry of online marketers selling masterclasses priced between 2 million and 3 million won (roughly $1,400 to $2,200). These vendors promise secret strategies to unlock steady monthly incomes in just 30 days. But veterans who have survived the trenches argue that these courses are largely a waste of money. The underlying mechanics—understanding platform algorithms, basic video editing, and tracking link placement—can all be learned for free. The bottleneck is never a hidden piece of insider knowledge; it is the sheer grit required to edit and upload content every single day without fail, weekends included.

Shifting Mindsets: From Salesman to Traffic Conductor

The single biggest mistake a beginner makes is trying too hard to act like a traditional salesperson. The moment a creator focuses desperately on pushing a specific mop, gadget, or kitchen tool, the video morphs into a clumsy infomercial. Viewers swipe away within the first second.

Successful creators undergo a fundamental paradigm shift: they realize they are not selling products; they are capturing attention. Platforms like TikTok and Instagram are, at their core, attention economies. Their primary metric for success is watch time. If a video keeps users glued to their screens, the platform's algorithm responds by blasting it out to a massive audience. Conversion to sales follows naturally as a byproduct of high volume traffic.

To capture this traffic, seasoned operators rely on a two-track content strategy. The first is the viral hook track, featuring bizarre, satisfying, or jaw-dropping gadgets that make viewers stop scrolling out of sheer curiosity ("What on earth is that?"). Comments and shares surge, signaling the algorithm to expand reach. The second is the problem-solving track, which targets everyday domestic annoyances—like cleaning stubborn bathroom tiles or organizing cramped studio apartments—directly triggering the impulse buying habits of single households and busy homemakers.

While automation tools that scrape Coupang's product pages to instantly generate template videos can save time, they carry a heavy penalty. Platforms easily flag these mass-produced uploads as low-quality, duplicate content, leading to swift account bans. Human touches, personal narration, and custom edits remain the baseline for survival.

The Mechanics of the 24-Hour Cookie and Payout Realities

To understand why people persist, one must look at how the compensation structure actually functions. Coupang Partners typically pays a baseline commission of around 3% of the purchase price, rising slightly to 4.5% for categories like beauty and fashion, while high-ticket electronics offer lower percentage rates that yield larger absolute cash returns per sale.

The real engine of profitability, however, is the 24-hour cookie tracking window. When a viewer clicks a creator’s affiliate link and enters the Coupang ecosystem, a tracking cookie drops into their browser for 24 hours. The magic—and the saving grace for many creators—is that the user does not actually have to buy the specific item featured in the video. If the viewer clicks the link to look at a 5,000-won dishcloth, leaves the app, and then later returns to buy a 1.5 million-won laptop or their weekly groceries, the creator still receives a cut of that entire basket.

Consequently, the entire game boils down to a single objective: getting users to click the link and open the app. Yet, creators must navigate strict regulatory boundaries. Under South Korea's Fair Trade Commission guidelines, every single post must explicitly disclose the affiliate relationship with a standardized disclaimer. Failing to do so—or attempting to hide the disclosure—invites permanent account bans and forfeiture of unpaid commissions.

Riding the Wave of a Contentious Ecosystem

Building a side hustle on top of Coupang means riding the waves of a corporate giant that frequently courts controversy. The platform's aggressive market dominance and marketing practices have drawn fierce regulatory scrutiny in South Korea. For instance, antitrust regulators recently hit Coupang with massive fines for deceptive marketing practices regarding its membership pricing—a reminder that platform-dependent gig workers always operate at the mercy of sudden policy shifts and corporate maneuvers.

Despite these regulatory clouds, Coupang's relentless expansion of its logistics network and delivery speeds continues to reinforce consumer trust, keeping conversion rates high for affiliate marketers. Yet, as competition intensifies and rivals like Naver's shopping ecosystems evolve, low-effort link-dropping is dying out.

For those watching from the outside, the takeaway is clear. There are no shortcuts, and no amount of expensive coaching can bypass the grinding reality of the first three months. Those who manage to build a sustainable digital pipeline do so not through luck or secret hacks, but by treating short-form content creation as a disciplined, daily craft.
