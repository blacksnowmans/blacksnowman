The Death of Korean Desk-Grinding: Why AI Is Breaking the Cult of ‘Endured Suffering’

October 25, 2026

It is 6:01 PM in a high-rise office tower in Seoul’s Yeouido financial district. A junior associate finishes his assigned tasks, packs his briefcase, and stands up to leave. The office goes dead silent. Heads stay buried behind dual monitors, but every eye in the cubicle farm tracks his movement. As he walks toward the elevator, he offers a polite bow to his team manager and utters the standard Korean farewell: “Sugo-hasyeossseumnida.” The manager gives a stiff, half-hearted nod, eyes narrowing at the clock. The silent accusation hangs thick in the air: You are leaving before me?

For decades, this subtle psychological warfare has formed the bedrock of white-collar life in South Korea. Surviving the corporate ladder meant arriving an hour early to wipe your senior's desk, staying late to drink with the department head, and enduring endless rounds of soul-crushing compliance. But this social contract is rapidly breaking down. Song Gil-young, one of South Korea’s most prominent big data specialists, cultural forecasters, and authors, has sparked a fierce national conversation with his critique of what he calls Sugo-inryu—the "Enduring Human," or the generation defined by manual toil and unquestioning obedience. His diagnosis is stark: in an era dominated by artificial intelligence, the blind diligence that built modern South Korea is no longer a virtue. It has become a path to professional obsolescence.

The Cruel Etiquette of ‘Sugo’ and the Architecture of Guilt

To understand the depth of this generational rift, one must look at the Korean language itself. The ubiquitous office parting phrase, sugo (수고), is composed of two Chinese characters: su (受, to receive) and go (苦, bitterness or suffering). Taken literally, the phrase does not mean "have a good evening" or "good work"; it means "endure suffering." There are few languages where the explicit endurance of pain is codified into daily polite conversation.

Within South Korean corporate hierarchy, the word is weaponized through strict linguistic etiquette. A junior employee is traditionally forbidden from telling an elder or senior manager to "Sugo-haseyo" ("Keep enduring suffering") as they depart for the day. To older executives, hearing that phrase from a subordinate sounds almost like a brazen curse—an insult implying that the junior is leaving them behind to struggle. Behind the veneer of polite corporate etiquette lies a raw, possessive instinct: the expectation that an entire team must suffer collectively until the boss decides it is time to go home.

This dynamic extends to arrival times as well. During South Korea’s high-growth industrial boom from the 1970s through the 1990s, arriving 30 or 60 minutes before contractual working hours was the gold standard of company loyalty. Today, younger workers view this practice as a form of structural violence. When an ambitious employee clocks in an hour early merely to project diligence, it subtly transforms punctuality into a crime for colleagues who arrive precisely when their contracts dictate. What was once celebrated as self-sacrifice has morphed into peer-to-peer surveillance.

Song describes this clash as the inevitable collision between an old apprentice society and the rise of the "nuclear individual" (haek-gaein). Young Korean professionals in their twenties and thirties no longer view themselves as disposable cogs within a lifetime corporate family. They recognize that their companies will not look after them into old age. Consequently, the guilt-laden rituals of the traditional office feel less like shared camaraderie and more like suffocating, obsolete theater.

‘Who Will Take Responsibility?’: Bureaucracy as an Existential Shield

If physical presence in the office is an engine of guilt, internal process has become an engine of stagnation. South Korea’s corporate apparatus is groaning under the weight of accumulated regulations. Whenever a past mistake or regulatory audit occurred, organizations created a new reporting layer, a new sign-off protocol, and an extra committee to ensure it never happened again. Over decades of industrialization, these procedures fused into an immovable labyrinth.

Organizations excel at adding rules, but almost never delete them. The result is a corporate landscape where original business goals—customer satisfaction, speed, and real problem-solving—are entirely displaced by process compliance. White-collar workers spend the majority of their days crafting documents designed solely to survive internal audits rather than create market value.

Whenever a subordinate proposes a simpler method or suggests removing a redundant procedure, senior management routinely deploys a conversation-ending weapon: “Will you take personal responsibility if something goes wrong?”

This question is rarely an invitation to exercise leadership; it is a defensive shield. Because no single mid-level worker can unilaterally underwrite corporate risk, the status quo remains frozen. It is institutionalized cowardice disguised as prudence. To combat this paralysis, Song advocates for what he terms a "Lightweight Civilization" (gyeongryang munmyeong). In an economy reshuffled by hyper-connectivity and software automation, bloated bureaucracies requiring five layers of managerial stamps cannot keep pace. Surviving requires paring away the procedural fat, shedding redundant oversight, and accepting calculated risks rather than chasing the illusion of zero error.

The Tragic Fate of the Middle Manager Who ‘Did What He Was Told’

The tragedy of this transition falls hardest on middle-aged managers who played entirely by the old rulebook. A generation of workers spent their twenties and thirties sacrificing personal lives, pouring drinks for their bosses, and executing orders without question. Their central professional identity was built on one defense: “I did exactly what I was told.”

Today, that defensive line offers zero protection. Generative AI and automated workflow engines summarize reports, analyze datasets, and track project milestones far faster and more transparently than any managerial gatekeeper. The skill of simply supervising others—sitting in meetings, relaying orders downwards, and stamping approval decks—has seen its market value evaporate overnight.

At the same time, the informal apprentice system that once passed skills down through generations has completely unraveled. In past decades, a rookie broadcaster or corporate trainee spent months shadowing a senior mentor (the sasu), enduring harsh personal rebukes to absorb trade secrets. Today, junior hires turn to YouTube tutorials, specialized digital platforms, or AI prompts for standardized technical guidance in seconds. Seniors, terrified of triggering formal complaints under South Korea's workplace harassment laws, keep their distance. The office floor has fallen into a silent standoff where seniors fear to coach and juniors have no interest in listening.

This structural slimming is visible across South Korea's high-profile sectors, particularly media. During the golden age of major terrestrial networks like KBS and MBC, producing a flagship entertainment broadcast required an entire armada: dozens of assistant directors, scriptwriters, and technical crew, supported by massive capital and state prestige. Today, former broadcast heavyweights run lean digital operations. Star producers like Lee Myung-han, who helped define modern Korean unscripted television, steadily migrated away from the cumbersome bureaucratic machinery of traditional networks toward cable (tvN), streaming platforms (TVING), and ultimately independent production houses like AoA Media. A top-tier media personality and a single director can now launch an internationally competitive program on YouTube with a skeleton crew, rendering the old broadcast armada obsolete.

Escaping the Famine Mindset: The Gelato Economy and the ‘Original Human’

Why did South Korean work culture become so obsessed with functional overwork in the first place? Song points to the lingering collective memory of post-war poverty. Half a century ago, survival depended on rapid, mass-scale industrial output. The cultural priority was clear: acquire a functional, mechanical skill quickly, execute it faster than your peer, and never stop grinding.

That famine mindset produced bizarre career bubbles in modern Korea:

  • The Chinese Language Boom: Following the normalization of diplomatic relations between Seoul and Beijing in the 1990s, universities saw an explosion of Chinese literature majors based on promises of lifetime employment. Today, that pure translation skill has been commoditized by bilingual returnees and real-time AI translation engines.
  • The Coding Bootcamp Frenzy: In recent years, thousands of humanities graduates poured into short-term programming academies under the slogan that "everyone must code." Before many could land long-term careers, tools like GitHub Copilot began generating baseline code with greater speed and fewer bugs than an entry-level programmer.

Treating human beings as functional machines produces what Song calls the "Copycat Human" (sabon-ingan)—workers trained to produce uniform, standardized results according to a corporate manual. Because the cost of reproducing standardized intellectual work is dropping to zero, copycat labor is being squeezed out of the market.

Song illustrates this economic shift using a stark retail comparison: the mass-produced supermarket ice cream bar priced at 600 won (about $0.45) versus an artisanal scoop of handmade organic gelato sold in an upscale Seoul cafe for 8,000 won (roughly $6.00). Functionally, both products solve the same biological problem: delivering sugar and cooling body temperature. If value were based purely on calories per won, the cheap industrial bar wins easily. Yet consumers eagerly pay more than ten times the price for gelato. They are not purchasing base function; they are buying taste, curation, philosophy, and origin. As an advanced economy matures, routine function drops in value while taste, authenticity, and distinct human narrative claim the premium.

To survive this divergence, Song argues that workers must transition from copycats to "Original Humans" (wonbon-ingan). His survival formula relies on three non-negotiable practices:

  • Do it yourself (Jikjeop handa): Stop relying on subcontractors, juniors, or corporate layers to do the actual thinking. Those who manage solely through verbal delegation lose the hands-on craft needed to command modern digital tools.
  • Publish it (Balhaeng handa): Internal documents hoarded inside a desk drawer generate zero standalone market value. Professionals must release their ideas, analyses, and work directly into the public sphere to build audience validation.
  • Put your name on it (Ireumeul geonda): Stop hiding behind the protective fog of the department logo. Longevity at a conglomerate does not confer individual skill. When corporate titles fall away, the only asset that endures is a verifiable, public track record built under your own name.

What to Watch Next

As this cultural transition accelerates across South Korea, several developments will signal whether corporate institutions can adapt or whether they will fracture under generational pressure:

  • Labor Contract Modernization: Watch how both major conglomerates (chaebol) and mid-sized enterprises respond to growing demands from younger white-collar unions for strict clock-out enforcement and transparent, merit-based compensation over traditional seniority-based pay ladders.
  • Corporate Restructuring in Administrative Roles: Pay close attention to early retirement packages (myeongye toejik) creeping further down the managerial ranks. As companies integrate enterprise AI workflows, middle-management layers dedicated purely to administrative review will face severe contraction.
  • The Rise of Solo-Operated Professional Brands: Track the migration of top Korean creative, legal, and engineering talent leaving traditional corporate institutions to establish boutique studios and independent practices. How successfully these independent operators build sustainable businesses will prove whether Song's "Lightweight Civilization" can replace the safety net of the traditional corporate umbrella.
