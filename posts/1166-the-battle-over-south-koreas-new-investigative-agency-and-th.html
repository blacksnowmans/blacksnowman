The Battle Over South Korea's New Investigative Agency and the Shadow of Prosecutorial Power

October 15, 2026

For decades, South Korea’s prosecutors wielded an almost untouchable combination of investigative and indicting powers, leading to deep public frustration over politically motivated probes and high-profile abuses. Now, as the administration of President Lee Jae-myung pushes forward with sweeping institutional reforms, the creation of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency aims to dismantle that monopoly once and for all. But as lawmakers gathered to vet the nominee to lead this powerful new body, the central question hung heavily in the air: can a product of the old prosecutorial system truly be trusted to tear it down?

The high-stakes confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong, nominee to head the newly established Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, has laid bare the contradictions, anxieties, and fierce political battles defining South Korea's ongoing legal overhauls. While the administration has signaled a broader shift toward pragmatic administrative competence—recently appointing National Tax Service Commissioner Lim Kwang-hyun as chief presidential secretary—all eyes remain fixed on whether the new investigative agency can escape the ghosts of South Korea's past.

Confronting the Legacy of Politically Motivated Investigations

As the confirmation hearing commenced, Kim Ji-yong adopted a humble posture, promising honest and straightforward answers. Yet, the mountain he had to climb was steep. South Korean society bears deep scars from the prosecution's historical reliance on targeted investigations, exhaustive "dust-sweeping" audits, and predetermined indictments designed to fit a desired narrative. Kim explicitly vowed that the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency would never repeat these outdated practices.

Skepticism runs deep regarding whether a former insider of the prosecution service can effectively lead an independent watchdog. During the hearings, Kim acknowledged the severe damage inflicted on the institution during the tenure of scandal-plagued former leader Yoon Suk Yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment. Kim conceded that Yoon's influence exerted a profoundly negative impact on the organization, a rare admission from within the ranks that underscores just how abnormal investigative practices had become during that era.

Yet, the public demands a structural break from the past, not mere apologies. The Serious Crimes Investigation Agency is meant to fill the vacuum left by stripped-down prosecutorial powers and establish itself as a truly independent investigative body. True reform means permanently separating investigation from indictment and insulating the agency from political pressure.

Contradictory Histories and the Burden of Past Approvals

Opposition lawmakers rigorously probed Kim's past record, zeroing in on instances where he allegedly showed passive resistance or complicity during controversial cases. Lawmakers questioned his involvement in protective actions surrounding figures like Han Dong-hoon and the controversial indictment of former presidential secretary Lee Kwang-chul. While Kim argued that he had expressed dissenting views internally, his refusal to submit key documents undermined his claims of transparency.

When pressed by lawmakers about his responsibility for aggressive and legally flawed indictments—such as the case surrounding Kim Hak-kee’s travel ban and the prosecution of Lee Kwang-chul, which the Supreme Court ultimately threw out—Kim offered personal apologies. However, critics argue that a commander who signed off on politically charged, overzealous indictments cannot wash away institutional responsibility with mere expressions of regret.

The tension peaked when opposition questioning framed Kim's past administrative approvals not as mere paperwork, but as enabling the abuses of a politicized prosecution. Critics likened his historical compliance to unlocking the door for thieves, arguing that voicing mild internal dissent offers no absolution for someone seeking to lead a clean-slate agency.

The Dilemma of Democratic Control Versus Political Interference

A major structural debate during the hearings centered on provisions in the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency Act granting the Minister of the Interior and Safety the power to direct specific investigations. Lawmakers questioned why an agency designed to root out corruption should answer to a cabinet minister when even the National Police Agency chief is shielded from direct ministerial control over specific cases.

Kim defended the provision as a mechanism for "democratic control," but critics warn it is a double-edged sword. Without strict neutrality from the executive branch, the new agency risks transforming from an unchecked prosecutorial monster into a political attack dog for the ruling administration. Kim responded to concerns about political pressure by invoking the classic refrain of investigators: "As someone who investigates, I always keep a resignation letter in my pocket." Whether a piece of paper can withstand the heavy hand of realpolitik, however, remains to be seen.

Further controversy erupted when opposition members accused Kim of double standards. While Kim had previously participated in a collective statement by senior prosecutors defending their own ranks, he simultaneously argued against allowing independent-minded dissenting voices within the service like prosecutor Im Eun-jung. Lawmakers sharply criticized the hypocrisy of viewing one's own collective actions as noble loyalty while dismissing identical behavior by others as insubordination.

What to Watch Next

As the confirmation dust settles, the real test for Kim Ji-yong—and for President Lee Jae-myung's broader reform agenda—begins now. Observers and the public should monitor several concrete developments:

    The Agency's First High-Profile Cases: Watch whether the newly launched body immediately takes on politically sensitive probes with absolute independence or shows hesitation when dealing with institutional allies.
  • Implementation of Oversight Mechanisms: Observe how the National Assembly and civil society monitor the Interior Ministry's investigatory command powers to prevent executive overreach.
  • Internal Personnel and Cultural Shifts: Track whether the agency purports a genuine generational shift by promoting prosecutors and investigators who broke away from old-guard practices, rather than recycling former loyalists.
  • Legislative Follow-ups: Monitor any upcoming statutory amendments designed to further clarify the separation of investigation and indictment powers.

The birth of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency represents a historic mandate to end decades of prosecutorial overreach. Whether Kim Ji-yong becomes a pioneer of genuine judicial reform or merely the guardian of a rebranded institution will depend entirely on his willingness to trade old institutional loyalties for strict, unyielding adherence to the rule of law.
