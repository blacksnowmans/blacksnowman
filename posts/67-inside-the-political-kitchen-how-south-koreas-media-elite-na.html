Inside the Political Kitchen: How South Korea’s Media Elite Navigates a Crisis of Trust

September 27, 2026

On a breezy afternoon in Mapo, a district in Seoul known for its vibrant culinary scene, three prominent figures in South Korean media gathered at a local favorite: Mapo Duck Farm. The group included veteran journalist Hong Sa-hun, lawyer and commentator Jung Pil-seung, and investigative journalist Lee Myung-soo. While the setting was informal, the conversation quickly shifted from the sizzling duck on the table to the volatile state of South Korean politics.

Hanging on the wall of the restaurant was a framed autograph from Lee Jae-myung, the current President of South Korea and leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal opposition party). This small detail served as a backdrop for a discussion that went beyond simple gossip, offering a raw, unfiltered look at the challenges facing the current administration and the broader media landscape.

The Sizzling Grill and the Political Chill

As the duck cooked, the tone shifted from lighthearted banter about traditional Korean spirits to the heavy burden of public office. The discussion focused on the recent televised press conference by President Lee. While the President attempted a more measured, lower-key approach compared to his typically assertive style, the reception from both the media and the conservative political bloc was icy.

Jang Dong-hyuk, a key figure in the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party), dismissed the event as a “hollow word-fest” designed to appease the left rather than address real national concerns. More harshly, former People Power Party leader Han Dong-hoon took to Facebook to compare the President’s conduct to the infamous 1987 "April 13th Constitutional Protection Declaration," a move perceived as an attempt to freeze the political status quo during the military dictatorship era.

The participants at the table argued that the problem is not merely a matter of messaging, but a deep-seated structural distrust. Lawyer Jung Pil-seung noted, "When people meet the President unofficially, they become 'unofficial power brokers.' They mistake proximity for actual authority, and that perception becomes a trap for the President himself." The consensus was clear: in a climate of intense polarization, any attempt at communication is immediately filtered through a lens of skepticism.

The Cost of Words in a Digital Media Landscape

The conversation took an introspective turn when the group touched upon an apology issued by Hong Sa-hun on Facebook. Hong had recently apologized for what he felt was a "rude" remark directed at Shin Jang-sik, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, during a broadcast. The incident highlights the immense pressure media figures face in an era where every utterance is scrutinized and amplified across social media.

The group reflected on how the nature of political broadcasting has evolved. Before the most recent change in government, much of the content was focused on satire and mockery. Now, with the political landscape shifted, the role of media has transitioned into a more combative, watchdog function. This shift has raised the stakes: a journalist’s word carries as much weight—and potential for fallout—as that of a professional in any high-stakes field like law or medicine.

The "Court Dismissal" Blunder

Perhaps the most critical point discussed was the recent legislative "haepning" (or incident) regarding the dismissal of public prosecutions. Just before the local elections, members of the Democratic Party floated the idea of a bill involving the dismissal of certain prosecutions. The timing was disastrous.

Even moderate voters, who might have agreed with the abstract logic that manipulated prosecutions should be nullified, were turned off by the political timing. The panelists noted that the President was reportedly baffled by the move, asking, "Are they trying to help me or feed me to the wolves?" The incident proved that in politics, as in rhetoric, logic is useless if the audience’s emotional walls are already sealed shut. As the participants noted, if voters have already decided they distrust you, no amount of logical policy explanation will penetrate that wall.

Data, Polls, and the Mirage of Reliability

The group also criticized the current state of public opinion polling in Korea. They highlighted how indices of "trusted politicians" often mirror nothing more than the frequency of media exposure. For example, some polls showed former Minister Han Dong-hoon near the top of reliability rankings, but this coincided precisely with a three-day period where he dominated headlines due to a high-profile parliamentary spat. The participants warned against reading too much into these numbers, noting that they often reflect the "recency bias" of the public rather than genuine, long-term confidence.

What to Watch Next

As the meal concluded, the group moved away from abstract theory toward concrete expectations. For the current administration to recover, they argued, it cannot rely on televised press conferences or clever rhetoric.

  • Cutting off unofficial channels: The influence of self-appointed "power brokers" surrounding the President must be neutralized.
  • Action over speech: The administration needs to prove its sincerity through policy results that address common-sense concerns rather than just partisan ones.
  • Recognizing the emotional threshold: Political leaders must accept that the "market" for their brand of communication is currently saturated with distrust; they must pivot to smaller, tangible wins to slowly regain credibility.

The conversation at Mapo Duck Farm was a reminder that in South Korea's high-octane political scene, the most important work often happens away from the cameras. Whether these media commentators can continue to bridge the gap between complex political reality and public understanding remains to be seen. For now, the takeaway is clear: the era of "word-fests" is over, and the era of "demonstrable action" has begun.
