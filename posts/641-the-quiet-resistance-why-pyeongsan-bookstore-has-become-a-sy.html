The Quiet Resistance: Why Pyeongsan Bookstore Has Become a Symbol of Political Longing in Korea

October 03, 2026

The image of Pyeongsan Bookstore flickered across my YouTube feed, and for a moment, it lingered in my mind. In a world that feels increasingly noisy and chaotic, the constant stream of distressing news and the exhausting nature of modern Korean politics—which seem designed to provoke fatigue rather than offer reassurance—can leave one feeling adrift. I found myself wondering: how are we expected to maintain our composure in these turbulent times?

I stumbled upon a conversation that seemed to address this exact frustration. What began as a lighthearted, almost casual mention of visiting Pyeongsan Bookstore—the private bookstore founded by former President Moon Jae-in in his rural retirement village—soon deepened into a profound meditation on the dignity we have lost and the true essence of leadership.

The Complexity Behind the Jokes: Seeking Connection in an Age of Scandal

The speaker in the video began by mixing humor with genuine political yearning. They admitted to wanting to visit the bookstore, noting with a hint of bitterness that while they knew they would never actually have the chance to sit down with former President Moon to discuss the "Obal-saju" incident (a complex, sensitive political controversy involving allegations of improper influence-peddling), the fantasy persists.

The speaker’s admission—that they simply wanted a space for honest, unfiltered conversation—highlights the helplessness an individual feels when facing major historical events. We are accustomed to the rigid, formal statements of politicians, but we rarely get a glimpse into the internal state of the figures at the center of history's turning points. The speaker mused, "Do I want to enter politics? Do I want to have to answer these questions at that level?" These aren't just questions of power; they are existential inquiries into how one endures political turmoil.

Pyeongsan Bookstore has, in effect, become a sanctuary. It is a place where people go not just to buy books, but to commune with the image of a leader who, despite being the target of endless political attacks, has chosen to retreat into the quiet rhythm of turning pages. It represents a collective desire to believe that, amidst the shouting, there is still a space for peace.

"Loose Solidarity" as a Survival Mechanism

The conversation also touched upon the speaker's connection with Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal writer and former minister who served under the Roh Moo-hyun administration. The speaker emphasized the idea that "we are all loosely connected." In a society defined by fierce, individualistic competition, this sentiment—that our personal struggles are not isolated and that even the quiet life in a remote village touches our own daily existence—serves as a necessary form of solidarity.

The Social Value of "Loose Solidarity"

While movements built on rigid, top-down organizations can often become dogmatic or even aggressive, a "loose solidarity" rooted in individual conscience and common sense provides the resilience needed to sustain a society. When fragmented individuals confirm each other's existence through shared empathy, they generate the energy needed to recover the daily lives that polarizing politics have damaged.

We have long been trapped in the illusion that politics will solve everything. In reality, modern politics has become the primary source of our collective exhaustion. In such an environment, the solution isn't another grand, loud declaration, but the creation of social "vessels"—places and dialogues—that allow us to listen to one another’s pain.

The Loss of Dignity: When Power Becomes a "Nanjang"

The speaker did not hold back in their criticism of the current political climate, describing it as a nanjang—a term for a chaotic, disorderly marketplace or a scene of utter bedlam. They invoked the image of the traditional seonbi (Confucian scholars of the Joseon Dynasty), arguing that politics should be conducted by people of scholarly depth, personal integrity, and dignity.

Instead, contemporary Korean politics is dominated by a lack of basic shame. When those in the upper echelons of power lack even the most fundamental dignity, the moral standards of the entire society plummet. The speaker didn't mince words, using the derogatory term ssangnom—a historical term for people of low, unrefined status—to describe those who have seized power without the moral qualification to hold it. They argued that these figures have privatized power, treating public office as a tool for personal gain and arrogance rather than a responsibility for the public good.

This is precisely why political fatigue is so high. We aren't just watching a disagreement; we are watching a degradation of the public square. When the people who are supposed to hold the country together lose their sense of decorum, the media, the judiciary, and the citizenry all begin to feel the strain of that rot.

Restoring Intellectual Dialogue

The final conclusion of the conversation was perhaps the most crucial: the importance of character and the restoration of deep, intellectual dialogue. The speaker noted that even the most perfect system will collapse if operated by people of low character. Conversely, even a flawed system can be sustained by people with the integrity to admit their mistakes, empathize with others, and control their own greed.

True "intellectual dialogue," as described by the speaker, isn't about using fancy terminology to look smart or alienating others. It is about listening to opposing voices and thinking deeply about how to solve real-world problems. They suggest that the "shameful" behavior of current leaders should serve as a banjeomgyosa—a "mirror" or "negative example"—that teaches us how to cultivate a more solid inner life.

What to watch next: As we look toward the future, the question is not who will win the next election, but whether the public can move past the urge to match the aggression of our leaders with our own bitterness. Watch for whether grassroots movements can successfully foster these "intellectual spaces"—small, quiet, and consistent efforts to build community outside of the influence of the mainstream, hyper-polarized political media. The real work of political reform in Korea may not happen in the National Assembly, but in the small, "loose" connections formed by citizens who choose dignity over outrage.
