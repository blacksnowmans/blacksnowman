The End of the "Borrow Everything" Era: South Korea’s 3% Interest Rate Reality

September 01, 2026

For years, the South Korean property market was fueled by a simple, intoxicating belief: that borrowing every available won to buy an apartment was the only path to middle-class security. But the landscape has shifted violently. With the Bank of Korea (BOK), the country’s central bank, pushing the base interest rate to 3.00% over two consecutive hikes, that long-standing rope of cheap credit has snapped. The sound echoing through the housing market isn't just the adjustment of a decimal point; it is a cold, clear warning that the era of aggressive, debt-fueled speculation is over.

The timing of these financial shifts coincides with a major cabinet reshuffle. The government has replaced the heads of the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT)—the agency responsible for national housing and infrastructure—and the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF). While the administration frames this as a strategic policy reset, the internal reality is different. This is not a shift in philosophy; it is the installation of "execution-oriented" officials—loyal hands and feet tasked with carrying out the presidential office's orders without hesitation.

We are watching a clash between the central bank, which is pouring water to extinguish an overheated property fire, and the administration, which is wielding the hammer of supply-side policy under strict orders from the presidential office. Where this leaves homeowners and those in the unique Korean jeonse (long-term deposit rental) system is a question of impending fallout rather than hopeful adjustment. Let’s look at the situation for what it is.

The Death of the "Borrow Everything" Era

The Bank of Korea, led by Governor Shin Hyun-song, is determined to curb the property bubble. With the base rate now at 3.00%, the "dot plot" of the Monetary Policy Committee—which maps out the future interest rate projections of its members—suggests there is no respite in sight. A majority of the committee members are pointing to 3.25%, and six have explicitly left the door open for a hike to 3.50%. For those hoping for a sudden pivot due to economic cooling, the message is grim: there is no downward path for interest rates in the immediate future.

The real bomb, however, is not just the interest rate itself, but the resulting collapse of the jeonse mechanism. In Korea, property value is tied to this unique system where tenants provide a massive lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent. The standard valuation formula for a property is roughly "Annual Rent Value divided by the Interest Rate."

Imagine an apartment with an annual rental value of about $18,000 (24 million KRW). When interest rates are 4%, the deposit—the jeonse price—is calculated at roughly $450,000 (600 million KRW). But when rates spike to 6%, that same rental value justifies a deposit of only $300,000 (400 million KRW). As interest rates rise, tenants find it cheaper to pay monthly rent than to take out massive loans for jeonse. This decline in jeonse values erodes the foundation of "gap investing," where buyers use the tenant's deposit to finance their purchase. When that support vanishes, landlords are forced into desperate sales, creating a vicious cycle where falling jeonse prices pull down the price of the homes themselves.

Policy as Execution, Not Planning

The recent cabinet reshuffle underscores a troubling trend: the loss of independent policy expertise. The promotion of Hong Ji-sun—a career administrator—to Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, and the shake-up of the Ministry of Economy and Finance, are not about bringing in fresh intellectual leaders. They are about ensuring that the presidential office’s will is executed with speed and zero friction.

When ministers become mere subordinates who lack the mandate to challenge the presidential office or pivot based on local market realities, the government loses its ability to respond to the nuance of a changing market. The current refusal to appoint a dedicated Secretary for Land and Transport, while stubbornly sticking to existing supply-side targets, suggests an administration that believes simply meeting paperwork quotas for housing units will satisfy a market currently being crushed by the weight of high interest rates.

This "execution-only" approach ignores the reality of the construction sector. With Project Financing (PF) drying up and construction costs skyrocketing, government-mandated schedules are increasingly untethered from the financial feasibility of private projects. The administration is prioritizing the appearance of action over the reality of a market in distress.

The Domino Effect: From High-End to Middle-Class

We are already seeing the domino effect in action. The most expensive apartments in Seoul—the top 20% of the market—were the first to turn toward a decline. This is a red flag: even the wealthiest investors, who usually hold significant cash reserves, have stopped buying. This hesitancy is now cascading down to the middle and upper-middle segments of the market.

Furthermore, the government’s attempt to revitalize urban renewal and reconstruction projects—intended to stabilize the market—carries a hidden danger. These projects require thousands of residents to move out simultaneously. When these families are forced into the rental market at the same time, it creates a massive demand shock. While these projects might one day increase supply, in the short term, they act as a "time bomb" for the rental market, likely causing a spike in monthly rent for working-class tenants by 2025.

Survival in a Changing Tide

For those sitting on the sidelines, the temptation to "buy the dip" is strong. However, given the clear trajectory of the central bank's tightening cycle, jumping in now is like trying to catch a falling knife. The market is heading for a period of extended stagnation where even heavily discounted properties may struggle to find buyers.

For those who have already "borrowed everything" (a practice known in Korea as yeong-geul, or "pulling from the soul"), the strategy must be one of liquidation and preservation. Do not be fooled by government rhetoric or minor regulatory tweaks; interest rates and the flow of money are the ultimate arbiters of asset value.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the volume of jeonse deposits being returned to tenants. If landlords begin to struggle with liquidity as they return these deposits, it will be the strongest signal that the property market’s underlying foundations are beginning to crack. In this environment, the wisest navigator is not the one holding onto the rudder, but the one who knows when to fold the sails before the storm fully hits.
