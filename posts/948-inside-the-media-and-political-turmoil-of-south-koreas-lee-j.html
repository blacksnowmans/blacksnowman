Inside the Media and Political Turmoil of South Korea's Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 10, 2026

One year into South Korea's current political cycle under President Lee Jae-myung, the mood across the country is not one of celebration, but of profound fatigue. The public square feels increasingly frayed, with citizens caught between the promises of a progressive administration and the grinding realities of governance. To understand why the political atmosphere has soured so quickly, one has to look beyond standard partisan talking points and examine how power, media, and public critique are colliding in real time.

Recently, prominent political commentator and writer Rhyu Si-min dissected this dynamic during a broadcast, cutting straight to the core of the administration’s struggles. Rather than offering a simplistic defense or attack, Rhyu pointed to deeper systemic issues: the friction over prosecutorial reform, the erosion of the traditional media landscape, and, perhaps most damagingly, the toxic influence of blind partisanship within the progressive camp itself. What is happening in South Korea today is not merely a series of political miscalculations; it is a tectonic shift in how politics and media interact in a digital democracy.

The Messy Reality of Prosecutorial Reform

Prosecutorial reform has been a perennial battleground in South Korean politics, stretching from the administration of the late liberal President Roh Moo-hyun through the term of Moon Jae-in, and now into the Lee Jae-myung era. The public mandate for change was clear: strip the prosecution of its sweeping, unchecked investigative and indicting powers, which historically enabled systemic political abuse.

However, a severe gap has emerged between President Lee’s initial blueprint and what his supporters expected. Instead of a transparent, head-on overhaul—what Rhyu described as a complete "rebuilding" of the institution—the administration often attempted to bypass direct political friction by pushing initiatives through task forces or cabinet ministers. When governments try to fundamentally restructure powerful state apparatuses through half-measures, pushback is inevitable.

Despite previous legislative efforts, including past attempts to separate investigation from indictment, residual investigative authorities and loopholes continue to protect entrenched bureaucratic interests. Rhyu’s critique highlights a fundamental truth about South Korean reform efforts: you cannot clean out a corrupt institution without completely gutting its rotten framework. Attempting to manage the bureaucracy rather than overhauling it has left the administration bogged down in institutional resistance.

The Danger of Blind Partisanship and Falling Approval Ratings

This institutional friction has coincided with a troubling decline in presidential approval ratings, driven in part by alienation among core supporters. Following recent local elections, public comments from the presidential office—touching on the delegation and assumption of absolute authority—sent shockwaves through the progressive base. To longtime supporters, language that hints at unchecked executive power feels dangerously detached from democratic norms.

More alarmingly, the administration’s defensive posture has been amplified by a vocal faction of pro-government commentators and ultra-loyalists. Over the past year, these groups have treated any constructive criticism of the president's policies as an act of treason, labeling internal dissenters as "traitors to the cause." When the president's inner circle or public defenders frame political disagreements as illegitimate attacks rather than engaging with valid policy failures—ranging from real estate regulations to agricultural policies—they actively alienate the very coalition that brought them to power.

Critique is not sabotage; it is the vital friction required to keep a government honest. When an administration surrounds itself exclusively with voices offering uncritical praise, it risks burning down its own political foundation from within.

The Collapse of Legacy Media and the Rise of "Partisan Journalism"

Parallel to these political fractures, South Korea's media ecosystem is undergoing a radical, irreversible transformation. For decades, legacy outlets—major conservative newspapers like the Chosun Ilbo and liberal ones like the Hankyoreh—acted as the gatekeepers of national discourse. Today, that monopoly has evaporated.

Fueled by ubiquitous smartphones and platforms like YouTube and Instagram, the public square has expanded far beyond traditional newsrooms. Legacy media outlets are struggling not simply because of economic headwinds, but because citizens no longer wish to consume top-down, pre-packaged narratives. Journalism has been democratized, stripped from institutional gatekeepers and placed directly into the hands of the public.

In this new environment, traditional outlets clinging to the illusion of detached neutrality are losing ground to overtly partisan, personality-driven digital channels that wear their ideologies proudly. While legacy media often hides conservative or establishment biases behind a mask of objectivity, digital platforms act openly as "partisan media," fighting explicitly in the corners of their respective audiences. For modern media consumers, authenticity and alignment matter more than a hollow performance of neutrality.

Meanwhile, traditional conglomerates—particularly construction and large capital firms—continue to buy stakes in media companies as a form of "conspicuous consumption," treating journalistic influence as a vestige of feudal power. But as Rhyu bluntly noted, holding a major media stake no longer guarantees political sway. Citizens scrolling on their phones can instantly see through corporate framing and institutional posturing.

The Double-Edged Sword of Algorithmic Polarization

Yet, this decentralized media revolution is far from a democratic utopia. The very algorithms that liberate citizens from traditional gatekeepers also trap them in echo chambers of confirmation bias.

When algorithms serve users only what they want to believe, political polarization intensifies. The extreme radicalization seen on both flanks of South Korean politics—whether detached conservative fringes or unyielding progressive zealots—feeds on this digital siloing. The inability to hear opposing viewpoints, combined with the treatment of political adversaries as existential enemies, poses a severe threat to democratic health.

Technology cannot be blamed for human tribalism; digital platforms merely amplify the raw impulses of the offline world. Navigating this era requires a conscious effort by citizens to step outside algorithmic comfort zones, filter out disinformation, and engage with uncomfortable facts.

What to Watch Next

"Critics are not here to be the concubines of power; they must be the first to sound the alarm when power strays."

As the Lee Jae-myung administration moves past its first turbulent year, several critical indicators will determine its trajectory:

  • The Handling of Dissent: Watch whether the presidential office continues to lean into insular, defensive rhetoric against its critics, or whether it opens the door to genuine policy corrections on key economic and civil issues.
  • The Institutionalization of Reform: Monitor whether stalled judicial and prosecutorial overhauls are quietly abandoned, or if the administration attempts a more transparent, structural redesign.
  • The Evolution of Digital Public Squares: Observe how political commentators and independent digital media platforms navigate their relationship with the state, and whether a healthier balance between partisan loyalty and objective critique can emerge.

Ultimately, the health of South Korean democracy no longer depends on what legacy newspapers print or what political elites dictate from above. It rests squarely on the ability of citizens, armed with smartphones and critical minds, to separate substance from spin and hold power accountable.
