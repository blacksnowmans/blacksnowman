A Friendly Fire Incident: Why Rhiu Si-min’s Warning to President Lee Jae-myung Shook the Ruling Party

October 30, 2026

In the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, words are often used as bayonets, but rarely do they cut as deep as when they are wielded by a friend. On a Tuesday that felt like any other in Seoul’s political district of Yeouido, Rhiu Si-min—a man often described as the "ideological architect" of the Korean liberal movement—dropped a rhetorical bombshell that sent the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s current ruling party, into a state of visible panic. His assessment was blunt: President Lee Jae-myung, the man Rhiu helped propel to the Blue House, might not actually finish his five-year term.

For a foreign observer, this might sound like standard political hyperbole. However, in the context of South Korea’s recent history, where the previous leader, Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol, was impeached and subsequently imprisoned, the suggestion that a presidency could end prematurely is not a metaphor—it is a trauma. When Rhiu Si-min speaks, the liberal base listens. A former Minister of Health and Welfare and a prolific writer, Rhiu has long been the "liberal conscience" for millions. For him to suggest that the Lee administration is facing an existential crisis is the equivalent of a primary structural engineer pointing out cracks in the foundation of a newly built skyscraper.

The reaction from the "Pro-Lee" faction—known in Korea as the Chin-myeong—was instantaneous and visceral. They didn't just disagree; they felt betrayed. The fallout from Rhiu’s comments has opened a window into the growing anxieties within the ruling party, revealing a government that is struggling to balance its reformist mandate with the harsh realities of a divided legislature and a public that is growing weary of constant political warfare.

The Beetle and the Reverse Current: Rhiu’s Scathing Metaphors

To understand why the Democratic Party is so upset, one must look at the specific imagery Rhiu Si-min used to describe President Lee Jae-myung’s governance. Rhiu is famous for his "Beetle Theory" (ttakjeong-beolle-ron), a metaphor he has used to describe political entities that appear hard and resilient on the outside but are hollow or stagnant on the inside. In his latest critique, he suggested that the Lee administration is currently like a ship caught in a powerful reverse current. While the President and his staff are rowing hard, Rhiu argues they lack a clear destination and the "unifying leadership" required to bring the crew—and the country—along with them.

Rhiu’s core argument is that the "political assets" Lee Jae-myung brought to the presidency are being squandered. He hinted that the current administration is beginning to mirror the "unilateralism" seen during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol, a period marked by executive overreach and a total breakdown in communication with the opposition. For the DPK, being compared to the man they fought so hard to impeach is the ultimate insult. Rhiu suggested that unless the President fundamentally changes his approach to governance and communication, the "disintegration of the camp" is inevitable.

"The President might not be able to fill his term."

This single sentence, uttered by one of the camp’s most influential speakers, provided a ready-made narrative for the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party. By voicing the "impeachment" or "resignation" subtext that usually only comes from the opposition, Rhiu has effectively lowered the shield that usually protects a sitting president from his own base. He argued that the current administration is failing to provide a vision that can overcome the "historical headwinds" and the fierce resistance from the remnants of the previous regime.

"A Grenade in the Barracks": The Fury of the Pro-Lee Faction

The response from the DPK’s inner circle was not one of reflection, but of defensive aggression. In South Korea, where mandatory military service is a foundational experience for most men, the metaphor of the "internal grenade" carries immense weight. Several high-ranking DPK officials and lawmakers from the Pro-Lee faction accused Rhiu of "throwing a grenade inside the barracks"—an act of ultimate betrayal where a soldier kills his own comrades while they are resting.

The fury stems from the timing. The Lee Jae-myung government is currently navigating a "minority government" reality, where despite holding the presidency, they face a hostile and emboldened opposition in the National Assembly. To the Pro-Lee faction, Rhiu isn't offering "constructive criticism"; he is providing the opposition with the ammunition they need to paralyze the government. They argue that at a time when the party should be circling the wagons to protect the President's reform agenda and address the livelihoods of the people, an influential "outsider" like Rhiu is sowing the seeds of doubt among the core supporters.

  • Rep. Jeon Yong-gi, a young and vocal DPK lawmaker, stated clearly that he "cannot agree with Rhiu’s remarks at all," arguing that shaking the President's leadership is not the way to restore the liberal camp's strength.
  • Kim Yong-nam, a former lawmaker, was even more blunt, suggesting that Rhiu is "dragging the President toward failure" rather than helping him, and that his timing was "entirely inappropriate."
  • Other party insiders have grumbled that Rhiu is acting like a "cynical spectator" rather than a participant in the difficult work of governing, critiquing the play from the comfort of the press box while the players are bleeding on the field.

The "internal grenade" rhetoric highlights a deep-seated fear within the DPK: that the coalition which brought Lee to power is fragile. If the intellectuals and the "influencers" of the movement start jumping ship, the grassroots supporters might not be far behind. This is especially sensitive for Lee Jae-myung, whose political career has been defined by his ability to maintain a fiercely loyal "fandom" of supporters who view any internal criticism as a form of heresy.

Strategic Silence: The Leadership’s Attempt at Damage Control

While the rank-and-file lawmakers were busy venting their frustrations, the official party leadership took a different, more cautious approach. Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the DPK leadership and a key strategist, tried to de-escalate the situation by devaluing Rhiu’s comments. When asked by reporters for his reaction to the "shortened term" remark, Kim simply replied, "There is no need to evaluate or respond to it."

This "no comment" strategy is a classic political maneuver designed to starve a controversy of oxygen. By refusing to engage in a back-and-forth with Rhiu, the leadership hopes to prevent the "disintegration" narrative from gaining further traction. Kim Min-seok also issued a broader warning to the party and its supporters, urging everyone to "refrain from the language of division." The goal is to project an image of a party that is too busy solving the nation's problems to be distracted by the musings of a single intellectual.

However, not everyone in the leadership was so dismissive. Han Min-soo, another DPK lawmaker, offered a more nuanced interpretation. He suggested that while the form of Rhiu’s criticism was provocative, the "intent" was likely to emphasize the need for unity and to sound an alarm for the camp to wake up. This "soft" interpretation attempts to bridge the gap between Rhiu’s stinging critique and the party’s need for stability. It acknowledges that there is a "crisis of communication" without admitting that the President’s term is actually in jeopardy.

The party is essentially facing a dual challenge. On one hand, they must suppress internal dissent to maintain a united front against the People Power Party. On the other hand, they cannot simply ignore the warnings of people like Rhiu Si-min, because his concerns often reflect the quiet anxieties of the moderate voters who decide elections. If the DPK becomes a "closed loop" that only listens to its own praise, it risks alienating the very people it needs to maintain its mandate.

The "Double Anxiety" of the Lee Jae-myung Governance Style

Beyond the immediate drama, the Rhiu Si-min controversy has forced a conversation about how President Lee Jae-myung communicates. One of the most pointed critiques came from Park Yong-jin, the Vice Chair of the Regulatory Rationalization Committee and a prominent moderate voice within the DPK. Park noted that while the government’s own PR efforts might feel "comfortable" to the President’s inner circle, the public is feeling a "double anxiety."

This "double anxiety" refers to the public’s fear that the government is both failing to solve economic issues and failing to maintain political stability. A major point of contention is the President’s reliance on direct SNS (social media) communication. While supporters love Lee’s "frank and uninhibited" style on platforms like X (formerly Twitter) and Facebook, critics—and even some allies—worry that it is "precarious."

The concern is that the President’s unfiltered messages often serve as a lightning rod for unnecessary controversy, distracting from the administration’s policy goals. When Rhiu Si-min talks about the administration lacking "vision," he is partly referring to this: a government that seems to react to the 24-hour news cycle via the President’s smartphone rather than laying out a long-term, stable roadmap for the country. The "SNS style" of leadership worked well for Lee as a populist candidate and a provincial governor, but as the President of a G10 nation, the stakes are significantly higher.

The "Rhiu Si-min incident" is a symptom of a larger struggle: the Democratic Party's transition from a "resistance movement" to a "governing power." In the resistance, firebrands and provocative speakers are assets. In government, they can become liabilities. The question now is whether the Lee administration will view this internal friction as a "bitter medicine" that can lead to reform, or as a "poison" that must be purged.

What to Watch Next

As the dust settles on this specific controversy, the long-term impact on the Lee Jae-myung administration will depend on several key developments:

  • A Shift in Communication: Watch for changes in how the Blue House (the presidential office) manages the President's public messages. If we see fewer late-night SNS posts and more formal, policy-oriented press conferences, it will be a sign that Park Yong-jin and Rhiu Si-min’s critiques were taken to heart.
  • Legislative Breakthroughs: The best way for the DPK to silence talk of a "shortened term" is to pass significant legislation. Watch how the party handles the upcoming budget negotiations with the People Power Party. If they can secure a win on "livelihood" issues (economic relief for the middle class), the "stagnation" narrative will fade.
  • Internal Discipline vs. Dialogue: Will the DPK leadership continue to attack those who offer "friendly fire," or will they create a formal space for internal dissent? If more moderate lawmakers begin to echo Rhiu’s concerns, it could signal a genuine split within the party.
  • Public Opinion Polls: If the President’s approval ratings dip significantly following this internal squabble, it will embolden the opposition to move from rhetorical attacks to more concrete legislative roadblocks.

In the end, the "grenade" thrown by Rhiu Si-min might not have blown up the barracks, but it certainly woke everyone up. Whether they wake up to a new era of more sophisticated governance or simply a more intense period of internal policing will define the remainder of Lee Jae-myung’s presidency.
