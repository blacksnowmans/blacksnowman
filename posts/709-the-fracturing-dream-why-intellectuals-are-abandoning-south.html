The Fracturing Dream: Why Intellectuals Are Abandoning South Korea’s Democratic Leadership

October 05, 2026

To watch a political movement lose its moral compass is a profoundly painful experience, especially when the betrayal comes from those who once promised to realize a "Great Harmony" (Daedong-sesang)—a vision of a more equitable and inclusive society. Recently, Professor Choi Bae-geun, a prominent intellectual and once-staunch supporter of President Lee Jae-myung, publicly withdrew his support. This is not merely a change of heart by a single academic; it is a scathing indictment of a government that has seemingly lost its way.

Choi’s cold confession that he no longer believes in the President’s self-reflection serves as a painful milestone for those who held onto the hope of deep-seated reform. His departure forces us to ask: What became of the movement that promised to dismantle old structures? This analysis seeks to peel back the layers of this disillusionment, examining how incompetent leadership and opportunistic politics have isolated a government and invited its regression.

The Broken Promises of Reform

Professor Choi’s declaration that "the Great Harmony will not come" strikes at the heart of the administration's failures. He argues that since the 2022 presidential election, the core goals of the administration—most notably the reform of the prosecution and a focus on public livelihood—have been systematically hollowed out. Drawing a parallel to the critique by writer Rhyu Si-min, who previously suggested the public was misled on prosecution reform, Choi admitted, "I was fooled."

This is not a mere emotional outburst. It is a warning about the reality facing the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the main liberal opposition party that currently holds the executive branch. The decision to appoint former prosecutors to key government roles was an early signal of a drift away from reformist principles. As Choi noted, the desire to "expand the base" by catering to moderate or conservative voters has effectively consumed the essence of the reform movement itself. When the pursuit of power necessitates compromising with the very systems the movement sought to change, the disillusioned base feels not just ignored, but betrayed.

This failure is further exacerbated by an economic policy that relies too heavily on semiconductors, ignoring the massive wealth inequality growing in the shadows. Choi’s comparison to the "Nokia risk"—where a country becomes over-reliant on a single industry while ignoring systemic shifts—highlights the government's economic ineptitude. Even more concerning are recent reports of potential government censorship regarding state tasks, suggesting a level of administrative isolation that is deeply antithetical to democratic values.

The Crisis of Leadership: Kim Min-seok and Party In-fighting

Much of the ire is directed at Kim Min-seok, the current leader of the Democratic Party. Professor Choi has been blunt, labeling him one of the most incompetent leaders in the party's history. According to this view, Kim is far more obsessed with positioning himself as a candidate for the 2030 presidential election than he is with party unity or governance.

Kim’s recent public appearances have done little to dispel this perception of political vanity. Whether it is his vague excuses regarding urban policy in Seoul or his performative focus on specific electoral battlegrounds (such as the Nakdong River front) in Busan, critics argue these are tactical events rather than policy-driven solutions. His attempts to consolidate power by creating a personal faction within the party have led to a "Myung-Chung War"—a reference to the internal friction between supporters of President Lee Jae-myung and factional leaders like Chung Chung-rae.

When the leadership is more interested in personal political survival than in party cohesion, the entire governing project suffers. This infighting provides a vacuum that the remnants of the conservative establishment are all too happy to exploit. As the party fractures, the "civil sovereignty" (dangwon jugwon) movement—a campaign to give party members more power—has ironically become a tool for factional infighting rather than democratic empowerment.

Confronting Historical Revisionism

Beyond internal party dynamics, there is a deep frustration with the government's failure to firmly address historical revisionism. Recently, members of the National Assembly have made remarks questioning the established truth of the May 18 Gwangju Democratization Movement, which was formally recognized by the Supreme Court as a democratic milestone. Despite these legal precedents, some politicians continue to label these events as "disturbances."

Professor Choi has urged the government to move beyond empty rhetoric and initiate legal action that carries teeth. He suggests a mass civil litigation strategy based on the Constitution, aiming to hold those who spread historical falsehoods accountable financially and legally. The message is clear: if the government truly stands for history, it must treat the negation of democratic atrocities as an attack on the state’s foundation. Failing to do so only emboldens "insurrectionist forces" who seek to rehabilitate the reputation of past military dictatorships.

Looking Ahead: A Test of Civic Consciousness

The ordeal of journalists like Hong Sah-hoon—who has been unfairly maligned as an "opportunist" by partisan commentators—is a microcosm of the current climate. It shows how the movement has begun to cannibalize its own critical voices. When a movement stops being about policy and starts being about tribal loyalty, it loses its ability to self-correct.

As we watch the Democratic Party grapple with these internal and external pressures, the question for the public is not just who is to blame, but what we are willing to accept. Supporting a government does not mean turning a blind eye to its failures; it means holding it to the high standards it promised to uphold.

What to watch next:

  • Party Reorganization: Keep an eye on whether the DPK leadership makes a genuine effort to reconcile with reformist factions or continues its push toward a singular, leader-focused structure.
  • Legal Precedents: Monitor if the proposed "civil litigation" against those denying historical truths gains any momentum, or if it remains purely performative.
  • Economic Policy Shifts: Watch for any meaningful changes in the administration's economic strategy regarding wealth distribution and industrial diversification, which will be the ultimate test of their governance capabilities before the next electoral cycle.

Politics is the art of balancing conflicting interests. Right now, South Korea’s liberals are failing that art, choosing instead to amplify conflicts to secure short-term power. The coming months will be the ultimate test of whether the administration can pivot back to the principled politics they promised, or if they will continue their slow descent into irrelevance.
