The High Cost of Perfection: Why South Korea’s Elite Students Are Dropping Out

October 12, 2026

For a teenager in South Korea, waking up each morning feels less like heading to school and more like deploying to a battlefield. The objective? A single, all-consuming goal: university admission. In recent years, the culture surrounding this has shifted in a dark and desperate direction. Retaking the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT)—known as "N-su"—and dropping out of high school are no longer seen as failures. Instead, they have been rebranded as calculated "strategies." A recent investigative report by the current affairs program Tracking 60 Minutes shed a harsh light on this landscape, asking a haunting question: What are we sacrificing when we offer up our children’s teenage years, and often their entire twenties, on the altar of private education?

The Trap of the "Grade 1" Obsession

Yang Jun-seong, an 18-year-old high school student, cleared his desk the moment his second year ended. In the current climate, even ranking first in his entire school wouldn't guarantee him a spot in a top-tier medical school—the current gold standard for success in Korea. The paralyzing anxiety that a single slip-up in one subject could render years of effort meaningless pushed him to quit school entirely.

His plan? To drop out, study independently, and ace the CSAT to secure a medical degree. His assertion that he "won't even go to university if he doesn't get into med school" isn't the bravado of an arrogant youth; it is a scream of frustration from a generation suffocated by the rigid, unforgiving nature of the internal assessment system.

The numbers bear this out. The number of high school dropouts has surged from roughly 12,800 in 2021 to over 18,600 by 2025. When the number of first-year high school students quitting crosses the 10,000 mark for the first time in history, it confirms that the public education system has ceased to be a reliable ladder for advancement. As public schools falter, the private education industry—especially in the affluent Daechi-dong district of Seoul—reaps the rewards.

The government recently implemented a new five-tier grading system, intended to ease competition by widening the bracket for top grades. Instead, the effect has been paradoxical. With 10% of students now eligible for the top "Grade 1," the pressure to be perfect has intensified. For students already sleeping less than three hours a night, this isn't a relaxation of competition; it is a new level of terror where any minor mistake is career-ending.

The "reset" strategy—dropping out to start over—is a myth that benefits only a few. For the majority, it is simply falling back into the same suffocating swamp of competition.

The Rise of "Pre-Retaking" and the Daechi-dong Machine

The romance of the high school experience has become a luxury no one can afford. A new phenomenon known as seon-jaesu ("pre-retaking") has emerged, where students drop out of high school to treat the private academy environment as their primary reality. They view time spent on school activities as a waste of time that could be better spent prepping for the CSAT.

Even more bizarre is the "re-entry" strategy: students seek out schools with lower academic standards to enroll as transfer students. By doing so, they can easily rank at the top of their new class, padding their records for university admissions. This turns the classroom into a cynical game of manipulation, where those with the resources to "game" the system widen the gap further, proving that in modern Korea, the experience of the admissions game itself has become a form of capital.

Currently, about 180,000 students across the country are classified as "N-su" students—those retaking the exam. Despite the warnings from experts like former Seoul National University admissions head Kwon Oh-hyun, who has long argued that the belief that one needs straight A's to get into a top university is a lie manufactured by the admissions industry, the obsession persists. Daechi-dong, which had virtually no specialized retake academies in 2016, now hosts over 1,600. It is a booming economy fueled by the fears of parents and the desperation of youth.

Economic Polarization and the "Golden Ticket" Syndrome

In the back alleys of Daechi-dong, studio apartments command monthly rents of over $1,500 (2 million KRW). Luxury "dormitory academies" that offer hotel-like amenities and parental surveillance cameras can cost over $3,000 a month, and even then, there are long waiting lists.

This creates a glaring divide. While wealthy students are sequestered into these controlled environments, others, like a student identified as Kang, spend their mornings delivering newspapers or working at convenience stores to pay their rent while studying for their fourth attempt at the exam. It is a grueling, inhumane process that forces young people to view their lives as a series of hurdles to be cleared rather than a path to be explored.

The OECD has termed this "Golden Ticket Syndrome." Because a top-tier degree is seen as the only way to ensure a stable, high-income life, young people are sacrificing their most vibrant years in a repetitive, soul-crushing cycle. This delay in entering the workforce and starting families is now a primary driver of Korea's demographic crisis. Even after the fall of the previous administration—with the transition from the era of the incarcerated former leader, the disgraced and imprisoned Mr. Yoon, to the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung—the monstrous machine of the entrance exam remains not only intact but more sophisticated in its reach.

What to Watch Next

The cycle of "N-su" is not merely an educational issue; it is the foundation upon which Korean social anxiety is built. As we look ahead, watch for these two key developments:

  • Policy Adjustments: Keep an eye on how the Ministry of Education responds to the mounting criticism of the five-tier grading system. Expect the administration to be pressured to find a balance between "fairness" and the current qualitative assessment methods that critics claim only fuel further private tutoring.
  • Societal Shift: Observe the growing discourse among youth activists regarding the "next step" after university. As the ROI (return on investment) of an elite degree continues to decline in the face of a changing job market, we may begin to see a shift in the perceived value of these "golden tickets."

True reform will not come from adjusting the grading brackets or the ratio of standardized tests to school grades. It requires a fundamental, agonizing reassessment of a society that views education as a weapon for survival rather than a tool for enlightenment. Until the day a student’s "tomorrow" is valued more than their "Grade 1," the lights in the academies of Daechi-dong will continue to burn all night long.
