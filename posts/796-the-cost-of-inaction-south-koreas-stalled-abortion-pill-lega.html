The Cost of Inaction: South Korea’s Stalled Abortion Pill Legalization

September 17, 2026

Watching a recent video on the ongoing controversy surrounding the legalization of Mifegyne (the abortion pill, often referred to in Korea as "Mifegyne") felt like a stark reminder that we have been living in a strange, limbo-like reality for far too long. Despite a landmark ruling by the Constitutional Court of Korea—the nation’s highest judicial body—in 2019 that declared the criminalization of abortion unconstitutional, thousands of women are still forced to navigate dangerous "gray markets," ordering pills from overseas with no medical oversight. One has to ask: how did we arrive at a point where this is considered the status quo?

While the law sits stagnant and the National Assembly and the government trade excuses, it is the citizens who bear the physical and legal risks. As the state abdicates its responsibility, this issue has been pushed into the shadows. It is time to pull back the curtain on why this fundamental health issue remains unresolved.

The Constitutional Court Ruling and the Lost Years

April 11, 2019, was supposed to be a turning point for women's rights and bodily autonomy in South Korea. The Constitutional Court issued a ruling of "non-conformity" regarding the abortion law, acknowledging that a woman’s right to self-determination must be upheld. Crucially, the court did not merely strike down the law; it set a deadline for the National Assembly to revise the legislation.

The court demanded that the law be updated by December 31, 2020. That date passed years ago, and yet, the government and the legislature remain paralyzed. In this legal vacuum, it is always the most vulnerable individuals who suffer. When the state refuses to act, it raises a fundamental question about the legitimacy of public authority: what is the purpose of the law if it fails to protect the very people it was created to serve?

The primary excuse for this delay has been a cycle of unproductive squabbling over the "number of weeks" for which pregnancy termination should be permitted. While medical, religious, and feminist groups clashed, political leaders—fearful of losing conservative votes or igniting culture wars—simply stepped back. The result is a tragedy of political cowardice. By refusing to find a compromise, the state has left women completely unprotected, effectively abandoning its duty to govern.

A Global Outlier

The absurdity of South Korea’s position becomes even clearer when viewed through an international lens. As of 2024, 101 countries around the world have officially legalized abortion pills, integrating them into standard, safe medical practice. Guaranteeing health rights and increasing access to safe medical procedures has long been a "global standard." Yet, South Korea remains an outlier, refusing to create clear regulations for pharmaceutical approval.

We are in the bizarre situation where a medicine that is prescribed safely in clinics worldwide is treated as contraband in Korea, forcing citizens to import pills personally. By failing to set clear rules, the government has inadvertently fostered a massive underground market. Women seeking these medications are forced to navigate unverified, often predatory, channels, shouldering the fear of counterfeit drugs or medical complications entirely on their own. This is not mere administrative sluggishness; it is a form of institutional violence.

The Cracks in the Cabinet: A Government at Odds

A window into this dysfunction opened during a cabinet meeting on July 14, 2023. The session, which included President Lee Jae-myung, Minister of Food and Drug Safety Oh Yu-kyung, and Minister of Gender Equality and Family Won Min-kyung, exposed the deep fissures within the administration.

Minister Won suggested that even if the Mother and Child Health Act had not yet been fully revised, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety could act administratively to authorize the medication. Minister Oh, however, insisted on a rigid, procedural approach, arguing that the entire legal framework must be perfected before any action could be taken. It was a classic display of bureaucratic friction: one side urging immediate action to alleviate suffering, and the other hiding behind the shield of proceduralism.

President Lee Jae-myung eventually interjected, cutting through the administrative jargon. "If we are talking about prescription medication," the President asked, "why is nothing being done?" He challenged the ministers directly, noting, "The rest of the world is doing this. By leaving it outside the law, the government might be avoiding direct responsibility, but the citizens are being pushed into danger."

The Hypocrisy of "Ethics" as a Tool of Control

Perhaps the most damning critique of this situation is that for decades, South Korean policymakers have blocked women’s autonomy under the guise of "ethics." By invoking vague social morals or outdated customs, decision-makers have attempted to dictate what women can and cannot do with their own bodies. It is a profound hypocrisy: individuals who have no personal stake in the outcome using their power to control the lives of others.

We have run out of time for the excuse of "waiting for social consensus." The Constitutional Court has already spoken, the international community has moved forward, and real-world harm is happening daily. The government’s role is not to wait for the perfect political climate; it is to ensure the safety and rights of its people through a functioning legal and administrative framework.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on the upcoming parliamentary sessions, where pressure is mounting to decouple the approval of abortion medication from the stalled debate on the Mother and Child Health Act. If the administration continues to prioritize "procedure" over public health, expect further legal challenges from civil rights groups who argue that the state’s inaction constitutes a violation of the constitutional rights granted in 2019.

The time for excuses has passed. It is time for a common-sense approach that treats reproductive health as a matter of medical safety rather than a political bargaining chip.
