Why South Korea's Ruling Party YouTube Experiment is Collapsing

October 08, 2026

Every morning at 7:00 AM, a political battleground opens across smartphone screens in South Korea. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the center-left party currently holding the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung—taking control of the media has long been an obsession. For years, liberal politicians have relied heavily on independent, massively popular YouTube commentators like Kim Ou-joon, dancing to the tune of external influencers to reach voters. Deciding they had enough of playing second fiddle, party leaders launched their own official morning current-affairs show: Minti07.

It was meant to be a masterclass in direct communication—a textbook approach where the party sets the agenda instead of reacting to it. Yet, barely a month after its September launch, the show's lead producer abruptly walked out, taking the program's skeleton with her. Viewership numbers have cratered, live concurrent viewers have plunged by two-thirds, and the project has laid bare a deeper, more uncomfortable truth about modern Korean politics: trying to turn YouTube into a state-run megaphone is a recipe for disaster.

The Illusion of the Copy-Paste Success Formula

When Minti07 burst onto the scene on September 1, the production team thought they had cracked the code. They recruited a top-tier main producer—referred to as Producer A—straight from Maebul Show, one of South Korea's most successful political commentary programs. The logic seemed foolproof: take a proven producer, replicate a winning format, and watch the audience flood in.

Instead, reality hit hard. Faced with the brutal physical toll of preparing a daily early-morning live broadcast and stifling production constraints, Producer A threw in the towel by October 7, after just 19 episodes. While a temporary crew has stepped in, the sudden flight of the program’s core brain just a month in exposes a deeply dysfunctional production structure. Audiences do not hand out loyalty based on a resume; they demand content that satisfies their intellectual curiosity.

The numbers tell a stark story of audience fatigue. The premiere episode pulled in a respectable 670,000 views—a flash in the pan. By the fourth broadcast, viewership had halved to 290,000. Concurrent live viewers, which started at a healthy 58,000, have now flatlined at around 20,000, with total views hovering near 150,000. The strategy to get a five-minute head start on rival morning broadcasts failed because the product itself offered viewers no compelling reason to stay.

The Chilling Effect of Party Discipline on the Airwaves

The rot goes deeper than production fatigue. On the very day news of the producer's departure broke, Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok appeared on Minti07 to deliver a blunt warning to party members. Amid internal anxiety and pushback over controversial political appointments—such as Kim Ji-hyung—some lawmakers had dared to voice public concern. Kim Min-seok’s response on live television was chilling: ruling party politicians and public servants needed "self-restraint" regarding their positions, hinting that the party leadership would actively rein in dissenting voices.

For viewers watching at home, the atmosphere felt eerily reminiscent of the heavy-handed control seen during the administration of jailed former leader Yoon Seok-yeol. For a ruling party senior official to use an official media platform to demand silence from dissenting lawmakers is a form of political self-harm. When a party begins treating internal debate not as a healthy sign of democracy, but as a dangerous "assault" on unity, fatigue among supporters quickly reaches a breaking point.

The irony is glaring. Just months prior, Kim Min-seok himself frequently stirred up intra-party friction with aggressive rhetoric under the banner of free expression. Yet when ordinary lawmakers offer mild, sensible criticism of presidential appointments, it is instantly branded as a divisive act that must be controlled. This double standard destroys institutional trust.

When the Presidential Office Invokes Foreign Far-Right Figures for Defense

This allergy to criticism is not confined to the party apparatus; it extends straight into the presidential office. Recently, critics have raised concerns over President Lee Jae-myung’s hyper-active social media use, arguing that the administration desperately needs a buffer group to refine and control his messaging.

Chief Spokesperson Kang Yu-jung stepped up to defend the president, arguing that world leaders—from Brazil’s Lula da Silva to Mexico’s Claudia Sheinbaum and France’s Emmanuel Macron—are active on social media. In an astonishingly ill-conceived defense, the office even cited the massive follower count of Japanese far-right politicians to prove that social media communication is the global norm.

Defending a progressive South Korean president by invoking foreign far-right figures is a desperate look. The public is not worried about how many minutes the president spends scrolling through apps; they care about the qualitative shockwaves sent by a casual post or a thoughtlessly shared article. When the presidential office responds to rational concerns with defensive wordplay—reminiscent of the Yoon Seok-yeol administration’s reflex to blame the media whenever things went wrong—it seals itself inside a soundproof bubble.

Why State-Engineered Media Cannot Beat Independent Voices

Ultimately, Minti07 was born out of the Democratic Party's desire to break free from the gravitational pull of independent commentators like Kim Ou-joon. The party wanted to become the definitive "textbook," relegating external YouTubers to mere "study guides."

However, an official, bureaucratically engineered broadcast cannot manufacture the raw, unfiltered entertainment and narrative drive of independent influencers with millions of subscribers. When a show's conclusion is pre-programmed to always be "the leadership is right and critics must exercise restraint," intelligent viewers change the channel. Political broadcasts die the moment they turn into mandatory study hall sessions.

True communication requires the courage to accept criticism and expose one's flaws, not the hubris to police speech. If the ruling party continues to smash the warning sensors instead of fixing the engine, the public backlash in upcoming elections will make a producer's resignation look like minor turbulence.
