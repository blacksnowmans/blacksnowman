The Way Out: How One Man's Search for Inner Stillness Transformed Financial Despair

September 15, 2026

Picture sitting at a desk with your bank account sitting at zero, bills piling up that you cannot pay, and the creeping dread of how you will feed your family tomorrow. For many people across South Korea, this is not a hypothetical exercise. It is the grim reality of an economy where household debt is crushing and career paths can vanish overnight. When people hit this kind of wall, the standard advice is always the same: work harder, hustle more, solve the problem. But what happens when the very effort you put into solving your problems is what is driving you deeper into despair?

This is the central question posed by Joseph Banner in his book The Way Out (웨이아웃), a work that details his descent into and emergence from severe financial ruin in Seoul back in 2006. At thirty years old, Banner had just abandoned South Korea’s grueling judicial examinations—the notoriously difficult legal tests that once offered a guaranteed ticket to elite status—while working mandatory public service duty and unexpectedly starting a family. With no income, mounting bills, and a deep sense of shame that forced him to hide past-due notices in deep desk drawers so his wife wouldn't see them, he was staring into an abyss. His story offers a stark window into a broader societal wound: South Korea's soaring suicide rates, particularly among thirty- and fortysomethings facing job loss, crushing debt, and institutional failure.

The Trap of Disguised Negative Thinking

When you are trapped in a room with no exits, the brain goes into overdrive. In Banner's case, every waking moment in 2006 was consumed by a frantic calculation of survival. He wasn't just sitting idle; he was thinking constantly. He was planning, plotting, and agonizing over potential business ideas, failed career choices, and ways to scrape together cash. To the outside world, this looks like being responsible. It looks like trying to fix your life.

However, Banner argues that this type of intense rumination is actually a dangerous psychological trap: "disguised negative thinking." When we ask ourselves questions like "Why did I make such a terrible decision back then?" or "How am I ever going to survive this?" under the guise of future planning, we are not solving anything. We are merely manufacturing a heavy, toxic atmosphere inside our own minds. Every time we clutch tighter to our worries in an effort to squeeze out a solution, we reinforce the fear and scarcity that generated the problem in the first place.

This is where the illusion of the hustle breaks down. Korean society places an immense cultural premium on relentless grit and sleepless grinding, often treating economic hardship as a personal moral failure. But Banner's experience suggests that treating panic with more panic only tightens the noose. The harder you claw at a quicksand pit, the faster you sink.

Shifting the Gaze from External Chaos to Inner Stillness

Realizing that external solutions were out of reach—the bills were still unpaid, and the bank account was still empty—Banner made a radical pivot. Instead of trying to force reality to change, he decided to turn his attention inward. He sat quietly in his room and chose to observe the storm of thoughts raging inside his head.

This was not about finding an immediate financial windfall. It was an admission of defeat regarding what he could control. He stopped treating every wandering anxious thought as an absolute truth and began to view them simply as passing weather patterns. Human brains, particularly under economic duress, are hardwired to sketch out catastrophic scenarios. By stepping back and watching those scenarios unfold without feeding them energy, he created a tiny wedge of space between himself and his panic.

From that space of quiet observation, he began replacing his automatic self-talk—the habitual spiral of self-loathing and despair—with deliberate, comforting affirmations. Even though his physical circumstances had not shifted by a single won, he began repeating to himself that things were unfolding as they should, that a larger unseen order was taking care of him, and that it was safe to let go of the frantic need to control every variable.

The Power of Physical Relaxation and Letting Go of Control

This mental shift was mirrored in physical practice. Banner began consciously releasing the deep muscular tension locked in his body through deep breathing and deliberate relaxation. When a person is chronically stressed, the body carries the weight of the world like an unbearable physical burden. By consciously softening the body, the grip of mental panic loosens as well.

What followed was not immediate riches, but a profound psychological release. By letting go of the exhausting drive to micromanage his survival, Banner found the mental clarity to pursue something he actually cared about: founding a small publishing house called "Thirty-Three Stairs" to bring metaphysical works to Korean readers. Though the first two weeks yielded zero sales, the desperation was gone. Eventually, word of mouth carried his work onto major bookstore bestseller lists, proving that letting go of frantic control is often the only way to allow new paths to open.

What to Watch Next

  • The Gig Economy and Precarious Labor: Observe how younger generations navigating the post-exam, post-corporate landscape in neighborhoods like Seoul's Daehak-dong are redefining traditional notions of employment and stability.
  • Your Own Daily Practice: Notice the next time you find yourself spinning out over a financial or professional worry, and test the radical act of pausing for ten minutes to simply breathe, step back, and observe the thought without trying to fix it immediately.
