The Silent Crisis: South Korea’s Epidemic of "High-Functioning" Depression

October 10, 2026

On any given weekday morning in Seoul, if you look around a crowded subway car, you will notice a recurring expression: a vacant, mask-like stare. To the casual observer, everyone looks like a functioning member of society. They are dressed for work, clutching their smartphones, and preparing to clock into offices across the city. But behind this veneer of productivity lies a quiet, growing crisis. Many of these people are essentially running on empty, fueling themselves with sheer willpower or medical interventions just to make it through the day. They aren't missing deadlines or failing at their jobs; they are, by all outward metrics, succeeding. Yet, inside, they are hollowed out, their capacity for joy and genuine human connection entirely extinguished.

The Hidden Hollow: When Success Masks Despair

In South Korean psychiatric clinics, a new profile of patient has become increasingly common. These individuals do not fit the stereotypical image of depression—they aren't sequestered in dark rooms or unable to leave their beds. On the contrary, they attend meetings, offer witty remarks to colleagues, and complete their tasks with precision. However, their internal landscape is barren. They often confess to doctors that while they are “working,” they feel nothing. The engine of their life has stalled, even as the gears continue to grind.

This condition, often described as "high-functioning depression," represents a dangerous disconnect between a person’s external social performance and their internal reality. According to the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service, a government agency that oversees medical claims, the number of patients treated for depression in South Korea reached nearly 1 million in 2022. The surge is particularly alarming among those in their 20s and 30s—the so-called MZ generation—where patient numbers have spiked by 80% and 70% respectively over the past five years. This statistic is a stinging indictment of the relentless pressure cooker that is modern South Korean society.

The Trap of Perfectionism and the “FOBO” Phenomenon

The roots of this crisis are structural, not just individual. Research analyzing over 40,000 university students in the U.S., U.K., and Canada revealed a 33% increase in “socially prescribed perfectionism” between 1989 and 2016. This is not a personal desire for excellence, but a pervasive societal message: if you don’t hit these targets, you are finished.

In South Korea, this pressure is amplified by a deep-seated fear of obsolescence, often referred to as FOBO (Fear of Becoming Obsolete). As artificial intelligence reshapes industries and the pace of life accelerates, workers feel like replaceable parts in an unforgiving machine. This existential dread is physically manifesting in the brain. Psychiatrists note that in depressed patients, the neural pathways responsible for dopamine—the chemical that drives anticipation and reward—have stopped firing. The simple joy of looking forward to a weekend or a meal has vanished, replaced by a cycle of negative, repetitive thoughts.

The Cultural Weight of "Hwa-byung" and Structural Violence

South Korea has long recognized a condition known as Hwa-byung, a "fire illness" born of repressed anger and suppressed emotions. Once considered a culture-bound syndrome, it is now being observed in foreign residents living in Korea, proving that the illness is a product of the social environment—an expectation to suffer in silence and swallow one's grievances. Among the younger generation, the prevalence of Hwa-byung symptoms is as high as 36.3%. Young people are caught in a paradox: they lack the energy to continue, yet they feel they cannot afford to stop.

The core of the problem lies in an unreasonable labor environment and an hyper-competitive system, yet patients often blame themselves, thinking, "I am just weak for not being able to endure this." This self-blame is the most tragic illusion created by high-functioning depression.

Current diagnostic tools often fail these patients. Standard assessments like the PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9) rely on observable symptoms like sleep loss or an inability to work. Because high-functioning individuals maintain their routines, they often fly under the radar. Statistics show that nearly 38% of office workers struggling with depression go undiagnosed for an average of 2.7 years before seeking professional help. The Korean Neuropsychiatric Association is now pushing for more nuanced screening that looks for patterns of "automatic self-criticism"—the constant, internal mantra that one is never doing enough.

Moving Forward: Breaking the Cycle of Endurance

There is a dangerous societal belief that one must "break" completely before they have the right to rest. This is a form of structural violence. Just as one would not hesitate to see a dentist for a decaying tooth, seeking help for a decaying mental state should be viewed as a basic health necessity, not a luxury or a sign of failure.

As South Korea navigates its current political and economic climate under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—following the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Seok-youl—the conversation about the "human cost" of national development is becoming unavoidable. We must demand a shift where personal well-being is prioritized over arbitrary KPIs and social expectations.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on evolving workplace mental health mandates from the Ministry of Employment and Labor. Furthermore, look for shifts in the public discourse as the medical community moves toward a "multi-dimensional" diagnosis model that focuses on internal emotional exhaustion rather than just functional output. For the individual, the most radical act of resistance today is acknowledging that you are not okay, and refusing to wear the mask any longer.
