The Ghost of the Prosecutors’ Republic: Why the Hearing for South Korea’s New Investigative Chief Sparked an Uproar

October 14, 2026

Inside the hearing room of the National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee, the atmosphere grew palpable with tension. Lawmakers leaned into their microphones, grilling Kim Ji-yong, the designated inaugural chief of South Korea’s newly established Major Crimes Investigation Agency. Under relentless questioning from Representative Youn Kun-young of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the nominee offered an evasive response about a critical indictment he had overseen years earlier: he could not quite recall signing off on it, though there was a "high probability" he did. Minutes later, cornered by documentation and sharp rebuke, Kim reversed course and admitted that he had indeed signed the paperwork.

For observers of South Korean politics, the spectacle was both jarring and deeply symbolic. The Major Crimes Investigation Agency—often referred to locally as the Jungsucheong—was conceived as the final institutional blow to dismantle the sprawling, monopolistic powers of the state prosecution service. Under sweeping judicial reforms championed by the progressive camp and continuing under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, direct investigative authority over major corruption and financial crimes is being stripped from prosecutors and handed to this new, independent body under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety. Yet the man chosen to serve as its very first commander is a veteran prosecutor whose past is intimately entangled with some of the most controversial, politically charged decisions of the previous era.

Selective Amnesia on the Approval Line

To grasp why Kim Ji-yong’s evasions triggered such fierce backlash, one must understand the saga of the so-called "violence under color of authority" case against senior prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong. The incident dates back to the volatile "Channel A affair," a high-profile scandal involving alleged collusion between a conservative television reporter and Han Dong-hoon, then a rising star prosecutor and the chief lieutenant of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

During a physical search and seizure of Han’s mobile phone, a scuffle broke out between Jeong, who led the raid, and Han. In response, the prosecution leadership aggressively mobilized to indict Jeong for physical assault under color of official duty. Kim Ji-yong, then serving as deputy chief prosecutor at the Seoul High Prosecutors’ Office, sat squarely on the administrative approval line that authorized the prosecution. When Youn Kun-young confronted him about this pivotal decision, Kim attempted to hide behind bureaucratic ambiguity:

"It is difficult to state definitively, but there is a high probability that I approved it. I am currently checking trial cards with the Ministry of Justice and the prosecution service to confirm the facts."

The explanation prompted incredulity across the chamber. As Youn sharply pointed out, this was not an obscure file from decades ago; it was an explosive confrontation that dominated national headlines and altered political careers. When pressed, Kim yielded, conceding he had approved the charges. More damaging still was the revelation surrounding Myung Jung-sik, then the internal inspection director at the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office. Myung had conducted a thorough review of the evidence and formally recommended against indicting Jeong. Kim and his superiors overrode their own internal safeguard, ramming the indictment through anyway. The case eventually reached the Supreme Court, which fully acquitted Jeong, exposing the charges as a politically motivated overreach designed to shield an elite insider.

When questioned about why he failed to submit any record of dissent when the Supreme Prosecutors' Office later launched an internal probe into the matter, Kim meekly replied that he would have submitted documents had he been asked, but "there was no such request." To his critics, this was a classic display of an elite bureaucrat looking the other way when his institution abused its power.

Double Standards and the Shielding of Allies

The scrutiny of Kim’s record did not end with the Jeong Jin-woong affair. Lawmakers also zeroed in on his role as head of the Criminal Department at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office during the contentious prosecution surrounding Kim Hak-ui, a former vice justice minister implicated in extensive bribery and sexual abuse scandals.

When Kim Hak-ui attempted to flee South Korea on a late-night international flight, officials moved swiftly to impose an emergency overseas travel ban. The prosecution subsequently retaliated by aggressively pursuing criminal charges against the reform-minded officials who had blocked the flight, including former presidential secretary Lee Kwang-cheol and immigration chief Cha Gyu-geun. As Representative Jeong Chun-saeng of the Rebuilding Korea Party highlighted, this indictment also crumbled upon judicial scrutiny, ending in definitive Supreme Court acquittals after five grueling years of legal battles for the defendants.

What caught the committee's attention was the stark asymmetry in how suspects were treated under Kim’s watch. The formal indictment explicitly documented that Yoon Dae-jin—a powerful prosecutorial insider widely known by the moniker "Little Yoon" due to his close ties to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—had called the presidential office to urge that Kim Hak-ui's departure be blocked. Yet while political figures like Lee Kwang-cheol were dragged into criminal court, Yoon Dae-jin was never booked or charged. Kim Ji-yong brushed this off at the hearing by claiming he had concluded no crime was committed by his colleague. It stood as a textbook illustration of selective justice: leniency for members of the prosecutorial fraternity, unyielding severity for external political targets.

The Irony of an Anti-Reform Prosecutor Leading a Reform Agency

The deepest contradiction hanging over the hearing room was structural. South Korea's judicial overhaul is predicated on breaking the prosecutorial monopoly by severing investigation from indictment. Under this model, the prosecution service is to be reduced to an indictment-only office, while independent agencies like the Major Crimes Investigation Agency conduct the legwork.

Kim Ji-yong's own institutional history stands in sharp opposition to this philosophy. In 2020, Kim signed a collective declaration by senior prosecutors protesting disciplinary proceedings and suspension against Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was then Prosecutor General. Kim had also repeatedly voiced vocal resistance against legislation stripping the prosecution of its investigative remit, warning of dangerous "investigative vacuums."

Watching Kim now declare his "full support" for prosecutorial reform in order to secure the agency's top job struck lawmakers as pure opportunism. During the session, Representative Park Duk-hyum of the conservative People Power Party pressed Kim to name the country’s preeminent "political prosecutor." Under duress, Kim conceded that Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol fit the label, and hesitated before acknowledging that Han Dong-hoon did as well.

For an electorate that watched Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency culminate in an unlawful attempt at martial law, followed by his impeachment, removal from power, and subsequent imprisonment, the unresolved legacy of the prosecutorial apparatus remains raw. The hearing underscored an uncomfortable reality: while the political figures at the apex have fallen, the high-ranking officials who executed and rubber-stamped their judicial maneuvers remain entrenched in the upper echelons of public life.

What to Watch Next

The contentious hearing leaves the government and parliament at a delicate crossroads. As the confirmation process moves forward, several concrete developments will determine the trajectory of South Korea's judicial landscape:

  • Parliamentary Confirmation Report: The National Assembly committee must decide whether to adopt a joint confirmation report. With the ruling Democratic Party and allied progressive parties expressing serious reservations about Kim's past conduct, approval is far from assured.
  • Presidential Appointment Decision: If parliament issues an unfavorable report or refuses adoption, President Lee Jae-myung faces a critical political calculus: push through the appointment of a compromised figure to avoid an administrative vacuum, or withdraw the nomination to preserve the moral legitimacy of the agency.
  • Organizational Culture of the MCIA: As the agency prepares to staff its ranks, scrutiny will focus on whether it inherits the old insular culture of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office or genuinely establishes itself as an accountable, citizen-facing investigative body.

A structural overhaul is only as credible as the individuals selected to enact it. If the inaugural head of South Korea’s flagship anti-corruption agency cannot convincingly account for his own past exercises of state power, the long-delayed separation of investigative and prosecutorial authority risks stumbling at its very inception.
