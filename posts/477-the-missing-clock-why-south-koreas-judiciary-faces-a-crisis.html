The Missing Clock: Why South Korea’s Judiciary Faces a Crisis of Credibility

September 30, 2026

Imagine standing at a subway turnstile, tapping your transit card, and knowing that your every movement is part of a digital trail. In modern South Korea, a global hub for technology, this is the norm—from primary school children tagging in at school to part-time convenience store workers logging their hours down to the minute. Accountability is woven into the fabric of daily life. Yet, a startling reality has recently surfaced: inside the very institutions where the nation’s laws are interpreted and enforced, this digital accountability is conspicuously absent.

The South Korean judiciary—the branch of government responsible for upholding the rule of law—has been found to have no systematic, standardized method for recording the working hours of its judges and administrative staff. For a public that is held to strict standards of professional conduct, the revelation that the "highest authority" lacks the most basic management tools feels not only like a bureaucratic failure but like a profound breach of trust.

The Illusion of the ID Card

For years, the public assumed that when a judge or court official tapped their government-issued ID card at the courthouse entrance, they were clocking in. It is a logical assumption in a hyper-connected society. However, recent scrutiny has revealed that these ID tags are functional only as security keys to open doors; they do not transmit or store time-log data.

This is a staggering disparity. While private-sector employees and general civil servants are held to precise schedules, often facing consequences for even minor tardiness, those within the court system appear to operate under a different set of rules. The absence of a fundamental time-tracking system is not merely an administrative oversight; it is an organizational vacuum. In any other professional environment, this would be considered a dereliction of duty. In the judiciary, it creates a "privilege of the invisible," where the caretakers of the law are shielded from the very scrutiny they apply to others.

The Question of Intentional Neglect

Is this a technical limitation or a choice? In a country that prides itself on world-class IT infrastructure, the idea that the court cannot implement a time-tracking system is difficult to swallow. A smartphone app or a simple server-side update could digitize attendance in less than a week. This leads to a persistent, uncomfortable question: has the judiciary intentionally left these systems unbuilt to allow for a flexible, unchecked work environment?

If this lack of infrastructure is a deliberate choice, it suggests a culture of entitlement that borders on institutional arrogance. The judiciary frequently demands that the public adhere to the letter of the law. When those who wield the gavel are exempt from the standard accountability expected of all civil servants, it undermines the perceived fairness of the entire system. It creates a "double standard" that erodes the moral authority of the courts.

The Weight of the National Public Service Act

The legal implications are significant. Under the National Public Service Act, which governs all state employees, Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—the head of the Supreme Court of South Korea—is a public servant subject to the same regulations as any other. The law clearly stipulates that public officials are bound by a duty of diligence, with fixed working hours typically set from 9:00 AM to 6:00 PM, excluding lunch.

When the leader of the judicial branch remains silent on the absence of these basic management protocols, it sends a message that the judiciary views itself as above the law it is meant to protect. If the Supreme Court cannot ensure its own members are fulfilling their basic contractual obligations, it loses the standing to critique the conduct of citizens or other government agencies. This is not just a scheduling error; it is a fundamental challenge to the principle that the law applies to everyone equally.

The Evidence Left Behind

While the court argues that it lacks an "attendance system," it cannot hide from reality. High-ranking officials, including the Chief Justice and Supreme Court Justices, travel in government vehicles driven by dedicated staff. These vehicles move through security gates, triggering automated logs and digital records of arrival and departure.

These entries are, by definition, electronic records. If these records exist, why is there no corresponding attendance data for the officials themselves? If the logs are missing or have been scrubbed, it points toward something far more sinister than administrative laziness: it suggests an active concealment of facts. Technology is, by nature, an honest witness. The data already exists within the building’s security infrastructure; the judiciary simply chooses not to use it to hold itself accountable.

What to Watch Next

The judiciary now stands at a crossroads. The public outcry over this "missing time" is not going to vanish, and the excuse of "no system in place" has reached its expiration date. To restore public confidence, the following steps are essential:

  • Transparency Audit: The Supreme Court must address whether vehicle log data has been utilized or ignored in tracking the actual working hours of high-ranking officials.
  • Systemic Reform: The implementation of a mandatory, transparent time-tracking system for all court personnel is no longer optional if the institution wishes to remain credible.
  • Leadership Accountability: Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae must provide a clear, public explanation regarding the long-standing neglect of these administrative standards.

Ultimately, the judiciary must decide whether it wants to be an institution that stands for equality or one that hides behind the veil of a "special" status. In an era where every citizen is measured, recorded, and held to account, the courts cannot afford to be the last place where the truth is obscured by a lack of basic documentation. If they want to regain the trust of the people, they must start by shining a light on their own front doors.
