The Jeonse Trap: Why South Korea’s Rental Crisis is Set to Explode in 2025

November 13, 2026

Imagine you are a 34-year-old office worker in Seoul, finally reaching the end of a two-year lease on your apartment. For months, you have watched the headlines, hoping for a "crash" that would finally bring home prices into a range you could afford with a mortgage. Instead, you find yourself in a bizarre economic purgatory. The price to buy the apartment has barely moved, but the cost to stay in it as a renter has suddenly spiked. Your landlord informs you that to renew your lease, you need to come up with an additional $36,000 (50 million KRW) in cash—immediately.

This is the "Jeonse Trap," and according to the latest data, it is about to get much worse. The Construction Economy Research Institute of Korea (CERIK), a leading private-sector think tank that monitors the nation’s building and property sectors, released a sobering outlook for the 2025 housing market during its recent seminar on November 12. Their conclusion? While the market for buying homes is cooling into a stagnant "wait-and-see" phase, the rental market is catching fire.

CERIK predicts that nationwide home sales prices will rise by a modest 2.0% next year. In contrast, the price of Jeonse—South Korea’s unique rental system where tenants provide a massive, lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent—is expected to jump by 4.5%. For the millions of Koreans who do not own their homes, this represents a "housing cost bomb" that threatens to wipe out household savings and stall social mobility.

The Decoupling of Sales and Rentals

To understand why this matters, one must first understand the weight of the 2.0% sales growth figure. In a healthy market, a 2.0% rise might be seen as a "soft landing." However, in the context of South Korea’s hyper-competitive real estate landscape, it signals a "transaction cliff." Buyers are not staying away because they no longer want to buy; they are staying away because they are being squeezed out by the Lee Jae-myung administration’s aggressive tightening of mortgage regulations and the persistence of high interest rates.

The Lee government, led by the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, has maintained a strict stance on household debt, viewing the massive mortgage bubbles of the previous era—specifically the "Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol" years—as a systemic risk to the economy. By lowering loan-to-value (LTV) ratios and tightening debt-service limits, the government has effectively raised the ladder of homeownership out of reach for the average worker. When people cannot buy, they must rent. This massive pivot of demand from the sales market to the rental market is what is driving the predicted 4.5% spike in Jeonse prices.

The 4.5% figure is more than double the growth rate of home sales. In practical terms, if a Jeonse deposit for a standard apartment in a decent Seoul neighborhood is roughly $360,000 (about 500 million KRW), a tenant will need to find an extra $16,200 (about 22.5 million KRW) just to keep their current roof over their head. In an economy where wage growth is largely stagnant, this is, as CERIK analysts put it, a "death sentence" for the financial stability of the urban middle class.

The Supply Drought and the Construction Crisis

The rental crisis is not just a result of high demand; it is being exacerbated by a catastrophic failure in supply. Jeonse prices typically stabilize when new apartment complexes are completed, as a wave of new units hits the market simultaneously. However, the pipeline is drying up. CERIK’s seminar highlighted that the construction industry is struggling to recover from a perfect storm of rising material costs and high financing rates.

While the institute forecasts that construction orders might see a 4.1% increase by 2027, this is a deceptive recovery. When adjusted for the skyrocketing costs of cement, rebar, and labor, the "real" volume of construction is actually shrinking. Many developers are sitting on land they purchased years ago, unable to start construction because the math no longer works. They cannot build apartments at a price that consumers can afford while still covering their own inflated costs.

"The slow recovery of construction orders is a time bomb for the housing market. It means that three to four years from now, the shortage of new move-ins will be even more acute than it is today," the report notes.

This supply drought is particularly severe in the Seoul Metropolitan Area, which includes the capital and its surrounding satellite cities. CERIK predicts that while the national average for home price increases is 2.0%, the Seoul area will likely see a 3.0% rise. This deepening regional polarization means that while provincial cities may see their property markets rot, the capital remains an impenetrable fortress of high costs, further alienating young professionals who are forced to move further and further away from their jobs.

The Shadow of Policy Uncertainty

Beyond the raw numbers of supply and demand, the 2025 market is being haunted by political and tax-related uncertainty. The Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party-controlled National Assembly are currently debating significant shifts in real estate taxation. These include potential changes to the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (a wealth tax on high-value property) and adjustments to capital gains taxes for multiple-home owners.

For a potential buyer, this creates a paralysis of "watchful waiting." If the government decides to ease taxes on sellers, a flood of second-hand homes might hit the market, lowering prices. If they tighten them further, the supply might vanish as owners "dig in" to wait for a friendlier political climate. This uncertainty acts as a cooling agent on the sales market but, ironically, heats up the rental market. People who are unsure about the future of taxes are choosing to rent for "one more term," adding even more fuel to the Jeonse fire.

The CERIK report also points to the "balloon effect" of government regulation. When the government restricts mortgages to prevent people from "Yeong-kkeul" (a popular Korean term meaning "pulling every last bit of soul" to buy a home), those people don't disappear. They simply move into the Jeonse market, driving up prices for those even poorer than themselves. The ladder of housing stability is not just being raised; it is being dismantled from the bottom up.

What to Watch Next

As we move into 2025, the South Korean property market will be defined by this agonizing tension between a frozen sales market and a boiling rental market. For foreign investors and observers, this situation offers a masterclass in how well-intentioned debt regulation can create unintended social crises in a market with unique structural quirks like Jeonse.

To understand the trajectory of the next twelve months, keep a close eye on these three indicators:

  • Bank of Korea Interest Rate Decisions: If the central bank begins to cut rates to stimulate the sluggish construction sector, it may provide relief to developers, but it could also trigger a new wave of borrowing that pushes Jeonse prices even higher as liquidity enters the market.
  • Apartment Move-in Statistics: Watch the quarterly reports on new apartment completions, especially in Gyeonggi Province and Incheon. Any further delays in these projects will be an immediate signal that rental prices in Seoul will spike.
  • The 2025 Tax Reform Bill: The National Assembly’s decisions on property taxes will determine whether multiple-home owners decide to sell their properties or continue to pass their tax burdens onto tenants in the form of higher rental deposits.

The message from the Construction Economy Research Institute is clear: 2025 will not be a year of recovery for the average Korean household. It will be a year of endurance. Whether the government can find a way to increase supply without reigniting a speculative bubble remains the most critical question facing the Lee Jae-myung administration today.
