The Stakes Behind the Push to Impeach South Korea's Chief Justice

October 19, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, the midpoint of South Korea's annual parliamentary audit rarely brings calm, but this autumn the temperature has reached a boiling point. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), having consolidated power under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, is now weighing an institutional weapon rarely brought against the pinnacle of the judiciary: the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. In committee rooms where lawmakers are ostensibly meant to review state administration, dry balance sheets have given way to constitutional brinkmanship, accusations of judicial bias, and fierce tactical skirmishes between the governing majority and the conservative opposition.

For international observers, South Korea's political landscape can appear in a perpetual state of hyper-partisanship. Yet the current friction goes beyond normal legislative posturing. It reflects a deeper, unresolved battle over institutional legitimacy in the wake of the political upheaval that saw former prosecutor and head of state, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, removed from office via impeachment and subsequently imprisoned. As the DPK seeks to cement its governance and advance sweeping judicial reforms, its offensive against the courts and its clashes during the ongoing audits reveal both the extent of its power and the acute vulnerabilities that threaten to distract from its policy agenda.

Confronting the Bench: The Impeachment Drive Against Chief Justice Cho

The push to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae moved from internal whispers to active floor strategy as senior Democratic Party voices, backed by minor progressive allies, framed the bench as an unelected redoubt of the old order. Progressive lawmakers argue that the courts have systematically undermined the constitutional separation of powers through biased rulings, necessitating legislative intervention. Under Article 65 of the South Korean Constitution, the National Assembly possesses the authority to impeach top officials, including judges, when they violate the supreme law in the course of their duties.

Pressure is not only building from within the ruling party's own hardline wing. Cho Kuk, leader of the progressive minor opposition Rebuilding Korea Party, has publicly demanded that the ruling coalition act decisively, calling for an immediate date for an impeachment vote on the Assembly floor. The message from the progressive base is clear: judicial reform cannot simply mean structural tinkering around the margins. To those holding this view, removing the Chief Justice is an indispensable safeguard to restore legal fairness after years of prosecutorial dominance under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure.

The conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), has mobilized for total resistance. Senior figures such as Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik and lawmaker Joo Jin-woo have fiercely condemned the impeachment maneuver, accusing the ruling party of taking the constitutional order hostage to shore up its partisan approval ratings. From the conservative viewpoint, attempting to unseat the head of the Supreme Court is an egregious assault on judicial independence and a brazen bid to subordinate the courts to legislative majorities. By framing the audit as an "impeachment roadshow," the PPP hopes to paint the DPK as an overreaching sovereign drunk on legislative power.

The Annual Audit Crossroads: Sovereignty, Defense, and Lingering Dynastic Scandals

These judicial tensions have completely overshadowed the broader National Assembly audit, the yearly month-long marathon in which lawmakers scrutinize state agencies, state-run corporations, and municipal bodies. Halfway through the proceedings, the hearing rooms have turned into ideological staging grounds, nowhere more clearly than in defense and foreign policy discussions.

A central flashpoint has been the long-deferred transition of wartime operational control (OPCON). Under current arrangements dating back to the Korean War, the United States military would assume operational command of South Korean forces if total war erupted on the peninsula. The ruling Democratic Party has stepped up its calls for a swift reclamation of wartime command, framing it as an essential benchmark of modern national sovereignty and independent defense capability. DPK lawmakers sharply criticized the PPP's cautious stance, warning that uncritical deference to Washington should not be confused with prudent alliance diplomacy. The PPP, by contrast, continues to argue that premature handover risks eroding deterrence against North Korea.

At the same time, the ruling party is refusing to drop legal controversies surrounding key figures from the previous era. DPK committee members have officially demanded renewed investigative scrutiny into allegations involving the daughter of prominent conservative lawmaker Han Dong-hoon. The dispute centers on whether academic and extracurricular commendations were improperly obtained or inflated through parental influence to aid university admissions. By demanding that prosecutors and inspectors verify whether these certificates were utilized in college entry, the ruling party is seeking to prove that the "fairness and common sense" slogans championed by the previous conservative administration masked pervasive institutional double standards.

An Unforced Error on Stage and the Battle for the Southeast

Yet for all the ruling party's assertive policy posture, the perils of everyday politics were on full display when party leader Kim Min-seok visited North Jeolla Province. While attending the Sunchang Fermented Sauce Festival, an unexpected protocol disaster unfolded. As popular singer HYNN (Park Hye-won) was performing her concert set, the music was abruptly cut so that Kim could take the stage and address the crowd. The reaction was swift and devastating: social media erupted in outrage, and the PPP seized on the moment to brand the ruling leadership as arrogant elites engaging in classic strong-arm protocol (gapjil) at the expense of ordinary citizens' cultural enjoyment.

Kim moved quickly to contain the fallout, offering an unequivocal public apology. He explained that although he had initially declined to speak, he walked up after the event emcee repeatedly announced his presence, adding that he felt deeply apologetic toward the artist and the audience. While the prompt apology mitigated a prolonged crisis, the incident underscored an enduring truth in Korean politics: grand ideological crusades can be instantly derailed if the public perceives politicians as dismissive of ordinary people's dignity.

The protocol misstep comes amid a delicate political balancing act for the DPK. The leadership is currently attempting an aggressive electoral push into the "Nakdong River Belt" in Busan and South Gyeongsang Province, the historic stronghold of South Korean conservatism. Historically, breaking the regionalist divide between the progressive southwest (Honam) and conservative southeast (Yeongnam) has been the defining ambition of Democratic icons, most notably the late President Roh Moo-hyun.

Speaking to local district heads in Busan, Kim declared that the central party leadership would oversee the metropolitan region directly, pledging transformational public investments. These include state backing for opening new Arctic maritime shipping routes and building an all-weather domed stadium at Busan's North Port redevelopment zone. Tied to this regional outreach is a broader national decentralization platform: the party has pledged to accelerate the second wave of relocating public state enterprises out of the congested Seoul capital region and into regional hubs, alongside educational modernization schemes designed to elevate provincial national universities to the caliber of top Seoul institutions.

The Human Ledger Behind the Partisan Noise

While partisan fireworks dominate prime-time broadcasts, the audit has also surfaced crucial realities regarding state welfare spending, public sector labor, and civic safety. Even during days consumed by impeachment rhetoric, party leaders paused to pay respects at the memorial of fire lieutenant Kim Jin-seok, who tragically died in the line of duty during an industrial blaze in Bucheon. The incident, punctuated by a heartbreaking public letter from the late firefighter's daughter, prompted rare bipartisan agreement to overhaul hazardous-duty compensation, staff shortages, and field equipment.

Elsewhere in the committee rooms, lawmakers deployed hard data to challenge administrative priorities across local and state entities:

  • Urban Healthcare Neglect: Lawmakers revealed that the Seoul Metropolitan Government, under conservative Mayor Oh Se-hoon, failed to allocate roughly 20.1 billion won (approximately $15 million) in vital healthcare and pharmacy subsidies for impoverished residents, even after collecting an unexpected 1.7 trillion won (about $1.25 billion) property acquisition tax windfall.
  • Outsourced Industrial Casualties: Audit records from state utility KEPCO showed that 10 out of the 12 workplace fatalities recorded in recent operations were subcontracted laborers, reigniting demands for stringent occupational safety oversight.
  • Infrastructure and Institutional Ethics: Disclosures highlighted persistent infrastructure disparities, such as 439 primary, middle, and high schools nationwide still lacking indoor gymnasiums, alongside ethical audits uncovering kickback schemes within state arts agencies.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly heads toward the conclusion of the audit season, the political landscape will be defined by three critical developments:

  • A Floor Vote on the Chief Justice: Watch whether the Democratic Party formalizes a joint impeachment resolution with the Rebuilding Korea Party. If submitted, the measure requires only a simple legislative majority to pass the Assembly, which would instantly suspend Chief Justice Cho from his official duties pending a trial at the Constitutional Court.
  • The OPCON and Defense Clashes: As bilateral defense dialogues and budget negotiations approach, observe how aggressively the DPK leverages its audit findings to mandate firmer timelines from the defense ministry regarding the wartime operational command handover.
  • Local Backlash and the Southeastern Campaign: Track public polling in Busan and the broader Yeongnam corridor. Whether the DPK's promise of large-scale infrastructure investments can overcome the lingering fallout from protocol blunders and intra-party turbulence will determine the viability of its nationwide electoral roadmap.
