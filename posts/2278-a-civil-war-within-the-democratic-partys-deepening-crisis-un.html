A Civil War Within: The Democratic Party’s Deepening Crisis Under President Lee Jae-myung

November 13, 2026

On the morning of November 12, the air inside the headquarters of the Democratic Party of South Korea—the nation’s ruling party—was thick with a tension that has been simmering for months. What began as a disagreement over party discipline has metastasized into an all-out public brawl between two of the party’s most influential heavyweights: Kim Min-seok and Jung Chung-rae. To an outside observer, it might look like a simple ego clash, but for those following the internal dynamics of the Lee Jae-myung administration, it represents a fundamental fracture in the coalition that currently governs South Korea.

At the heart of the dispute is a toxic cocktail of allegations involving a fringe religious group, accusations of "targeted purges" using party mechanisms, and a breakdown in the very communication channels meant to keep the ruling party stable. As President Lee Jae-myung attempts to drive his legislative agenda forward, his own party is increasingly distracted by a "civil war" that threatens to alienate its core supporters and stall the machinery of government.

The Truce That Wasn't: Media Maneuvers and Mutual Distrust

The conflict reached a boiling point on November 12 when Kim Min-seok, a senior lawmaker and key figure within the Democratic Party (DP) leadership, took to the airwaves to offer what looked like an olive branch. Kim suggested that the ongoing friction between himself and Jung Chung-rae—another powerful senior lawmaker known for his staunch support from the party’s grassroots—could be resolved through direct dialogue. "I am ready to meet and talk this through," Kim told reporters, framing himself as the reasonable party seeking unity.

The response from Jung Chung-rae was swift and cutting. By 10:49 AM that same day, Jung dismissed the offer as a disingenuous "media play." He characterized Kim’s public overture as "selling one’s own brand" (jagi jang-sa) rather than a sincere attempt at reconciliation. Jung’s grievance was simple: if Kim truly wanted to talk, why did he tell the press before picking up the phone? To Jung and his supporters, Kim was not seeking peace; he was seeking a PR victory by making Jung look like the obstacle to party unity.

This exchange highlights a deep-seated rot in the party’s internal culture. In the hierarchical and often consensus-driven world of South Korean politics, taking a private dispute public is often seen as an act of aggression. By late that night, around 11:42 PM, Kim reiterated his call for dialogue, but Jung’s camp remained icy. Jung demanded a "sincere apology to the party members" before any talks could begin. The two men are now operating on parallel tracks, with no common ground in sight. For the Lee Jae-myung government, having two pillars of the party at each other's throats is a recipe for legislative paralysis.

The Ghost of Shincheonji and the Pride of the Party Base

To understand why this feud is so visceral, one must look at the "Shincheonji" allegations. For those outside Korea, Shincheonji is a fringe religious organization that gained international notoriety during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. In the Korean political context, it is a "third rail" issue. Allegations of links to Shincheonji are frequently used to discredit politicians, suggesting they are controlled by, or have made deals with, a secretive and controversial group.

The current infighting is fueled by an explosive rumor known as the "Shincheonji Three-Party Secret Alliance." This theory alleges that certain elements within the Democratic Party have engaged in a clandestine partnership with the group to influence party affairs. While the party leadership under Kim Min-seok has attempted to brush these rumors aside with a "self-apology" (a term critics use to describe an apology issued by the accused to themselves to close a case), the wound remains open.

For the DP’s rank-and-file members, who take immense pride in the party’s identity as a progressive, democratic force, the mere suggestion of a Shincheonji connection is an insult. Jung Chung-rae has tapped into this anger, arguing that the leadership’s attempt to move past the scandal without a thorough, transparent investigation is a betrayal of the party’s values. He isn't just fighting Kim Min-seok; he is positioning himself as the defender of the "party’s soul" against a leadership he portrays as opaque and dismissive of the grassroots.

The Weaponization of the "Party Member Maintenance" Task Force

As the rhetoric has heated up, the conflict has shifted into the realm of party bureaucracy. The Democratic Party recently established a "Task Force for Party Member Maintenance" (Dangwon Jeongbi TF). On paper, its goal is to clean up membership rolls and ensure party discipline. However, Jung Chung-rae’s allies see it as something far more sinister: a weapon being used to conduct "targeted investigations" against political rivals.

On the afternoon of November 12, Park Kyu-hwan, a former member of the party’s Supreme Council (the DP’s highest decision-making body) and a close associate of Jung, officially requested an internal inspection of the Task Force itself. Park alleged that the TF is being used to hunt for dirt on Jung and his supporters to push them out of the party’s inner circle. "The Task Force is carrying out an unreasonable, targeted investigation," Park claimed, calling on the party leadership to intervene.

This is a dangerous development for any ruling party. When internal disciplinary bodies are accused of being factional hit squads, the legitimacy of the entire party structure is called into question. If the Task Force is indeed being used to purge the "pro-Jung" faction, it mirrors the very tactics the Democratic Party criticized during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence. The irony is not lost on the DP’s critics, who argue that the party is beginning to replicate the "prosecutorial" style of governance it once vowed to destroy.

A Crisis of Leadership: From National Policy to Local Scandals

The fallout from this senior-level feud is starting to trickle down, affecting the party’s discipline at all levels. While Kim and Jung trade barbs in Seoul, local party officials are embroiled in their own controversies. In the city of Geoje, a local council member named Kim Kyung-seup—a member of the Democratic Party—is currently facing a disciplinary motion for "parking abuse" (gapjil). Witnesses allege he used his position to bully others over a parking dispute, an act that has sparked outrage in the local community.

In normal times, a local scandal like this might be handled swiftly by the provincial party branch. But with the national leadership distracted by their own "civil war," there is a sense that the inmates are running the asylum. Heavyweights like Park Ji-won, a veteran politician and former intelligence chief, have tried to intervene, telling the feuding seniors to "keep moving forward" and stop the public bickering. "Do you want to keep fighting forever?" Park asked rhetorically on the morning of November 12.

The problem for President Lee Jae-myung is that this internal noise drowns out his administration’s achievements. Whether it is debates over the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax or navigating complex international relations, the DP needs a unified front. Instead, they are providing a daily soap opera for the media. The "pro-Jung" camp is reportedly waiting for Kim Min-seok’s 100th day in his current leadership role to launch a fresh offensive, suggesting that this conflict is nowhere near its end.

The Core of the Conflict: The Democratic Party is currently caught between a leadership that wants to "move on" from damaging scandals through top-down discipline and a grassroots-backed faction that demands a "purge of the purgers." As long as the "Shincheonji" allegations remain unaddressed to the satisfaction of the party base, any attempt at a "truce" will be seen as a cover-up.

What to Watch Next

The stability of the Lee Jae-myung administration depends on how these internal fires are managed. In the coming weeks, keep an eye on three specific indicators:

  • The Inspection of the Task Force: If the party leadership grants Park Kyu-hwan’s request to inspect the "Party Member Maintenance TF," it may signal a retreat by Kim Min-seok. If they refuse, expect the "pro-Jung" faction to escalate their protests, potentially leading to a formal split or a series of resignations.
  • The "100-Day" Milestone: Kim Min-seok’s 100th day in his current post will be a critical juncture. Watch for whether Jung Chung-rae uses this date to release more specific evidence or allegations regarding the "Shincheonji" link.
  • Public Opinion and Presidential Approval: If the DP’s internal polls show a significant drop in support among the core "progressive" base, President Lee Jae-myung may be forced to step in directly to mediate—a move that carries high risk, as it could tie his own personal reputation to the outcome of the feud.

For now, the Democratic Party remains a house divided. While they hold the reins of power in the Blue House and the National Assembly, their greatest enemy is not the opposition People Power Party, but the growing distrust within their own ranks. As the sun set on November 12, the only thing both sides could agree on was that the battle is just beginning.
