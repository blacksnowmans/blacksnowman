Cult Allegations and Factional Wars: The Democratic Party’s Internal Crisis

October 26, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, the atmosphere surrounding the leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—South Korea's current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung—has turned from strategic confidence to internal siege. What began as a whisper about a controversial religious organization has exploded into a full-scale factional war that threatens to stall the legislative momentum of the government. At the center of the storm is Shincheonji, a secretive religious group often described as a cult by its detractors, and an internal investigation into whether its members have systematically infiltrated the ruling party’s ranks.

For a foreign observer, the intensity of this conflict might seem baffling. In many democracies, religious groups participating in politics is a matter of lobbying or voting blocs. In South Korea, however, the name "Shincheonji" carries a unique political and social toxicity. The group gained international notoriety in 2020 as the epicenter of South Korea’s first major COVID-19 outbreak, but its history of alleged political meddling goes back much further. Now, the DPK finds itself in a "civil war" over how to handle an internal report on the group’s influence within the party. The question—to publish the findings or to keep them buried—has become a proxy battle for power among the party’s top brass.

The Ghost of Shincheonji in Korean Politics

To understand why this internal rift matters, one must understand the weight of the "Shincheonji" label. Founded in the 1980s, the group is viewed with extreme suspicion by mainstream Korean society due to its secretive recruitment tactics and its alleged practice of directing members to join political parties to gain influence. In previous years, the conservative People Power Party (PPP)—currently the main opposition—was frequently dogged by rumors of ties to the group, especially during the 2022 presidential primary. Now, the tables have turned, and the ruling DPK is the one under the microscope.

The current crisis was sparked by allegations that a significant number of Shincheonji followers had registered as DPK party members. In South Korea’s political system, "party members" (dang-won) are crucial because they vote in primaries to select candidates for office and leadership positions. If a secretive organization can mobilize thousands of disciplined voters to join a party, they could theoretically influence who represents the party in national elections.

The DPK leadership recently conducted an internal probe into these claims. However, the controversy truly began when reports surfaced that the party leadership was planning to keep the results of this investigation secret. The mere suggestion of a cover-up sent shockwaves through the party. Choi Min-hee, a prominent DPK lawmaker, was forced to go on the record to state that no official decision had been made to hide the report, but the damage was done. The suspicion that the leadership might be protecting certain individuals—or simply trying to avoid a public relations disaster—has fueled a narrative of "secrecy over transparency."

A Public Explosions of Factional Tensions

The conflict reached a boiling point when Kim Min-seok, a member of the DPK’s Supreme Council (the party’s highest decision-making body), made what many are calling a "bombshell" claim. Kim suggested that there were indeed organized efforts by Shincheonji to influence the party. However, his failure to immediately provide hard evidence to back up these claims transformed him from a whistleblower into a target for his own colleagues.

The pushback was led by Han Min-soo and Jung Chung-rae, both influential figures within the party’s "pro-transparency" or "pro-Cheong" faction (referring to supporters of the party's ideological core). Han Min-soo publicly slammed Kim Min-seok, characterizing his comments as "public intimidation" that created unnecessary speculation without providing facts. Han’s demand was simple: if Kim has proof of a "Shincheonji alliance" within the party, he must present it immediately or face the consequences of destabilizing the ruling party during a critical legislative session.

Jung Chung-rae, known for his direct and often provocative rhetoric, upped the ante with a statement that has since gone viral among the party’s base: "The one who hides the truth is the culprit." This phrase, while simple, carries immense weight in the context of Korean political scandals. By framing the non-disclosure of the report as an admission of guilt, Jung has cornered the party leadership. He argues that by hesitating to release the full investigation, the DPK is essentially admitting it has something to hide, thereby handing a massive political weapon to the opposition.

The Opposition Watches from the Sidelines

The People Power Party (PPP), led by figures like Kim Jae-seop, has watched this "circular firing squad" with a mix of amusement and sharp criticism. Kim Jae-seop recently lambasted the DPK, stating that the ruling party is still dominated by "old-school politicians" (gutae-jeongchi) who prefer backroom deals and factional warfare over governance. He argued that the only way for the DPK to reform is to purge these elements.

For the PPP, this is a moment of clear political gain. The opposition is still reeling from the fallout of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment and subsequent imprisonment, a period marked by what many saw as the height of political incompetence. By highlighting the DPK's current internal chaos, the PPP is attempting to paint the ruling party as equally dysfunctional. They are framing the "Shincheonji" crisis not as a religious issue, but as a symptom of a party that is "its own worst enemy."

The irony is not lost on political analysts. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, the DPK was the one criticizing the government for a lack of transparency and internal power struggles. Now, as the party in power, the DPK is finding that the burden of transparency is much heavier when the secrets being revealed are their own. The opposition is leveraging this to stall the DPK’s legislative agenda, arguing that a party that cannot even manage its own membership list is unfit to lead major national reforms.

What is at Stake for the Lee Jae-myung Administration?

This is not just a petty argument over a religious group; it is a battle for the soul and the efficacy of the Lee Jae-myung government. President Lee currently holds a significant mandate to pursue sweeping judicial and economic reforms. However, a president is only as strong as his party’s unity. If the DPK is seen as being infiltrated by a controversial cult, or worse, seen as covering up such infiltration, its moral authority to lead these reforms evaporates.

The "Shincheonji" issue acts as a "black hole" for political discourse. For every day the DPK leadership spends arguing over the investigation report, a day is lost on discussing the economy, the housing market, or relations with the North. On October 25 alone, over 20 major news headlines were dedicated solely to the spat between Han Min-soo, Kim Min-seok, and Jung Chung-rae. For a government trying to maintain high approval ratings, this is a disastrous allocation of political capital.

Furthermore, the conflict highlights a growing divide within the DPK leadership between those who prioritize "party stability" (often meaning keeping scandals quiet) and those who prioritize "ideological purity" and transparency. If the "transparency" faction wins, the resulting report could lead to a massive purge of members, which would be chaotic in the short term. If the "stability" faction wins and keeps the report secret, the party faces a long-term drip-feed of leaks and accusations that could haunt them until the next election.

What to Watch Next

As the DPK grapples with this crisis, there are several concrete developments that will signal which way the wind is blowing:

  • The "Full Disclosure" Decision: Watch for an official announcement from the DPK Supreme Council. If they release the full, unredacted findings of the Shincheonji investigation, it will signal a victory for the "pro-transparency" faction and likely lead to a period of internal house-cleaning.
  • Kim Min-seok’s Evidence: Kim is currently under immense pressure to produce the "bombshell" evidence he alluded to. If he fails to do so, his political career within the leadership may be effectively over, as he will be blamed for inciting a "self-destructive" conflict.
  • Legislative Deadlock: Observe the National Assembly’s committee meetings. If the opposition PPP successfully uses this scandal to delay key government bills, it will be a sign that the DPK’s internal rift is having real-world consequences for the Lee Jae-myung administration’s policy goals.
  • Base Reaction: The DPK’s core supporters are highly active online. Their reaction to Jung Chung-rae’s "the one who hides is the culprit" mantra will determine how much pressure the leadership feels to be open. If the base turns against the leadership, a major reshuffle may be inevitable.

The Democratic Party of Korea finds itself at a crossroads. To maintain its position as a reformist ruling party, it must prove that it is not susceptible to the very "old-school" tactics it once criticized. Whether they can purge the influence of secretive groups like Shincheonji while maintaining internal unity remains the biggest test of their leadership to date.
