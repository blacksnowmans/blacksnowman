The War for Progressive Media: Why President Lee Jae-myung Is Breaking With Kingmaker Kim Ou-joon

November 06, 2026

When the head of state in one of the world's most hyper-connected democracies boots up a direct YouTube broadcast to announce that he reads raw insults and online venom because he is "not trapped in algorithms," it sounds like modern political bravado. In South Korea, however, this remark was not just casual banter aimed at digital natives. It was a calculated detonation inside the progressive movement.

By declaring that he operates outside the algorithmic bubble, President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea deliberately dismantled a protective narrative woven around him by Kim Ou-joon, the indisputable heavyweight of South Korean progressive podcasting and alternative media. For months, Kim had shielded the president against simmering internal discontent by claiming that Lee was isolated by biased algorithmic feeds and gatekeeping aides. When the president publicly kicked that shield aside, he sent an unmistakable signal: the center of progressive narrative power no longer resides in the studios of independent media influencers. It has moved entirely to the presidential office.

Breaking the Protector’s Shield: Lee Jae-myung vs. Kim Ou-joon

To understand the earthquake this remark has triggered, one must grasp the unique role Kim Ou-joon has played in South Korean politics over the past decade. Long before traditional television and newspapers lost their monopoly, Kim built an empire of irreverent political broadcasting, transforming from an underground satirist into a progressive kingmaker whose backing could make or break legislative careers. During the tumultuous opposition years—when Lee Jae-myung faced relentless investigations under the administration of Yoon Suk-yeol—these alternative media channels served as a vital armor, rallying loyalist factions and framing every prosecutorial probe as a partisan witch hunt.

Now, following the political upheaval that saw Yoon Suk-yeol impeached, ousted from office, and subsequently imprisoned, the Democratic Party holds executive power. With Lee installed as president, the dynamic between the state apparatus and its former media defenders has radically shifted. Kim Ou-joon had attempted to maintain his role as the administration's chief interpreter and protector, offering the "algorithm trap" excuse whenever presidential decisions drew fire from the base.

Lee’s direct rebuttal dismantled that dynamic in an instant. By asserting that he reads the bitterest critiques himself and navigates information freely, the president made two clear statements:

  • He no longer requires Kim's protection: A sitting president with the full weight of the state behind him will not be framed as an insulated captive needing an internet broadcaster's defense.
  • He is reclaiming narrative dominance: The administration is taking direct ownership of its communications, refusing to allow external commentators to dictate the boundaries of its political reality.

Yet critics suggest this display of self-sufficiency conceals a perilous political hubris. Claiming to digest raw internet vitriol and unvarnished comments is often an illusion of openness. Inside presidential palaces, unfiltered public sentiment is rarely what lands on executive screens. Dismissing structured vetting systems in favor of browsing spicy social media threads risks confusing abrasive fringe commentary with genuine popular sentiment—a tendency some domestic observers term a "judge complex," wherein the leader believes only he has the wisdom to sift truth from noise.

The Hardline Insurgency: Lee Dong-hyung and the Fight for Progressive Eyeballs

The fracture between the presidential office and Kim Ou-joon has not occurred in a vacuum. It has exposed an increasingly vicious civil war within the pro-government new media ecosystem. As the traditional dominance of Kim’s broadcast network has waned, a faction of hyper-loyalist, hardline pro-Lee streamers—most notably channels anchored by host Lee Dong-hyung and operations like the "New Lee Jae-myung" channel—has moved aggressively to claim supremacy.

For months, these emerging pro-Lee channels have harbored deep resentment toward Kim Ou-joon. Their grievance stems from the perception that Kim’s commentary has become too guarded, offering bland, ritualistic defenses rather than passionately driving the administration’s core agenda. When President Lee publicly dismissed Kim’s "algorithm shield," he effectively handed these rival creators the ideological authorization they had been waiting for.

This factional conflict is also driven by significant financial incentives. South Korea’s partisan YouTube universe is backed by a lucrative commercial engine:

  • Digital storefronts and direct brand sponsors: High-traffic progressive streams routinely run profitable commerce tie-ins with affiliated brands such as E-Plex, Deep & Dutch, Sisa Geongeon, Horang Kitchen, and I'm Good.
  • Monetization pipelines: Fan donations, routed through platforms like PayPal and direct bank transfers via KB Kookmin Bank, represent substantial revenue streams that follow subscriber engagement.

To capture a larger share of this lucrative progressive viewership, rival creators must weaken Kim Ou-joon’s near-monopoly. By drawing the battle lines around loyalty to Lee, hardline streamers have laid an ideological trap. If Kim responds to the president's snub by openly criticizing the administration, these pro-Lee channels stand ready to brand him an "anti-Lee" saboteur or a "watermelon"—the derogatory progressive term for a politician or pundit who is progressive on the outside but conservative within. Caught between offering increasingly irrelevant defenses and risking public excommunication from the loyalist base, Kim finds his strategic room for maneuver severely constrained.

Governing by Livestream: The Pitfalls of Performative Transparency

This struggle over media control is unfolding alongside an unprecedented shift in how the executive branch operates. In an effort to project absolute contrast with the secrecy that defined Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, the Lee administration has leaned heavily into performative transparency, including the decision to broadcast State Council meetings live to the public.

When questioned about the potential chill this puts on candid internal debate, President Lee flippantly responded that the scrutiny is "tolerable for now." But governance analysts point out that what may feel manageable to a media-savvy president can be crippling to the machinery of government. When Cabinet sessions become live television broadcasts, authentic policy conflict is driven underground. Ministers and civil servants, aware of cameras broadcasting their every expression to partisan commentators, naturally retreat into scripted presentations and deferential praise. Constructive dissent is stifled, turning critical policy evaluations into political theater.

A similar tension surrounds the administration's revival of the Special Inspector system. Under Yoon Suk-yeol, the failure to activate this independent monitor became a defining symbol of executive overreach and proximity to corruption. The Lee administration has championed the office's restoration as institutional proof of its integrity. Yet institutional mechanisms alone do not generate trust. Without genuine autonomy and a willingness to investigate the president's own inner circle, institutional symbols risk becoming little more than public-relations props.

From Persecuted Rebel to Sovereign: The Danger of the Victim Frame

The deeper philosophical dilemma facing South Korea's leadership was recently captured by veteran progressive writer and commentator Rhyu Si-min. Rhyu observed that the most relentlessly persecuted politician in modern South Korean history has finally ascended to the nation’s supreme executive office. It is an extraordinary political arc, but one that carries a profound psychological challenge.

The narrative of the besieged victim is an unmatched weapon when leading an insurgency from the opposition benches. It rallies loyalists, justifies political combat, and excuses procedural aggression. However, when held by the supreme commander of the state, that very same narrative becomes an institutional liability.

Despite wielding the full apparatus of government, President Lee frequently slips back into the rhetoric of an embattled outsider, tacitly signaling to his fandom that he still requires their defensive shield. Whether managing flagging approval numbers among young men in their twenties or countering legislative friction, the administration continues to lean on the emotional fervor of partisan internet creators. A head of state, however, is not an individual to be protected by citizens; he is the constitutional actor tasked with protecting them. Clinging to the memory of persecution while directing the powers of the state invites institutional blindness and risks repeating the insularity that brought down previous governments.

What to Watch Next

The fracturing of the progressive media landscape marks the end of the post-impeachment honeymoon period and signals a new, more fragmented phase in South Korean political communications. As this internal realignment plays out, several concrete developments will determine the political fallout:

  • Kim Ou-joon’s editorial response: Watch whether Kim chooses to absorb the president’s rebuff quietly to protect his standing with the base, or whether his broadcasts begin airing calibrated, veiled critiques of presidential aides to reassert his independent authority.
  • The escalation of streamer factionalism: Monitor whether hardline pro-Lee channels, led by Lee Dong-hyung and affiliated creators, intensify their offensive to formally label legacy progressive figures as "anti-Lee," potentially alienating moderate voters and older progressive factions.
  • The sustainability of live-streamed governance: Observe whether the administration quietly scales back live Cabinet broadcasts or faces policy missteps resulting from scripted, conflict-averse internal discussions.
  • The real test for the Special Inspector: Track whether the newly revived watchdog is granted the independence to scrutinize the administration's own commercial and political networks, or whether it functions merely as a rhetorical contrast to Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration.

Having conquered the highest office in the country, Lee Jae-myung no longer faces an existential threat from state prosecutors or conservative rivals. Instead, his most immediate test lies in managing the sprawling, monetization-driven media ecosystem that helped lift him to power—and deciding whether he will govern for the digital base, or for the country at large.
