Fractured Loyalties: The Data War Within South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

October 27, 2026

On a Tuesday afternoon at the National Assembly in Seoul, a seminar hall usually reserved for dry policy debates became the epicenter of a political earthquake. The event, co-hosted by the Hankyoreh (a major progressive daily newspaper), the Korean Political Science Association, and STI (a prominent political research firm), was titled "The Current State of Democracy and Party Politics." It should have been a scholarly look at voter sentiment; instead, it provided the ammunition for a narrative that the ruling party is cannibalizing itself from within.

The data presented at the seminar sparked a firestorm of headlines claiming that 55% of "New Lee Jae-myung" supporters—those who joined the fold relatively recently—had deserted the president in just four months. To an outside observer, this looks like a government in freefall. However, for those navigating the treacherous waters of South Korean progressivism, the numbers are less a reflection of reality and more a tool in a sophisticated "divide and conquer" operation. This is not just about a dip in the polls; it is a battle for the soul of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s current ruling party, as it struggles to balance its reformist agenda with a toxic internal culture of anonymous trolling and opportunistic "political entrepreneurs."

The 55% Mirage and the Distortion of Political Data

The headlines were designed to shock. By framing the data as a "55% desertion rate" among new supporters, media outlets and opposition critics suggested that President Lee Jae-myung’s mandate is crumbling. The survey categorized supporters into "Old Lee" (long-term loyalists) and "New Lee" (voters who shifted toward him during and after the narrow presidential victory). While the "Old Lee" desertion rate stood at a more stable 20%, the volatility of the "New Lee" group was presented as a fatal blow to the administration’s momentum.

But a closer look at the sociological context reveals a more nuanced picture. These "New Lee" supporters are not necessarily moving toward the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition. Instead, they are expressing exhaustion. The desertion is not a rejection of President Lee’s policy goals—such as stabilizing the volatile housing market or tackling inflation—but a reaction to the relentless internal sniping and conspiracy theories that have come to define the party’s online periphery.

The danger here is the "mirage effect." When data is stripped of its context, it allows political opportunists to manufacture a crisis. By focusing on the 55% figure, critics are attempting to force the DPK into a defensive crouch, making it easier to peel away the very voters who provided the slim margin of victory in the last election. The real story isn't that these voters have changed their minds about the president’s platform; it’s that they are being driven away by a house divided against itself.

The Recycling of the "Sillajen" Ghost

Perhaps the most disturbing aspect of this internal friction is the resurgence of the Sillajen scandal. For the uninitiated, Sillajen was a South Korean biotech firm at the center of a massive stock manipulation controversy years ago. During the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former president who was impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence), the prosecution attempted to link prominent liberal figures—most notably Rhyu Si-min—to the scandal.

Rhyu Si-min, a former health minister and arguably the most influential intellectual voice in the liberal camp, was eventually cleared of any wrongdoing. The "Sillajen frame" was widely seen as a politically motivated hit job by the then-prosecution service. Yet, in a bizarre twist of political irony, this "spoiled bait" from six years ago is being recycled today—not by the conservative opposition, but by a faction within the Democratic Party’s own supporter base.

"It is staggering to see individuals claiming to support the president using the same discredited scripts used by Jin Joong-kwon or Lee Un-ju years ago to attack our own intellectual assets," noted one party veteran.

The target of these attacks is often the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation, a symbolic institution dedicated to the legacy of the late former president who remains a patron saint of Korean progressivism. By attempting to "investigate" Rhyu Si-min and the foundation over settled issues like Sillajen, these internal critics are effectively doing the opposition’s work for them. They are attempting to decapitate the party’s moral and intellectual leadership under the guise of "purifying" the movement. It is a classic case of the revolution devouring its own, fueled by "political entrepreneurs"—YouTubers and influencers who profit from high-tension conflict and sensationalism.

The "Emergency Committee Gallery" and the Cult of Anonymity

To understand where these attacks are coming from, one must look at the darker corners of the Korean internet, specifically the "Democratic Party Emergency Committee Gallery" on DC Inside (a massive, Reddit-like forum). This space has become a breeding ground for what some call "New Ilbe" tactics, referring to the notorious far-right website Ilbe known for its extreme misogyny and hate speech.

The culture in these anonymous galleries is one of mockery and "witch hunting." They have popularized the meme "Moon-Cho-Teol-Rae-Yu," an acronym targeting the pillars of the liberal establishment:

  • Moon: Former President Moon Jae-in
  • Cho: Cho Kuk, the former Justice Minister and current head of a progressive think tank
  • Teol: Kim Eo-jun, a powerful liberal media personality (nicknamed "Gong-teol")
  • Rae: Choi Wook, a popular talk show host
  • Yu: Rhyu Si-min
The narrative pushed by these trolls is that President Lee Jae-myung can only truly succeed if these "old" figures are purged. They use AI-generated memes and conspiracy theories linking disparate events—from the Burning Sun nightclub scandal to various financial frauds—into a grand "deep state" narrative.

This is not harmless online chatter. It has real-world consequences for party discipline. When long-term party members (some with over a decade of membership) speak out against this toxicity, they are met with "disciplinary petitions." These anonymous trolls use the party’s own democratic mechanisms to harass and silence anyone who calls for unity. This "digital McCarthyism" threatens to alienate the moderate base that the DPK needs to govern effectively.

The Ghost of 0.7 Percent

Why does a 3.5% shift in core supporters or a 55% shift in "new" supporters matter so much? Because the DPK is haunted by the number 0.7%. That was the razor-thin margin by which Lee Jae-myung won the presidency (roughly 250,000 votes out of 34 million cast). In such a polarized environment, there is no such thing as a "small" defection.

While some pundits, like Kim Jun-il, have suggested that the party can afford to lose a few "fringe" supporters, this is a dangerous complacency. If the Lee administration is to succeed in its core missions—reforming the real estate market and stabilizing the livelihood of the working class—it needs every ounce of political capital it can muster.

The administration is currently fighting a war on two fronts. On one side is the People Power Party (PPP), which is eager to capitalize on any sign of internal DPK weakness to obstruct legislative reform. On the other side are the internal "political entrepreneurs" who would rather see the party burn than lose their influence over the online narrative. These individuals often threaten to "defect" to conservative figures like Han Dong-hoon or Oh Se-hoon if their demands aren't met, revealing their support to be transactional rather than ideological.

The focus on internal purges is a massive distraction from the "Min-saeng" (people's livelihood) issues that actually matter to voters. While trolls argue about Rhyu Si-min’s hypothetical involvement in a six-year-old scandal, the average Korean is worried about the price of groceries and the rising cost of housing. Every hour spent defending the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation from internal attacks is an hour not spent on the legislative floor.

What to Watch Next

The next few weeks will be a litmus test for the Democratic Party’s internal stability and its ability to tune out the noise of the anonymous galleries. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The "Agio" Event: On October 31, Rhyu Si-min and veteran journalist Byeon Sang-wook are scheduled to hold a public talk. This event will be a significant indicator of whether the party's intellectual leadership can maintain its connection with the grassroots despite the online smear campaigns.
  • The Pyeongsan Village Pilgrimage: Many rank-and-file party members are planning visits to Pyeongsan Village, the residence of former President Moon Jae-in. This movement serves as a counter-signal to the "Moon-Cho-Teol-Rae-Yu" purge narrative, emphasizing continuity and respect for the party's history.
  • Legislative Focus: Watch whether the Lee administration can successfully pivot the national conversation back to real estate reform and inflation. If the government can deliver tangible economic wins, the "55% desertion" narrative will likely evaporate.
  • Internal Discipline: Will the DPK leadership take a firm stand against the "disciplinary petitions" being used as weapons by anonymous trolls? The party's response to this digital harassment will determine if it can protect its long-term members from "witch hunts."

The Democratic Party stands at a crossroads. It can either succumb to the centrifugal forces of internal malice and opportunistic influence-peddling, or it can remember the lesson of the 0.7% margin: that unity is not a luxury, but a requirement for survival. In the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, the greatest threat to the Lee Jae-myung presidency may not be the opposition in the National Assembly, but the "friends" who are all too willing to burn the house down to keep themselves warm.
