The "Last Place" CEO: Cronyism and the Fracturing of South Korea’s Meritocracy

October 06, 2026

Imagine applying for a high-stakes executive role at a major public utility. You navigate a rigorous recruitment process, only to finish dead last in both your written application and your interview. In most professional settings, this would be the end of your candidacy. Yet, in the murky waters of South Korean public sector appointments, this failure was exactly what secured Ha Dong-geun the presidency of the Korea District Heating Corp (KDHC), a state-run entity responsible for supplying heat and power to millions. He now oversees the agency with an annual salary of approximately $175,000 (240 million won), a position that has become the latest flashpoint in a growing national debate over political patronage.

The Anatomy of a "Patronage Appointment"

The scandal surrounding Ha’s appointment surfaced following an investigative report by the Chosun Ilbo, South Korea’s leading conservative newspaper. According to documents provided by an opposition lawmaker, Ha’s performance during the recruitment process in April was abysmal. Out of eight candidates who passed the preliminary document screening, Ha ranked eighth—dead last. In the subsequent interview stage, which involved five candidates, he ranked last again.

Perhaps most concerning was the assessment of his professional expertise. In the category evaluating "knowledge and experience in the relevant field," not a single member of the evaluation committee awarded him an ‘A’ grade. Despite this, the Public Institution Executive Recommendation Committee narrowed the field to five, and following a formal recommendation by the relevant minister, Ha was installed as president. While the government may argue that the formal, legal procedures were followed, this case exposes how bureaucratic "check-the-box" exercises can be used to legitimize decisions that are fundamentally at odds with merit-based hiring.

Critics point out that Ha’s background as an environmental activist—while laudable—provides little relevance to the technical complexities of managing an energy utility. The outrage is amplified by the perception that KDHC, a state-owned enterprise funded by taxpayers, has effectively been repurposed as a retirement fund for political loyalists.

The "Seongnam Line" and the Death of Fairness

The central keyword in this controversy is the "Seongnam Line." Ha Dong-geun shares deep, long-standing ties with President Lee Jae-myung and Kim Hyun-ji, a powerful figure who serves as the President’s personal secretary. The connection is intimate enough that Ha reportedly officiated Kim’s wedding.

This is not an isolated incident. The pattern is becoming painfully familiar: appointments are awarded not based on professional competence, but on proximity to the inner circle. Another example is Nam Gwang-woo, the current head of the Korea Institute of Environmental Industry and Technology. Nam, who draws a salary of about $130,000 (180 million won), was also a product of the same Seongnam-based civic groups as the President and Kim. Despite finishing third in document screening and second in interviews, he was selected over the first-place candidate.

When these appointments become routine, the damage to internal organizational culture is profound. It sends a demoralizing message to career professionals who have spent decades honing their expertise: that the "last place" candidate in a political network is more valuable than the top-performing internal specialist. For a government that rose to power on the promise of "fairness," this contradiction is particularly corrosive.

Signs of a Lame Duck

Why is this happening now, less than two years into the administration? Historically, such blatant patronage appointments tend to consolidate toward the middle or end of a term, when a government’s political capital begins to wane. The fact that these controversies are erupting so early suggests a premature loosening of the administration's grip and a decline in discipline. It indicates that the mechanisms meant to ensure objective hiring have been hollowed out by political gatekeepers.

The public, once desensitized to the "customary" nature of these appointments, is starting to view them as blatant special treatment. Even core supporters of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal opposition, have been largely silent in defending this case. The numbers—ranking dead last and receiving a six-figure salary—are simply too stark to gloss over with partisan rhetoric. It is a sign that the administration’s moral authority is being depleted.

The Institutional Crisis: Governance as a Reward

Public institutions belong to the taxpayers, not to the administration of the day. Statistics regarding the performance of these institutions are grim: many public entities led by "parachute" appointees frequently receive the lowest possible management evaluation grades. When leadership is based on loyalty rather than skill, the quality of services provided to the public inevitably suffers.

The argument sometimes used by political insiders—that these positions are "naturally" reserved for those who helped win the election—is only persuasive when the government maintains a strong moral high ground. In the current climate, that argument rings hollow. The public is not asking for anything radical; they are simply demanding that within the public sector, competence should outweigh personal connections.

Currently, about 16.8% of the 298 heads of public institutions in South Korea are considered "parachute" appointees. When one in six leaders is appointed for political reasons rather than professional merit, it acts as an anchor on the country’s economic efficiency.

What to Watch Next

The administration now faces a critical juncture. The "Kim Hyun-ji line" has become a label that threatens to undermine the legitimacy of every policy initiative this government undertakes. To recover, the government must move beyond hollow procedural defenses and address the systemic rot:

  • Institutional Reform: Will the government increase the proportion of independent, external experts on recruitment committees?
  • Transparency: Will there be moves to mandate the public disclosure of candidate evaluation scores to prevent "last-place" winners?
  • Political Accountability: How will the President address the growing perception that the administration is prioritizing "Seongnam Line" cronyism over the national interest?

The public is no longer willing to accept that "legal" is synonymous with "ethical." If the administration continues to prioritize patronage over performance, it risks accelerating its own decline, proving that the "flower path" it believes it is walking may actually be a path toward a political precipice. Whether this serves as a wake-up call for reform or merely another chapter in the cycle of political entitlement remains the most critical question for the coming months.
