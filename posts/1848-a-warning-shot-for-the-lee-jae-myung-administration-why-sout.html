A Warning Shot for the Lee Jae-Myung Administration: Why South Korea’s Progressive Voters Are Turning to the Rebuilding Korea Party

November 02, 2026

Inside South Korea’s ruling liberal coalition, a quiet anxiety is beginning to build. Following the historic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, President Lee Jae-myung took office backed by a progressive movement that expected swift, uncompromising reform. Yet, early November 2026 tracking polls revealed a startling shift in public mood. Rather than consolidating support behind the ruling party, a crucial slice of liberal voters is actively directing their support toward a minor alternative: the Rebuilding Korea Party, a sharp-edged progressive party founded by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk.

This trend is not a random fluctuation, nor is it a sign that progressive voters are converting to conservatism. Instead, it represents a deliberate warning shot aimed directly at President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). Frustrated by perceived legislative hesitation, party infighting, and an overly cautious political strategy, core supporters are using opinion polls as a tactical weapon to demand a more aggressive reform agenda.

The Poll Numbers That Shocked the Ruling Liberal Coalition

The latest poll conducted by Realmeter in the first week of November 2026 delivered numbers that caught political strategists in Seoul off guard. The main opposition conservative party, the People Power Party (PPP), saw its support drop by 2.4 percentage points to 40.2%. Yet, the ruling DPK failed to capitalize on the opposition's decline, rising only modestly by 2.3 percentage points to 38.3%—remaining stuck below the crucial 40% mark.

The true surprise of the survey was the performance of the Rebuilding Korea Party. Gaining 1.4 percentage points to reach 8.1%, the party hit its highest polling level since November 2024. For a minor third party operating in a heavily polarized two-party system, breaking past 8% is a major political milestone.

A closer look at the demographics behind this surge reveals a striking paradox:

  • Over 90% of respondents who support the Rebuilding Korea Party rate President Lee Jae-myung’s performance negatively.
  • These voters are not conservative defectors; they are disappointed liberals who feel the current administration lacks reform velocity.
  • Supporters are using opinion poll calls to signal their dissatisfaction, effectively handing the ruling party a political "red card."

Rather than seeking an entirely different political path, these voters are expressing deep frustration that the Lee Jae-myung administration and the DPK are failing to project the decisive momentum they promised during the election campaign. When the ruling party appears hesitant, its base moves to where the political messaging feels clearer and sharper.

Factional Cracks and the Blame Game Inside the Democratic Party

This drift in voter sentiment comes at a time when internal strife inside the DPK is boiling over into the public sphere. The friction came to a head when DPK lawmaker Lee Hae-sik published a contentious Facebook post targeting the party leadership's approach toward a potential merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party.

Since August, when Kim Min-seok took over leadership of the DPK, the ruling party had established a special task force aimed at broader liberal unity, floating ideas of integrating Cho Kuk’s party by early next year. However, Lawmaker Lee Hae-sik sharply criticized the proposed January deadline, publicly questioning who authorized such a timeline and arguing that rushing into structural integration was procedural recklessness.

The conflict goes deeper than procedural timelines. In his post, Lawmaker Lee sought to shift blame for past local election defeats—most notably the loss in the Seoul mayoral race—onto former party leader Jeong Cheong-rae, arguing that a failure to merge cleanly before the local elections cost the party key municipal victories. Yet, political observers were quick to point out the hypocrisy in Lee's argument:

"Blaming external merger timing ignores direct operational accountability. Lawmaker Lee Hae-sik served as the general campaign chief for candidate Jung Won-oh in that very Seoul mayoral election. Blaming former leadership for tactical failures on the ground is a classic exercise in dodging responsibility."

This willingness to engage in public recriminations while ignoring campaign missteps has alienated core supporters, reinforcing the perception that DPK insiders are more focused on internal power dynamics than on advancing their legislative platform.

The Shadow of the 49.1% Mandate and Media-Prosecution Pressures

To understand the depth of voter impatience, one must look back at how President Lee Jae-myung secured the presidency. Lee won the election with 49.1% of the vote. While solid, that figure fell short of an overwhelming majority, despite taking place in the wake of the political upheaval surrounding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's removal from office.

Why did Lee’s vote share cap out at 49.1% under such favorable conditions? Progressive strategists point to years of intense institutional pressure. For years, the prosecution service and conservative media outlets engaged in what supporters describe as a relentless "demonization" campaign against Lee Jae-myung, showering his campaign with unverified allegations and high-profile raids that eroded his appeal among moderate swing voters.

While President Lee himself has humbly remarked that his failure to cross the 50% mark was due to his own shortcomings, his core base sees things differently. They believe the administration remains bogged down by the lingering systemic biases of the judiciary and the press. Supporters expect the ruling party to dismantle these institutional obstacles head-on. Instead, the DPK often appears paralyzed by a double-bind: hesitant to push aggressive bills out of fear of public backlash when polls are high, yet paralyzed by a perceived lack of momentum when polls fall.

Absorption vs. Alliance: What Comes Next for Progressive Reform

As the political landscape evolves, the relationship between the ruling DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party will be a defining factor in South Korean politics. While some DPK figures favor absorbing the smaller party into a single entity, public sentiment suggests otherwise. A poll conducted by the Hankook Ilbo revealed that even within the DPK’s own voting base, opposition to a full merger (41.1%) outweighs support for it (32.7%).

Liberal voters increasingly prefer the Rebuilding Korea Party to remain independent, acting as a "catfish" in the political tank—an energetic challenger that forces the larger, more sluggish DPK to move faster and stay sharp. Cho Kuk's party recently demonstrated this independence by taking its first official party-line stance against a DPK-led bill, demonstrating that it will not simply serve as a rubber stamp for the ruling party.

As DPK Leader Kim Min-seok recently noted, the era of simply negotiating power-sharing deals between party elites is over. Moving forward, political observers should watch three key dynamics:

  • Legislative Velocity: Watch whether the DPK responds to the poll warning by accelerating its core judicial and media reform bills, or if it doubles down on moderate consensus-building.
  • Electoral Coordination: Look at upcoming by-elections, such as key races in districts like Pyeongtaek B, to see if the two progressive parties form tactical candidate-unification alliances without forcing an organizational merger.
  • Internal Discipline: Monitor whether President Lee Jae-myung can rein in internal factional spats and pivot his administration from defensive governance to proactive agenda-setting.

Opinion poll numbers rarely lie. The 8.1% surge for the Rebuilding Korea Party shows that progressive voters are tired of excuses and internal scapegoating. For President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea, the path forward requires listening to their base: less time spent on internal politics, and more focus on delivering the decisive reforms they were elected to achieve.
