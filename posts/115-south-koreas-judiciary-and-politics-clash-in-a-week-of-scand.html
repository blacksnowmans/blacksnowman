South Korea's Judiciary and Politics Clash in a Week of Scandal

September 29, 2026

As the Chuseok autumn harvest festival came to a close, South Korean politics was thrust into a turbulent storm of overlapping crises. For a brief moment, the country had paused to share family meals and worry about holiday weight gain, but that normalcy evaporated overnight. By Monday, September 28, a series of explosive reports erupted from the National Assembly, the judiciary, and the diplomatic corps, exposing deep-seated fractures in South Korea's governance. From the unprecedented decision to summon the nation's top judge to a parliamentary audit, to an allegedly doctored lunch receipt violating anti-graft laws, and a high-stakes diplomatic fallout over North Korean prisoners of war, the nation finds itself gripping the edges of its seat.

Breaking Decades of Tradition: The Chief Justice Summoned

For decades, an unwritten rule governed South Korea's constitutional order: the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court would attend the opening session of the National Assembly's parliamentary audit, offer a brief greeting, and immediately leave. It was a gesture meant to preserve the delicate separation of powers between the judiciary and the legislature. But on September 28, the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, driven by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal party), shattered that convention entirely. They voted to summon Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae as a standard witness for the parliamentary audit.

Opposition lawmakers from the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) protested fiercely before staging a mass walkout just before the vote. The ruling bloc stood its ground, justifying the move as a necessary check against what they termed an unconstitutional delay in appointing Supreme Court justices. Critics argue this move deals a severe blow to the independence of the judiciary, threatening the constitutional balance of power. Yet, a closer examination reveals that the judiciary largely brought this crisis upon itself.

Chief Justice Cho's public response did little to soothe tensions. Walking into work, he made little effort to hide his frustration, complaining publicly that the presidential office had issued bare demands for new appointments without offering valid reasons. When powerful institutions operate for too long without meaningful oversight, they risk losing their internal compass. The Democratic Party's floating of potential impeachment proceedings against the Chief Justice stems from this accumulated distrust. While an actual impeachment faces steep political hurdles and potential backlash, the undeniable reality is that the head of the judiciary marched straight into the political arena and invited the blow.

A Questionable Lunch Receipt and Anti-Graft Law Violations

Institutional authority often crumbles not from grand, sweeping conspiracies, but from small, unseemly details. A investigative report by the independent outlet Newstapa laid bare a scandal regarding Chief Justice Cho's official expense accounts that borders on farce.

On January 15, Cho held a lunch meeting at an upscale Korean restaurant in Seoul's affluent Gangnam district with Lee Soo-young, the head of the Law Times, a specialized legal newspaper. According to official expense records released by the Supreme Court, the lunch cost a total of 525,000 won (about $380) for 11 attendees. That breaks down to roughly 47,000 won per person—tucked safely below the 50,000 won limit mandated by South Korea's Improper Solicitation and Graft Act at the time.

However, that neat accounting fell apart upon closer inspection. Newstapa reporters visited the restaurant and discovered that the cheapest meal on the menu was 65,000 won; a 50,000-won option did not exist. Furthermore, Lee Soo-young, the guest from the Law Times, testified that only seven people actually attended—four from the Supreme Court and three from the newspaper. To fit the legal spending cap, the Supreme Court administrative staff allegedly inflated the attendee count to 11 on official documents.

This is far from a minor administrative error. The Law Times is South Korea's sole legal-affairs newspaper, holding an intimate reporting relationship with the Supreme Court. For the head of the judiciary to receive high-end hospitality exceeding legal limits from a closely tied media outlet—and to falsify public expense records to conceal it—strikes at the heart of legal ethics. When the institution tasked with punishing lawbreakers bends the rules for a luxury meal, public outrage is a natural consequence.

Diplomatic Fallout Over North Korean POWs

While domestic institutions bickered, a separate crisis unfolded on the international stage. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy publicly revealed during a United Nations address that two North Korean prisoners of war captured in the conflict would be repatriated to South Korea.

Seoul and Kyiv had originally agreed to keep the arrangement strictly confidential. Sending captured combatants to a third country rather than leveraging them in negotiations with Russia carries immense political sensitivity for Ukraine. More importantly, absolute secrecy was vital to safeguard the physical safety of the defectors now arriving in South Korea, as well as the families they left behind in the North.

Ukraine's unilateral disclosure broke that trust. When South Korean officials lodged complaints, Kyiv reportedly claimed no such confidentiality agreement existed. For South Korean conservatives, however, the incident immediately became ammunition for domestic politicking. Rather than condemning Ukraine's diplomatic recklessness, some conservative lawmakers rushed to border regions to stage solo protests, accusing the government of trying to hide the truth out of deference to Pyongyang.

This political framing ignores the very real human stakes involved. True national security requires protecting defectors and their families, not turning a delicate intelligence and humanitarian issue into a blunt instrument to bludgeon the administration.

Navigating an Unsteady Political Landscape

Recent public opinion polls illustrate just how precariously the political parties are treading. Realmeter surveys indicate a slight rebound in President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings to 37.9%, while other polls, such as those by News Tomato, show negative sentiments hovering near 60%. Party support ratings remain locked in a tight, within-the-margin-of-error race between the ruling Democrats and the conservative opposition.

Yet these shifting numbers point to a deeper truth: the public's appetite for structural reform remains strong. Judicial and prosecutorial overhauls are not mere political playthings designed to boost or bust a president's ratings; they are necessary steps to dismantle elite cartels that have long operated above the law. Whether through parliamentary scrutiny of the judiciary or investigations into falsified expense accounts, South Korea's institutions are being forced to confront their own accountability.
