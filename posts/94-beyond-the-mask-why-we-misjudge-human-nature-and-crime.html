Beyond the Mask: Why We Misjudge Human Nature and Crime

September 27, 2026

We have all heard the phrase, "They are such a good person." When someone is kind to us, treats us to dinner, and always takes our side, we want to believe that their very essence is virtuous. But is the human heart really so simple? Can it be reduced to a single, favorable dimension?

Listening to Pyo Chang-won—one of South Korea’s most prominent criminal profilers—it becomes chilling how often we are deceived by outward appearances and temporary acts of kindness. He reminds us of a harsh truth: human nature is not revealed when someone is treating you well; it is revealed in the critical moments when a person must choose between their own self-interest and what is right.

Every time a heinous crime makes the news, the public is quick to insist that the perpetrator "looked like a criminal from birth." This narrative is not only ignorant but dangerous. The reality is that these offenders often walk among us as ordinary neighbors, or even as model students or successful stars. Crime is not a simple matter of being born "evil," nor is it solely the product of one's environment. It is the result of three gears grinding together: innate disposition, upbringing, and the individual choices one makes. To understand why we are so often blindsided, we must look past our flawed methods of judging others.

The False Comfort of Judging by Appearances

We often use the "tough times" test—the idea that you only know someone’s true colors after they’ve survived a crisis—to judge character. But a profiler’s view is more sobering. Pyo recalls a childhood friend, someone he fought and reconciled with, who was sentenced to death at nineteen for a brutal crime. The shock of realizing that the person you thought you knew intimately could commit such an act is a lifelong burden. We saw a similar phenomenon in the 1990 case of Hong Soon-young, who kidnapped and murdered a kindergartner to demand a ransom; she was a face that once smiled in the same social circles as her victims. These events prove how superficial our understanding of others really is.

Pyo argues that we must adopt a colder, more objective criterion: "Is this person choosing interest over righteousness?" Kindness is not a synonym for morality. If the foundation of that kindness is based on your "utility"—your value to them—they will turn on you the moment the situation changes. We misidentify those who are useful to us as "good people," when we should be watching which way their internal scale tilts. If you cannot see the calculator hidden behind a sweet smile, you are the one who will bear the cost of the inevitable betrayal.

Furthermore, society clings to the "physiognomy" myth—the idea that you can spot a criminal by their eyes or "vibe." When a criminal’s photo is released, the internet erupts with claims that they "looked like a killer." If that were true, crime could be easily prevented. In reality, serial killer Yoo Young-chul lived in a Seoul studio apartment, chatting with neighbors and buying groceries, and no one saw a "murderous gaze." The danger of this belief is that it acts as a defensive mechanism: by convincing ourselves that criminals are a different "species" from us, we feel safe. It is an arrogant lie that keeps us from confronting the complex reality of human choices.

Nature, Nurture, and the Myth of Inevitability

The history of criminology is littered with attempts to simplify human behavior. In the 19th century, Cesare Lombroso tried to link crime to physical features like high cheekbones. While that theory has long been debunked, modern science—such as the study of the MAOA gene (sometimes dubbed the "warrior gene")—also suggests that genetics are not a death sentence. They are merely a potential trigger.

One of the most striking examples of this is the case of neuroscientist James Fallon. While studying the brains of psychopaths, he discovered that his own brain scan showed the exact same structural patterns as those of the killers he researched. Digging into his family history, he found a long line of murderers. Yet, Fallon grew up to be a renowned scientist. Why? Because of his mother and grandmother. They provided consistent behavioral reinforcement—punishing bad behavior and rewarding prosocial actions. This illustrates the power of the "biosocial" approach: even those with a genetic predisposition can remain within societal norms if provided with the right environment and upbringing.

Tragically, society often ignores this scientific reality, instead branding the children of criminals as "born to be bad." When a child of a known criminal gets into a schoolyard fight, neighbors whisper, "The bloodline never lies." This deterministic prejudice is a form of violence that pushes vulnerable children toward the very future they are trying to escape. We must hold the criminals responsible for their actions, but we must stop treating their children as if their lives are already written.

The Bystander Effect and Our "Silent" Classrooms

The violence we see in society is often just a magnified version of the classroom. Pyo Chang-won often asks audiences of elite professionals—judges, doctors, executives—if they witnessed school bullying in their youth. Almost no one raises their hand to say they didn't. Yet, only about 10% admit to being victims or perpetrators. Where were the other 90%? They were the silent "bystanders."

These bystanders, who grew up afraid to intervene lest they become the next target, are now the parents teaching their own children the same survival strategy: "Don't get involved, just keep your head down." This is the core of our social decay. When these children become adults, they are the ones who watch workplace bullying and do nothing. When their own children become bullies, they are the parents who hire lawyers to protect their child’s reputation rather than teaching them accountability.

We are currently obsessed with "retributive justice" content—online videos where bullies are "punished" in satisfying ways. While understandable, this thirst for revenge doesn't heal the victim. We demand the "demonization" of the bully but remain incredibly stingy when it comes to funding real psychological support and long-term care for the victims. We need a fundamental shift in education: instead of teaching children how to survive by being invisible, we must teach them the courage to stand together.

What to watch next:

  • Policy shifts: Watch for changes in how school bullying is addressed—is the focus shifting from purely punitive measures toward victim-centered healing?
  • Social discourse: Pay attention to the "bystander" conversation. As the, "just don't get involved" mentality is increasingly criticized, look for new grassroots initiatives that encourage students to intervene safely.
  • Legal focus: Monitor how courts handle cases involving "relational crimes" (like stalking or dating violence). Are they treating these as isolated "crimes of passion," or are they beginning to recognize the patterns of systemic narcissism and control identified by experts like Pyo?
