How South Korea Survived Its Constitutional Crisis: From Martial Law to a New Presidency

September 30, 2026

Late on the night of December 3, 2024, the streets of Seoul were quiet until the sudden, jarring broadcast of an emergency decree shattered the evening peace. For international observers watching from afar, the images unfolding on television screens seemed surreal—soldiers in full combat gear and military helicopters descending upon the National Assembly in an echo of South Korea’s authoritarian past. Yet, this was happening in the 21st century, in one of Asia’s most vibrant and technologically advanced democracies. What followed over the next six months was an extraordinary test of constitutional resilience, pitting an overreaching executive against a mobilized citizenry and a resolute parliament. For those outside Korea trying to understand how the nation weathered this political earthquake, the sequence of events offers a masterclass in how democratic institutions—when backed by ordinary people—can check the abuse of power.

The December 3 Emergency Decree and the Night Armed Forces Entered Parliament

The crisis began at 10:27 p.m. when then-President Yoon Suk Yeol appeared on live television to declare emergency martial law. Justifying the decree as a necessary step to eradicate "anti-state forces" and protect the constitutional order, the administration attempted to abruptly suspend basic civil liberties. The phrasing relied on vague, ominous tropes that instantly evoked dark memories of military dictatorships from decades prior. Rather than restoring order, the decree plunged the nation into immediate shock and profound anxiety.

Within hours, the situation escalated from rhetoric to physical confrontation. Military helicopters touched down on the grounds of the National Assembly, and heavily armed troops forced their way into the building. For millions watching live feeds on their phones and televisions, the sight of soldiers deployed against the nation's elected representatives was deeply jarring. Yet, resistance materialized instantly. Lawmakers scaled fences and ordinary citizens rushed to the Assembly grounds, literally throwing themselves in front of the advancing troops to block their entry. This tense standoff at the legislative gates laid bare a stark reality: the armed forces of a democratic state were being turned against its own people, and it was left to unarmed citizens and legislators to defend the republic.

The Legislative Shield: Parliament Defies the Decree

Inside the barricaded National Assembly, under the leadership of Speaker Woo Won-shik, the legislature moved with rapid, decisive authority. Defying the surrounding military pressure, lawmakers rushed to the plenary hall. In a stunning display of cross-party unity driven by a shared imperative to defend the constitution, a resolution demanding the immediate lifting of martial law was introduced. All 190 members present voted in favor, passing it unanimously.

When the outcome was announced, cheers erupted inside the hall and echoed among the crowds of citizens holding vigil outside. It was a vital institutional firewall. Faced with a unanimous legislative demand and mounting public defiance, the executive branch collapsed inward. Just hours after deploying troops to purge supposed threats, the administration capitulated. Yoon formally announced the withdrawal of the military and accepted the National Assembly's demand through a cabinet meeting. The attempt to subvert democratic norms had withered under the harsh light of institutional procedure and collective resistance.

The Stalled First Impeachment and the Outpouring of Public Anger

The relief of having turned back the decree was short-lived. Recognizing that a president who attempts to subvert the constitution cannot remain in office, the opposition moved swiftly to introduce an impeachment motion. However, on December 7, 2024, the political reality of a divided parliament struck a bitter blow. The vote failed to reach the required two-thirds majority because ruling party lawmakers largely boycotted the process, leaving the total vote count at 195 and preventing the ballot boxes from even opening.

Public fury was immediate and widespread. When institutional politics faltered, the citizenry took to the streets. What followed was a nationwide mobilization unlike anything seen in recent years. From Yeouido in front of the National Assembly in Seoul to Dongseong-ro in Daegu, Seomyeon in Busan, and the historic 5.18 Democratic Square in Gwangju, millions of citizens braved the bitter winter cold. Waving candles and chanting for the administration's resignation, the protests transcended traditional regional and generational divides. This massive wave of public pressure made it clear that the electorate would not tolerate political maneuvering or impunity.

Constitutional Reckoning: Impeachment and Removal from Office

Staring down the barrel of relentless public outrage, the political landscape shifted. On December 14, 2024, just a week after the first failed attempt, a second impeachment motion came to the floor of the National Assembly. This time, the vote yielded a resounding 204 votes in favor out of 300 total, reflecting a massive fracture within the ruling party as lawmakers bowed to public sentiment and voted to suspend the president.

The final arbiter was the Constitutional Court. On April 4, 2025, acting Chief Justice Moon Hyung-bae delivered a unanimous verdict from the bench, formally stripping Yoon of the presidency. The ruling definitively established that the emergency decree was an unconstitutional assault on the rule of law. For a nation bearing the historical scars of past authoritarian rule, the court's unanimous decision reaffirmed that no individual—regardless of office—stands above the constitution.

A New Democratic Mandate and the Road Ahead

With the presidency vacant, South Korea's electoral machinery swung into motion to fill the leadership vacuum. The 21st presidential election was scheduled for June 3, 2025. In an atmosphere charged with the desire for political renewal, voters went to the polls in high numbers. Lee Jae-myung emerged victorious with 49.42% of the vote, taking the oath of office the following day.

The swift transition back to a functioning government under a newly elected leader demonstrated the resilience of South Korea's democratic framework. While the martial law crisis and subsequent impeachment left deep psychological scars on the national psyche, it also reinforced a fundamental truth: democracy in South Korea is not merely a passive system of governance inherited from the past, but an active, hard-won reality sustained by citizens willing to defend it.
