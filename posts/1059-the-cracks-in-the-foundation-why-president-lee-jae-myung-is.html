The Cracks in the Foundation: Why President Lee Jae-myung is Facing an Internal Revolt

October 13, 2026

There is a distinct chill settling over the halls of power in Seoul. For months, the South Korean political sphere has been buzzing with rumors of an impending "big reveal"—a whistleblower event that threatens to shake the foundations of the current administration. While the honeymoon phase following President Lee Jae-myung’s election seemed to suggest a period of stability, the reality beneath the surface is far more volatile. A series of high-profile ruptures between the president and his former allies has turned this speculation into a genuine crisis.

To the casual observer, these might look like mere squabbles. But for those watching the shifting tectonic plates of Korean politics, these aren't just disagreements; they are the warning signs of a regime losing its core.

The Falling Out: Choi Bae-kun and the Myth of 'Position Seeking'

On October 4, the political landscape shifted when Professor Choi Bae-kun, a prominent intellectual who was once a stalwart supporter of Lee, officially withdrew his support. This was not a private grievance; it was a public excommunication that highlighted the growing divide within the liberal camp.

President Lee’s response was swift and sharp. Through social media, he attacked his former ally, accusing him of "demanding a high-ranking position despite a lack of ability" and characterizing his criticism as an "internal attack disguised as calls for radical reform."

This dynamic—a powerful leader publicly delegitimizing a critic by labeling them a disgruntled job-seeker—is a tired trope in Korean politics, but it is one that resonates poorly with the public. By attacking the critic's motives rather than addressing the substance of their critique, the administration has signaled a defensive, perhaps even desperate, posture. The core of Choi’s frustration lies in a perceived betrayal of the campaign promises Lee made to the working class. When a leader dismisses principled dissent as mere opportunism, they don't just alienate a critic; they alienate the voters who saw that critic as a champion of their own ideals.

The Politics of Isolation: Why Even Allies are Keeping Their Distance

If the public fallout with Professor Choi was bad, the silent distancing of Rhyu Si-min—a legendary figure in the liberal movement and a former minister—is perhaps more damaging. When asked about a potential private meeting with the president, Rhyu was blunt: he had no interest in one. He argued that the administration’s current trajectory regarding prosecution reform and its general governing philosophy had veered so far from its original promises that it felt like a deception of the public.

Rhyu’s refusal to engage isn’t just personal; it is a calculated survival strategy. History has shown that when leaders at the pinnacle of power stop listening to uncomfortable truths, they inevitably surround themselves with sycophants. By refusing a private audience, Rhyu is effectively signaling that the current administration has become a space where dissent is no longer welcomed, but punished. It is a grim reflection of the "yummy" nature of power: the tendency of those at the top to filter out anyone who isn't singing their praises, eventually leaving the leader in a vacuum of their own making.

The Erosion of the Base: Beyond Mere Poll Numbers

The alarm bells are now ringing in the data. Recent polling shows a sharp, erratic decline in support for President Lee. While his approval ratings hovered around 80% in the early days of his presidency, they have plummeted to historic lows in recent weeks.

Crucially, political analysts like Kim Ou-joon—a prominent media personality—have noted that this is not a typical fluctuation. The support that is bleeding away is the "core support"—the dedicated voters who stood by Lee during the chaotic downfall of the previous administration under the now-imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol.

When a government loses its base, it loses its momentum. The administration’s attempts to blame their woes on "misunderstandings" or "communication issues" are failing to satisfy a public increasingly cynical about the government's handling of the economy. Real estate policy and tax burdens remain the primary sources of public anxiety, and the internal bickering among the president's own party members suggests a lack of a cohesive, effective strategy to address these existential concerns.

The Personnel Dilemma: Loyalty Over Merit

Perhaps the most damning criticism facing the Lee administration is its approach to appointments. The recent naming of Kim Ji-yong as head of the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency has sparked outrage, not just for the choice, but for the philosophy it represents. Critics, including veteran journalist Jang In-soo, have pointed out a pattern: the president appears to have an aversion to independent-minded, sharp intellectuals, preferring instead those who display unquestioning loyalty to the center of power.

The logic provided by the administration—that "smart, outspoken people are exhausting to work with"—reveals a profound insecurity. A government that prioritizes "peace of mind" for the president over competence and integrity is a government that has ceased to be a vehicle for reform. By placing individuals who are willing to serve the current power structure into influential investigative roles, the administration is eroding the very institutions that were meant to hold power accountable.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will be a litmus test for the administration. Here is what to watch:

  • The "Big Reveal": The rumors surrounding a major exposure by a high-ranking figure are reaching a fever pitch. If a credible whistleblower steps forward with evidence of institutional rot, the administration’s current defensive strategy may collapse entirely.
  • Legislative Deadlock: Keep an eye on the National Assembly. As the administration’s influence wanes, its ability to pass reform legislation will be tested. Any further failure to deliver on economic promises will likely push the core base even further away.
  • Internal Party Realignment: Watch for signs of a rebellion within the Democratic Party. If party heavyweights begin to distance themselves from the Blue House to save their own political futures, the president will effectively become a lame duck.

President Lee Jae-myung stands at a crossroads. He can continue to surround himself with "yes-men" and dismiss his critics as enemies, or he can acknowledge that the trust he was granted is not an infinite resource. Power is fleeting, and the public, once disillusioned, is notoriously difficult to win back. The "big thing" that many anticipate might not be a single event, but the slow, painful dissolution of a government that forgot who it was supposed to serve.
