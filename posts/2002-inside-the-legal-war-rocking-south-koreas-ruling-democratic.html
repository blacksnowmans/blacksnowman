Inside the Legal War Rocking South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

November 06, 2026

Inside South Korea's political epicenter, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is facing a dramatic judicial showdown that threatens to shatter internal party unity. Rank-and-file members have taken the drastic step of filing a legal injunction seeking the immediate suspension of party chairman Kim Min-seok, alongside a lawsuit to nullify his election victory. What began as internal grumbling over how the leadership handled a high-profile convention scandal has now exploded into a full-scale legal war over party governance and democratic legitimacy.

To understand the anger of the party faithful, one must look back at the chaotic leadership convention that brought Kim Min-seok to power. During the campaign, serious allegations emerged claiming that members of Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious movement in South Korea frequently accused of secretive political infiltration—had registered in large numbers to sway the outcome. Party members repeatedly demanded full disclosure of survey data and sample statistics to verify whether the voting process had been compromised. Instead of releasing the records, Chairman Kim dismissed the suspicions entirely, declaring that claims of Shincheonji involvement, anti-factional plotting, or secret alliances were without substance.

The refusal to release raw data rapidly fueled accusations of a cover-up. In South Korean progressive politics, internal party democracy is a deeply sensitive issue. Historically, the Democratic Party operated under a system where the votes of elite delegates carried up to twenty times the weight of regular paying members. Grassroots members fought for years to reform this structure, with internal polling showing that 85.3 percent of paying members supported a strict 1-person, 1-vote principle to ensure equal voting rights.

However, in an interview with the Kyunghyang Shinmun newspaper, Kim Min-seok raised eyebrows by admitting that full disclosure of the data could cause bigger problems, suggesting instead that the party focus on "institutional improvements" to prevent outside infiltration. Critics and dissidents within the party immediately seized on the comment, viewing it as a thinly veiled attempt to alter party rules and roll back voting equality under the pretext of defending against external cults.

This apparent pivot triggered open friction among several prominent Democratic Party lawmakers. Figures such as Jung Chung-rae, Choi Min-hee, Han Min-soo, and Lee Seong-yoon pushed for full transparency. Meanwhile, Park Sun-won, head of the party's Shincheonji Task Force, faced criticism for failing to publish concrete audit findings, putting on what opponents called an aggrieved performance rather than delivering clear facts. On the other side of the aisle, party loyalists including Kim Tae-sun, Jeon Yong-gi, and Jung Jin-wook rushed to defend the chairman, accusing internal critics of stoking needless division.

A Crowd-Funded Injunction and Allegations of Data Leaks

Left with what they view as a leadership unwilling to self-correct, dissatisfied members turned to the judiciary. The legal action to suspend Chairman Kim Min-seok and annul his election is being spearheaded by attorney Lee Jae-il, a prominent legal figure who previously served as legal counsel to President Lee Jae-myung. To ensure broad participation, the litigation team set the cost at 33,000 KRW (about $24 USD) per person, covering all legal fees from the initial injunction through appeals up to the Supreme Court. The organizers aim to gather between 1,000 and 5,000 co-plaintiffs, drawing inspiration from a similar court battle during the tenure of former conservative leader Lee Jun-seok, when 5,000 plaintiffs successfully pushed court proceedings forward.

The legal onslaught extends beyond the party chair. Plaintiffs are also filing criminal complaints and requesting reviews by the party’s Ethics Tribunal over alleged personal data leaks during the leadership race. A central flashpoint involves Assemblywoman Kim Nam-hee, who allegedly sent mass promotional text messages to party members nationwide. This raised severe data privacy concerns after fellow lawmaker Moon Jung-bok publicly admitted that standard campaign teams lacked access to the full national member database. The plaintiffs contend that sensitive internal contact lists were illegally funneled to favored candidates, violating South Korea’s Personal Information Protection Act.

In tandem with data abuse complaints involving political figures like Kim Han-me, party members are demanding ethical sanctions against lawmakers who dismissed the Shincheonji allegations as fabrications. Critics argue that figures like Yoon Jong-geun inflicted secondary damage on rank-and-file members by mocking legitimate demands for an audit. For the plaintiffs, the lawsuit is not merely a factional scuffle, but a fight to restore procedural legitimacy to South Korea's ruling party.

President Lee Jae-myung, Media Experiments, and Foreign Policy Pressure

This internal party crisis unfolds against a complex backdrop for President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea's progressive head of state. Facing shifts in public sentiment, President Lee recently launched a targeted media initiative titled President’s Y, a pre-recorded YouTube program hosted by former MBC anchor Son Jeong-eun. The letter "Y" stands for "Why," designed to let the president explain the rationale behind key national decisions directly to citizens. The move came shortly after prominent progressive commentator Kim Eo-jun openly criticized the president on his popular program News Factory, claiming President Lee had become "trapped in algorithms" and disconnected from critical feedback.

While President Lee maintained in the broadcast that he actively reads critical comments online, critics argue his administration has tended to dismiss constructive warnings from traditional supporters as malicious attacks by political opponents. The president’s approval ratings previously slipped into the low 30s, prompting concern among veteran progressive commentators like Yu Si-min. Although the administration saw a temporary 12.9 percentage point bump in support among voters in their twenties by emphasizing multicultural and foreign worker policies, observers warn that relying on demographic shifts while ignoring the core progressive base risks permanently damaging the administration's political foundation.

Compounding these domestic political headaches are major foreign policy dilemmas. The Cabinet recently approved expanding the deployment area of the navy's Cheonghae Unit from the Gulf of Aden into the Strait of Hormuz. With tensions escalating between Iran and Israel, critics inside the progressive camp view this maneuver as an indirect military deployment that risks dragging South Korean forces into a foreign conflict. Many view the move as a concession to security and defense cost-sharing pressures from the U.S. administration under Donald Trump, arguing that the leadership should be honest with voters rather than framing the escalation as a routine operational update.

Grassroots Rallies and the Democratic Path Ahead

As the court case moves forward, grassroots activists and progressive media figures are taking their fight to the streets. A major offline event titled the Sejong-Bongha-Pyeongsan Path of Democracy has been scheduled for November 7, 2026. The day-long pilgrimage begins at the grave of the late former party chairman Lee Hae-chan in Sejong, attended by former First Lady Kim Jung-sook and family members. Participants will then travel to Bongha Village to pay respects at the grave of former President Roh Moo-hyun, before ending the journey in Pyeongsan Village for a meeting with former President Moon Jae-in.

Logistics for the event involve fifteen chartered buses departing from major hubs including Jamsil and Bupyeong in the Seoul metropolitan area, Suwon, and Daejeon. To keep costs manageable for participants, ticket prices were set at 54,000 KRW (about $39 USD), with any shortfall covered directly by the independent media outlet Sisa Tapa TV.

Following the pilgrimage, activists plan to stage large-scale rallies in Yeouido, the political center of Seoul housing the National Assembly. Organized alongside progressive YouTube personalities including Lee Gwan-won, Lee Myung-soo, and Jang In-soo, the demonstrations aim to keep public pressure on the party leadership. Unlike political movements that rely on public donation drives—which often run afoul of donation collection laws—the organizers are funding the stage setups and broadcasting equipment using existing corporate revenues and banner advertisement sales from community vendors. Items sold through these networks include black beans at 17,000 KRW (about $12 USD) per kilogram, Shine Muscat grapes, Sejong pears, and traditional sikhye rice punch, creating a self-sustaining ecosystem of grassroots support.

What to Watch Next

As the legal battle and public protests gather momentum, several critical developments will determine the trajectory of South Korea's ruling party:

  • The Injunction Decision: All eyes are on the judiciary to see if judges grant the injunction suspending Chairman Kim Min-seok, which would plunge the ruling Democratic Party into an unprecedented leadership vacuum.
  • Data Privacy and Ethics Investigations: The progress of criminal complaints against Representative Kim Nam-hee regarding candidate database leaks will test whether party authorities and legal investigators are willing to enforce data privacy laws against sitting lawmakers.
  • Frictional Dynamics in Parliament: Watch whether dissenting lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae and Choi Min-hee form an organized bloc within the National Assembly to force full disclosure of the Shincheonji audit data.
  • President Lee's Base Strategy: How President Lee Jae-myung balances his administration's broad outreach with the demands of his traditional progressive base—especially regarding controversial overseas troop deployments—will dictate his legislative momentum heading into future elections.
