The Gyeonggi Audit Paradox: Why South Korea's Ruling Party is Attacking Its Own Star Governor

October 14, 2026

Walking into the audit room of the Gyeonggi Provincial Government on October 13, an observer would have expected a standard political theater: the opposition relentlessly attacking the governor while the ruling party lawmakers provided a shield of defense. In South Korean politics, party loyalty usually trumps administrative scrutiny during the annual National Assembly audits—a month-long period where the legislature grills government agencies.

However, what unfolded was a scene that defied conventional political logic. Choo Mi-ae, the Governor of Gyeonggi Province and a heavyweight within the ruling Democratic Party (DP), found herself under a more savage barrage of criticism from her own party colleagues than from the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition. The air was thick with a collective hostility that felt scripted yet visceral. To a foreign observer, it looked like a political circular firing squad.

The superficial topics were administrative: budget adjustments, provincial debt ratios, and personnel delays. But beneath the surface of "policy verification" lay a high-stakes power struggle involving the Serious Crime Investigation Office (SCIO), a new agency designed to be the crown jewel of South Korea’s judicial reform. The DP's internal war is not about Gyeonggi’s accounting; it is about who gets to define the future of the prosecution service and whether any individual—even a popular governor—is allowed to publicly disagree with the President.

A Civil War Disguised as Administrative Oversight

The Gyeonggi Provincial Government oversees a region of nearly 14 million people, effectively a "mini-state" surrounding Seoul. Its governor is often considered the second most powerful elected official in the country. Normally, DP lawmakers would use this audit to highlight the successes of their "star" governor to build momentum for the party. Instead, they took turns trying to dismantle her.

Lawmaker Lee Kwang-jae attacked Choo for a perceived lack of consultation with local municipalities regarding budget subsidies. Chae Hyun-il questioned the province's financial health, citing a 13% debt ratio and suggesting Choo’s declaration of a "financial emergency" was unrealistic political grandstanding. Kim Young-bae, another DP member, went as far as shouting over Choo, cutting off her answers with aggressive gestures usually reserved for bitter political enemies.

The intensity was baffling. If there were genuine issues with the province’s finances, they could have been addressed through dry data and policy papers. Instead, the questions shifted toward a more telling theme: "Why is the governor opposing the President's appointments?" and "Why is she interfering in central politics?"

This is where the mask of the administrative audit slipped. The DP lawmakers were not acting as independent legislators; they were acting as an enforcement squad for the Blue House (the presidential office). Their target was Choo Mi-ae’s vocal opposition to the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the SCIO. By attacking her performance as governor, they were attempting to strip her of her political standing and force her into silence on the national stage.

The SCIO and the Ghost of Judicial Reform

To understand why this internal rift is so deep, one must understand the Serious Crime Investigation Office (SCIO). For decades, the South Korean Prosecution Service held a monopoly on both the power to investigate and the power to indict. Reformers, including current President Lee Jae-myung, have long argued that this concentration of power leads to political bias and corruption. The solution was "investigative-prosecutorial separation," stripping the prosecutors of their investigative wings and handing those powers to a new, independent body: the SCIO.

The SCIO is intended to be the final nail in the coffin of the "prosecutorial republic." Therefore, the person chosen to lead it must, in the eyes of reformists, be someone with an ironclad commitment to this separation. This is why the nomination of Kim Ji-yong caused an explosion.

Kim Ji-yong is a career prosecutor. More importantly, he was one of the high-ranking officials who signed a collective statement opposing the disciplinary actions taken against Yoon Suk-yeol back when Yoon was Prosecutor General. At that time, Choo Mi-ae was the Minister of Justice and was leading the charge to hold Yoon accountable for various abuses of power. (Yoon Suk-yeol was eventually impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence.)

For Choo Mi-ae and the more radical reformist wing of the DP, appointing Kim Ji-yong to lead the SCIO is a betrayal of the movement's core values. They argue that putting a man who defended the old guard in charge of the agency meant to dismantle that guard’s power is a logical fallacy. Choo has been vocal, stating that "to remain silent while knowing the problem would be a historical mistake." She even offered to testify at his confirmation hearing to detail his unsuitability.

The Sanctification of Presidential Appointment Power

Within the mainstream of the Democratic Party, however, the priority has shifted from "reform at all costs" to "stability and loyalty." The dominant faction, often referred to as the pro-Lee (Jae-myung) wing, views any public dissent against a presidential nominee as an act of rebellion. The logic is simple: the President has the mandate, he has made his choice, and the party must fall in line to protect the administration’s authority.

In the halls of the National Assembly, DP lawmakers have gone to great lengths to "shield" Kim Ji-yong. Lawmaker Chae Hyun-il claimed, somewhat creatively, that Kim was not actually a "pro-Yoon" prosecutor but was actually "closer to being anti-Yoon." Lee Sang-sik defended Kim’s signature on the protest statement as a mere "fulfillment of professional duty."

This "shielding" has turned into a sanctification of the President's power. Party leadership has reportedly warned Choo Mi-ae to "focus on her own role" and stop "adding a spoon to the central political pot." Kim Min-seok, the DP leader, publicly urged "restraint," a polite political term for a "gag order."

"You can respect the President and still oppose his appointments. You can support the President and still doubt the capability of his nominees. That is how a democracy works."

The tragedy for the DP is that by framing all dissent as "internal shooting" or "disloyalty," they are killing the very diversity that allowed the party to grow. When a party becomes an echo chamber where "loyalty" is the only currency, it loses the ability to self-correct. The SCIO was born from the blood and sweat of citizens who protested in the streets for judicial justice; to see it potentially handed to a member of the old guard while the ruling party bullies its own members into silence is, for many supporters, a bitter pill to swallow.

The Arrogance of "Where Else Will They Go?"

This internal friction is taking a visible toll on the DP's public standing. Recent polling by Realmeter, a major South Korean polling agency, shows a worrying trend for the ruling party. In mid-October 2026, the DP's approval rating dropped to 35.5%, while the opposition PPP climbed to 41.3%—a lead outside the margin of error.

The response from some DP veterans, however, has been a masterclass in political arrogance. Veteran lawmaker Park Ji-won, appearing on a popular YouTube show, suggested that even if voters are disappointed with the DP, "they won't be able to vote for the People Power Party anyway."

This "hostage-taking" mindset—the belief that the base has no choice but to support the DP because the alternative is "unthinkable"—is a dangerous gamble. It treats the core supporters (often called "home rabbits" in Korean political slang) as a guaranteed asset rather than a group whose trust must be earned. History shows that when a base feels betrayed or ignored, they don't necessarily switch sides; they simply stop showing up. They stay home, they spoil their ballots, or they become politically cynical.

Choo Mi-ae’s popularity, particularly among women in their 20s and 30s (she won 75.9% of the vote among young women in the gubernatorial election), was built on her image as a "fighter" for reform. By attempting to "crush" her for the sake of a controversial appointment, the DP leadership risks alienating the very demographic that gave them power. The youth of South Korea are particularly sensitive to "unfairness" and "hypocrisy"—the two things the Kim Ji-yong appointment and the subsequent bullying of Choo Mi-ae seem to embody.

What to Watch Next

The fallout from the Gyeonggi audit is far from over. As the SCIO begins its work, every move made by Kim Ji-yong will be scrutinized through the lens of this controversy. If the agency fails to aggressively pursue judicial reform, the blame will fall squarely on President Lee and the DP leadership that silenced the skeptics.

Keep an eye on the following developments:

  • The SCIO's First Cases: If the new agency focuses on low-level crimes while ignoring systemic corruption within the prosecution or the elite, it will confirm the fears of the reformist wing.
  • Choo Mi-ae’s Next Move: As a governor with a massive mandate, she has the "bully pulpit." Whether she continues to challenge the party line or retreats into provincial administration will signal the strength of the DP's internal "loyalty" enforcement.
  • Polling Trends among Young Voters: Watch if the DP can recover its double-digit lead among women in their 20s and 30s. If these numbers continue to slide, the party may face a crisis in the upcoming local elections.
  • Internal Party Discipline: Will the DP leadership move to formally discipline Choo or other vocal lawmakers like Park Ju-min and Yoon Kun-young? Such a move would signal a definitive shift toward a more centralized, authoritarian party structure.

The Democratic Party currently holds the presidency and a comfortable legislative environment. But as the Gyeonggi audit showed, a party that spends more energy attacking its own "conscience" than listening to its voters is a party in peril. History has shown that the South Korean electorate is quick to punish the arrogance of power. For the DP, the "historical mistake" Choo Mi-ae warned about might already be in motion.
