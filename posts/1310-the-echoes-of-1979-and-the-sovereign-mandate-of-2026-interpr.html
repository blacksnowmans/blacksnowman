The Echoes of 1979 and the Sovereign Mandate of 2026: Interpreting South Korea’s Shifting Political Winds

October 19, 2026

On a crisp October morning in Busan, the atmosphere is heavy with the weight of memory. Forty-seven years ago, these streets and those of neighboring Masan were the flashpoints of a desperate struggle against the Yushin dictatorship of Park Chung-hee. Today, as President Lee Jae-myung stands before the monument commemorating the Buma Democratic Uprising, the event serves as more than just a historical tribute. It has become a lighthouse for understanding the current turbulent waters of South Korean politics, where historical justice, judicial distrust, and a new "sovereign diplomacy" are converging to redefine the national identity.

For an outside observer, the latest polling data from the third week of October 2026 might look like standard political flux. President Lee’s approval rating has rebounded to 41.4%, a stable plateau despite economic headwinds. However, the numbers beneath the surface tell a far more radical story. They reveal a public that is increasingly impatient with traditional power structures, from the supreme court to the main opposition party, and a nation that is signaling a historic pivot in its relationship with its most important ally, the United States.

Reclaiming the Spirit of Busan and Masan

To understand why President Lee’s attendance at the 47th anniversary of the Buma Democratic Uprising matters, one must understand the decades of enforced silence that preceded it. In October 1979, when students and citizens took to the streets to demand the end of authoritarian rule, the state-controlled media labeled the movement a mere "riot" or "disturbance" (soyo). For forty years, the families of those killed or disappeared were forced to grieve in the shadows, their loved ones branded as criminals rather than martyrs.

The case of Yoo Si-jun is particularly haunting. Killed by state violence in Masan, his body was autopsied without family consent and buried in haste by the regime to hide the evidence of brutality. It was not until the administration of Moon Jae-in (the liberal predecessor to the current government) that Yoo was officially recognized as a victim of state violence in 2019. This was a sharp departure from the previous conservative administration of Park Geun-hye—the daughter of the dictator Park Chung-hee—whose "truth commission" was stacked with loyalists who spent more time obstructing the investigation than uncovering the facts.

By attending this year’s ceremony, President Lee Jae-myung became only the second sitting president in history to do so. In his address, he framed the uprising not as a relic of the past, but as the "decisive lightning bolt" that brought down a dictatorship and established the constitutional spirit that governs Korea today. This is a deliberate political positioning. By elevating the Buma Uprising, the Lee administration is drawing a direct line between the 1979 protesters and its own mandate, contrasting its historical reverence with the perceived indifference of the previous era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was removed from office by impeachment and currently remains in prison.

Sovereign Diplomacy and the 41% Threshold

While history provides the moral foundation, the current administration’s "sovereign diplomacy" (jaju oegyo) provides the political momentum. In a recent cabinet meeting, President Lee made waves by declaring that South Korea is a "sovereign and independent nation" that will no longer tolerate "servile or flunkeyist" attitudes in international relations. While he did not name names, the target was clear: the United States.

This shift comes at a time of heightened tension. With the return of transactional foreign policy in Washington, South Korea has faced mounting pressure regarding trade imbalances and demands for massive investments—such as the controversial Alaskan energy projects. For a country that has historically viewed the U.S. alliance as its primary security blanket, Lee’s rhetoric is a bold gamble. Yet, the data suggests it is a gamble that is paying off domestically. According to recent polls by Realmeter and Gallup Korea, 56% of the population supports this assertive stance. The sentiment is consistent across most regions and age groups, save for conservative strongholds and younger men who lean toward the opposition.

This "diplomatic premium" is likely what saved the President’s approval rating this week. Despite a volatile week for the KOSPI (South Korea’s benchmark stock index) and a bruising confirmation hearing for the new Prosecutor General nominee, Kim Ji-yong, Lee’s numbers held steady at 41.4%. For a leader in the middle of his term, maintaining a floor above 40% is a sign of a robust core support base that prioritizes national dignity and sovereign interests over short-term economic fluctuations.

A Judiciary Under Fire: The Impeachment Sentiment

Perhaps the most shocking revelation from the October polls is the collapse of public trust in the judiciary. In a country where the courts have traditionally been seen as the final arbiter of truth, a majority of the public—51.7%—now supports the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. This is an unprecedented figure for the head of the Supreme Court, an institution that usually enjoys a degree of insulation from partisan bickering.

The anger is not confined to the fringes. While 84% of supporters of the Democratic Party of Korea (the ruling party) favor impeachment, the sentiment has also breached the center. Even in Seoul, the political heart of the country, support for the Chief Justice's removal sits at 52%. The public perception is that the judiciary has become a bastion of the "old guard," more interested in protecting its own institutional privileges and siding with conservative interests than in advancing the judicial reforms promised by the current administration.

This distrust extends to the prosecution as well. The nomination of Kim Ji-yong as Prosecutor General has met with a lukewarm reception. While not a total rejection, nearly 20% of the public remains undecided, waiting to see if he will truly pursue the "complete and irreversible" reform of the prosecution service. For the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, more ideologically "pure" progressive party led by Cho Kuk—the skepticism is even higher, with 90% of their supporters viewing the nominee as unfit. This internal pressure from the left ensures that President Lee cannot afford to be moderate in his reform agenda.

The Paradox of Fairness: Han Dong-hoon’s "Karma"

The most significant threat to the opposition, the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party), currently centers on its prominent lawmaker and former Justice Minister, Han Dong-hoon. Han, who rose to fame as a protégé of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, built his political brand on the concept of "fairness" and "rule of law," famously leading the aggressive prosecution of former minister Cho Kuk over his daughter’s academic credentials.

In a twist of political irony, Han is now facing a nearly identical scandal. Reports of "fake credentials" regarding his own daughter’s academic portfolio have ignited a firestorm. The latest polling shows that a staggering 74.6% of the public believes a formal investigation into Han is necessary. Most damagingly for the opposition, more than 50% of the People Power Party’s own supporters now agree that Han should be investigated.

"The blade that Han Dong-hoon used to dismantle his political opponents has now turned back toward him," noted one political analyst. "When your entire brand is built on being the 'untainted prosecutor,' a scandal involving family privilege is not just a PR problem—it’s a terminal blow to your political legitimacy."

The data shows that young men in their 20s and 30s, a demographic that previously gravitated toward Han’s "tough on crime" persona, are fleeing his camp in droves. This group is hyper-sensitive to "chance" (parental influence) in university admissions and employment. For them, Han’s alleged hypocrisy is an unforgivable betrayal of the meritocratic ideals he claimed to defend. As he faces additional complaints regarding the leaking of investigative records, Han’s path to the presidency looks increasingly obstructed by the very legal system he once commanded.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves into the final months of 2026, the political landscape is being redrawn by a public that is no longer content with the status quo. The convergence of historical reckoning and modern-day reform suggests several key areas to watch:

  • The Judiciary’s Response: Will Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae attempt a major internal reform to stave off the impeachment momentum, or will the Democratic Party of Korea move forward with a formal motion in the National Assembly? Given the 51% approval for impeachment, the political cost of doing so has dropped significantly.
  • The Prosecution’s New Era: Watch for the first actions of Kim Ji-yong if his appointment is finalized. If he fails to move aggressively against the remaining "Yoon-era" remnants in the prosecution, he could lose the support of the Rebuilding Korea Party, which acts as the ideological vanguard for the ruling coalition.
  • The Conservative Reorganization: With Han Dong-hoon’s "fairness" brand tarnished, the People Power Party is in a state of quiet panic. Watch for the emergence of a new conservative figure—likely one who can distance themselves from the legacy of the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol while offering a more moderate alternative to the Lee administration’s sovereign diplomacy.
  • Trade Negotiations with the U.S.: The 56% support for sovereign diplomacy will be tested the next time a high-level trade delegation visits Washington. If President Lee can secure a deal that protects Korean industries without causing a rupture in the security alliance, his approval ratings could break the 45% barrier, giving him a clear mandate for his final years in office.

The 1979 protesters in Busan and Masan fought for a voice in a system that tried to silence them. Forty-seven years later, that voice has transformed into a powerful, sometimes volatile, public opinion that is demanding accountability from the highest levels of government and a seat at the table of global powers. In Korea, the past is never truly dead; it is merely waiting for the next poll to prove its relevance.
