South Korea’s High-Stakes Political Warfare: Special Counsel, Judicial Reform, and the Battle Over a $600 Billion Budget

November 09, 2026

Inside the National Assembly in Yeouido—the island district in Seoul that serves as the beating heart of South Korean politics—the atmosphere on November 8 felt less like a legislative session and more like a battleground. Within hours of a landmark vote, the parliament descended into an all-out partisan war with no clear exit strategy.

The immediate spark was the passage of the "Fabricated Prosecution" Special Counsel Bill. Driven by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the legislation aims to launch a sweeping investigation into alleged prosecutorial abuse and targeted frame-ups carried out during previous years. But as the bill cleared the floor, the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) exploded in fury, taking their fight out of the chamber and onto the streets, calling the measure a partisan shield designed to protect current President Lee Jae-myung.

This explosive clash is not taking place in a vacuum. It has immediately spilled over into every major legislative priority on the calendar: a massive 821 trillion KRW (about $600 billion) national budget review, a contested 162 trillion KRW (about $120 billion) tech and energy fund, and the high-stakes nomination of a new Minister of Justice. For observers watching South Korea from abroad, understanding this clash requires looking past the daily partisan rhetoric and examining the deep-seated war over the country’s judicial system and economic future.

The "Fabricated Prosecution" Special Counsel: A $28 Million Reckoning with the Past

The passage of the Special Counsel Bill by a 167-vote margin marked a decisive escalation in South Korea's ongoing saga of prosecutorial reform. The legislation authorizes a mammoth special investigation team—the largest in South Korean history—backed by a budget of 38 billion KRW (about $28 million) and a staff of up to 309 personnel, surpassing even the historic special counsel teams that investigated past presidential scandals.

To understand why the ruling party pushed so aggressively for this team, one has to understand the unique power historically held by South Korea's prosecution service. Unlike in many Western democracies, Korean prosecutors traditionally held exclusive power over both criminal investigations and indictments, giving them immense leverage over political figures. During the tenure of former prosecutor turned president Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was later impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—the prosecution faced widespread criticism for weaponizing political investigations and selectively orchestrating indictments against rivals while turning a blind eye to insider corruption.

For the ruling DPK, the newly passed special counsel is a long-overdue mechanism to clean house, restore public trust in the judiciary, and hold accountable those who abused prosecutorial discretion. The vast size of the 309-member team reflects what proponents view as a deep web of legacy misconduct that requires systematic unearthing.

Predictably, the opposition PPP reacted with fierce resistance. PPP leader Han Dong-hoon immediately denounced the bill as a "privilege bill," arguing that half of the cases slated for investigation involve figures tied to the current presidency. PPP lawmaker Jung Jeom-sig specifically condemned the inclusion of high-profile cases like the West Sea shooting incident, framing the special counsel as a political vendetta against prosecutors who simply did their job under previous administrations. Rather than debating legal specifics inside committee rooms, opposition lawmakers rallied outside parliament, mobilization efforts reaching into traditional conservative strongholds like the Daegu and North Gyeongsang (TK) regions.

The DPK quickly dismissed these street protests as a transparent attempt to protect "political prosecutors." From the ruling party’s perspective, the conservative opposition is terrified of what impartial investigators will uncover when they dig into how evidence was gathered and indictments were structured during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency.

A Civilian Reformer at Justice: The Clash Over Lee Yong-chul’s Nomination

As if the special counsel fight were not enough to paralyze parliament, President Lee Jae-myung’s nomination of Lee Yong-chul as the new Minister of Justice injected fresh fuel into the fire. The announcement came alongside a broader presidential staff reshuffle, prompting an immediate barrage of criticism from conservative ranks.

Opposition figures immediately branded the appointment as a calculated move to manipulate high-profile ongoing legal cases, including the long-running Daejang-dong land development trial. Senior PPP figures resorted to inflammatory rhetoric, accusing the nominee of being a political proxy and labeling the nomination a ploy to undermine the courts.

However, a closer look at Lee Yong-chul’s background paints a very different picture. A legal veteran with 30 years of legal experience, Lee also served as the head of the Defense Acquisition Program Administration, demonstrating substantial executive experience in managing complex state bureaucracy. The DPK has strongly defended his nomination, highlighting that dismantling entrenched prosecutor cartels requires a decisive legal expert from outside the traditional prosecution hierarchy.

For decades, the Ministry of Justice was heavily staffed by active or former senior prosecutors, creating an insular culture where the ministry often served the institutional interests of prosecutors rather than providing democratic oversight over them. Appointing a civilian legal veteran aligns with the long-held objective—pursued since the previous administration of Moon Jae-in—to separate investigative duties from prosecutorial powers and strip the Justice Ministry of its prosecutor-centric bias.

The DPK has warned the opposition to stop fabricating conspiracy theories around standard cabinet appointments. In the ruling party's view, the PPP’s immediate attack on the nominee before confirmation hearings even begin demonstrates an opposition locked in a defensive panic, fearful that systemic judicial reform will finally become reality.

War Over the $600 Billion Budget: The $120 Billion Future Fund

While headlines remain focused on judicial fights, an equally consequential battle is playing out inside the Assembly’s Special Committee on Budget and Accounts, which kicked off its departmental reviews for the upcoming fiscal year.

At stake is the government's proposed 821 trillion KRW (about $600 billion) national budget. The central lightning rod is the newly created 162 trillion KRW (about $120 billion) Future Response Fund. Designed by the Lee administration to finance strategic national priorities—such as artificial intelligence (AI), advanced biotechnology, and the transition to clean energy—the fund is intended to buffer South Korea's export-reliant economy against global supply chain disruptions and economic volatility.

The opposition PPP has launched a scorched-earth campaign against the fund, labeling it a "presidential slush fund" and threatening deep budget cuts. Opposition leaders argue that the massive allocation represents fiscal recklessness and government overreach.

The DPK counters that blocking these investments during a delicate economic period—marked by market volatility and intense global competition in semiconductor and green tech sectors—is height of political irresponsibility. Depriving the state of investment capital today, ruling party lawmakers argue, will leave South Korea’s future industries stranded.

The budget fight has also triggered friction with local governments. Regional assemblies and municipal leaders have raised alarms that prioritizing national strategic funds could squeeze local shared tax allocations, demanding that roughly 63 trillion KRW (about $46 billion) in regional fiscal support be protected. Meanwhile, when the administration proposed using portions of the Future Fund to modernize aging equipment and improve working conditions for local firefighters, opposition lawmakers dismissed the plan as a political distraction intended to divert attention from controversial statements made by senior DPK lawmakers. What should be routine policy debates over equipment and local funding have transformed into leverage points in a larger ideological war.

Filibusters and Street Rallies: Parliamentary Gridlock Spills Outside

Beyond the budget and cabinet appointments, a long queue of essential reform bills is now caught in the crossfire. The ruling DPK has signaled its intent to push through key legislative measures before the current regular parliamentary session ends, including the Democratic Merit Act and a trio of housing supply bills aimed at stabilizing urban real estate markets.

In response, the PPP is preparing to resort to endless filibusters—unlimited parliamentary debates designed to stall legislation indefinitely. While filibusters are a legal mechanism for minority parties to express dissent, using them systematically across multiple routine and reform bills threatens to grind normal legislative operations to a complete halt.

The standoff reached a dramatic peak as conservative lawmakers expanded their protests beyond the National Assembly grounds, holding public rallies where some speakers openly raised the prospect of impeaching President Lee Jae-myung. The escalation drew a furious response from the ruling party.

"The People Power Party has lost its mind to be talking about impeaching President Lee," said DPK Representative Han Byeong-do. "What audacity is this from a party that brought down constitutional order under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol?"

Han’s sharp rebuke underscores the core political reality in Seoul today: the ruling party views the conservative opposition as an unrepentant force trying to destabilize a democratically elected administration, while the opposition sees itself fighting a desperate rearguard action against systemic reforms that threaten its political base.

What to Watch Next in Seoul

As the year-end legislative deadline approaches, South Korea’s political calendar is packed with critical junctures that will determine whether the political system can function or remain paralyzed:

  • Special Counsel Appointments and First Subpoenas: Watch for the formal selection of the special prosecutors and the announcement of their initial investigative scope. Early subpoenas issued to former prosecutors or officials tied to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure will immediately trigger another wave of political skirmishes.
  • Justice Minister Confirmation Hearings: Nominee Lee Yong-chul’s upcoming parliamentary hearing will be a brutal test. Pay attention to whether the PPP attempts to boycott the session or focuses its questioning on specific ongoing trials versus broader ministry oversight.
  • The Dec. 2 Budget Deadline: The legal deadline for passing the 821 trillion KRW budget is fast approaching. Watch whether the DPK uses its parliamentary majority to pass the budget and the 162 trillion KRW Future Fund unilaterally if negotiations remain deadlocked, or if a last-minute compromise on local tax distribution is reached.
  • Filibuster Marathons: As the DPK brings housing and reform bills to the floor, observe how long the PPP can sustain its filibusters and whether public frustration over stalled housing legislation pushes either side back to the negotiating table.
