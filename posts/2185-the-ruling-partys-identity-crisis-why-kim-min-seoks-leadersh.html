The Ruling Party’s Identity Crisis: Why Kim Min-seok’s Leadership is Cracking

November 10, 2026

On a recent afternoon in Seoul, a strange silence fell over the headquarters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s current ruling party. Kim Min-seok, the party’s chairman, abruptly canceled his entire public schedule. He vanished from a planned YouTube appearance, skipped a scheduled luncheon, and, most notably, was absent from a general meeting of party lawmakers where the very future of the party’s legislative agenda was being debated. In the high-stakes world of Korean politics, a party leader going "dark" without explanation is rarely a sign of peace; it is usually a sign of a house on fire.

The rumors began immediately. Some whispered of a secret, desperate meeting with Jung Chung-rae, a powerful former member of the party’s Supreme Council and a frequent internal rival. Others pointed toward the Blue House—the executive office of President Lee Jae-myung. The prevailing theory among political insiders is that a rift has opened between the party leadership and the presidency. Kim Min-seok, once seen as the bridge between the grassroots and the administration, now appears to be a leader who has lost control of his own ranks and, perhaps more dangerously, the confidence of the President.

This disappearance is a symptom of a much larger malaise. Just 100 days into his tenure, Kim Min-seok is facing a catastrophic collapse in public support. Rather than delivering the "strong, clear message" he promised, the DPK under his watch has become mired in internal scandals, policy flip-flops, and a perceived subservience to the executive branch that has left voters cold. For a ruling party preparing for the next general election, the current trajectory is not just a dip; it is a dive toward a political graveyard.

The Mathematics of a Political Collapse

When Kim Min-seok took the reins of the Democratic Party, he made a bold, perhaps reckless, public pledge: he would raise the party’s approval ratings by at least 10 percentage points within his first 100 days. In the volatile landscape of South Korean public opinion, such a promise was designed to project confidence. Instead, it has become a millstone around his neck.

According to recent data from Realmeter, a prominent South Korean polling agency, the reality has been the exact opposite of Kim’s goal. Just before the party convention that saw him rise to power, the DPK enjoyed a healthy approval rating of 47.9%. By the first week of November, that number had plummeted to 36.5%. This 11.4 percentage point drop represents a massive evaporation of the party’s base. To even reach his original goal now, Kim would need to engineer a miracle bounce of over 21 points to hit 57.9%—a figure that is mathematically and politically improbable in the current climate.

The warning signs are even more dire when looking at "negative" support. In a separate survey by Research View, voters were asked which party they would "absolutely not vote for" in the upcoming general election. The DPK topped this list at 50.6%, significantly higher than the 34% recorded by the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition. This is a staggering irony: while 57.7% of the public says they want to "judge" or "punish" the current government (a common sentiment in mid-term cycles), they simultaneously view the ruling party itself with such distaste that they cannot see it as a viable vessel for their hopes.

The DPK is currently suffering from what analysts call a "hollowed-out" base. The party has increasingly become dominated by "hardline pro-Lee" factions—those fiercely loyal to President Lee Jae-myung—while alienating moderate youth and institutional liberals. By failing to address internal risks when they first appeared, the leadership allowed the "wound to rot," leading to the systemic instability we see today.

Self-Inflicted Wounds and the "Shincheonji" Trap

In politics, external attacks are expected. It is the self-inflicted wounds, however, that are often fatal. For Kim Min-seok, the most baffling of these has been the "Shincheonji" controversy. Shincheonji is a controversial religious movement in South Korea, often described by critics as a cult, which gained international notoriety during the COVID-19 pandemic. In Korean politics, any perceived link to the group is toxic.

The controversy did not start with an opposition attack. It began internally. During the struggle for party dominance, Kim Min-seok reportedly leveraged accusations involving the group to sideline his rival, Jung Chung-rae. It was a tactical move intended to secure his own position, but it backfired spectacularly. By bringing the "Shincheonji" name into the party’s internal discourse, Kim effectively dug a grave for his opponent but ended up falling into it himself. The party is now trapped in a cycle of explanations and denials that have nothing to do with the "civil livelihood" (min-saeng) issues—like inflation and housing—that voters actually care about.

"Chairman Kim Min-seok didn't just dig a grave; he created a massive sinkhole that is swallowing the entire party," says Lee Ji-eun, a vocal critic within the party. "And while the party sinks, there are ten other lawmakers standing around the edge with hand trowels, digging even deeper to protect their own small patches of turf."

This "sinkhole" metaphor has resonated with the public. It describes a leadership that is so focused on internal power struggles and "political trading" with rivals like Jung Chung-rae that it has lost the ability to govern. The absence of the Chairman from key meetings is seen not as a tactical retreat, but as the behavior of a leader who simply does not know how to climb out of the hole he created.

A Party of Rubber Stamps and Missed Reforms

Beyond the scandals, there is a growing sense that the DPK has lost its identity as an independent legislative body. Under the current administration, the party has increasingly been criticized for acting as a "rubber stamp" for the Blue House. This was most evident during a recent internal meeting where Chun Joon-ho, the deputy floor leader, publicly lashed out at fellow lawmakers for making "uncoordinated" comments about tax reform.

The tax in question is the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (known in Korea as Jongbudu-se), a highly sensitive wealth tax on property owners. When individual DPK lawmakers suggested easing the tax to appeal to middle-class voters, the leadership—fearing the displeasure of President Lee Jae-myung—immediately moved to silence them. This lack of policy coordination suggests a party that is more afraid of the President’s "mood" than it is concerned with sound fiscal policy. The perception is that Kim Min-seok was summoned to the Blue House specifically because he failed to keep his lawmakers in line, further damaging his image as a leader of equal standing to the executive.

Furthermore, the party is facing accusations of "reform fatigue." At the start of his term, Kim promised to be aggressive in judicial and institutional reform. He specifically targeted Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, calling for his resignation and promising a full parliamentary investigation into allegations of misappropriated funds. However, as the months passed, these promises evaporated. No roadmap was presented, and no investigation was launched.

For many party members, this feels like a repeat of past failures. They refer to it as "Lee Nak-yon Season 2." Lee Nak-yon, a former party leader and Prime Minister, was often criticized for his "grave and cautious" approach, which many felt led to the party’s eventual loss of power to the previous conservative administration before the current President Lee Jae-myung took office. By hesitating at the moment of decision, Kim Min-seok is being accused of the same paralysis.

The November Deadline and the Path to Reshuffle

The Democratic Party is now approaching a hard deadline. November 24 marks Kim Min-seok’s 100th day in office, and the calls for a "total leadership reshuffle" are growing louder. The internal consensus is shifting: if the approval ratings do not recover and the leadership cannot provide a clear path forward, Kim may have no choice but to resign to protect the party’s chances in the upcoming general election.

There are already alternative movements forming. Some reformers are calling for a "National Innovation Tour," led by figures like former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook. The idea is to bypass the stalled central leadership and go directly to the "grassroots" members to reignite the party's reformist energy. These figures argue that the DPK cannot survive as a mere shield for the President; it must reclaim its role as a party that listens to the people's concerns regarding the economy, local development, and judicial fairness.

What should observers watch for next? The key indicators will be:

  • The 100-Day Performance Review: On or around November 24, expect a wave of internal criticism that will determine if Kim Min-seok can maintain his seat.
  • The "Choe Kang-wook" Variable: Watch if outside reformist figures begin to draw larger crowds than the official party leadership, signaling a shift in the party's power center.
  • Blue House Relations: Any further "disappearances" or sudden summons to see President Lee Jae-myung will indicate that the friction between the party and the presidency has reached a breaking point.

For the DPK, the stakes could not be higher. If they cannot fix their leadership crisis, they risk entering the next election not as a dominant ruling force, but as a fractured organization that the public has already decided to "absolutely not vote for." In the end, the most dangerous thing for a political party isn't a strong opposition—it's the silence of a leader who has run out of things to say.
