South Korea’s Live Commerce Dilemma: High Volume, Squeezed Margins, and the Search for Global Growth

November 06, 2026

As the late-year chill settles over South Korea, the country's retail giants and digital platforms are ramping up their engines for the golden quarter of shopping. In Seoul's corporate headquarters and tech hubs, studio lights stay on late into the night. Presenters pitch everything from high-end cosmetics to gaming hardware directly to smartphones across the nation. Yet beneath the bright banners and live-stream countdowns, South Korea’s live commerce market is undergoing a quiet, grueling recalibration.

South Korea has long served as a global testbed for mobile retail. The country boasts near-universal smartphone penetration, dense urban logistics, and a population comfortable buying groceries or luxury goods via messaging apps. But recent third-quarter financial earnings and market moves reveal a stark split. While platform engagement and gross sales figures continue to reach record heights, the cost of acquiring customers, building infrastructure, and battling regulatory scrutiny is testing the limits of profitability. As Korean platforms attempt to turn live video into hard revenue, they are discovering that scaling top-line sales is far easier than maintaining healthy bottom-line margins.

The Profitability Paradox: High Volume vs. Squeezed Margins

The latest third-quarter financial disclosures highlight how differently domestic platforms are handling this transition. Tech powerhouse Kakao—the operator of KakaoTalk, South Korea’s ubiquitous messaging app used by over 90% of the population—reported a solid operating profit of 240.3 billion won (approximately $175 million), up 7% year-over-year. The core driver of this growth was its "TalkBiz" segment, which integrates targeted advertising, gift-giving services, and live commerce directly into the chat interface.

Kakao’s Chief Executive Officer, Shina Chung, announced ambitious plans to hit 10 million Monthly Active Users (MAUs) for the company's artificial intelligence services before the end of the year. Kakao's strategy relies on keeping users locked within its ecosystem, leveraging AI to tailor product recommendations directly inside the messaging flow. By embedding shopping features into an app South Koreans already open dozens of times a day, Kakao has managed to expand its commerce business without blowing up its marketing expenditures.

The contrast could not be sharper at CJ OnStyle, the home shopping and digital commerce arm of media conglomerate CJ ENM. While CJ OnStyle reported that its mobile live commerce transaction volume nearly doubled compared to the previous year, its operating profit cratered by 46.8% to just 6.7 billion won (around $4.9 million). The company pointed to elevated spending on future business initiatives as the main culprit. In practice, CJ OnStyle has been pouring money into production infrastructure, celebrity partnerships, and aggressive digital ad campaigns to shift viewers away from traditional cable television and onto its mobile app. The result is a classic retail trap: expanding market footprint while burning through cash.

This dynamic exposes a fundamental challenge facing legacy retail operators. Transforming a traditional home shopping business into a mobile-first live streaming hub requires sustained capital expenditure. If those investments fail to yield long-term customer retention, platforms end up running high-volume, low-margin digital infomercials that enrich creators and production agencies while eroding platform profitability.

Entertainment as Retail: How Creators and Content Drive Sales

As consumer attention spans shrink, traditional sales pitches are losing their punch. South Korean consumers increasingly ignore standard promotional broadcasts in favor of entertainment-driven content led by popular digital creators and micro-influencers.

A striking example occurred on Naver Shopping Live, the live-streaming marketplace operated by Naver, South Korea’s dominant search engine and e-commerce portal. Skincare brand Arosell teamed up with popular internet creator RalRal for a interactive live broadcast. Combining comedic banter, real-time viewer Q&A, and steep limited-time discounts, the stream shot to the number one ranking across all Naver live commerce streams during its broadcast window. The event proved that modern live commerce relies far more on fandom and entertainment value than on traditional product demonstrations.

At the same time, major retail platforms are leaning heavily into specialized, highly targeted streaming events to capture niche audiences during peak shopping seasons. E-commerce giant 11Street (11st)—an established player backed by telecommunications leader SK Telecom—launched its annual "Grand 11st" promotional event by blending AI-powered product recommendations with exclusive broadcast deals. Among its headline events was a live-streamed flash sale for gaming PCs equipped with Emtek's GeForce RTX 50 series graphics cards, designed specifically to capture tech enthusiasts willing to make high-value purchases on short notice. Meanwhile, health supplement manufacturer Koryo Eundan linked Naver’s digital "Today’s Pop-up" feature with live shopping streams to push joint care health products.

For brands and merchants, however, this shift presents a double-edged sword. Partnering with top-tier creators or securing prime slots on major platforms delivers immediate volume, but it also forces sellers into constant price discounting and high commission splits, making long-term merchant viability an ongoing question mark.

Crossing Borders: Short-Form Video and K-Beauty's Global Reach

Faced with a saturated domestic market and sluggish domestic consumer spending, South Korean commerce players are turning their eyes overseas. The primary vector for this international expansion is the integration of short-form video content with instantaneous checkout features.

Global tech players are already setting the pace. ByteDance, the parent company of TikTok, has been refining its ecosystem by embedding direct shopping links into micro-drama videos and short clips. This setup lets users purchase items featured in a 60-second video with a single tap. South Korean talent agencies and content firms are moving quickly to align with this trend. Korean media firm The Eight Entertainment was recently invited as a featured speaker at the "2026 TikTok Live EU Premium Agency Roundtable," highlighting how Korean production techniques and streamer management practices are being exported into European and global live-streaming networks.

Simultaneously, South Korean cosmetics and lifestyle products are finding rapid growth in non-traditional export markets via cross-border live streaming. On Shopee, Southeast Asia’s dominant e-commerce platform, orders fulfilled by South Korean sellers shipping into Brazil jumped by 212% year-over-year. By leveraging local influencers in Latin America to host streams featuring K-beauty products, Korean merchants are overcoming traditional distribution and language barriers.

For South Korean commerce platforms, the future battleground is clear. Domestic success is no longer enough; success depends on building seamless infrastructure that converts international viewers into paying customers on foreign platforms.

Regulatory Cracks and Community Balance: Coupang's Fines and Traditional Merchants

Beyond sales metrics and global strategy, South Korea’s e-commerce landscape faces intensifying legal and social pressure. The rapid growth of dominant digital platforms has sparked fierce debates over market concentration, data privacy, and ethical governance.

A recent court ruling delivered a major blow to Coupang, South Korea’s largest e-commerce platform, often dubbed the "Amazon of South Korea." The court upheld the enforceability of an administrative fine exceeding 400 billion won (approximately $290 million) connected to regulatory enforcement and consumer privacy controversies. The massive penalty serves as a stark warning to the tech sector: hyper-efficient delivery networks and dominant market share do not insulate companies from heavy regulatory oversight when compliance and consumer data security fall short.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, government agencies and retail conglomerates are attempting to bring smaller, brick-and-mortar merchants into the digital fold. Retail giant E-Mart recently partnered with the traditional market in Andong, North Gyeongsang Province, to host cooperative live commerce streams aimed at selling regional food products nationwide. Similarly, the regional office of the Ministry of Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs) and Startups in Gyeongsangnam-do province sponsored the "Donghaeng" (Togetherness) Festival, designed to train local business owners in basic streaming techniques.

While these win-win initiatives earn public relations goodwill, industry insiders remain skeptical about whether public-sector live streams can offer permanent relief to small merchants. Without ongoing marketing support and digital management training, one-off streaming events rarely generate predictable, repeatable revenue for small traditional vendors competing against venture-backed digital brands.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters the final stretch of the year-end shopping rush, the live commerce ecosystem sits at a critical turning point. The market is shifting away from rapid user acquisition toward cold financial discipline. Moving forward, several key signals will reveal where the market is headed:

  • Margin Recovery vs. Investment Spending: Watch whether legacy home shopping channels like CJ OnStyle can rein in mobile acquisition costs and improve operating margins in upcoming quarterly reports, or if tech-native giants like Kakao will continue eating away at their market share.
  • The Speed of AI Commerce Integration: Track how effectively Kakao hits its target of 10 million AI service users by year-end, and whether personalized AI shopping assistants translate into higher average order values across domestic storefronts.
  • Cross-Border Short-Form Conversions: Observe whether Korean sellers can sustain high sales growth in emerging markets like Latin America via TikTok and Shopee, and whether domestic platforms can launch their own international streaming tools.
  • Regulatory Enforcement and Platform Accountability: Monitor how Coupang handles its regulatory hurdles and whether South Korea's antitrust regulators introduce stricter compliance frameworks for live commerce operators and data protection.
