Why South Korea's Ruling Party Is Bleeding Support: Inside the Progressive Civil War

October 05, 2026

For months, supporters of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party watched in disbelief as an administration that once enjoyed comfortable public approval ratings above 60 percent saw those numbers crater into the 30s. To the casual observer tracking South Korea's fast-paced political landscape from abroad, a collapse of this scale looks like standard voter fickleness. But inside the party machinery and across progressive media circles, the rot was visible months ago, festering around the time of the June 3 local elections.

The plunge exposes a deeper, more bitter factional struggle within the liberal camp. The party's core grassroots base—the fiercely loyal, highly mobilized members who kept the movement alive through decades of opposition—feel alienated, marginalized by a new wave of opportunistic insiders. Understanding this crisis requires looking past the ballot box victories and examining a fractured party at war with its own identity.

The June Local Elections and the Rise of Factional Opportunism

On paper, the June 3, 2026 local elections were a sweeping triumph for the Democratic Party, which secured 12 out of 16 major metropolitan mayoral and gubernatorial seats nationwide. Yet, in a bitter political paradox, this electoral dominance coincided with a steep decline in President Lee Jae-myung’s governing momentum. While the party won seats, it lost its soul.

Under the previous party leadership of Jung Chung-rae, the progressives maintained a disciplined, reform-minded front. But following shifts in the party apparatus, that leadership gave way to what critics within the base call the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction—derisively labeled "parasites of Lee’s name." During the local election candidate nomination process, these rising figures allegedly traded on President Lee's name to push through controversial, top-down picks that baffled local party members. Strategic missteps in Seoul, Busan, and Pyeongtaek sowed deep distrust, signaling to the party's bedrock supporters that factional self-interest was overriding democratic accountability.

A 30 percent approval rating is not merely a dip into the moderate pool; it is a warning sign that the core base is staying home. Blaming the loss of centrist voters ignores the foundational truth of South Korean progressive politics: a party cannot win by alienating the people who built it.

The Prosecution Reform Milestone and the Leadership Vacuum

To understand what the party's base is mourning, one must look at what Jung Chung-rae actually delivered. Prosecution reform has long been the holy grail of South Korea's progressive movement—an existential crusade to dismantle an overreaching and historically politicized investigative apparatus.

Earlier in 2026, overcoming fierce internal resistance from opportunistic factions, Jung’s leadership spearheaded a historic legislative overhaul. This culminated in the landmark removal of Article 198 of the Criminal Procedure Act, stripping prosecutors of secondary investigative rights—the first structural shift of its kind since the law was enacted in 1954. By October 2026, this momentum birthed a completely restructured system separating investigative and indictment powers through the launch of dedicated public indictment and major crime investigation agencies.

Jung delivered the structural reforms the base demanded. Yet, rather than building upon this historic legislative legacy, the current party leadership has sidelined reformist voices in favor of internal political maneuvering. Party members are not nostalgic for Jung simply because of the past; they miss the uncompromising leadership that actually delivered structural change when it mattered.

The Marginalization of Progressive Icons and Self-Destructive Infighting

The fracturing of the Democratic Party extends beyond parliamentary politics into the broader progressive ecosystem. Recently, prominent progressive figures like You Si-min—a legendary intellectual, former minister, and spiritual successor to the late liberal icon President Roh Moo-hyun—have faced coordinated campaigns aimed at silencing their critiques of the current administration.

When ruling party lawmakers publicly pressure independent commentators to fall in line, they misread the psychology of the movement. You Si-min is not a hired political consultant; he is a living symbol of the progressive movement’s conscience. Attempting to isolate him is a self-defeating maneuver that only accelerates the alienation of the party's core base.

As one prominent progressive metaphor goes, politics is a contest between "stones" (opportunistic newcomers trying to displace the foundation) and "rocks" (the enduring party members who form the bedrock). Current party strategists are obsessed with capturing elusive centrist voters by sanding down the party's ideological edges. In doing so, they are discarding the rocks and wondering why the house is collapsing.

What to Watch Next

The current crisis in South Korea's ruling camp is entering a critical phase. As public discontent registers clearly in opinion polls, several key developments will determine whether the progressives can steady the ship ahead of future legislative battles:

  • Grassroots Mobilization: Watch whether rank-and-file party members heed calls to stay within the party and fight for internal democracy, or if frustration boils over into mass defections and third-party organizing.
  • The Fate of Reform Legislation: Keep an eye on how the newly launched investigative agencies and restructured prosecution systems actually function on the ground, and whether the administration tries to water down their mandate to appease moderates.
  • Intra-Party Power Struggles: Monitor relations between President Lee Jae-myung's inner circle and the ousted reformist faction led by figures like Jung Chung-rae. If the leadership continues to marginalize core ideologues, the factional split could become permanent, permanently crippling the progressive majority.

South Korea's progressive movement was built on sacrifice, institutional reform, and grassroots solidarity. As the party grapples with its plunging numbers, the leadership faces a stark choice: respect the bedrock supporters who built the party, or watch a historic political coalition crumble from within.
