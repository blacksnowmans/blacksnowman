A 'Pack of Hyenas': How a Leaked Telegram Message Sparked a Media Feud Inside South Korea's Ruling Party

November 12, 2026

In the carpeted hallways outside meeting rooms in South Korea's National Assembly, press photographers and video journalists routinely wait for hours. They squat on tiled floors with heavy telephoto lenses, monitoring every doorway in hopes of capturing a subtle facial twitch, a whispered exchange, or a tense departure between politicians. For the press corps, it is standard political photojournalism. But to Choi Min-hee, a high-ranking Supreme Council member of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea, those waiting cameras looked like something altogether different.

Taking a quick photo of the waiting reporters, Choi uploaded the image into a private Telegram group chat filled with her political supporters. Along with the picture, she attached a scathing caption: she called the journalists a "pack of hyenas" who were lurking in the halls strictly to shoot footage meant to "stir up internal division" within the party.

When the leaked message burst out of the private chat room and into national news headlines, it triggered a week-long firestorm that exposed deep-seated friction between South Korea’s ruling political establishment and the press. It also laid bare an escalating debate inside the ruling party itself over how lawmakers communicate with their base—and whether hyper-partisan digital platforms are replacing traditional journalism as the preferred arena for political influence.

From Private Chatrooms to Public Outrage: The "Hyena" Remark

To understand how a single comment in a Telegram chat caused a major political scandal, one must understand the unique mechanics of South Korean political organizing. Lawmakers and party leaders frequently maintain private, highly curated messaging channels on platforms like Telegram or KakaoTalk with thousands of enthusiastic supporters. These digital hubs function as virtual echo chambers where politicians share behind-the-scenes thoughts, rally activists, and shape partisan messaging away from the public eye.

When Choi uploaded the photograph of the press corps to her supporter channel, her framing went beyond routine political frustration. By describing journalists doing basic stakeout work as animals hunting for discord, she effectively told her core supporters that mainstream reporting on the party was fundamentally malicious.

The controversy compound almost immediately when the message leaked to opposition lawmakers and rival media outlets. Choi’s initial response was not an apology, but anger toward the person inside the private room who had exposed her words. She publicly expressed frustration that an insider had taken a screenshot and shared it outside the group, framing the breach of privacy as the primary problem.

By blaming an internal leaker rather than addressing the substance of her insult, Choi amplified public backlash. Critics pointed out that a prominent figure in the ruling party—especially one who sits on its highest executive decision-making body, the Supreme Council—cannot disown remarks made to thousands of followers simply because they were meant to stay behind closed doors.

Press Associations Threaten Legal Action as Hostility Escalates

The reaction from South Korea's media organizations was swift and furious. The Korea Press Photographers Association and the Korea Video Journalists Association issued joint statements strongly condemning Choi's remarks as a direct insult to working journalists and an attempt to delegitimize public scrutiny.

Press representatives gathered at the Sotonggwan, the official press center inside the National Assembly, to hold protest rallies. They argued that capturing politicians' movements and interactions in public corridors is a fundamental duty of democratic journalism, not a malicious conspiracy to sow political discord.

"Calling journalists a pack of hyenas frame standard newsgathering as an act of political sabotage," the photographers' association declared, warning that the remark crossed from political friction into outright defamation and character assassination.

Going beyond press releases, the association announced it was formally reviewing legal action against Choi for defamation and infringement of personal rights. The threat of lawsuit elevated the dispute from a routine political gaffe into a potential legal battle over the rights of journalists covering public officials inside government facilities.

Internal Rifts and YouTube Partisanship: Criticism from Within the Democratic Party

While the opposition party predictably seized on the scandal, the most damaging blows to Choi came from inside her own political house. The ruling Democratic Party found itself divided over how to respond, with prominent figures raising alarm over the party's deteriorating relationship with the press.

Representative Kim Woo-young, a lawmaker belonging to the party’s dominant faction allied with President Lee Jae-myung, publicly took to social media to reprimand Choi. Kim argued that treating journalists covering the Assembly like scavengers was unacceptable for a ruling party leader.

Kim went a step further, calling out what he described as a dangerous double standard in how some lawmakers view the media landscape:

  • Scorn for traditional media: Lawmakers attack accredited, mainstream court and parliament reporters whenever uncomfortable questions or images emerge.
  • Praise for partisan YouTube channels: The same lawmakers court, defend, and appear on massive partisan YouTube channels that cater exclusively to hard-line loyalists and generate lucrative engagement through outrage.

This internal critique highlighted a growing trend in South Korean politics. Over the past few years, partisan political YouTube channels have gained immense influence, often commanding hundreds of thousands of live viewers and driving the narrative for core party bases. For some politicians, pleasing these partisan online outlets has become more rewarding than answering tough questions from traditional newsrooms.

The feud also brought back into focus Choi’s past history with individual reporters. Critics recalled previous incidents where she called out individual journalists by name on social media—such as prominent political reporter Jang Yoon-sun—effectively signaling to her followers to target those reporters online. For media watchdogs, the "hyena" remark was not an isolated slip of the tongue, but part of a consistent pattern of encouraging online mobs against working journalists.

The Forced Apology, Erased Evidence, and Opposition Attacks

Faced with growing calls for accountability from press unions, legal warnings, and pushback from her own party colleagues, Choi finally stepped before the cameras at the Sotonggwan press center one week after her comments leaked. Standing before the very photojournalists she had insulted, she bowed her head in a formal public apology.

Choi admitted that her language was "excessive and inappropriate," explaining that she had merely shared a "fleeting personal feeling" with supporters during a moment of heightened emotion. However, the atmosphere inside the press center remained icy. Reporters peppered her with sharp questions regarding her underlying views on press freedom and whether her apology was genuine or merely forced by political pressure.

Immediately following the press conference, Choi's team took drastic action to contain further fallout: they abruptly shut down and deleted the Telegram chat room where the original comment had been posted. The chat administrator announced to members that the room was being disbanded because normal operations had become impossible due to continuous leaks.

Critics were quick to characterize the sudden deletion as an attempt to destroy evidence and scrub a politically hazardous space rather than genuinely addressing the culture within her supporter base.

Meanwhile, the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party, used the scandal to attack the broader Democratic Party administration. Opposition leaders, including Representative Jeong Jeom-sig, accused the ruling party of harboring an underlying "media phobia" and treating independent news outlets as enemies whenever reporting did not favor the government.

What to Watch Next

The controversy surrounding Choi Min-hee has brought a temporary pause to her online commentary, but the underlying tensions between South Korea's ruling camp and the media remain unresolved. As the political landscape adjusts, several concrete developments will show whether this scandal leads to lasting changes:

  • Legal decisions by press associations: Watch whether the Korea Press Photographers Association proceeds with its formal defamation filings or considers Choi's public apology sufficient to drop legal proceedings.
  • Ruling party rules on private supporter groups: Keep an eye on whether the Democratic Party leadership introduces official guidelines or codes of conduct for how lawmakers manage private Telegram and KakaoTalk channels with loyalist bases.
  • Shift in YouTube vs. mainstream media strategy: Observe whether ruling party leaders tone down their appearances on hyper-partisan YouTube channels in favor of traditional press briefings as President Lee Jae-myung's administration seeks to maintain broad public appeal.

In South Korea's high-stakes political environment, the camera lens is an inescapable tool of public accountability. When politicians begin viewing those lenses as the eyes of scavengers rather than the eyes of the public, the health of democratic debate inevitably suffers.
