Inside the Democratic Party's Panic: Why South Korea's Ruling Party is Facing Calls for an Emergency Leadership Overhaul

October 20, 2026

Walk the busy streets of Seoul’s financial district or sit quietly in a crowded political lounge in Yeouido, and you can practically feel the anxiety radiating through South Korea's political establishment. For the ruling Democratic Party—the progressive powerhouse currently backing President Lee Jae-myung—the mood has shifted dramatically from post-election euphoria to a quiet, suffocating dread. Behind closed doors, senior lawmakers are warning of an impending wipeout in the upcoming parliamentary elections, pointing to plummeting approval ratings and a string of self-inflicted scandals that threaten to tear the party apart from the inside.

When the ruling party begins openly floating the extreme option of dismantling its own leadership in favor of an emergency committee, it signals that the alarm bells aren't just ringing—they are shattering the glass. To understand why South Korea's progressives are staring down a potential political catastrophe, we have to look past the standard party lines and examine the convergence of sagging poll numbers, a paralyzed leadership, and an ugly, public feud that has exposed deep fractures within the movement.

The Shrinking Lead and the Fear of a Capital Region Collapse

In South Korean politics, the greater Seoul metropolitan area—home to roughly half the nation's population—is the ultimate battlefield. Win the capital region, and you generally win the election. Lose it, and your legislative agenda is dead on arrival. That is precisely why recent internal polling data has sent shockwaves through the Democratic Party's headquarters.

According to recent tracking data, including closely watched surveys by Gallup Korea, the gap between voters supporting the current Lee Jae-myung government (at around 34%) and those favoring the opposition conservative bloc (at 47%) has compressed to just 13 percentage points. For a governing party, this isn't just a warning light; it is a flashing red siren. Historically, South Korean legislative elections show that unless a sitting president maintains an approval rating comfortably above 45%, the ruling party struggles to secure a parliamentary majority. When ratings dip below that threshold, candidates in fiercely contested capital districts tend to fall like autumn leaves.

Senior party veterans have not minced words. In recent reports, prominent lawmakers have warned that if the trajectory continues, the Democratic Party could easily lose its status as the country's primary negotiating bloc in the National Assembly. What makes this panic particularly acute is that the party holds the presidency. Voters handed the progressives power following the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Seok-yeol, tasking them with reforming a fractured state and easing the economic burdens of everyday citizens. Instead, lawmakers on the ground complain that the party is squandering its governing premium, leaving grassroots organizers feeling abandoned and terrified of an electoral backlash.

Leadership Paralysis and the Debate Over an Emergency Council

At the center of this turbulence sits the leadership team headed by Kim Min-seok. Hopes were high when the current leadership took office, but critics argue they have repeatedly fumbled the political narrative, failing to project a clear policy compass or outmaneuver the conservative opposition.

President Lee Jae-myung has repeatedly urged the party to pursue practical, efficient, and swift politics to deliver tangible results for the public. Yet, rank-and-file members argue that the Kim Min-seok leadership has delivered neither pragmatism nor speed, bogging itself down instead in internal friction and sluggish political reflexes. As confidence evaporates, the pressure to dissolve the current leadership and install an Emergency Response Committee (비상대책위원회) has reached a boiling point.

Under Article 112-3 of the Democratic Party's constitution, an emergency committee can be formed if a "state of emergency" arises, such as a majority vacancy among supreme council members. Unlike the conservative People Power Party, whose internal rules set rigid numerical thresholds and strict six-month limits for emergency boards, the Democratic Party relies on a more comprehensive definition of crisis. Practically speaking, forcing out a sitting leadership requires either mass resignations by top officials or a formal declaration of emergency by the party’s Central Committee—a path fraught with bitter internal power struggles, but one that desperate lawmakers increasingly view as their only escape hatch.

A Bitter Feud and the Shadow of the Shincheonji Controversy

As if an eroding voter base weren't enough, the party was dealt another self-inflicted blow by a vicious public feud between lawmaker Park Sun-won and former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook. The dispute erupted into a full-scale legal battle centered around alleged phone conversations regarding the controversial religious sect Shincheonji, which played a notorious role in past national political controversies.

When Choi revealed details of the alleged communication, Park hit back aggressively, filing a defamation complaint and claiming he didn't even have Choi's phone number or any relationship that would prompt such a conversation. However, that defense quickly unraveled as critics pointed out the logical inconsistency of a seasoned national security veteran—who previously served as a senior presidential intelligence secretary and a key official at the National Intelligence Service—claiming ignorance of a prominent political ally's contact information. Adding fuel to the fire, investigators later noted multiple phone numbers registered under Park's name.

The optics are disastrous. Park previously headed the party’s special task force targeting Shincheonji—a weapon the progressives once used effectively against their conservative rivals. For the former head of that task force to be bogged down in a legal war of words with a party colleague over private phone logs looks less like strategic governance and more like political self-harm. Disillusioned party insiders have privately lamented that they feel trapped in a self-destructive cycle of mutual recrimination while external challenges mount.

What to Watch Next: The Call for a Full Party Gathering

With grievances spilling out into the open, party insiders are demanding an immediate, full-scale general meeting of lawmakers (의원총회) to thrash out their differences in the open rather than plotting behind closed doors.

Here is what to watch in the coming weeks:

  • The push for an emergency board: Watch whether rank-and-file lawmakers force a showdown with the Kim Min-seok leadership, and whether enough supreme council members step down to trigger constitutional mechanisms for an emergency committee.
  • The legal fallout of the Park-Choi feud: Monitor how the party handles the disciplinary or legal consequences of the Shincheonji communications scandal, which threatens to drag the party's internal ethics into a messy public trial.
  • Capital region polling shifts: Keep an eye on subsequent independent polling from agencies like Gallup Korea to see if the gap between government supporters and opposition critics widens or continues to squeeze the progressive base.

Power is ultimately tethered to the shifting tides of public sentiment. If the Democratic Party's leadership continues to misread the anxiety of voters in the capital region and prioritizes internal disputes over structural reform, the upcoming legislative cycle could deliver a harsh verdict. In times of crisis, half-measures are a slow form of suicide; how the progressives choose to handle the next few weeks will determine whether they can steady the ship before it hits the rocks.

Without brutal self-reflection and structural renewal, no amount of political maneuvering will win back alienated voters.
