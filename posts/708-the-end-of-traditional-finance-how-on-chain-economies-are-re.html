The End of Traditional Finance: How On-Chain Economies are Rewriting Global Power

October 05, 2026

We are living through a period where the warning that "the end of finance is coming" no longer sounds like hyperbole. With a smartphone in your pocket, global assets can cross borders in a fraction of a second, and the state-monopolized authority to issue currency is being challenged by the rise of on-chain ecosystems. South Korean author Oh Tae-min, a prominent observer of these trends, argues that this is not merely a technological fad. It is a tectonic shift—a massive, unstoppable tide of new financial order and a fundamental redistribution of power that is reshaping our lives.

The Redistribution of Global Financial Power

While money and power are often viewed as moving on the same track, they actually operate in distinct realms. In the emerging financial ecosystem, true power is defined by "visibility" and the regulatory ability to control or sever capital flows at critical moments. Historically, national governments held power by keeping a tight grip on financial data and regulating the entry and exit of foreign capital. Domestic central banks, such as the Bank of Korea, once held absolute sway over interest rates and exchange rates. Today, that autonomy is slipping away.

The center of gravity is shifting away from traditional financial hubs like London or New York toward a borderless, on-chain global market. Much like the Eurodollar market weakened national states fifty years ago, modern dollar-pegged stablecoins are now directly challenging the monetary sovereignty of individual nations. Even if a local regulator, such as the Financial Services Commission (FSC)—South Korea’s top financial regulator—delists a token, if that asset continues to trade on global on-chain markets, the state’s actual control has effectively evaporated.

South Korea finds itself in a precarious position. The nation has been preoccupied with defending its domestic market from external pressures rather than exercising the imagination required to build a global platform. The recent retreat of major conglomerates, such as NXC (the holding company behind gaming giant Nexon), from the crypto exchange market—including selling off stakes in the exchange Korbit and offloading the European exchange Bitstamp—highlights the limitations of large Korean firms. These companies often flee at the first sign of "reputational risk," leaving the market to operate in a gray zone of regulatory friction and instability.

The Bottleneck of Exchange Platforms

While power decentralizes, capital is moving with ruthless efficiency. Protocols like Hyperliquid have achieved unprecedented productivity, with just 11 programmers driving a platform that processes massive volume. The global Decentralized Finance (DeFi) market is projected to reach over $420 billion (roughly 570 trillion won) by 2035. In this environment, the "bottleneck" where money accumulates is the exchange platform.

This is why traditional giants like Samsung, Mirae Asset, and Hana Bank have been eyeing crypto platforms like Dunamu (the operator of Upbit) and Korbit. They fear that if they do not secure the bottleneck where information converges and capital flows, they will be relegated to the status of a "financial colony" in the upcoming machine economy. However, while the EU has moved forward with the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation to impose strict obligations on providers, Korea’s regulatory imagination remains trapped in the practices of a decade-old startup era. The risk is that, just as Netflix colonized the domestic broadcasting industry, we may soon see foreign apps like Robinhood facilitating the trade of Korean stock tokens, leaving the domestic financial system obsolete.

The Standardization War: Ethereum vs. Big Tech

Twelve years ago, when Ethereum first appeared, tech giants dismissed it as something they could easily replicate in a week. They were wrong. Today, the crypto ecosystem has effectively standardized on Ethereum. Aside from specialized exceptions like Polymarket or Hyperliquid, almost all on-chain activity relies on Ethereum compatibility. It has become the "Yahoo of the internet era"—the indispensable infrastructure of the new web.

Why didn't tech giants like Google or Meta dominate this space? It wasn't a lack of desire, but a fear of the "gray zone" risks. If Google had built a platform that enabled decentralized betting on the US presidential election, the US Congress would have dismantled it immediately. The decentralized, permissionless nature of Ethereum allowed it to survive the very controversies that made it too dangerous for Big Tech to touch.

This risk-averse culture is mirrored in Korean boardrooms. Author Oh Tae-min notes that high-ranking executives in the country are often desperate to enter the crypto space, but are paralyzed by the fear of being labeled "scammers" or damaging their reputations. While entrepreneurs in the crypto space are willing to risk everything, the "salaryman executives" of Korea’s conglomerates remain trapped by their corporate hierarchy, effectively benching the nation from the new financial revolution.

The Future: AI and the Machine Economy

The current dominance of large web portals is paradoxical: they thrive because of the "inconvenience of payment." Because it is too tedious for a user to pay 10 won for a single news article, users accept an ad-heavy, portal-dominated system. However, the advent of "Agentic AI"—where AI agents make autonomous, micro-payments on behalf of users—will dismantle these middleman structures. In a machine economy where autonomous drones or cars pay for services via blockchain, the need for centralized platforms will vanish.

For the individual investor, the strategy is clear: stop obsessing over short-term volatility or the "winter" seasons of the crypto market. The real trend is the movement of capital toward the most efficient infrastructure. As nations struggle with aging populations and soaring pension costs, they will inevitably resort to printing currency, further devaluing fiat money. Over a 40-year horizon, holding onto assets that are immune to this inflation—like Bitcoin, with its strictly capped supply of 21 million—is not just an investment; it is a necessity for survival.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on how Korean regulators transition from "punitive, post-hoc enforcement" to setting frameworks for tokenized assets. The speed at which global institutional capital integrates with the Ethereum-based infrastructure will be the final signal that the transition from traditional finance to an on-chain economy is complete.
