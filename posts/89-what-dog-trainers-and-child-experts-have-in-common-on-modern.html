What Dog Trainers and Child Experts Have in Common on Modern Parenting

September 03, 2026

When scrolling through online video platforms, unexpected crossovers sometimes catch the eye, blending worlds that initially seem to have nothing in common. A recent viral video pairing Kang Hyung-wook—often affectionately dubbed South Korea's "Dog President" for his prominent role as a celebrity canine behaviorist—with Choi Min-jun, a well-known South Korean expert on raising sons, did precisely that. At first glance, the premise of intersecting dog training with child-rearing sounds like an odd punchline. Yet, as the conversation unfolds, the parallels are striking, pointing to a fundamental truth about authority, affection, and how we treat living beings.

Raising children, much like training pets, often pushes caregivers to their emotional limits. Frustration bubbles up when things do not go according to plan, accompanied by silent or vocalized questions familiar to parents everywhere: "Why don't they understand how I feel?" or "Why won't they just listen?" This unexpected dialogue between two very different specialists offers a sharp critique of conventional parenting habits, forcing a deep look at what adults routinely overlook in their daily interactions with the younger generation.

The Fine Art of Boundaries and Affection

Both Kang and Choi emphasize a central pitfall: showering a dependent with unconditional indulgence under the guise of pure love is the fastest way to foster unruliness. Society often misinterprets discipline as harshness or rigid control, but the true core of effective guidance lies in maintaining proper distance. Hovering too closely—invading a child's personal space and attempting to micromanage their every move—is just as damaging as emotional neglect or complete permissiveness.

Parents often default to endless verbal explanations, hoping that lecturing will substitute for actual experience. However, children, particularly boys before their verbal and abstract reasoning fully mature, process the world through physical engagement and direct consequences. A hundred iterations of "you shouldn't do that" typically register as mere background noise. True learning occurs when children are permitted to make choices and directly experience the outcomes—whether positive or negative—of their own behavior.

Shifting from Control to Autonomy

As children enter adolescence, household dynamics shift dramatically, often making them feel like entirely different entities to live with. During this volatile stage, a parent's most common error is instilling a sense of helplessness through messages like, "You can't do anything without me." This mindset slowly poisons a child's developing sense of independence.

While strict rule-setting and heavy oversight are necessary during the preschool years, authority must gradually evolve into delegated autonomy once children pass elementary school age. Behind a parent's desperate urge to control often lies their own anxiety rather than the child's actual incapacity. Adults frequently resort to intimidation or micro-management to mask their own vulnerabilities and fear of failure. Accepting a child's developmental clumsiness is not a sign of parental failure, but rather the necessary price of admission for long-term growth.

Furthermore, traditional face-to-face, high-pressure interrogation styles rarely work when trying to communicate with boys. Emotional breakthroughs are far more likely to happen organically when adults sit side-by-side with children—building Lego, playing a video game, or tackling a physical activity together. Sharing a collaborative task and experiencing the tangible reward of mutual effort does far more for self-esteem than any lecture ever could.

Resisting Premature Judgments

Even seasoned counseling professionals like Choi caution against the trap of premature labeling. Adults frequently fall into the habit of permanently boxing children into rigid definitions, assuming that a child's current behavior is an unchangeable trait rather than a temporary state. Categorizing a child with absolute statements cuts off their room to breathe and mature.

Instead of jumping to conclusions, caregivers are encouraged to treat challenging behavior as a hypothesis to be tested—asking "Why did they act this way?" rather than writing them off entirely. Raising a child to adulthood takes decades, a timeline vastly longer than the lifespan of a household pet. This sprawling, unquantifiable period of waiting tests the absolute limits of a parent's patience, which ultimately forms the bedrock of a child's future resilience.

Embracing Deficits as Catalysts for Growth

Modern culture places immense pressure on parents to engineer perfect environments, pushing children toward flawless academic credentials and pristine personalities. Yet, enduring personal growth often stems from navigating imperfections. Experiencing small deficits and weathering mild hardships gives children the motivation to explore the world and develop coping mechanisms independently.

Ultimately, a parent's job is not to live life on a child's behalf, nor is it to smooth away every single obstacle. The role is closer to that of a surf instructor—holding the board steady so the rider can eventually catch the waves on their own terms. When parental anxiety flares up or frustration sets in because a child refuses to conform to expectations, taking a step backward is often the most productive move.

True communication begins the moment we stop treating dependents as personal extensions of ourselves and start recognizing them as autonomous, independent individuals. Cultivating deeper patience and more precise personal boundaries remains the ultimate challenge for anyone guiding the next generation.
