South Korea's Prosecution Reform Faces a Crucial Test as New Agencies Prepare to Launch

September 07, 2026

For decades, South Korea’s powerful prosecution service has operated as a nearly unchecked center of state authority, shaping national politics and wielding an effective monopoly on criminal investigations and indictments. Now, that 78-year-old system is facing its biggest structural overhaul yet. Scheduled for launch on October 2, two new entities—the Prosecution Office, intended to handle solely indictments, and a newly established Major Crimes Investigation Agency—are set to replace the traditional Prosecutors' Office. Yet, as this massive transition approaches, the general public remains largely indifferent, while mainstream media either stokes anxiety about state paralysis or glosses over the institutional details.

To view this shift merely as a technical update to the legal code risks repeating painful history. Critics, including lawmakers like Kim Yong-min of the main opposition Democratic Party of Korea, warn that the current reorganization risks becoming an empty shell—a change in name only that leaves the underlying personnel, budgets, and institutional culture entirely intact.

Why Personnel Retention Threatens to Undermine Reform

The core objective of separating investigation and indictment powers is not just to draw new organizational boundaries, but to break up the vast bureaucratic network and insular culture of the traditional prosecution service. Lawmaker Kim Yong-min has sharply criticized the Ministry of Justice's proposed staffing plan, pointing out that true reform requires a drastic reduction in overall personnel.

Instead, the government's current blueprint plans to absorb nearly 6,000 to 7,000 investigative officers and support staff into the new structures alongside the statutory limit of 2,292 prosecutors. This preservation of manpower carries a familiar historical risk. Critics point to the post-World War I German military, which was restricted to 100,000 troops but bypassed the limitation by commissioning all enlisted personnel as officers, allowing it to rapidly rebuild when the time came. Leaving the core headcount of 2,292 prosecutors untouched while simply changing agency nameplates leaves the door wide open for the old prosecutorial apparatus to reconstitute itself overnight.

While mass layoffs are politically difficult in a democratic society, a fundamental restructuring of this scale demanded significant downsizing. The current legislative proposals, however, lean heavily toward preserving existing staff. Rather than curbing overreaching institutional power, critics argue the plan amounts to a high-tech upgrade for the very bureaucracy it seeks to reform.

New Oversight or Expanded Power? The Controversy Over "Judicial Control"

Adding to concerns about the reform's substance are recent proposals by the Ministry of Justice to establish a "Judicial Control Division" and a dedicated task force to handle joint investigative bodies. Under this framework, if police fail to comply with prosecutorial demands for supplementary investigations, cases could be routed to this new division for disciplinary review.

The terminology itself has sparked intense debate. In democratic theory, judicial control is exercised by independent courts over administrative agencies, not by prosecutors—who frequently style themselves as "quasi-judicial"—over police investigators. Lawmakers and critics argue that giving prosecutors the power to police lower-level investigative agencies inverts proper accountability, allowing an institution that itself requires oversight to instead tighten its grip on others.

Furthermore, newly introduced provisions for "pre-indictment investigations" threaten to grant prosecutors broad discretion to preview cases before formally accepting them. This creates a risk of selective enforcement, where politically sensitive cases can be quietly stalled while others are fast-tracked. With the prosecution retaining its monopoly on arrest warrants, civil society and police agencies have few avenues to challenge delayed or buried cases.

The Legal Elite and the Leadership Dilemma

Questions about the sincerity of the overhaul extend to the leadership candidates slated to head the new Major Crimes Investigation Agency. Prominent names floated for the top posts—such as Kim Kwan-jung, Kim Ji-yong, Lee Yoon-je, and Moon Hong-joo—are predominantly former prosecutors and judges.

Entrusting a specialized agency meant to combat major crimes to traditional legal elites rather than veteran field investigators has drawn accusations of entrenched legal elitism. Critics argue that figures steeped in the hierarchical, prosecutor-centric culture of the past are ill-equipped to foster a horizontal, practical-minded investigative culture. True reform, reform advocates argue, requires looking outside traditional legal circles to ensure the new agencies are not simply old institutions wearing new labels.

Constitutional Reform as the Ultimate Remedy

Many reform advocates argue that legislative adjustments alone will continually run up against institutional resistance and constitutional disputes, making a broader constitutional overhaul essential. Proposals for a sweeping revision of the 1987 constitutional framework aim to permanently delimit prosecutorial power and adapt the state apparatus to a more mature democracy.

Past attempts at piecemeal constitutional reform have frequently bogged down in partisan wrangling. Proponents argue that achieving durable change requires broad political alignment and a direct mandate from the public to restructure governance and address structural overreach across state institutions.

What to Watch Next

As the October 2 launch date approaches, several key developments will determine whether the new system brings genuine accountability or merely cosmetic change:

    Final Staffing Decrees: Watch for any last-minute amendments to the Ministry of Justice's staffing ordinances regarding the transfer of investigative personnel into the new offices. Legislative Oversight: Monitor debates in the National Assembly over the specific powers granted to the newly proposed judicial control and pre-indictment review units. Leadership Appointments: Observe who is ultimately selected to head the Major Crimes Investigation Agency and whether appointments break from traditional prosecutorial backgrounds. Police-Prosecution Relations: Track how jurisdictional boundaries and dispute mechanisms operate between the police and the newly minted agencies during initial high-profile cases.
