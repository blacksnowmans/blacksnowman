The Rhetoric of Resistance: South Korea’s Judicial Battles and the Global Far-Right Parallel

October 26, 2026

Inside the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly, the air is thick with the vocabulary of an existential struggle. On October 25, the usual legislative back-and-forth was replaced by a more visceral form of combat. Jeong Jeom-sig, the floor leader of the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—stood before reporters, his voice rising as he took a sharp gulp of water. He was there to denounce a proposed parliamentary investigation into Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. To Jeong, this wasn't a routine check on judicial power; it was a "bad-faith trick," a "trial run for impeachment," and an "evil" attempt to intimidate the highest court in the land.

This scene is not an isolated incident of partisan bickering. It represents a deepening fissure in Korean democracy, where the language of the street and the rhetoric of the far-right are increasingly migrating into the halls of power. As the government of President Lee Jae-myung—the current leader from the liberal Democratic Party of Korea—seeks to enact judicial reforms and address what it describes as the "eroded fairness" of the previous era, the opposition has retreated into a defensive crouch. This domestic friction is beginning to mirror the populist surges seen across Europe, where mainstream political discourse is being swallowed by the provocative grammar of the far-right.

A War over the Judiciary

The immediate flashpoint is the Democratic Party’s move to review a state audit of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. To understand why this matters, one must look at the current state of the Korean judiciary. The position of Associate Justice has sat vacant for over seven months, a paralysis that has slowed the wheels of justice. The People Power Party views any attempt to scrutinize the Chief Justice as a direct assault on judicial independence. However, the governing Democratic Party argues that the probe is necessary to uncover the remnants of "judicial interference" that allegedly took place during the years when Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was in power.

Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence, left behind a polarized legal landscape. The current Lee Jae-myung administration has framed its agenda as a "restoration of normalcy," aiming to fix the administrative and judicial structures it claims were weaponized by the previous leadership. By labeling the investigation into the Chief Justice as "evil" and a "trick," the PPP is effectively attempting to shut down the conversation before it can begin. Jeong Jeom-sig’s claim that "dismissal [of the probe] is the only answer for the country" suggests a refusal to engage with the actual merits of the audit, choosing instead to frame the issue as a zero-sum battle for survival.

This "fear marketing" is a strategic choice. By framing a standard parliamentary procedure as a precursor to a constitutional crisis, the opposition manages to galvanize its base. Yet, it does so at the cost of public trust. When the main opposition party focuses more on building a "political shield" than on the actual oversight of the executive branch—the primary purpose of a parliamentary audit—the victims are the citizens waiting for a functioning court system.

The Normalization of Extremist Language

The decay of political decorum is perhaps most evident in the recent spat between Lee Jun-seok and Choo Mi-ae. Lee Jun-seok, a prominent lawmaker and the leader of the minor Reform Party, has built a career on high-impact, media-savvy critiques. His latest target was Choo Mi-ae, a veteran heavyweight of the ruling Democratic Party who once served as Justice Minister. Choo, while discussing the need for structural reform in fire service funding, used the dramatic metaphor of "political immolation," stating she would do her job even if she were "burned at the stake."

Lee Jun-seok’s response was swift and biting, accusing her of "provoking the fire services" for the sake of a "political show." He tied her remarks to the previous provincial administration of President Lee Jae-myung (during his time as Governor of Gyeonggi Province), claiming she was merely trying to revive her "Chu-darc" persona—a play on Joan of Arc—to distract from financial mismanagement.

While Choo’s metaphor was certainly provocative, the ensuing debate illustrates a troubling trend: the disappearance of policy in favor of personality. Instead of debating the actual mechanics of fire service budgets or provincial debt, the conversation devolved into a fight over a single word. Lee’s strategy of "logical nitpicking" and "short-form politics" is designed for social media virality, not legislative progress. This style of communication, which prioritizes mockery over substance, shares a disturbing lineage with the communication methods of the global far-right, where the goal is to trigger the public's "medulla oblongata" rather than appeal to their reason.

A Leadership Vacuum and the Scramble for Power

Within the conservative camp, the struggle for a post-Yoon identity is resulting in internal fractures. Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul, recently saw a political revival after being acquitted in an appellate court case. This legal victory has thrust him back into the spotlight as a potential future leader, creating a complex "triangle of tension" between himself, Lee Jun-seok, and Han Dong-hoon, the former interim leader of the PPP.

This internal jockeying has drawn criticism from within the conservative circle. Kim Keun-sik, a former vision strategy chief for the party, described Lee Jun-seok’s behavior—praising Oh Se-hoon while simultaneously sniping at Han Dong-hoon—as "cowardly." The criticism points to a larger problem: the opposition lacks a clear, unified vision to counter the high approval ratings of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Instead of offering policy alternatives, the PPP and its offshoots are stuck in a cycle of factional infighting, waiting for judicial rulings like Oh’s to dictate their political momentum.

Even the leadership of the PPP itself seems to be in a state of "stagnant survival." Jang Dong-hyeok, the current PPP representative, has reached a record for the longest tenure in his position, but critics call him a "master of holding on" rather than a transformative leader. While he recently visited the memorial of a legendary conservative figure to pledge "unity," the reality is a party pulled toward the far-right fringe. When an opposition party becomes more focused on who can attack the government with the most "vivid hostility" rather than who can propose the best path forward, it ceases to be a functional alternative and becomes a vessel for grievance.

The Shadow of the Global Far-Right

South Korea’s domestic political temperature cannot be viewed in a vacuum. Similar forces are tearing at the fabric of European democracies. In France, Marine Le Pen of the National Rally (RN) is making a concentrated push for the presidency, using the slogan of "restoring the rule of law" to mask a deeply divisive agenda. Despite her rising poll numbers, the resistance is massive; over 400,000 citizens recently took to the streets across France, with protesters in Paris and Orleans clashing with police and demanding that "Le Pen go to prison."

Like the Korean opposition, Le Pen uses chaos as a tool. The more the streets are filled with tear gas and tension, the more she can pitch herself as the candidate of "order" and "strong authority." This is a familiar script. It mirrors the rhetoric used by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during his rise to power, where he promised "fairness and common sense" but ultimately presided over a period that many now see as a regression of democratic values.

In Germany, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) is moving further to the extremes. Internal emails recently revealed that Alice Weidel, once considered a moderate face of the party, has aligned herself with the hardline nationalist wing. Despite 63% of Germans supporting continued aid to Ukraine, the AfD continues to push a pro-Russian, isolationist line that fractures national consensus. Similarly, in the UK, Nigel Farage and Reform UK are labeling the current Labour government’s immigration policies as a "betrayal of Brexit," using the language of "delusion" and "treachery" to undermine the legitimacy of the sitting government.

These movements are not just about shifting to the right; they are about the "politics of hate." Instead of addressing the complexities of economic inequality or global instability, these actors find scapegoats. Whether it is the judiciary in Seoul, immigrants in Paris, or "the establishment" in London, the tactic is the same: identify an enemy, use inflammatory language to dehumanize them, and present yourself as the only one capable of "crushing" the threat.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward the end of the year, several key developments will determine if the country can move past this era of high-decibel hostility:

  • The Fate of the Judicial Audit: Watch whether the Democratic Party proceeds with the probe into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. If it does, expect the PPP to escalate its rhetoric to include calls for mass protests, potentially bringing the "street politics" of France to the streets of Seoul.
  • The PPP’s Internal Reorganization: The acquittal of Mayor Oh Se-hoon has changed the math for the conservative party. Watch if a "moderate coalition" can form between Oh and other figures, or if the party continues to drift toward the "hostility-first" strategy favored by its more extreme elements.
  • The Impact of Global Trends: The success or failure of far-right movements in Europe and the upcoming elections in other major democracies will embolden or dampen the spirits of Korea's own hardline factions. Keep an eye on how Korean political influencers—especially on YouTube and social media—adapt the tactics of the European far-right.
  • Public Sentiment on "Attention Politics": As the public grows weary of the "Shorts-style" political spats exemplified by the Lee Jun-seok vs. Choo Mi-ae row, there may be a growing appetite for "boring but effective" policy discourse. Whether the Lee Jae-myung government can successfully pivot the national conversation back to livelihood issues (min-saeng) will be the ultimate test of its resilience.

Democracy is often described as a conversation. When that conversation is replaced by shouts of "evil," "trickery," and "immolation," the democratic process stalls. The challenge for South Korea, and indeed for the world, is to remember that the ultimate safeguard of a nation is not the volume of its politicians’ voices, but the organized discernment of its citizens.
