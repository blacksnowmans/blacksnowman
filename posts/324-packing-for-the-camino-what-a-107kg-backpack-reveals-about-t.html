Packing for the Camino: What a 10.7kg Backpack Reveals About the Santiago Pilgrimage

September 07, 2026

To anyone who has never set foot on the trail, the Camino de Santiago sounds like a romantic stroll across the Spanish countryside. But standing at the starting line of an 800-kilometer (roughly 500 miles) trek after leaving behind a job or a routine life, the reality sets in fast. The very first question you must answer is brutal: What do you actually carry? For travelers heading out on post-career journeys, a pack is never just a bag of travel gear. It is a calculated survival kit for weeks of physical endurance. At 10.7kg (about 23.5 pounds), a backpack holds far more than extra socks and toothbrushes—it holds the literal weight of tomorrow's physical survival.

While daydreamers imagine postcard vistas, experienced hikers know the reality involves throbbing plantar fasciitis and relentless battles with bedbugs. Even K-pop star Lee Su-hyun of the sibling duo AKMU has spoken openly about struggling with foot pain while completing the pilgrimage. Gear on the Camino is not a collection of vacation accessories; it is your final line of biological defense. Looking closely at a seasoned traveler's packing list reveals a great deal about the hidden realities of tackling Spain's most famous historic trail.

Survival Gear: Managing Pack Weight, Footwear, and Trail Safety

The entire enterprise begins with the backpack itself. Choosing a large capacity pack—such as a 68-liter Osprey bag—turns a piece of luggage into a portable home. When veterans call the pack the single most important item, they are not exaggerating; if the bag fails, the trip is over. Keeping the total weight under 20% of body weight is standard practice, making 10.7kg a carefully calibrated threshold for what a human body can handle over dozens of miles a day.

Smart pilgrims also pack a smaller daypack for luggage transfer services, proving that stubbornness is less useful than strategy. Recognizing when to let a van carry your heavy gear is not cheating; it is smart self-care. Meanwhile, trekking poles act as essential third and fourth limbs. Without them, descending steep, rocky inclines with a loaded pack multiplies the stress on knees and ankles exponentially.

Security and hygiene require similar ingenuity. Veteran packers often wrap their belongings inside plastic garbage bags within their packs—not just for waterproofing, but to keep bedbugs from climbing the fabric and to create a rustling sound that deters thieves in shared dormitories. Adding personal mementos like a scallop shell (the traditional symbol of the Camino) or a small bell ensures that gear is both functional and deeply personal. Hostel accommodations along the route have improved over the years, but bedbugs travel via luggage, making nightly vigilance essential to reaching the destination.

Clothing and Personal Care: The Aesthetics of Efficiency

When it comes to wardrobe, less is definitively more. Most seasoned pilgrims bring just three sets of clothing: one long-sleeve/long-pant combination, two short-sleeve/short-pant sets, and a comfortable base layer to sleep in. Layering handles unpredictable weather swings, while specialized items like toe socks (known among hikers for preventing friction blisters) act as a secret weapon against foot injuries.

Toiletries and health supplies double as daily first-aid routines. Vaseline applied generously to the soles of the feet before pulling on socks forms a greasy barrier against blisters. Sunscreen is non-negotiable under the punishing Spanish sun, and a lightweight travel towel dries quickly in shared hostel bathrooms. Earplugs and eye masks are absolute prerequisites for surviving multi-bed dormitories (*albergues*), where the chorus of fellow hikers snoring can easily derail the next day's walk.

In our view, the grueling process of packing is less about gathering items and more about confronting one's own anxieties. Every traveler knows the temptation of tossing in "just in case" items, only to curse those same objects halfway up a mountain. The value of a well-curated pack lies in its reliance on hard-earned experience rather than wishful thinking. The Camino is ultimately an exercise in subtraction—a test of what you can leave behind just as much as what you can carry.

Emergency Rations and Tech: Staying Connected and Fueled

Food logistics on the trail require balancing immediate nutrition with emergency backup. While local cafes and bakeries dot the route, carrying instant meal packs and portable tubes of spicy chili paste (*gochujang*) provides a psychological anchor when remote stretches run dry or local cuisine proves too unfamiliar. Soft collapsible water bottles save space when empty, and chocolate bars offer an instant, morale-boosting calorie spike when fatigue sets in.

Electronics play an equally vital role in modern pilgrimage life. Bone-conduction headphones allow walkers to enjoy music or audiobooks while keeping their ears completely open to approaching traffic or trail hazards. Headlamps are mandatory for pre-dawn departures, letting smart travelers beat the punishing midday heat.

Crucially, the rise of smartphone apps for booking hostel beds has turned mobile phones and power banks into lifelines. Spontaneous walking used to mean finding a bed on a whim, but today's crowded trails make advance digital planning a necessity. A dead battery can mean sleeping on a park bench.

Ultimately, the Camino de Santiago serves as a microcosm of life itself: you shoulder what you can manage, pack the tools you actually need, and move forward at your own pace. The 10.7 kilograms carried across these Spanish paths represent a quiet, stubborn promise to reach the end. For anyone lacing up their boots this season, may the trail bring healthy knees and a quiet mind.
