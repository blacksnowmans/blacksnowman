Judicial Impeachment and the Battle for Reform: Inside South Korea’s October Audit Storm

October 21, 2026

The air inside the National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul’s political heart, is thick with more than just the usual autumn chill. As the annual government audit—a weeks-long marathon of parliamentary scrutiny known as Gukjeong Gamsa—reaches its midpoint, the halls are buzzing with a word that carries the weight of a constitutional sledgehammer: impeachment. On October 20, the political landscape shifted from policy debates to a high-stakes confrontation over the future of the judiciary, the rights of laborers, and the very stability of the current administration.

For an outside observer, the scene might look like standard partisan bickering. But in South Korea, where the memory of presidential removals and sweeping judicial reforms is fresh, these maneuvers represent a fundamental struggle over the separation of powers. At the center of the storm is Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, who now finds himself in the crosshairs of a relentless offensive by the liberal opposition. Meanwhile, the ruling Democratic Party, led by President Lee Jae-myung, is attempting to balance a radical reformist agenda with the pragmatic realities of governing a country facing housing shortages and economic headwinds.

The Judiciary Under Fire: The Impeachment Gambit

The most explosive development of the day came from the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP), a progressive minor party that has positioned itself as the "vanguard" of judicial reform. Representative Shin Jang-sik of the RKP made waves by declaring that the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is not just a possibility, but a necessity. According to Shin, allowing Cho to finish his term would be an "indelible stain on the history of judicial justice."

To understand why this matters, one must look at the role of the Chief Justice in South Korea. The head of the Supreme Court holds immense power over the administration of the courts and the appointment of judges. For the RKP and its supporters, the current judiciary is seen as a bastion of conservative resistance that has failed to purge itself of the legacy of the previous administration. They argue that the courts have been slow to act on "pro-people" reforms and have maintained a biased stance in high-profile political trials.

However, the Democratic Party (DP), South Korea’s current ruling party, finds itself in a delicate "wait-and-see" position. While the DP holds a commanding majority in the National Assembly, its leadership is wary. Kim Min-seok, a top member of the party’s leadership, is reportedly agonized over the decision. If the DP joins the RKP in a formal impeachment motion, it risks a "boomerang effect"—a moderate backlash from voters who might see the move as "legislative tyranny." If the impeachment fails to pass the Constitutional Court, the political fallout could be devastating for President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

On the other side of the aisle, the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, has reacted with fury. They characterize the impeachment drive as an attempt to "tame the judiciary" and a direct assault on the constitutional order. Jung Jum-sik, the PPP’s floor leader, accused the liberal camp of turning impeachment—a tool of last resort—into a common weapon for political warfare. The tension reached a boiling point during the audit of the Daegu High Court, where lawmakers traded shouts over the independence of the bench.

A Labor Dilemma: The Mega-City Act and the 52-Hour Week

While the headlines are dominated by the judicial fight, a quieter but equally significant battle is brewing over the "Special Act on Mega-Regional Special Zones." This bill is a cornerstone of the Lee Jae-myung administration’s plan for balanced regional development. The goal is to create economic hubs outside of Seoul to combat the extreme centralization that has plagued the country for decades.

The friction arises from a "two-track" strategy employed by the Democratic Party. Han Byeong-do, a key DP lawmaker, concurrently introduced two versions of the bill: one that includes exemptions to the 52-hour maximum work week and one that does not. This unusual move highlights a deep rift within the liberal camp. The 52-hour work week is a landmark achievement for South Korean labor, intended to curb the country’s infamous "overwork culture." To many in the labor movement, offering exemptions in these new special zones feels like a betrayal of core values.

The PPP opposition has seized on this, arguing that the 52-hour limit is too rigid for businesses and should be relaxed entirely to boost competitiveness. Meanwhile, the Ministry of Employment and Labor is treading carefully, stating it will coordinate with the National Assembly based on the results of "social dialogue." For the foreign investor or observer, this debate is a bellwether for South Korea’s economic direction. Is the country moving toward a more flexible, business-friendly labor market, or will it double down on worker protections even at the cost of regional growth?

President Lee’s "Blitzkrieg" on Housing and Safety

Amidst the legislative chaos, President Lee Jae-myung used a cabinet meeting to project an image of a government focused on the "bread and butter" issues of the people. Flanked by Prime Minister Han Seong-sook and Chief of Staff Im Kwang-hyun, the President issued a directive for a "housing supply blitzkrieg."

South Korea’s housing market remains one of its most volatile political issues. President Lee’s order to drastically shorten the time from site preparation to groundbreaking is an attempt to reassure a public frustrated by high prices and a perceived lack of supply. He also emphasized the expansion of "high-quality public rental housing," signaling a shift toward more direct government intervention in the market compared to the previous administration under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

President Lee also took a firm stance on industrial safety. After receiving a report from Labor Minister Kim Young-hoon regarding a decrease in workplace fatalities, the President expressed frustration at the National Assembly for stalling legislation that would impose heavier fines on companies responsible for such deaths. "It is suffocating," the President remarked, referring to the legislative gridlock. By framing labor safety as a matter of "respect for life," Lee is attempting to maintain his base of support among workers while portraying the opposition as obstructionist.

The Warning Signs: Polling and Political Friction

Despite the administration's active policy push, the political winds may be shifting. A recent poll by Yeonron-josa Gong-jeong showed the opposition People Power Party leading with 47.3% support, while the ruling Democratic Party trailed at 33.3%. This double-digit gap is a massive alarm bell for the Lee administration.

For the ruling party, these numbers suggest that the public may be weary of constant political warfare and the focus on judicial "settling of scores." The PPP has used this momentum to launch a broad offensive, criticizing the government’s rhetoric on North Korea and dredging up past controversies to question the administration's morality. This shift in public sentiment explains why the DP leadership is so hesitant to pull the trigger on the impeachment of the Chief Justice; they know that one wrong move could alienate the swing voters they need for future elections.

Furthermore, the internal dynamics of the liberal camp are becoming more complex. The Rebuilding Korea Party, led by figures like Hwang Hyeon-seon, is already looking toward next year's local elections, signaling that they will not hesitate to challenge the DP from the left if they feel the ruling party is becoming too cautious. This "healthy tension" (or "fratricidal competition," depending on who you ask) within the liberal bloc will likely define the next year of Korean politics.

Audit Revelations: From Trees to Patents

The government audit also unearthed several specific failures that highlight the gap between political rhetoric and bureaucratic reality. Two findings stood out for their sheer scale of mismanagement:

  • The Pine Wilt Disease Crisis: Lawmakers revealed that 1.77 million trees are currently infected with pine wilt disease. This was described as a "complete failure" of a 40-year-old prevention policy. One lawmaker joked that if the trend continues, the lyrics to the national anthem—which mentions Korea’s sturdy pines—might need to be rewritten.
  • Waste in the Research Sector: It was discovered that government-funded research institutes are holding onto 5,122 "unused patents." Over the past five years, about $5.3 million (7.3 billion KRW) in taxpayer money has been wasted just on maintenance fees for patents that serve no practical purpose.

These revelations serve as a reminder that while the elite in Yeouido argue over constitutional theories and impeachment, the machinery of the state is often failing in mundane but costly ways. For the average citizen, the sight of lawmakers trading insults while millions of trees die and millions of dollars are wasted on dormant patents is a source of deep cynicism.

What to Watch Next

As the National Audit continues, several key markers will determine the direction of South Korean politics in the coming months:

  • The "Post-Audit" Decision: Keep a close eye on the Democratic Party’s internal discussions once the audit concludes. If they decide to move forward with the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, it will signal a "total war" scenario between the executive/legislative branch and the judiciary.
  • The Mega-City Compromise: Watch for which version of the regional development bill gains traction. If the DP drops the labor exemptions to appease its base, it may face criticism for failing to stimulate the economy. If it keeps them, it risks a revolt from labor unions.
  • The 14% Gap: President Lee Jae-myung needs to narrow the polling gap with the PPP. Expect a pivot toward more populist "living standards" policies in the next few weeks as the administration tries to win back moderate voters.
  • Judicial Rulings: Any major court ruling involving political figures will now be viewed through the lens of the impeachment threat. If the courts rule against liberal interests, expect the RKP to ramp up the pressure to an unbearable degree.

In the end, October 20 was a day that showed South Korean democracy at its most vibrant and its most volatile. The stakes are high: the independence of the courts, the rights of the Korean worker, and the political survival of the current administration are all on the table. For the people of South Korea, the hope remains that the dust of Yeouido will eventually settle into policies that make their lives—and their trees—a little more secure.
