Why South Korea's Housing Crisis Just Broke President Lee Jae-myung's Approval Rating

November 14, 2026

For months, the steady climb of Seoul’s apartment prices has served as a slow-burning fuse in South Korean politics. Every weekly data release brought the same grim news for prospective buyers shut out of the market, turning quiet frustration into open anger. Now, that fuse has finally reached the explosives.

According to a Gallup Korea poll, President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating has fallen to 39%, dropping below the psychologically vital 40% threshold for the first time. More critically, his disapproval rating climbed to 50%, creating a painful "dead cross." When asked why they disapproved of the president's performance, respondents gave a single answer above all others: housing policy.

The slip is not merely about a few lost percentage points in a weekly survey. It reflects a growing skepticism among Lee’s core supporters who are beginning to wonder if the administration is simply incapable of taming the country's relentless property market. Compounding the damage, a botched attempt by the ruling party to ease tax burdens was reversed in just four days, leaving voters questioning the government’s competence and consistency.

The 40% Floor Gives Way Under the Weight of Real Estate Discontent

For any South Korean administration, an approval rating of 40% acts as a psychological line in the sand, representing the minimum political momentum needed to govern effectively without facing crippling gridlock. Falling below that line signals a profound shift in public trust.

While some respondents in the Gallup survey cited general economic management as a positive factor, soaring housing costs completely overshadowed those gains. Dissatisfaction with property policy led the list of negative evaluations at 17%. Compounding matters, criticisms regarding government morality ticked upward to 12%. When policy failures are paired with perceived moral or administrative incompetence, a government’s bounce-back ability plummets rapidly.

Nowhere is this fatigue more intense than in Seoul, where apartment prices have risen for an astonishing 92 consecutive weeks. Traditional tools like interest rate hikes and lending curbs have failed to break the public's obsession with owning "one single, high-quality home" in the capital. Instead, repeated regulations have fostered a paralyzing fear of missing out permanently, convincing prospective buyers that if they do not purchase a home right now, they never will.

💡 At a Glance: The November Poll & Housing Pressures

  • President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating drops to 39%, while disapproval hits 50%.
  • Housing policy ranks as the number one reason for negative evaluations at 17%.
  • Policy U-turns on property taxes expose widening leadership rifts within the ruling camp.
  • Seoul's apartment prices notch 92 consecutive weeks of increases, alienating metropolitan voters.

A Four-Day Policy U-Turn Exposes Ruling Party Disarray

The political fallout was accelerated by a self-inflicted wound within the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main center-left party. Seeking to court middle-class voters ahead of upcoming political battles, the party’s floor leadership floated a plan to raise the comprehensive real estate holding tax exemption threshold for single homeowners to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million).

The initiative was designed as an olive branch to taxpayers feeling squeezed by soaring property valuations. Yet, facing immediate pushback from the presidential office and party hardliners who feared abandoning core tax principles, the plan was unceremoniously scrapped just four days later.

The abrupt reversal made the administration look chaotic and indecisive. Critics quickly labeled the episode a failure of basic governance, arguing that the ruling party threw out a major tax adjustment without securing internal consensus first. For everyday citizens trying to navigate a volatile market, watching the government debate and retract major tax changes in less than a week reinforced a damaging narrative: nobody at the top knows how to fix the housing crisis.

🔥 The Cost of Policy Inconsistency

Scrapping the tax adjustment after four days was not merely a tactical pivot; it was an admission that the ruling coalition lacks a coherent strategy for managing the real estate sector. Treating housing policy like a trial balloon creates market uncertainty and deepens public cynicism.

Housing is a matter of basic livelihood, not a political game. Treating family financial security as collateral for internal power struggles must stop immediately.

The Opposition Presses Its Advantage on Jeonse and Buying Strains

Sensing vulnerability, the conservative opposition People Power Party seized on the turmoil, framing the tax reversal as a crude political stunt designed to win votes rather than solve structural problems. Opposition lawmakers argued that the administration’s handling of the issue proved it was entirely out of touch with the economic realities facing ordinary households.

Beyond the high-profile tax debates, the broader crisis on the ground involves a severe squeeze in the rental market. Surging purchase prices have directly pulled up *jeonse* (lump-sum housing deposit) rates, while available rental units have dried up, forcing many renters into monthly payments they can ill afford. For young couples and first-time job seekers, the path to housing stability feels increasingly blocked.

The political danger for the Democratic Party is acute. A party that historically champions the working and middle classes risks alienating its base if it cannot keep basic shelter affordable. As long as the opposition can credibly campaign on the slogan of government incompetence in housing, the ruling party's grip on the greater Seoul metropolitan area—crucial for any national election—will remain in jeopardy.

Broader Economic Successes Overshadowed by a Single Structural Failure

What makes the drop in approval particularly frustrating for the administration is that it contrasts sharply with other areas of governance. In the same Gallup survey, positive evaluations of President Lee often pointed to general macroeconomic management, trade performance, and basic livelihood policies.

Yet all those positive indicators are swallowed by the giant black hole of real estate. No matter how much export numbers improve or general wages creep upward, if housing costs outpace everything else, the average citizen feels poorer. When home prices or rental deposits surge by amounts that dwarf yearly salary increases, the macro-economy feels irrelevant to a family staring down an impossible monthly ledger.

Compounding this is the rising criticism of government morality and communication. When policies fail, voters are remarkably forgiving if leaders own up to mistakes with humility. But when officials project defensiveness—or worse, shift blame to former administrations like that of imprisoned former President Yoon Seok-youl—the public perceives arrogance.

What to Watch Next

As President Lee’s administration navigates this critical dip below 40%, several concrete milestones will dictate whether the government can recover its footing:

  • The Next Weekly Polling Cycle: Watch whether 39% proves to be a temporary floor or if negative sentiment deepens as winter rental pressures mount in Seoul.
  • Legislative Discipline on Housing Bills: Pay attention to whether the ruling party and the presidential office can present a unified, finalized stance on property taxes and supply targets, ending the public policy flip-flops.
  • Real Supply Signals: Monitor whether the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport can roll out credible, fast-tracked urban supply measures that reassure prospective buyers that inventory is actually on the way.

For now, the message from the public is unmistakably clear. Macroeconomic wins matter, but until the administration proves it can restore sanity to the cost of a roof over a citizen's head, the political price will continue to mount.
