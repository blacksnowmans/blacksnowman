South Korea’s Judiciary at a Breaking Point: The High-Stakes Case of Han Duck-soo

November 11, 2026

On November 19, the Supreme Court of South Korea will convene for a hearing that has effectively paralyzed the nation’s political landscape. The case is that of Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister currently serving a 15-year sentence in the first instance for insurrection. With his detention period set to expire just one day after the hearing, the judiciary finds itself at a crossroads: either finalize a ruling or witness the release of a high-profile convict accused of challenging the constitutional order. This timing has turned a standard legal procedure into a volatile flashpoint, dragging the judicial branch into a direct confrontation with the legislative wing of government.

The Looming Release and the Backlash Against the Judiciary

The Supreme Court’s decision to schedule the hearing for November 19—a mere 24 hours before Han’s legal detention period ends—has sent shockwaves through the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the liberal party currently in power under President Lee Jae-myung). The implication is clear: if the court fails to reach a final verdict or issue a new detention warrant during this window, Han will walk free to await his trial outside of prison.

For the ruling party, this is not just a procedural matter; it is a potential failure of justice. They have labeled the court’s slow handling of the case as an act of "judicial abandonment." The rhetoric has intensified rapidly, with senior party members warning that if an "insurrectionist" is allowed to walk free, it would signal the start of a "real war." Some factions within the broader ruling coalition have even begun discussing the potential impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, signaling a dangerous breakdown in the separation of powers where the legislature is now openly threatening to dismantle the leadership of the judiciary over its handling of a single, highly sensitive case.

"Jailbreak Laws" and the Collapse of Parliamentary Diplomacy

Simultaneously, the political atmosphere is being further poisoned by a fierce debate over the "Fabricated Prosecution Special Counsel Act." The People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has been staging protests outside the presidential office, decrying the act as an "Lee Jae-myung Jailbreak Law." Their argument is that the legislation is a thinly veiled attempt to neutralize the prosecution's authority and protect the current administration from legal scrutiny.

In response, the Democratic Party has dismissed these claims as an attempt to undermine the legitimacy of a democratically elected government, even going so far as to suggest that the opposition’s obstructionism constitutes a secondary "insurrection plot." When both major parties begin lobbing labels like "jailbreak" and "insurrection" at each other, the space for compromise evaporates. The legislative process, intended for deliberation, has devolved into a street-level turf war, with both sides treating legal reform as a zero-sum game for political survival.

The Long Shadow of the Impeached Past

The tension is inseparable from the lingering ghost of the previous administration. Even with the former president, 윤석열 (Yoon Suk-yeol), now imprisoned following his impeachment, the conservative opposition remains deeply entangled in the fallout of his era. Recent remarks by figures like Lee Jin-sook—who suggested that the responsibility for the declaration of martial law was a personal matter for "Mr. Yoon" rather than a party issue—have only served to infuriate the public and the ruling coalition.

By refusing to fully renounce or apologize for the constitutional crisis caused by the previous government, the opposition has found itself unable to move past the shadow of the man in prison. This perceived "defensive" stance regarding the past has hardened the ruling party's resolve to push for "judicial normalization" through the aforementioned special counsel laws. The opposition's failure to draw a clean line between its current political identity and the misconduct of the impeached administration has effectively turned every legal challenge into a referendum on the country’s recent, turbulent history.

What to Watch Next

The immediate future hinges on two critical developments:

  • The November 19 Supreme Court Hearing: This is the ultimate test for the judiciary. Whether the court manages to extend the detention or reach a verdict will determine whether the current political crisis cools or ignites into open confrontation.
  • The November Parliamentary Session: The Democratic Party is poised to bring the Special Counsel Act to a vote. Observers should expect a repeat of the gridlock seen in previous months, likely involving filibusters and further mass protests from the opposition.
  • The Stability of the Judiciary: Keep a close eye on the rhetoric surrounding Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. If the ruling party follows through on threats of impeachment, it would represent an unprecedented constitutional crisis, signaling that the judiciary is no longer viewed as an independent arbiter by the political majority.

As the nation looks toward the end of November, the fundamental question remains: can South Korea’s institutions function when the political stakes are so high that every legal ruling is viewed through the lens of partisan warfare? For now, the answer appears to be "barely," as the line between justice and politics continues to blur, leaving the public to wonder if the rule of law can survive the pressures of a deeply polarized state.
