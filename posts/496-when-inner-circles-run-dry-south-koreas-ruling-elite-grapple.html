When Inner Circles Run Dry: South Korea's Ruling Elite Grapple with Sliding Approval

September 09, 2026

There is a familiar, almost theatrical rhythm to political decline in Seoul. No matter the administration or the party in power, the script remains stubbornly consistent. The moment public opinion polls begin to sour and the president's numbers dip into uncomfortable territory, a predictable headline unfurls across the morning news: a clandestine gathering of the leader's closest confidants, huddled behind closed doors to diagnose a crisis of confidence. For outside observers trying to understand South Korean politics, these moments offer a rare X-ray of how power actually operates, and where it begins to fray.

The latest iteration of this ritual involves the "Group of Seven"—a tight-knit circle of original loyalists to South Korean President Lee Jae-myung, drawn from the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party. Long before Lee consolidated national power, this inner circle stood by his side. But when several current and former lawmakers from the group met recently to review a sharp slide in the president’s approval ratings, their conclusions were not triumphant strategies or bold policy pivots. Instead, they reportedly confessed a blunt and unsettling truth: they had no idea what to do next.

To understand why this moment matters, we have to look past the superficial drama of leaked meeting notes. The helplessness expressed by Lee's core allies is not merely a reflection of a difficult political season; it is a symptom of a deeper structural malady within South Korea’s political culture—one defined by echo chambers, a refusal to look past good intentions, and an unhealthy reliance on tribal defensiveness.

The Anatomy of a Closed-Door Confession

The gathering took place in the wake of a recent six-minister cabinet reshuffle. For a group whose political identities are fused with the president's rise—including senior figures like lawmakers Jung Sung-ho, Kim Young-jin, and Moon Jin-seok—the meeting was ostensibly called to confront a bruising reality. According to reports leaking from the session, the assessment was bleak: approval ratings had effectively halved from their peaks, and while every party member was privately anxious, nobody could point to a clear catalyst for a turnaround.

Some attendees even questioned whether the "Group of Seven" should officially disband to avoid fueling public perceptions of backroom cronyism now that the government is past its honeymoon phase. Yet the irony is stark. The very people who claim they should step back to prevent accusations of informal shadow politics are the exact ones who rush together the moment a crisis hits. It demonstrates that long-standing personal networks outside formal government structures still pull the real levers when the pressure mounts.

Why did seasoned political operatives throw up their hands and declare there was "no magic bullet"? The answer lies in a fundamental misdiagnosis of the patient. When a government’s numbers collapse, treating it like an external virus—blaming hostile media, stubborn voters, or opposition sabotage—ensures that every prescribed medicine will fail. Because these core aides are too close to the seat of power, wrapped in a cocoon of self-protection and anxious not to offend the president's sensibilities, their analytical instruments are fundamentally warped. History shows that whether it was the MERS outbreak under the conservative Park Geun-hye administration or real estate scandals under liberal predecessor Moon Jae-in, numbers only rebounded when leaders stopped hiding behind statistics and fundamentally shifted how they listened to the public. The Group of Seven's paralysis is not a lack of political skill; it is a profound failure of perception.

The Trap of Mind-Reading and Moral Superiority

This insular mindset thrives on a cognitive trap that has come to dominate contemporary South Korean political discourse: what can best be described as "mind-reading logic." When a policy fails or public discontent boils over, discussion rarely centers on empirical outcomes or administrative missteps. Instead, fierce debates immediately pivot to divining the moral purity and hidden intentions of the actors involved.

Apologists for the administration frequently frame President Lee’s motivations as inherently good, portraying him as a leader whose sole happiness is derived from bettering citizens' lives through progressive policy. Conversely, anyone who dares to demand accountability, offer constructive criticism, or ask hard questions is instantly dismissed as acting out of selfish motives, bad faith, or partisan malice. In this binary framework, if the leader's intentions are pure by definition, then any policy failure or drop in support cannot possibly be his fault. Critics, meanwhile, are reduced to bad actors whose data and warnings do not even warrant a fair hearing.

This is where good intentions become a dangerous political exemption. In a democracy, voters do not cast ballots to evaluate the warmth in a leader's heart; they judge the concrete economic and social results of governance. When "good intentions" are deployed as a blanket defense against failure, a political party effectively strips itself of the capacity to self-correct. Every internal warning is treated as an attack from an enemy rather than feedback from a citizen.

Rewriting History to Defend the Present

As the administration's defensive machinery kicks into gear, it often relies on historical revisionism to deflect criticism. A favorite talking point among the president's defenders involves drawing comparisons to the scandal-plagued years of the Moon Jae-in administration. When critics today demand that President Lee step forward and apologize directly for mounting controversies, defenders counter by asking: *"Did you demand that President Moon apologize personally every time his justice ministers clashed with prosecutors or scandals erupted? You were silent then, so your demands now lack moral authority."*

The argument crumbles upon even a casual glance at the historical record. Former President Moon did, in fact, bow his head and issue direct, public apologies at major inflection points of crisis. When then-Justice Minister Cho Kuk resigned amid a bitter national polarization in October 2019, Moon explicitly addressed the public to express deep regret for causing social conflict. He did so again when unprecedented friction erupted between another justice minister and the prosecutor-general. To claim that past leaders were never held to this standard is simply false—they weathered those demands by eventually confronting them directly rather than pretending the storm did not exist.

Constructing a political defense on manufactured historical memory only deepens public cynicism. Administrations that invent their own past will inevitably miscalculate their present.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this uneasy political chapter, several concrete indicators will reveal whether the ruling camp is capable of breaking out of its defensive shell:

  • Changes in Tone from the Inner Circle: Watch whether key figures like the Group of Seven continue to echo defeatist talking points or if they begin acknowledging specific policy missteps in areas like economic management and cost-of-living pressures.
  • Shifts in Public Opinion Methodology: Pay attention to standard weekly tracking polls (such as Realmeter or Gallup Korea). A temporary bounce driven by a single diplomatic event will tell us little; a sustained stabilization will require tangible, corrective policy changes that address voter fatigue.
  • Handling of Institutional Friction: As debates continue over police powers, prosecutorial reform, and economic regulation, observe whether the administration treats legislative and public pushback as an existential threat to be crushed or as a signal to negotiate.

Power is a notoriously intoxicating environment, and proximity to it often blinds leaders to the ground-level realities experienced by ordinary citizens. Until South Korea's ruling elite stop viewing every critical voice as an enemy faction and start measuring their success by outcomes rather than intentions, closed-door meetings will continue to produce nothing more than shared sighs and empty rooms.
