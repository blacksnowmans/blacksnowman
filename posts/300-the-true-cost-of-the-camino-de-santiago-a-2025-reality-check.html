The True Cost of the Camino de Santiago: A 2025 Reality Check

September 07, 2026

For many, the image of the Camino de Santiago—the ancient pilgrimage route across northern Spain—is one of rustic simplicity: a traveler with a single backpack, walking for weeks while spending only a few dollars a day. It is a romantic vision of shedding worldly burdens and finding clarity through the rhythm of one's own footsteps. However, as one recent traveler who spent 34 days trekking the "French Way" (the most popular route starting in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port) discovered, the reality is far more expensive than the folklore suggests.

The traveler revealed that their total expenses for the journey reached approximately 8.1 million won (about $5,800). Even when isolating the 34 days spent exclusively on the trail, the cost exceeded 3.5 million won (about $2,500). As inflation hits European travel and the "budget" reputation of the Camino begins to fray, many prospective pilgrims are left wondering: how much does it actually cost to walk to Santiago today?

Beyond the "Budget" Myth: Breaking Down the 34-Day Trek

The total figure of 8.1 million won includes flights from South Korea, sightseeing in Paris and Barcelona, and gear purchased before departure. However, the core daily expenses while on the trail paint a stark picture. With the exchange rate hovering between 1,620 and 1,650 won per euro, every coffee and bed took a significant bite out of the budget.

The daily average expenditure on the trail came to about 100,000 won (about $70). This isn't the price of extreme luxury; it is the cost of a comfortable, sustainable pace. It includes a mix of public and private hostels, regular meals at local eateries, and the occasional need for transportation to bridge gaps in the route. For those who prioritize physical comfort—avoiding the crowded, first-come-first-served public albergues (pilgrim hostels) in favor of private rooms—this price point is the new baseline for a stress-free journey.

The Rising Cost of Sleep and Sustenance

Accommodation is the first hurdle for any pilgrim. Public albergues, which cost between 6 and 15 euros ($7–$17), remain the cheapest option but come with the inherent risk of having no vacancy for late arrivals. Private albergues, offering more amenities and the ability to book in advance, can cost 15 to 25 euros ($17–$28). When fatigue sets in or privacy becomes a necessity, travelers often turn to hotels or Airbnb rentals, which can easily exceed 30 to 40 euros ($33–$45) per night.

Food expenses are similarly deceptive. While the romanticized version of the Camino involves modest picnics, the reality of walking 20 kilometers a day often leads to a desire for convenience. A simple "café con leche" and a sandwich can cost 7–8 euros ($8–$9), and a standard pilgrim’s set meal (menú del día) at a restaurant can set you back 15 euros ($17). Over 34 days, these "small" costs accumulate into a significant drain, with the traveler spending over 1.49 million won ($1,070) on food alone.

Transportation and the "Jump" Trap

One of the biggest pitfalls for budget-conscious pilgrims is the "jump"—taking a bus or train when physical limits are reached or when time is tight. During this 34-day trip, transportation costs exceeded 560,000 won ($400).

The most painful expense was the last-minute flight from Santiago de Compostela to Barcelona. Because the exact completion date of a long-distance walk is impossible to predict, booking early is rarely an option. While a flight could have cost as little as 72,000 won ($52) if booked six months out, the spontaneous booking resulted in a price of over 270,000 won ($195). It is the "tax" of flexibility that every long-distance walker must eventually pay.

Investing in the Basics: Why Gear Matters

Perhaps the most vital lesson from this 2025 journey is the importance of investing in gear—specifically footwear. The traveler spent over 800,000 won ($575) on preparation, with the highlight being a 358,000 won ($255) pair of "Norda 001" trail running shoes.

While the initial cost might seem exorbitant, the traveler reported completing the 800km journey without a single blister or foot-related stoppage. In the context of a long pilgrimage, quality gear is not a luxury; it is an insurance policy. Other essentials, such as a high-quality backpack (Deuter Futura 34L) and specialized sun protection, proved to be worth every won. Trying to save money on fundamental gear is often a false economy that leads to injury or the premature end of the pilgrimage.

What to Watch for Next

If you are planning your own Camino, consider these takeaways:

  • Budget for Comfort: Do not rely on outdated "budget" figures from five or ten years ago. Plan for at least $70–$80 per day to ensure you can afford rest when your body demands it.
  • Prioritize Footwear: Your shoes are your most important piece of infrastructure. Test them thoroughly at home and be willing to spend more on high-performance trail footwear.
  • The "Flexibility" Tax: Accept that you will spend more on last-minute transport and private accommodation than you would on a standard vacation. Build this "flexibility buffer" into your total savings.
  • Analyze Your Style: Are you a "hardcore" pilgrim aiming for the lowest possible cost, or a recreational trekker? Your budget should be a reflection of your physical needs, not an arbitrary target.

Ultimately, the Camino de Santiago remains a transformative experience. While the financial barriers have risen, the value lies in the journey itself. As the traveler concluded, the money spent was not merely an expense, but an investment in the thousands of footsteps that define the experience. By building a realistic budget, choosing the right gear, and setting a pace that honors your own limits, the trek remains as rewarding as ever.
