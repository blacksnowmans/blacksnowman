A Tightrope Walk in Seoul: Polls, Judicial Battles, and the Budgetary Reality of Heroes

October 20, 2026

The corridors of the National Assembly in Seoul are currently thick with the peculiar, high-octane energy of the National Audit season. This annual month-long ritual, where the legislature grills every corner of the executive branch, is a time of political theater, policy exposure, and sharp elbows. But beneath the usual shouting matches and stacks of documents, a more subtle shift is occurring in the bedrock of South Korean public opinion. As of October 19, the political landscape is defined by a delicate rebound for the presidency and a deepening standoff over the very foundations of the country’s judicial and emergency services.

For President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP), the latest numbers offer a rare moment of breathing room, yet no cause for celebration. Meanwhile, the opposition is grappling with internal fractures and a radical new push to impeach the nation’s top judge. To understand why this matters, one must look past the daily headlines and into the structural tensions of a country still healing from the constitutional trauma of the recent past.

The Pulse of the People and the Minsaeng Strategy

The most recent data from Realmeter, a prominent South Korean polling agency, shows President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating rebounding to 37.3%, a 2.1 percentage point increase from the previous week. This marks the first upward tick in three weeks, halting a downward slide that had begun to worry the Blue House. The bump is largely attributed to a pivot toward what Koreans call minsaeng—a term that translates to "the people's livelihood" but carries a heavy political weight, signifying bread-and-butter economic issues and public safety.

The Lee administration has recently doubled down on a "tough on crime" stance and a series of grassroots economic tours. In a country where the cost of living and personal safety are perennial top-of-mind concerns, these gestures appear to have resonated with a segment of the electorate. However, a 37% approval rating remains a precarious position for any leader. For many ordinary citizens—those struggling with job security and high inflation—the government’s efforts still feel more like rhetoric than reality. The "rebound" is less a surge of popularity and more a stabilization of the base.

The party support figures tell an even more complicated story. The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, currently holds a slight lead at 40.9%, while the ruling Democratic Party stands at 36.2%. The gap has narrowed to 4.7 percentage points, placing them within the margin of error. This suggests a electorate that is deeply skeptical of both sides. The PPP, still struggling to redefine itself after the catastrophic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, seems to be holding onto a "concrete" support base of about 40%, yet it fails to expand. The ruling DP, despite holding the presidency, has yet to demonstrate the kind of overwhelming policy efficacy that would allow it to pull away from its rivals.

The takeaway for an outside observer is a nation in a state of watchful waiting. The public has moved past the immediate shock of the martial law attempts during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, but they have not yet fully committed to the current administration’s vision. They are fatigued by the constant demonization of "the other side" and are waiting to see which party can actually deliver a more stable, affordable life.

A Third Party’s High-Stakes Gamble Against the Judiciary

While the two main parties bicker over poll numbers, the Rebuilding-Korea Party (RKP)—the influential third party led by Shin Jang-sik and associated with the prominent political figure Cho Kuk—is playing a much more aggressive game. On October 19, the RKP leadership held a meeting at a highly symbolic location: the memorial stone in front of the National Assembly that commemorates the lifting of the December 3rd martial law (an event from the previous administration's failed power grab).

There, the RKP unveiled a motion to impeach Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. Their argument is that the judiciary has become a "bastion of the old guard," failing to adequately address the illegalities of the previous era and acting as a brake on necessary reforms. For the RKP, impeaching the Chief Justice is not just a legal maneuver; it is a declaration of war against what they describe as a "cartel" of judicial and prosecutorial elites.

This move puts the ruling Democratic Party in a difficult spot. The RKP is demanding that the DP join a joint task force and hold a general meeting of its lawmakers to support the impeachment. Shin Jang-sik, the RKP leader, has been blunt, telling the DP that only the "remnants of the Yoon Suk-yeol era" would fear a political backlash. He is essentially daring the ruling party to be as radical as its base often demands.

The DP leadership, however, is being far more cautious. Impeaching a sitting Chief Justice is a "nuclear option" in Korean politics. If it fails, or if it is perceived by the public as a political vendetta, it could trigger a massive backlash that would wipe out the President’s recent polling gains. The PPP has already slammed the move as a blatant attempt to paralyze the courts and protect political allies. For the DP, the dilemma is classic: do they follow their most vocal supporters into a high-stakes battle for "judicial justice," or do they prioritize the stability required to manage the economy? The decision they make in the coming weeks will likely define the remainder of the National Audit season.

The Cost of Protection: Firefighters and Political Gaffes

The human cost of political failure was brought into sharp relief this week following a tragic factory fire in Bucheon that claimed the life of Fire Lieutenant Kim Jin-seok. President Lee Jae-myung visited the wake to pay his respects and posthumously awarded the officer the Order of Service Merit. This moment of national mourning, however, was quickly overshadowed by a political firestorm involving Chu Mi-ae, a veteran lawmaker and former Justice Minister known for her uncompromising style.

Chu Mi-ae drew intense criticism for remarks that were interpreted as suggesting that firefighter budgets were being "sucked up" or mismanaged at the provincial level. Although she later apologized, the damage was done. The PPP and the New Reform Party (a smaller centrist opposition group) launched a coordinated attack, accusing her of insulting the sacrifices of first responders. Even within her own party, the leadership had to step in; DP Chairman Kim Min-seok urged his colleagues to remain humble until the public’s anger subsided.

Beyond the "he-said-she-said" of the gaffe, the incident exposed a systemic flaw in how South Korea treats its heroes. In 2020, firefighters were officially transitioned to "national" status, a move intended to ensure they received equal pay and equipment regardless of whether they worked in a wealthy city or a poor rural province. However, the reality is a shell game. As noted by Lee Gi-in of the New Reform Party, the central government still only provides about 8.6% of the funding for the fire service. The remaining 91.4% is still foisted upon local governments, many of which are cash-strapped.

"We call them heroes and wear black ribbons at their funerals, but when it comes time to sign the check for their safety equipment, the central government is nowhere to be found."

This "national in name only" status is a point of deep resentment. Politicians of all stripes are quick to take photos at funerals, but slow to fix the lopsided budgetary structure that leaves local fire stations underfunded. The current controversy isn't just about a poorly chosen word by a veteran politician; it’s about a state that claims responsibility for its citizens' safety while refusing to pay for it.

Internal Fractures and the New Legal Frontier

The National Audit has also turned its spotlight on the newly formed Public Prosecution Office (PPO). This agency is a centerpiece of the Lee administration’s legal reforms, designed to separate the power to investigate from the power to indict—a move aimed at curbing the historically outsized influence of the state prosecutors. During the first-ever audit of the PPO, the two parties clashed predictably. The PPP argued that stripping prosecutors of their investigative powers has led to inefficiencies and a rise in unsolved crimes, while the DP maintained that the reform is the only way to prevent the "politicization" of the law that occurred during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time.

While this ideological battle raged in the audit rooms, the PPP was dealing with its own internal "civil war." The party leadership’s decision to appoint Yoon Jae-bok as a local chair in Yongin triggered a fierce revolt from Yang Hyang-ja, a high-profile member of the party's top council. Yang has threatened legal action, accusing the leadership of a "self-destructive" lack of transparency and abandoning the principle of fair competition. This internal strife highlights the PPP’s struggle to find its footing; it is a party caught between its traditional hierarchical roots and a desperate need to show a more modern, democratic face to the public.

What to Watch Next

As the National Audit enters its final stretch, the political temperature in Seoul is likely to rise further. Here are the three key areas to monitor:

  • The Impeachment Threshold: Watch for whether the Democratic Party officially adopts the Rebuilding-Korea Party’s motion to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. If the DP moves forward, it will signal a full-scale confrontation between the executive/legislative branches and the judiciary.
  • The "Minsaeng" Budget: Following the funeral of the fallen firefighter, there will be intense pressure on the government to increase the central government's share of the fire service budget beyond the current 8.6%. Any failure to do so in the upcoming budget session will be seen as a betrayal of the President's "people-first" rhetoric.
  • The Poll Convergence: If the gap between the DP and PPP remains within the margin of error for another month, expect both parties to take more extreme "partisan" stances to shore up their core bases ahead of next year's local elections.

In the end, the numbers on a Realmeter chart or the shouting matches in a committee room matter less than the question of whether the state is functioning for those it is meant to protect. Whether it is a firefighter in Bucheon or a young job-seeker in Seoul, the "middle" of the Korean electorate is less interested in who wins the impeachment battle and more interested in who can make the system work again.
