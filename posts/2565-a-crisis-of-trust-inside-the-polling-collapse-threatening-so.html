A Crisis of Trust: Inside the Polling Collapse Threatening South Korea’s Ruling Camp

November 20, 2026

When political authority begins to leak in Seoul, it rarely happens with a single dramatic collapse. Instead, it starts with a quiet shift in the numbers—a erosion of core support here, a sharp spike in hardened hostility there, until the structural weight of South Korea’s political system turns fully against the sitting power. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and President Lee Jae-myung, that shift appears to have arrived.

A recent poll by local media outlet News Tomato has sent shockwaves through the ruling establishment. The survey reveals not only that a majority of the public wants Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok to step down over a mounting controversy involving the secretive religious sect Shincheonji, but that President Lee’s own approval rating has tumbled to 36.9 percent. Worse still for the administration, the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has surged ahead in party preference, capitalizing on growing fractures within the ruling camp. What is unfolding in Seoul is not merely a temporary dip in public sentiment, but a classic display of the structural traps that have plagued South Korean presidents throughout the country's democratic era.

Cracks in the Stronghold: The Resignation Crisis of Kim Min-seok

The headline figure from the News Tomato poll is stark: 51.6 percent of all respondents believe Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok should resign from his position. By contrast, only 21.3 percent felt the matter should be put to rest under current conditions, and just 15.1 percent believed an official public apology to party members would suffice. In short, more than double the number of citizens demand his exit compared to those advocating for compromise or containment.

To understand why this issue carries such destructive force, one must understand the political sensitivity surrounding Shincheonji in South Korea. The fringe religious group has long been a lightning rod for public outrage and political suspicion. Allegations linking party leadership to the group strike directly at the Democratic Party’s core identity and moral standing. Within the party’s base, the feeling is not merely one of disappointment, but of deep betrayal—a sense that internal factional battles and murky ties have dragged the party’s integrity through the mud.

Even more alarming for the ruling party is the mood in Honam, the southwestern region encompassing the Jeolla provinces and the city of Gwangju. Honam has historically served as the political bedrock and moral compass of South Korean liberalism. During the last national convention, Kim enjoyed robust support from this region. Yet in the News Tomato survey, 44.7 percent of Honam respondents called for his resignation. While a combined 47.4 percent favored either wrapping up the situation (28.2 percent) or issuing a public apology (19.2 percent), the fact that outright resignation emerged as the single largest choice in the party’s traditional sanctuary indicates a profound rupture in trust.

When a party’s core stronghold begins to waver, its leadership loses the authority required to command discipline. With Kim’s leadership paralyzed, the Democratic Party now finds itself drifting at a moment when it faces critical legislative battles, including contentious discussions over judicial reform and high-profile impeachments.

The Disciplinary Split Over Rep. Choi Min-hee and the Media Battle

The crisis of leadership is compounded by intense internal squabbling over how to handle disciplinary action against lawmaker Choi Min-hee. Polling figures on this issue reflect a deeply polarized public, split between calls for severe punishment and demands for restraint.

According to the survey, 33.5 percent of respondents favored the harshest penalty: outright expulsion from the party. However, looking deeper into the data reveals a far more nuanced picture. Those advocating for no disciplinary action at all stood at 20.3 percent, while 14.8 percent favored a light penalty such as an official warning. Combined, the opinion favoring a warning or lighter treatment reached 35.1 percent—slightly edging out the demand for expulsion. Other respondents were scattered between suspension of party officer status (13.9 percent) and suspension of general membership (9.8 percent).

This five-way division in public opinion underscores the complex context surrounding the case. A significant factor in this split is the fragmented way information reaches the public. While mainstream media outlets have tended to highlight bite-sized, controversial soundbites, alternative media channels—specifically progressive YouTube platforms—have taken on an influential role by circulating the full transcripts published by investigative outlets like Sisa Journal.

Because the News Tomato survey was conducted just as these full transcripts were beginning to circulate, much of the initial public reaction was formed without complete context. As progressive commentators have pointed out, when lawmakers and the public react solely to sensationalized headlines rather than verified facts, disciplinary measures risk becoming political witch hunts. Passing severe penalties under the influence of skewed headlines threatens to leave behind deep scars and fuel further factional infighting within the party.

Inside President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Trap and the Single-Term Lame Duck

While the party struggles with internal strife, President Lee Jae-myung faces an equally daunting challenge at the executive level. His approval rating dropped 3.7 percentage points from two weeks prior to stand at 36.9 percent, while negative evaluations surged to 59.2 percent. However, the most dangerous metric lies in the intensity of that dissatisfaction.

A granular look at the data shows that the hardened veto group—those who state the president is doing "very badly"—has ballooned to 47.6 percent. In contrast, the core enthusiastic base—those who state he is doing "very well"—has shrank to a mere 13.0 percent. When nearly half the electorate harbors intense negative feelings toward an administration, passing major policy reforms becomes extraordinarily difficult. Hardened negative sentiment rarely reverses course, meaning that routine government achievements are easily drowned out by public hostility.

This dynamic highlights the stark difference between holding regional executive office and occupying the presidency under South Korea’s constitution. As Governor of Gyeonggi Province, Lee enjoyed high approval ratings and effective control over administration. Regional leaders operate in an environment where re-election is permitted, keeping bureaucrats aligned and regional media relatively manageable. Major political battles occur primarily during election seasons.

The presidency, however, is a single five-year term. Under South Korea's constitutional framework, the moment a president passes the midpoint of their term, bureaucratic loyalty fades as officials prepare for the next administration. Presidents face relentless scrutiny from opposition parties, a national media landscape freed from any honeymoon period, and a public that quickly pivots toward the next generation of political contenders.

Historical precedents paint a sobering picture of this single-term curse:

  • Kim Young-sam (1993–1998): After ramming through a controversial labor reform bill in late 1996, his administration collapsed under public backlash, corporate bankruptcies, and corruption scandals involving his son, culminating in his resignation from the ruling party late in his term.
  • Kim Dae-jung (1998–2003): Marred by high-profile financial scandals involving close aides and his sons during his fourth year, the Nobel laureate was forced to resign as party leader and formally renounce party ties to stave off total legislative paralysis.
  • Roh Moo-hyun (2003–2008): Seeking to break the cycle through aggressive political combat and grand coalition proposals, Roh found that aggressive pushback against critics only reinforced public perceptions of stubbornness, causing his approval ratings to stagnate in the low 20s.

For President Lee, the recent drop to 36.9 percent suggests that former management styles that worked in local government—such as direct public call-outs on social media or aggressive scolding of cabinet officials—are now being perceived by national voters as authoritarian or unyielding, compounding his political vulnerability.

Party Support Reversal and the Rise of the 'New Lee' Faction

The executive slump has inevitably dragged down party morale. The conservative main opposition People Power Party now leads the Democratic Party by 5 percentage points, standing at 39.4 percent to the DPK’s 34.4 percent. Yet the most concerning development for the ruling camp is not just the deficit, but the ideological fragmentation occurring inside its own base.

This internal divide was vividly illustrated in the poll's findings on real estate taxation. When asked about raising the tax deduction threshold for non-resident single-home owners under the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (Jongbuse) to 1.4 billion KRW (approximately $1.05 million USD), the overall public leaned against the proposal, with 43.8 percent opposing and 38.0 percent in favor. Yet within the Democratic Party's own self-identified supporters, a majority actually *favored* the tax cut.

This outcome exposes a deep rift between traditional progressive values and a rising internal group dubbed the "New Lee" faction. Historically, the Democratic Party’s core voters favored robust property taxation to curb real estate speculation. However, a significant segment of President Lee’s newer coalition consists of voters motivated less by classic liberal ideology or historic party loyalty, and more by personal economic self-interest or anti-establishment rhetoric directed against older party elites.

When a ruling party’s base fractures between traditional ideologues and pragmatic economic self-interest groups, policy coherence breaks down. Traditional loyalists grow fatigued by compromises that sacrifice long-held values, while newer transactional supporters abandon the party the moment their immediate economic demands are not met.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters a volatile political period, the coming weeks will reveal whether the ruling camp can stabilize its foundation or if it will succumb to early late-term decay. Key benchmarks to watch include:

  • The Fate of Kim Min-seok: Watch whether the Democratic Party leadership calls an emergency council to address the 51.6 percent resignation demand, or if Kim attempts to dig in. A failure to address the sentiment in Honam could lead to open rebellion from regional lawmakers.
  • The Choi Min-hee Disciplinary Vote: Pay close attention to how the party ethics committee handles Choi’s case once the full transcripts are fully digested by the public. A decision to push for outright expulsion could ignite a war between party factions, while a simple warning may draw sharp criticism from opposition critics.
  • Presidential Style and Cabinet Reshuffles: Track whether President Lee shifts his public communication strategy. Observers should watch for potential cabinet reshuffles designed to reset public expectations and regain the initiative from the opposition People Power Party.
