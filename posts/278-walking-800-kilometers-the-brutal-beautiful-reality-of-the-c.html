Walking 800 Kilometers: The Brutal, Beautiful Reality of the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Picture waking up every single day to the dull, throbbing pain of fresh blisters, weighing your backpack down to the exact gram, and bracing yourself for a sudden downpour or freezing winds. For many, the Camino de Santiago—the legendary network of pilgrimage routes leading to the shrine of apostle Saint James in the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela in northwestern Spain—is romanticized as a peaceful journey of spiritual self-discovery amid picturesque European landscapes. But for those who actually lace up their boots and walk it, the 36-day, 800-kilometer trek from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in France is far closer to an exercise in gritty, raw survival.

South Koreans have developed a passionate fascination with the Camino, regularly ranking among the top nationalities for completing the route. Yet, beneath the surging popularity and the desire to check a major item off a bucket list lies a grueling physical test. Examining a seasoned traveler’s 36-day journey reveals what it actually takes to survive the elements, manage your gear, and keep your body intact across 800 kilometers of unpredictable terrain.

The Gear Trap: Why Less Is More, Until It Isn't

Your backpack is essentially your home and your physical limiter on the trail. Choosing a 30-liter pack—such as the Arc'teryx Aerius—is an intentional exercise in constraint. Humans have an innate psychological urge to fill empty space, so capping your volume at 30 liters forces a brutal gear diet. However, radical minimalism carries its own risks. Pushing equipment to its limits can backfire; one traveler found their pack’s frame support snapping under the strain, forcing them to resort to heavy-duty tape just to hold the bag together.

When starting with a pack weighing over 12 kilograms (about 26 pounds), extra weight inevitably creeps in through food and sudden necessities. Experienced pilgrims emphasize that certain items are non-negotiable. A poncho with actual sleeves is mandatory—not just to keep dry when the weather turns hostile, but to keep your hands from freezing while gripping trekking poles. Portable power banks are another absolute lifeline, especially when historic pilgrim hostels, known as albergues, lack accessible wall outlets, occasionally forcing travelers to rely entirely on stored battery power for days.

Your Feet Are Your Only Currency

If your feet fail you on the Camino, 800 kilometers transforms into an absolute torture test. Swelling is a guaranteed biological response to walking massive daily distances, meaning seasoned walkers routinely buy footwear two to three sizes larger than their normal fit, paired specifically with the exact hiking socks they plan to wear on the trail. Wearing toe socks layered with traditional hiking socks is a common strategy to drastically cut down on friction blisters.

Trekking poles are not optional accessories; they are vital load-bearing equipment, particularly when descending steep slopes where the impact on knees can become excruciating. Weather extremes compound the physical toll. Spring and early autumn conditions can see temperatures plummeting to -2°C (28°F) at dawn before soaring into the sun-drenched afternoon, demanding a strict layered-clothing approach. Neglecting minor exposed areas like the back of your hands or your lips can leave you sunburned and chapped raw within days.

The Financial and Logistical Realities

Completing the Camino is far from a budget-free endeavor. Fluctuating currency exchange rates can dramatically shift out-of-pocket expenses, requiring a solid budget of around 2 million won (roughly $1,400 to $1,500 USD) excluding international flights. Smart budgeting often comes down to lifestyle choices on the ground. Dining out on pilgrim menus every night adds up quickly, whereas buying fresh ingredients at local village markets and cooking communal meals can slash food costs to just 3 to 4 euros per day.

Accommodation requires strategic flexibility. Public albergues operate strictly on a first-come, first-served basis with zero reservations allowed, meaning early arrivals secure a bed while latecomers must scramble. While messaging apps like WhatsApp are common, direct phone calls remain the most reliable way to lock down private rooms when public spaces fill up. Language barriers, surprisingly, tend to dissolve into basic English numbers and reservation phrases rather than advanced Spanish fluency.

Finding Inner Resilience Over Cinematic Epiphanies

Popular media often portrays long-distance pilgrimages as life-altering catalysts that instantly rewrite a person's entire existence. The reality is much quieter and more grounded. Walkers often return home realizing they haven't magically transformed into entirely different people, but rather gained a quiet, hard-earned confidence: the firm reassurance that while they may not be the fastest, they possess the grit to keep moving forward steadily and persistently.

The physical benefits—like temporary weight loss—tend to fade once normal daily routines resume. What stays behind is an upgraded internal stamina. The Camino does not hand out neat, tidy answers to life's complex questions; instead, it forces you to sit with your own thoughts, endure physical discomfort, and listen to your internal voice without the constant static of modern life.

Walking is the most honest, stripped-down form of endurance. The trail strips away status, distractions, and comfort, leaving behind only the next step. For anyone considering taking on the 800-kilometer challenge, the ultimate takeaway is simple: stop obsessing over finishing fast, and start focusing on the steady rhythm of your own pace.
