The Disconnect: Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Rose While His Party Tanked

September 22, 2026

For many observers of South Korean politics, the current landscape feels like a perpetual cycle of disappointment. When the administration changed hands, there was a palpable sense of hope for sweeping reform. Yet, as recent developments unfold, that optimism has curdled into confusion and frustration. Even as the administration positions itself as a champion of "prosecutorial reform," the recent spate of administrative blunders and personnel controversies has left even its most loyal supporters questioning the path forward.

The latest polling data offers a jarring paradox: President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has finally ticked upward to 35% after nine weeks of stagnation, yet the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea’s main liberal party—has suffered a staggering seven-percentage-point plunge in support. How can the President gain ground while his own party hemorrhages support? The answer lies in a disconnect between the administration's messaging and the harsh reality of "institutional inertia"—the shadow of the old establishment that still looms large over the current government.

The Warning Light in the Polling Data

The most recent survey by Korea Political Research paints a complex picture. President Lee’s positive assessment saw a marginal rise from 34.7% to 35%, finally breaking a nearly three-month downward trend. However, the Democratic Party’s support cratered from 40.5% to 33.6%. This is far from a victory lap; the administration’s approval among those in their 30s remains dismal, with a 79.5% negative rating. This demographic, along with moderate voters, is signaling a clear "warning light" to the government.

The internal strife within the Democratic Party is at the heart of this collapse. Instead of acting as a stable, disciplined support system for the President, the party’s mainstream leadership has become mired in infighting and what many voters perceive as "politics of hatred." Critics argue that the President’s slight recovery is essentially a call for a course correction—a mandate to pivot toward genuine moderate expansion rather than the self-destructive maneuvers currently on display. When the ruling party fails to act as a shield and instead becomes an anchor, the base inevitably begins to drift.

Furthermore, the public has begun to evaluate the Lee administration and the Democratic Party as a singular unit of accountability. Despite the government’s attempts to tout minor improvements in macroeconomic indicators, the average citizen—struggling with a rising cost of living—is not feeling the relief. When the political class prioritizes internal power struggles over the daily economic anxieties of the people, the public’s trust withers. Without addressing this fundamental divide, the upcoming election cycle could prove disastrous for the current leadership.

The Ghost of "Prosecutor-Media Collusion"

A particularly sore point for supporters is the administration’s handling of the legacy of "prosecutor-media collusion" (a term for the unethical, symbiotic relationship between investigative journalists and the prosecution to leak sensitive information for political gain). Critics were outraged when an official from the Ministry of the Interior and Safety dismissed past high-profile scandals as mere "journalistic ethical violations."

To understand the depth of this anger, one must look back at the controversy involving Han Dong-hoon, a former powerful figure in the prosecution, and a former reporter from the conservative outlet Channel A. The allegation involves the reporter attempting to coerce an imprisoned executive into framing a prominent progressive politician, all while signaling a connection to the prosecution’s top brass. To downplay this as a "violation of ethics" is to ignore a clear, systemic abuse of power. For many, seeing this history whitewashed suggests that the new administration is far more interested in shielding former power-players than in dismantling the corrupt systems that allowed them to flourish.

When those who were once the "enforcers" of the previous conservative order start popping up in key positions under the new, supposedly reformist government, supporters are left wondering: What was the point of the struggle? The appearance of institutional "laundering," where former enemies of reform are integrated into the current administration under the guise of "experience," is perceived as a betrayal of the very movement that brought the government to power.

The Contradiction of the New Investigative Agency

Perhaps the most baffling decision has been the nomination of Kim Ji-yong, a senior prosecutor at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, to lead the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA). The SCIA was designed to be the crown jewel of the government’s prosecutorial reform—a body meant to separate investigation from prosecution to curb prosecutorial overreach.

The contradiction is stark. In 2022, Kim Ji-yong was at the forefront of the prosecution's fierce public campaign against the very "Separation of Investigation and Prosecution" legislation that paved the way for the SCIA. At the time, he publicly decried the reforms as a "betrayal of the socially vulnerable" and a contradiction that would prevent necessary investigations. Now, he claims that his past objections were misunderstood, arguing he only opposed the method, not the reform itself.

This "gaslighting" of the voter base has triggered intense backlash. Even the Minister of the Interior and Safety, Yoon Ho-joong, has been criticized for an arrogant, confrontational attitude toward party members and lawmakers who question the nomination. By installing a former critic of reform at the helm of the very agency meant to implement it, the administration is inadvertently confirming the deepest fears of its supporters: that the government lacks the genuine will to complete the structural changes it promised. Nominating a "fox to guard the henhouse" is not just a strategic error; it is a profound failure of political integrity.

What to Watch Next

As the administration prepares for a high-stakes press conference, the eyes of the public are fixed on whether the President will address the "elephant in the room." The critical issues to watch are:

  • The fate of the SCIA nomination: Will the President yield to the mounting pressure and withdraw the nomination of Kim Ji-yong to signal a commitment to genuine reform?
  • The internal party dynamic: Will the President clearly distance himself from the current party leadership’s toxic infighting and demand a pivot toward a more unified, reform-oriented platform?
  • The tone of the response: Will the administration continue its defensive posturing, or will it admit to the disconnect between its personnel choices and the voters' mandates?

The danger for the administration is not just lower polling numbers—it is the loss of its defining identity. If the government continues to trade its core principles for the sake of administrative convenience or political compromise with the old establishment, it risks losing the very coalition that put it in power. The next few weeks will determine whether the administration has the courage to prune its own ranks and return to the promises of the "light-filled revolution" its supporters once championed.
