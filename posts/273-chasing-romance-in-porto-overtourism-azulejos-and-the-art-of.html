Chasing Romance in Porto: Overtourism, Azulejos, and the Art of Slow Travel in Portugal's Second City

September 07, 2026

Red-tiled roofs stack up against the riverbanks, caught in a stiff breeze blowing in from the Atlantic. Porto, Portugal’s historic second city, has long held a reputation as a place tailor-made for romance. Travelers come here to leave a piece of their hearts, or to nurse a coffee on a century-old cafe chair while imagining the magical sparks that once inspired J.K. Rowling. But behind the photogenic facades and dazzling blue-and-white tilework lies a quieter tension: a city straining under the weight of tens of thousands of annual visitors, and locals fighting to preserve their everyday lives beneath the tourist tide.

Stained Glass and Azulejos: The Living History of São Bento Station

Most cities treat a train station as a mere portal for transit, but Porto’s São Bento Station demands you stop the moment you walk through the doors. Built at the turn of the 20th century on the charred ruins of a 16th-century monastery, the station feels less like a commuter hub and more like a public art museum. If Seoul Station anchors South Korea’s modernization in steel and concrete, São Bento drapes its historical skeleton in the vivid, narrative blues of Portugal's national soul.

The station’s defining feature is its expanse of roughly 20,000 hand-painted *azulejos*—traditional tin-glazed ceramic tiles whose name stems from an Arabic word for "small polished stone." It took painter Jorge Colaço twelve grueling years, from 1905 to 1916, to complete the murals depicting pivotal moments in Portuguese history and rural life. In an era obsessed with instant output and algorithmic speed, standing before a work that demanded twelve years of human patience offers a humbling counter-rhythm.

Yet this canvas is also a functioning public transit nexus. The daily friction between tourists snapping photos and commuters trying to catch their trains highlights a growing urban fracture. Admiring the art without blocking commuter walkways is the bare minimum required to keep this architectural treasure functional for another century.

The Etiquette of Visiting: Beyond the Language Barrier

Many foreign travelers hesitate to visit Portugal out of anxiety over the language barrier. In reality, English proficiency in Porto is remarkably high, especially in the hospitality sector. Because tourism forms a massive pillar of the national economy, basic English is more than enough to navigate streets, order meals, and ask for directions.

The real issue isn't vocabulary; it’s demeanor. Across Europe, anti-tourism movements are surging not simply because visitor numbers are up, but because of a breakdown in basic respect. Locals do not demand fluent Portuguese; they demand that visitors respect their living spaces. Shoving past an elderly resident on a crowded bus or leaving beer cans in a residential alley leaves a deeper scar than any language barrier ever could.

The true currency of travel is not just the money you spend, but the acknowledgment of the lives unfolding around you. When you buy from a neighborhood shop or match a local's gaze with a polite nod, you step away from being an invader and toward being a guest.

A Century of Sugar and Salt: Majestic Cafe and Lello Bookstore

Opened in 1922, Majestic Cafe pulls Porto's clock hands back a century. Beneath elaborate chandeliers reminiscent of a European palace, travelers pay homage to the enduring rumor that J.K. Rowling drafted early chapters of *Harry Potter* in a corner booth. It is an establishment where aging is worn like a badge of honor.

A sit-down order of hibiscus-infused sangria (about $11 / €10) and *francesinha*—Porto's decadent, heavy-duty sandwich stacked with layers of meat and melted cheese (about $30 / €27)—runs up a total bill of around $41 (51,800 KRW). While steep for a casual lunch, you are paying for the atmosphere of a century-old institution. The *francesinha*, a local evolution of the French *croque monsieur*, is a masterclass in culinary excess, drenched in a spiced tomato and beer sauce that diners routinely wipe clean from the plate.

A short walk away stands Livraria Lello, a bookstore famous enough to require a timed ticketing queue (about $10 / €9). Frequently cited as one of the world's most beautiful bookshops and said to have inspired Hogwarts' moving staircases, it operates more like a theme park for literature lovers than a quiet corner shop. To prevent the space from being overrun purely by selfie-takers, the bookstore deducts the entry fee from any book purchased inside—a clever, pragmatic filter designed to protect the integrity of the space.

Neighborhood Gems and the Golden Hour at Jardim do Morro

Away from the major commercial corridors, neighborhood kitchens like *Tasca dos Mercadores* offer a masterclass in local dining. The house specialty—salt-crusted sea bass—arrives at the table as a dramatic tableside performance. A waiter deftly cracks open the salt shell, strips away the skin, and drizzles olive oil over the steaming white fillets with surgical precision. Paired with a clay-pot octopus rice featuring a crispy bottom crust that hits close to home for anyone who loves scorched rice, it is a reminder of how deeply food anchors a traveler's memory.

As the afternoon wanes, the crowds converge on Jardim do Morro across the river. Visitors can skip the grueling uphill trek by taking the Gaia Cable Car (about $8 / €7 one-way) to reach the cliffside park. As the sun dips toward the Atlantic, painting the Dom Luís I Bridge and the riverbanks in deep amber hues, the square transforms into an impromptu street festival fueled by acoustic guitars and cheap wine.

Unlike some European tourism capitals buckling under the strain of over-commercialization, Porto manages to retain a raw, human warmth. But keeping that warmth alive requires self-restraint from those who pass through.

What to Watch Next

    Support Independent Spaces: Look for local markers, family-run cafes, and shops that reinvest in the community rather than international chains funneling profits offshore. Respect the Commute: Remember that landmarks like São Bento Station and Livraria Lello are active community spaces, not open-air film sets. Keep a Light Footprint: Choose slow-paced experiences—whether sleeping on a restored historic sailboat or walking through residential neighborhoods—that value Porto's longevity over quick consumption.

Porto does not ask much of its visitors. It simply asks that you look closely, speak softly, and leave the view as pristine as you found it.
