The Erosion of Trust: South Korea’s Judiciary Under Fire

September 30, 2026

In South Korea, the judiciary has long been viewed as the ultimate bastion of democratic stability—the neutral referee that stands apart from the volatile arena of partisan politics. However, recent weeks have seen that image crumble. Between allegations of lavish, improperly recorded dining expenses by the Supreme Court Chief Justice and growing concerns over the stalling of trials involving First Lady Kim Keon-hee, the public is beginning to ask a jarring question: Is the judiciary still independent, or has it become a shield for political power?

These controversies are not merely administrative lapses. They represent a fundamental shift in how the nation’s highest court relates to the executive branch, signaling a potential regression to an era where the bench looked over its shoulder before rendering a verdict.

The Chief Justice and the Politicization of the Bench

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the head of the Supreme Court, has recently faced intense scrutiny for behavior that critics argue is overtly political. While a Chief Justice must maintain strict neutrality, Cho’s recent rhetoric and actions have led to accusations that he is catering to the interests of the conservative bloc, specifically the People Power Party (the ruling conservative party). Skeptics worry that he is attempting to position himself as a symbol of conservative restoration rather than a guardian of procedural justice.

The core of the concern is whether the judiciary is acting as a check on power or, as many critics suggest, as an accomplice. When the head of the judiciary appears to be aligned with the sitting President’s agenda, the essence of judicial independence is hollowed out. Whether this stems from a misguided belief that the courts must serve as a conservative bulwark or simply a lapse in judgment, the result is the same: a profound loss of public faith in the rule of law.

The Expense Account Scandal

The breakdown in professional integrity is perhaps most visible in the Supreme Court’s use of operational funds. South Korea’s Anti-Corruption and Bribery Act (often called the Kim Young-ran Act) generally limits meal expenses to roughly $35–$40 (50,000 KRW) per person. Yet, audit reports reveal that the Chief Justice and his office frequented high-end hotels and omakase restaurants, often far exceeding these limits.

Journalistic investigations have uncovered a systemic effort to hide these costs. In several instances, records claimed that large groups were present to artificially lower the "per-head" cost, bringing it under the legal limit. In one case, a restaurant reported that 38 people had dined there, yet the venue only had a physical capacity of 19 seats. This is not just a clerical error; it is the falsification of official documents by the very institution meant to enforce the law. As opposition lawmakers noted during the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee hearings, such blatant manipulation of records for public funds is deeply damaging to the court's reputation.

"The fact that the institution tasked with maintaining the sanctity of the law is manipulating records to bypass it is a sad portrait of today's South Korean judiciary."

The "Kim Keon-hee" Judicial Standoff

The controversy surrounding the judiciary's independence is compounded by the handling of multiple legal cases involving First Lady Kim Keon-hee. The Supreme Court’s decision to refer one of these cases to an en banc (full bench) hearing—a rare and time-consuming procedure—has sparked widespread suspicion that the court is intentionally dragging its feet.

Critics argue that by moving the case to an en banc hearing, the court is effectively delaying a final ruling until the political climate becomes more favorable or the urgency of the matter fades. With four separate cases tied to the First Lady—including allegations involving Deutsch Motors and various election law violations—the public is increasingly impatient. In one instance, a trial took nine months just to reach its first hearing, a pace that many argue ignores the judiciary's own internal rules for timely justice.

The perception is that whenever a case involves the First Lady, the judicial process slows to a crawl or encounters "procedural" roadblocks that prevent a clear resolution. This has led to the persistent, biting criticism that the Supreme Court has become a "playground" for political maneuvering rather than a place for impartial judgment.

What to Watch Next

As the scandal continues to unfold, there are three critical areas to watch in the coming months:

  • The Outcome of Expense Audits: Will there be actual accountability for the falsified expense reports, or will the blame continue to be deflected onto low-level administrative staff?
  • Trial Timelines: Watch for whether the en banc hearings and ongoing lower-court trials speed up or if they continue to stagnate. A continued delay will likely be seen as definitive proof of judicial bias.
  • Public and Legislative Pressure: The National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee will likely continue to probe the administrative failures of the Supreme Court. How Chief Justice Cho responds to these inquiries will determine whether he can salvage any semblance of his office's remaining authority.

Ultimately, the judiciary’s strength is derived entirely from public trust. If the courts are perceived as a shield for the powerful or a tool for political survival, they lose the only power that truly matters: the legitimacy to interpret the law. The coming months will be a litmus test for whether the South Korean judiciary can return to its role as an independent arbiter or if it will continue its slide into a state of political subservience.
