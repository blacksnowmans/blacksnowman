Inside Seoul’s Chip Selloff: Why a U.S. Antitrust Lawsuit Spooked a Fragile Market

November 16, 2026

At the opening bell in Seoul on November 16, South Korean equities looked calm enough, edging up 0.28%. Then 10:00 a.m. arrived, and the floor vanished. By the closing bell, the KOSPI—South Korea’s benchmark stock index—had tumbled 3.59% to finish at 5,100.50 after sliding as far as the 5,060 range intraday, a roughly 190-point drop. The tech-heavy secondary KOSDAQ index fared even worse, cratering 4.86% to 665.64.

For individual Korean investors watching their brokerage apps, the session turned into a rout. Declining stocks outnumbered advancing ones more than two-to-one on the KOSPI and four-to-five-to-one on the KOSDAQ. The epicenter was unmistakable: the country’s heavyweight semiconductor complex. Samsung Electronics fell more than 4% into the 196,000 won range (about $150), while rival memory giant SK Hynix plunged nearly 8%. Affiliates linked to the chip supply chain buckled alongside them, with SK Square—the investment arm that holds a major stake in SK Hynix—dropping 9.7% and component maker Samsung Electro-Mechanics sliding around 8%.

What triggered the sudden freeze was news out of a California courtroom reviving a class-action DRAM price-fixing lawsuit against Samsung, SK Hynix, and U.S. rival Micron Technology. Yet anyone looking closely at American antitrust law knows the lawsuit will take years to resolve and faces steep legal hurdles. The real story behind Seoul’s crash was not the threat of an imminent legal defeat, but a fragile market choked by record foreign selling and mounting anxiety that the artificial intelligence memory boom may be nearing a cyclical peak.

A $1.5 Billion Foreign Exit and a Market Without a Floor

The defining number of November 16 was the sheer scale of foreign capital rushing for the exits. Foreign investors dumped between 1.69 trillion and nearly 2 trillion won (roughly $1.3 billion to $1.5 billion) in net spot equities, concentrating their fire on electronics and heavyweight chipmakers. Domestic retail investors stepped in to absorb about 1.7 trillion won (roughly $1.3 billion) of that supply, but with Korean institutional investors largely stepping aside, individual buyers could not hold the line.

Derivatives trading underscored just how bleak sentiment had turned. Foreigners sold KOSPI 200 futures heavily from the open, only trimming a fraction of those bearish bets in the closing auction—a classic pattern of pressing spot prices lower while profiting on futures. Throughout the day, the KOSPI 200 futures basis stayed firmly negative, a condition known as backwardation. Under normal market conditions, futures should trade at roughly a +1.5-point premium to the spot index to reflect carrying costs. Seeing futures trade well below spot signaled that traders were actively betting on further declines.

That gloom spilled across Asia’s semiconductor supply chain. Taiwan’s benchmark TAIEX sank 5.74%, dragged down by an drop of more than 8% in ASE, a key chip-packaging partner to Nvidia, while Japan’s Nikkei slipped around 2%. On popular Korean financial YouTube channels such as SamproTV, retail traders vented that the domestic market had lost hope. Beneath the frustration lay a structural vulnerability: after liquidity steadily drained out of Seoul’s market from July through September, Korean equities have lacked reliable domestic buyers to cushion sudden foreign outflows.

While semiconductor equipment and substrate suppliers on the KOSDAQ—such as Jusung Engineering and Simmtech—were hammered, capital rotated aggressively into defensive pockets with visible earnings. South Korean non-life insurers rallied on strong third-quarter earnings and expectations of lower claim payouts on indemnity health and auto policies: DB Insurance jumped around 7%, Hanwha General Insurance rose 5%, Hyundai Marine & Fire gained 4%, and both Heungkuk Fire & Marine and Samsung Fire & Marine added 2%. Select exporters and healthcare names also held firm, including Samyang Foods—which defended the 1.2 million won level (about $920) on solid overseas sales and plans to expand its Vietnam factory—alongside Samsung Biologics, Samsung Epis Holdings, aesthetic medical firms PharmaResearch and Hugel, defense leader Hanwha Aerospace, and Hana Financial Group.

The DRAM Antitrust Case and the Shadow of 1999

The legal headline that rattled investors came from the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. A judge denied a motion by Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, and Micron to dismiss a class-action lawsuit brought by 14 consumers and three small PC builders and distributors, clearing the case to proceed toward trial.

The plaintiffs allege that the three memory makers—who dominate global DRAM production—used the AI-driven shift toward High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) as cover to artificially restrict supplies of standard DRAM used in PCs and smartphones. According to the complaint, contract prices for standard 8-gigabit DDR4 PC memory surged 805%, from $2.21 in October 2022 to $20.00 recently, forcing Apple to raise MacBook and iPad prices by 15% to 30% and pushing up gaming console costs. Plaintiffs accuse the manufacturers of funneling capital and advanced packaging resources like through-silicon via (TSV) lines exclusively into HBM, prematurely shutting down older DDR3 and DDR4 production, and reserving long-term supply agreements for giant clients. The chipmakers counter that those moves were independent business decisions needed to recover from the severe 2022 semiconductor downturn and transition to next-generation technology.

For veteran Korean market watchers, the word "collusion" immediately revives painful memories of 1999 to 2002. When DRAM prices fell below production cost back then, manufacturers did collude to prop up prices—sparking a U.S. Department of Justice criminal investigation that ended with a $300 million fine for Samsung, a $185 million fine for Hynix (now SK Hynix), and prison sentences in U.S. federal facilities for several Korean sales executives, while Micron escaped criminal penalties through a leniency deal.

Yet a much more recent precedent points in the opposite direction. In 2018, the class-action law firm Hagens Berman sued the same three companies over the 2016–2017 DRAM price surge, arguing that SK Hynix and Micron followed Samsung’s lead in slowing capacity expansion. A federal court dismissed that suit in 2020, and an appeals court upheld the dismissal in 2022, ruling that adjusting output in response to rivals’ moves is lawful, rational profit-seeking in a free market rather than illegal collusion.

Under Section 1 of the U.S. Sherman Antitrust Act, "conscious parallelism"—watching a competitor cut supply and doing the same—is not a crime. Without a smoking gun such as emails, call logs, or meeting records proving an explicit agreement, plaintiffs face a steep climb.

Even if the latest suit advances, federal antitrust litigation typically grinds through three to four years of discovery, expert testimony, and hearings before reaching any judgment, making a resolution before the second half of 2026 highly unlikely. Treating a procedural denial of dismissal as an imminent multibillion-dollar penalty misreads the legal reality. In an already exhausted market, however, unverified legal risk becomes a convenient excuse to sell.

Nvidia’s Boardroom Drama and the AI Compute Squeeze

The selloff also exposed deeper unease across the global AI hardware trade. Early in the November 16 session, Korean chip stocks briefly found support on rumors of a comeback by Jensen Huang at Nvidia. Investors still long for his charismatic leadership to anchor Nvidia’s growth roadmap, yet the reality inside Nvidia’s boardroom makes an early return unlikely. The company’s 10-member board—featuring heavyweights such as a former Goldman Sachs vice chairman, a former Eli Lilly chief information officer, a former Taco Bell president, a former NFL chief marketing officer, and a Caltech professor—is navigating intense friction between growth ambitions and Washington’s national security agenda. Even with backing from tech figures like Elon Musk, Huang’s aggressive global expansion and appetite for the Chinese market have repeatedly clashed with White House and Commerce Department export controls, leaving investors uncertain about how boldly Nvidia’s new leadership can invest.

At the same time, cracks are showing among the AI industry’s biggest spenders. Dario Amodei, CEO of generative AI startup Anthropic, recently made the case for taking the company public while highlighting the crushing financial strain of securing computing power. Anthropic has committed hundreds of billions of dollars in cloud spending to Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud, yet forecasting how many GPUs and how much HBM a frontier AI lab will need one or two years out is nearly impossible—order too little and fall behind, order too much and buckle under fixed costs.

That tension has ignited a tug-of-war between Big Tech platforms and memory suppliers. Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon have openly balked at the prices of both Nvidia GPUs and the HBM and high-capacity server DRAM supplied by Samsung and SK Hynix. The result is a striking valuation gap in Seoul: even as Samsung’s annual operating profit leaps from a prior 57 trillion won level (about $44 billion) toward a 100 trillion to 130 trillion won era (roughly $77 billion to $100 billion), its share price remains stuck below previous peaks. Financial markets are already pricing in fears that Big Tech capital expenditure cuts, data center power bottlenecks, and interest rates could bring an early "peak-out" for memory earnings—while looming mega-IPOs from Anthropic and SpaceX threaten to pull passive global funds away from incumbent tech stocks.

Why Foreign Capital Favors Micron Over Korean Giants

Comparing the stock performance of the three memory leaders from their February 27 peaks through mid-November reveals how heavily global capital flows dictate winners and losers:

  • Micron Technology: Rose 93.7%, climbing from $412.37 to $798.64, with a maximum drawdown of 36% from its peak—ranking first in both gains and downside resilience.
  • SK Hynix: Gained 14.6%, moving from 1,061,000 won to 1,216,000 won (roughly $815 to $935), but suffered a brutal 59% drawdown from its peak, the worst volatility among the three.
  • Samsung Electronics: Slipped 4.8%, edging down from 216,500 won to 206,000 won (about $167 to $158), with a 45% maximum drawdown.

For U.S. institutional managers looking to hold a single memory name, Micron offers dollar-denominated simplicity without geopolitical overhang. By contrast, with the South Korean won swinging sharply between 1,360 and 1,150 to the dollar, foreign funds have little incentive to sit patiently through currency turbulence in Korean equities.

What to Watch Next in Seoul and Silicon Valley

For investors trying to separate short-term panic from long-term fundamentals, four concrete signposts will determine what comes next:

  • KOSPI 200 futures and local buying: Watch whether the futures basis climbs back out of backwardation toward its normal +1.5-point level, and whether Korean institutional funds step in to cushion foreign spot selling.
  • The California discovery docket: Rather than reacting to procedural headlines, track whether plaintiffs uncover any direct evidence of inter-company coordination during the multi-year discovery phase.
  • Big Tech spending and mega-IPO timing: Listen for signs of capital expenditure restraint at cloud hyperscalers, progress toward IPOs at Anthropic and SpaceX, and how Nvidia’s board balances U.S. export rules against AI demand.
  • Currency stabilization: Until the won stops whipsawing inside its wide 1,150 to 1,360 range, global investors are likely to treat Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix as high-beta trading vehicles rather than steady core holdings, regardless of how fast their profits grow.
