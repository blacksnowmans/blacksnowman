Inside South Korea's Political Meltdown: Why the Ruling Party is Fleeing a Sinking Ship

October 06, 2026

For anyone scrolling through South Korean political YouTube channels or online communities lately, a single refrain echoes continuously: "Give up power." Following a tense presidential election, the current political landscape has found no stability. Instead, a relentless series of explosive disputes has erupted—from friction between the presidential office and the Democratic Party to botched personnel vetting. Even staunch supporters of the Democratic Party, long known for their resilience, now sigh and shake their heads at the daily news cycle. What on earth has turned the entire board into such a mess? On the surface, it looks like garden-variety personnel squabbles over chief of staff nominees or political affairs secretaries. But dig deeper, and you find a self-inflicted wound born from a simple truth: those who wield power have forgotten the basic decency and restraint required to manage it.

The Reluctance to Serve as 'Human Shields'

When the ruling party looks for a new presidential chief of staff, it runs into an awkward reality: nobody wants the job. As President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings plummet into the 30% range, the position has ceased to be an honor and has instead transformed into what insiders call a "funeral party"—a suicide mission to go down with a sinking presidency. After Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik offered to resign to take the blame for failed personnel checks and sagging polls, his successor search drifted into a fog. Key loyalists within the pro-Lee Jae-myung faction whose names were tossed around—such as Representatives Kim Young-jin, Park Sung-jun, Lee Hae-shik, Chun Joon-ho, and Han Jun-ho—all declined to step forward. Even politicians who once acted as if they would give their all for the president suddenly realized they had no appetite for boarding a sinking vessel.

If a sitting lawmaker from the densely populated greater Seoul area is pulled out of parliament to run the presidential secretariat, it triggers a by-election in a region already hostile due to public backlash over real estate policies. If the opposition wins those seats, a government already teetering on lame-duck status would instantly freeze into a vegetative state. As a stopgap measure, the presidential office turned to Kim Min-ki, the current Secretary General of the National Assembly and a former three-term lawmaker from Yongin, Gyeonggi Province, who remains largely unknown to the general public. Critics have dismissed this as a temporary fix—akin to urinating on a frozen foot in winter. Unless the president alters his governing approach and abandons heavy-handed real estate regulations or rushed prosecutorial overhauls, appointing a chief of staff like Kim is nothing more than artificial respiration for a dying administration.

Personnel Friction and the Deafening Silence of the Top

The personnel turbulence does not stop at the chief of staff; it extends to the political affairs secretary slot, where former Senior Presidential Secretary Choi Jae-sung has been heavily rumored as a candidate. Choi, a veteran politician who served four consecutive terms in the National Assembly, was once a core figure in the pro-Moon Jae-in faction. His political career seemed to reach an honorable conclusion in April 2022 when he formally declared his retirement from politics, stating he was stepping down alongside the trials and glories of former President Moon. Yet, with a change in administration, he crept back into the fold, currently serving as a special advisor to Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok.

The moment Choi's name surfaced for the political affairs secretary post, severe pushback erupted among party members and grassroots supporters. Comments online reflect deep skepticism, with critics pointing out that loyalists harbor lingering bitterness over past factional clashes and how Choi handled internal disputes during previous local elections. This resistance stems from a deeper structural flaw: the chronic lack of direct, empathetic communication from the top leadership. As party heavyweights have noted on public broadcasts, when political factions clash, the root of the crisis always traces back to the highest echelons of power. An administration that relies on emotional reactions rather than collaborative systems will inevitably find its public support evaporating.

Rigged Primaries and the Looming Shadow of Seoul's Mayoral Race

The internal rot goes far beyond staffing choices. Discontent is boiling over regarding how the ruling party managed its recent national convention, with members openly calling the process entirely unfair. Promised procedures—such as revealing preference-voting outcomes before finalizing tallies—were bypassed, and specific polling data for certain candidates were withheld while isolated preference results were dumped onto the public. For an administration that rose to power on slogans of fairness and transparency, abandoning basic procedural integrity has deeply alienated its base.

This institutional distrust casts a dark cloud over the upcoming Seoul mayoral by-election. The local political terrain in Seoul is already hostile to the ruling party, and even traditional strongholds in the Jeolla provinces are experiencing a 50-50 split in voter sentiment. Inside the party, a pervasive sense of dread has taken hold; some figures privately joke that they are practically praying the by-election gets canceled altogether to dodge a crushing defeat. Desperate for a viable contender, party leadership has reportedly floated trial balloons to unexpected figures, but these maneuvers look less like genuine talent acquisition and more like desperate window dressing. Meanwhile, opposition figures like Cho Kuk, head of the Rebuilding Korea Party, have signaled intentions to run candidates across all districts, further complicating the progressive coalition's path forward.

Im Eun-jung's Public Rebuke and the Crisis of Reform

Amid this overarching administrative drift, a massive internal explosion occurred when senior prosecutor Im Eun-jung launched a blistering public critique against the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as head of the newly established National Office of Investigation (NOI). Im openly denounced the selection, pointing out past failures to handle disciplinary complaints regarding high-profile misconduct and insisting that the inaugural head of the investigative body must symbolize uncompromised integrity rather than repeating past mistakes.

This unprecedented public rebuke by a figure once considered a symbol of prosecutorial reform sent shockwaves through the ruling bloc. Legal and political analysts interpret Im's outburst through three lenses: first, a tactical reshuffling that placed her in a senior regional leadership role in Gwangju; second, an accumulation of long-simmering frustration over the heavy presence of legacy prosecution figures within the administration; and third, a desperate political warning flare. At a time when partisan loyalists find it difficult to check the administration's missteps, Im stepped forward to air the grievances of reformers who feel betrayed by hollowed-out promises.

What to Watch Next

  • The Chief of Staff Appointment: Watch whether the presidential office ultimately appoints a heavyweight political figure or settles for a low-profile manager, which will signal whether the administration is trying to fight back or simply battening down the hatches.
  • Seoul Mayoral Coalition Talks: Monitor how the Democratic Party handles potential candidate negotiations with minor opposition groups like the Rebuilding Korea Party, which will determine whether the progressive camp enters the upcoming local elections unified or deeply fractured.
  • Prosecutorial Reform Backlash: Keep an eye on how the administration responds to internal dissent from reform-minded public servants like Im Eun-jung, as their continued defiance could trigger a wider rebellion within the state apparatus.
