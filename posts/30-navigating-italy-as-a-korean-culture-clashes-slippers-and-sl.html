Navigating Italy as a Korean: Culture Clashes, Slippers, and Slow Dining

September 24, 2026

Packing for a trip to Italy is usually an exercise in daydreams—picturing cobblestone streets, ancient ruins, and endless plates of pasta. But for many South Korean travelers, those daydreams are quickly punctured by a very specific brand of travel anxiety. Questions that rarely cross anyone's mind back home suddenly loom large: Is it acceptable to wear slides to the local grocery store? Why did the waiter just snatch my dinner plate away while I was still chewing?

For South Koreans, whose daily life is defined by lightning-fast digital infrastructure, maximum operational efficiency, and a culture of ultimate convenience—often summed up by the national ethos of palli-palli ("hurry, hurry")—stepping into Italy can trigger a mild cultural whiplash. In South Korea, stepping out to the corner convenience store in a pair of cheap rubber slides is a national pastime. Speed in restaurants is prized, and salads arrive drowned in dressing as a refreshing prelude. In Italy, however, these everyday habits collide with a deeply entrenched set of social rules regarding aesthetics, pacing, and culinary philosophy. Yet, these moments of friction are rarely about hostility; they are simply the result of two very different ways of moving through the world.

The Slipper Dilemma: Footwear, Fashion, and Social Respect

To an average South Korean, a pair of molded rubber slides—ubiquitously known as "three-stripe slippers"—is the ultimate footwear of convenience. They are slipped on for everything from walking the dog to catching a flight. But in Italy, wearing casual indoor slides out on the street can mark a traveler instantly as a cultural outsider, drawing looks that border on disapproval.

This is not merely about snobbery. In Italian society, dressing with a baseline level of decorum when stepping out into public is viewed as a fundamental sign of respect for the people around you. It is a tacit social contract. While South Korea's urban culture leans heavily into relaxed comfort, Italian street style demands intention. Even in bustling tourist hubs like Milan and Rome, where sheer volume of visitors has numbed locals to eccentric outfits, a sharp eye will notice that local residents rarely abandon their sense of presentation.

Fortunately, not all open-toe footwear is banished under the Italian sun. Thong sandals (infradito) and stylish strappy sandals (sandali) occupy a completely different category. For locals, these are legitimate pillars of summer fashion, worn proudly on the beach or downtown. The line is drawn not at bare feet, but at the *purpose* of the shoe. Leaving the ultra-casual indoor sliders at home and packing a pair of intentional, design-conscious sandals allows travelers to stay cool under the Mediterranean heat without unintentionally offending local sensibilities. It is a minor adjustment that instantly shifts how you are perceived—and how your travel photos turn out.

Why the Waiter Took Your Plate: The Aesthetics of the Course

Few things startle a South Korean traveler quite like a waiter swooping in to clear a half-finished plate of pasta. In a Seoul restaurant, clearing a table prematurely signals that you are outstaying your welcome, or that the staff wants you out to turn the table over. In Italy, it is quite the opposite.

Italian waitstaff are trained professionals who view their work with immense pride. A meal is not a logistical problem to be solved quickly; it is a structured narrative running from antipasto to dolce. Clearing a finished plate swiftly is not a rush-job to kick you out; it is an act of fundamental hospitality, designed to clear the stage for the next movement in the culinary symphony. When a waiter asks, *"Signori, Tutto bene?"* (Is everything alright?), they are checking the pacing of your evening, not rushing your wallet.

This structural approach contrasts sharply with the high-turnover engines of South Korea’s booming dining sector, where quick meals are the default. In traditional Italian trattorias, lingering over a table for two or three hours is an ordinary act of community and relaxation. If you wish to linger longer or simply want your dishes brought out together, a polite phrase like *"All together"* or a reassuring *"Si, tutto bene"* with a smile is all it takes to align expectations.

Salads Are Not Side Dishes: Unraveling the Italian Menu

Another classic point of confusion occurs when a traveler orders a pizza, a pasta, and a salad all at once, only to watch the waiter look bewildered. South Korean dining encourages a decentralized table where all dishes—including vegetable sides—land simultaneously so everyone can pick and share.

In Italy, the menu follows a strict hierarchy: Antipasto, Primo, Secondo, Contorno, and Dolce. What visitors often call a "salad" falls under *Contorno*—essentially a vegetable accompaniment meant to support the main protein (Secondo) course. Ordering it out of sequence disrupts the kitchen's rhythm. Furthermore, when that salad arrives completely naked of dressing, do not assume the restaurant is low-grade or unfriendly. It is an expression of culinary philosophy: the belief that ingredients should be tasted in their pure state, and that seasoning is an intimate act best left to the diner.

Tables across Italy are lined with olive oil, balsamic vinegar, salt, and pepper precisely because dressing a dish beforehand risks compromising the freshness of the greens. Mixing your own dressing at the table is part of the ritual.

Coperto and Coffee: Decoding the Bill

Finally, financial surprises at the end of a meal can sour an otherwise pleasant evening. Many South Korean tourists are startled to find an extra charge labeled *coperto* on their Italian receipt—usually ranging from €1 to €3.50 per person.

Often mistaken by confused visitors as a tourist scam, the *coperto* is actually a standard service and table charge deeply rooted in Italian hospitality economics. Unlike in South Korea, where a table of five might feel social pressure to order five distinct dishes, Italian restaurants are generally relaxed if a group shares plates or orders sparingly. The *coperto* keeps the lights on to compensate for that flexibility, and it is the primary reason why tipping culture does not exist in the same predatory way it does in other Western nations. Understand it as an upfront cost for the right to sit back, order a final espresso or tiramisu, and watch the piazza roll by without feeling rushed.

Traveling is ultimately the art of throwing yourself into unfamiliar structures and letting them reshape your perspective. The rules governing an Italian meal or a stroll down a Roman avenue are not obstacles designed to frustrate outsiders; they are an invitation to slow down. Embracing that slowness might just be the best souvenir you bring home.
