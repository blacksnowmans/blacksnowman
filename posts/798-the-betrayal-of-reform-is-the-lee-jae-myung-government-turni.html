The Betrayal of Reform: Is the Lee Jae-myung Government Turning on Its Own?

September 17, 2026

There is a growing sense of disillusionment among the very people who fought to bring the current administration to power. For supporters of South Korea’s progressive movement, the vision was clear: a democratic government that would finally dismantle the deep-seated abuses of power. Instead, they are witnessing a surreal spectacle—the administration they hand-picked is now turning its blade against its own ideological base. The recent, simultaneous raids on former lawmaker Yoon Mee-hyang and the media outlet Jaju Sibo under the National Security Act are not just administrative actions; to many observers, they feel like the opening shots of a political purge.

Is this a haunting echo of the military dictatorships of the past, or the start of a calculated new political order? To understand what is happening, we must look past the surface of daily news and confront the uncomfortable reality of what is occurring inside the progressive bloc.

Weaponizing Reform: The Return of the National Security Act

The recent use of the National Security Act—a controversial law dating back to 1948 that has long been criticized as a tool to silence political dissent—against progressive media like Jaju Sibo (a publication with ties to the National Liberation, or "NL," faction of the Korean left) has sent a chill through the movement. In a democratic society, law enforcement should handle espionage or insurrection through standard criminal codes. Choosing the National Security Act suggests something else: an intent to frame and suppress specific ideological voices.

When a progressive government uses the same tools once used to oppress its predecessors, it is not "reform"—it is a self-inflicted wound. The goal of the long-standing "Prosecution Reform" movement was to restrain the arbitrary power of the state, not to simply change who holds the whip. By purging internal dissent and enforcing ideological conformity, the current administration risks becoming a mirror image of the very authoritarian structures it vowed to dismantle.

This pattern is mirrored in the treatment of former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae. Choo, a figure once viewed as the face of reform, has recently faced intense attacks for calling out the structural rot within the prosecution. When she likened certain prosecutors to the Nazi bureaucrat Adolf Eichmann, labeling them "spies" for the organization over the public, she was expressing the frustration of a reformer who realized that the system is designed to protect its own at all costs. Instead of listening, the party leadership has allowed her to be sidelined and vilified, signaling that the current power structure values loyalty to the organization over the cause of true reform.

The Shadows of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

The unease is compounded by questions surrounding the "New Lee Jae-myung" government. President Lee Jae-myung, who rose to prominence as a highly capable administrator in Seongnam, is now facing scrutiny over his long-standing ties to various factions within the progressive movement, including the "Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance." While political labels like "NL" (National Liberation) or "PD" (People's Democracy) are often used to categorize the ideological spectrum of the Korean left, the underlying issue today is one of governance: has the administration abandoned its philosophical roots in favor of a "victory-at-all-costs" mentality?

Many supporters who cheered for Lee’s "cider-like" (refreshing and decisive) political style are now worried that this decisiveness has turned into a mechanism to crush criticism. When the President spends his time in the early hours of the morning tracking down and engaging with social media accounts that criticize him, it suggests a leader disconnected from his duty. A democracy is not preserved by silencing voices on social media; it is strengthened by engagement. By alienating core progressive figures—such as Cho Kuk or Chung Cheong-rae—the government is effectively isolating its most principled allies, leaving behind a power base built on conformity rather than conviction.

The Duty of a Democratic Citizen: A Return to Critical Support

The current situation, marked by the filing of complaints against journalists and the aggressive targeting of political rivals even within the progressive camp, feels less like governance and more like a settling of scores. The chilling sentiment among some party insiders—that "if you aren't with the leader, you’re finished"—is a sign that the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party) is losing its way.

If we have learned anything from the history of South Korean democracy, it is that blind loyalty is a path to ruin. We supported Lee Jae-myung because we wanted competence and the strength to wield power for the public good. But power without a clear philosophical compass is merely a tool for domination.

It is time for the democratic citizenry to return to the practice of "critical support." This means backing the government when it aligns with our values, but being the first to raise a voice of dissent when it strays. We cannot afford to be silent observers while the values we fought for are hollowed out from within.

What to Watch Next

  • The Presidential Press Conference: The upcoming public address will be a critical litmus test. Will the administration continue to shift blame onto external actors, or will it acknowledge the growing frustration of its own base?
  • Internal Party Dynamics: Keep an eye on whether figures like Choo Mi-ae or other reform-minded progressives are further marginalized or re-integrated. This will indicate whether the party is narrowing its scope or attempting to broaden its coalition.
  • Legislative Moves: Watch for future appointments to key agencies like the proposed "Major Crime Investigative Agency." If these appointments continue to mirror past patronage structures, it will confirm the trend of "reform in name only."

We remain committed to the progress of this nation, but we will not remain silent while the democratic principles we hold dear are compromised. The history of our struggle has taught us one thing: democracy is not a destination achieved by electing a leader; it is a constant, demanding process of holding power accountable.
