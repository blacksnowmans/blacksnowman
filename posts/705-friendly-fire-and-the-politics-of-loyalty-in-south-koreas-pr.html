Friendly Fire and the Politics of Loyalty in South Korea’s Progressive Movement

October 05, 2026

For anyone watching South Korean politics from the outside, the inner mechanics of the country’s progressive movement can look like a unified front against conservatism. But spend enough time listening to political commentators, YouTube broadcasts, and party insiders, and a very different picture emerges. It is a space fractured by bitter factionalism, where the most vocal defenders of the administration often seem determined to tear down their own allies.

This dynamic was recently brought into sharp relief by You Si-min, a prominent South Korean author, veteran progressive figure, and former minister. In a widely discussed video commentary, You took aim not at the conservative opposition, but at the behavior of pro-government commentators and pundits within the progressive camp. His critique went beyond standard political commentary; it laid bare the uncomfortable reality of how South Korea's reformist politics are being hollowed out from the inside by performative loyalty and vicious friendly fire.

The Illusion of Alignment and the Cowardice of Punditry

To understand why You Si-min’s comments struck such a nerve, one has to look at the peculiar ecosystem of South Korean political media. Following recent presidential press conferences and shifts in the political landscape, commentators who once bent over backward to spin or defend controversial executive decisions have found themselves cornered by the sheer bluntness of presidential remarks—particularly regarding the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, a long-running flashpoint in Korean legal reform.

For years, these pundits have operated as ideological shock absorbers, twisting logic to match whatever position the administration held at the moment. When a leader's actual, unfiltered worldview finally breaks through the public relations packaging, it often causes whiplash. The shock for observers isn't necessarily that a politician holds a certain view; it is the realization that sycophantic commentators spent months constructing elaborate falsehoods to deny what was plainly obvious.

This creates a profound crisis of accountability. A responsible political commentator, when faced with the collapse of their previous arguments, should be willing to reexamine their past assertions and admit error. Instead, much of South Korea's political commentary landscape has devolved into an exercise in shameless spin. Eager to maintain access, secure media slots, or catch the eye of powerful figures in government, these pundits churn out defensive sophistry. They offer personal attacks against critics—and even against moderate figures within their own broad coalition—while treating basic factual consistency as an unnecessary luxury.

When Loyalty Becomes Sabotage

The most damaging aspect of this ecosystem is the phenomenon known locally as "internal shooting"—the practice of progressives attacking other progressives to prove their own ideological purity. Today, tuning into a progressive YouTube channel or political talk show often means watching pundits compete to see who can deliver the harshest attacks against fellow travelers. Figures who have spent decades building the progressive movement are frequently subjected to ad hominem attacks by younger or more opportunistic party staffers and digital commentators trying to signal their allegiance to current power centers.

The irony is entirely lost on these commentators. They convince themselves they are protecting the administration, when in reality, their behavior is a form of political self-harm. Moderate voters and the broader public view these unhinged attacks with exhaustion and disgust. Rather than shoring up support, this performative aggression erodes the coalition's credibility.

Worse still, this dynamic creates a toxic psychological barrier for the electorate. When a low-level party official or a prominent pro-government broadcaster viciously attacks a beloved figure within the movement simply to curry favor, everyday supporters feel a deep sense of betrayal. The psychological link between the voter and the candidate begins to fray. When supporters start thinking, "If this toxic person claims to support my candidate, maybe my candidate isn't worth supporting after all," the political cost is immediate and steep.

Fracturing the Big Tent: Moon, Cho, and Kim

This self-destructive impulse is nowhere more evident than in how certain progressive commentators treat the movement's own heavyweights. The South Korean progressive camp is a broad coalition. Among voters who generally support the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea's main liberal opposition-turned-ruling party, opinions vary wildly. Some are devoted loyalists of former President Moon Jae-in; others view his administration with mixed feelings. Similar divisions exist regarding figures like Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, and influential media figures and podcasters like Kim Ou-joon or prominent politicians like Chung Sye-syun or Jung Cheong-rae.

This diversity of opinion should be a sign of a healthy, pluralistic political ecosystem. Instead, a radical fringe of pro-government commentators has made a habit of openly disparaging former President Moon Jae-in, treating him as a political foil they need to discredit to elevate their current standing.

The strategic illogic of this is staggering. Does any rational person believe that a conservative or independent voter who dislikes Moon Jae-in will suddenly vote for the current progressive agenda just because a pro-government pundit is trashing Moon? Of course not. Swing voters simply watch from the sidelines, laughing at the infighting.

Meanwhile, the core base—such as the voters who powered the Rebuilding Korea Party and gave near-unanimous support to progressive initiatives—watches in dismay as their past leaders are dragged through the mud. When pundits attack figures like Kim Ou-joon or You Si-min to prove their loyalty to current power structures, the effect on presidential approval ratings is swift and downward. Pundits sacrificing broad political solidarity for short-term personal positioning are systematically dismantling the very coalitions that brought them to power.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates its complex political calendar, foreign observers should look past the daily theater of parliamentary debates and monitor three specific indicators within the progressive camp:

  • Shifts in digital media consumption: Pay attention to whether mainstream progressive audiences begin tuning out extremist YouTube channels in favor of more sober, analytical commentary. A drop in viewership for the most aggressive "internal shooters" would signal a grassroots backlash against toxic loyalty contests.
  • Party discipline and messaging: Watch how mainstream party leadership handles officials or allied commentators who engage in public attacks against former leaders and coalition partners. Silence from leadership is often interpreted as tacit approval.
  • Voter alienation in polling trends: Track the relationship between approval ratings and independent or minor-party support (such as the Rebuilding Korea Party base). When internal friction spikes, look for corresponding dips in broader coalition enthusiasm rather than just headline numbers.

Politics is rarely brought down solely by external enemies. As South Korea's current political battles demonstrate, the most dangerous threats often wear the same jersey, hold the same microphone, and claim to fight for the exact same cause.
