Pragmatism Over Posturing: How Lee Jae-myung’s Twin Summits Signal a New Era for South Korean Diplomacy

November 02, 2026

In early November, the diplomatic machinery at the presidential office in Seoul began moving at an unprecedented pace. After months of political volatility following the impeachment and removal of former president Mr. Yun Suk-yeol, South Korea’s foreign policy apparatus is working to regain its footing. President Lee Jae-myung of the center-left Democratic Party of Korea, who assumed office with a mandate to rebuild institutional stability and restore state functions, is hosting back-to-back bilateral summits with two strategic partners: Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and East Timorese President José Ramos-Horta.

To an outside observer, welcoming the leader of Turkey immediately before the head of state of East Timor might seem like an eclectic mix. However, for political and market analysts tracking East Asia, this week of high-level meetings represents a calculated test of President Lee’s pragmatic "sales diplomacy." Under the previous administration of Mr. Yun Suk-yeol, South Korea’s foreign strategy faced widespread criticism for prioritizing rigid ideological alignment over concrete economic interests. Now, the Lee administration aims to demonstrate that South Korea can re-establish itself as a results-oriented global player capable of turning ceremonial handshakes into binding nuclear power contracts, defense industry exports, and expanded diplomatic reach in Southeast Asia.

Turkey, Defense, and Nuclear Power: Rebuilding a Key Bridge to Europe and the Middle East

Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan arrived in South Korea for a state visit spanning November 2 to November 4, with the official summit with President Lee Jae-myung taking place on November 3. This marks the first state visit by a Turkish president to South Korea in eight years, carrying significant symbolic and commercial weight for both nations.

While South Korea and Turkey have long maintained warm historic ties—frequently referring to each other as "brother nations" due to Turkey’s troop contributions during the Korean War—substantive economic output between the two countries has lagged in recent years. High global interest rates and shifting supply chains created hurdles for South Korean companies seeking reliable access points into Middle Eastern and European markets. Furthermore, during Mr. Yun Suk-yeol’s tenure, strategic initiatives in these regions often suffered from foreign policy miscalculations, leaving several high-profile trade negotiations stalled.

The stakes for the November 3 summit center on tangible economic and industrial cooperation. Turkey occupies a crucial geopolitical position bridging Europe, Asia, and the Middle East. As a NATO member with growing influence across Eastern Europe and the Levant, Ankara represents a critical market for South Korea’s expanding export sectors—specifically in nuclear energy construction and defense technology, often referred to globally as "K-defense."

The Industrial Weight of Nuclear and Defense Ties

Turkey is currently pursuing major infrastructure expansion, including new nuclear power plant projects, alongside efforts to modernize its domestic defense capabilities. South Korea’s battle-tested nuclear construction capacity and reliable defense supply chains present an attractive prospective partnership for Ankara. However, converting mutual interest into finalized agreements requires diplomatic finesse and meticulous structural planning.

In past years, Korean industry observers grew weary of premature announcements regarding prospective foreign procurement that failed to materialize into binding contracts. For the Lee administration, the challenge is to move beyond superficial agreements and establish legally sound financial and technical frameworks. Securing multi-billion-dollar deals in energy and defense would expand South Korea’s industrial footprint across Europe and the Middle East, offering a crucial test for Lee's practical approach to international trade.

Expanding South Korea’s Footprint in ASEAN: The Historic Visit from East Timor

Following the conclusion of the Turkish summit, President Lee turns his attention to Southeast Asia. President José Ramos-Horta of East Timor is conducting an official visit to South Korea from November 5 to November 7, featuring a bilateral summit on November 6. This meeting marks the first official visit by an East Timorese head of state to South Korea since the two nations formally established diplomatic relations.

Historically, South Korean foreign policy has focused disproportionately on the "Four Big Powers"—the United States, China, Japan, and Russia. Under Mr. Yun Suk-yeol, this focus often translated into a factional style of diplomacy that neglected horizontal partnerships with emerging partners in Southeast Asia. Engaging with East Timor represents an intentional effort by the Lee administration to broaden South Korea’s diplomatic scope within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN).

East Timor, currently preparing for full accession to ASEAN, holds significant strategic potential. The nation possesses vast marine resources and sits at a critical junction for maritime security and climate vulnerability in the Indo-Pacific. President Ramos-Horta, a Nobel Peace Prize laureate, brings considerable diplomatic standing to discussions that extend beyond standard trade into regional stability, resource development, and climate action.

Building Balanced Partnerships in Southeast Asia

For South Korea, deepening ties with East Timor requires moving past traditional paradigms of basic development assistance or simple labor sourcing. Instead, the focus is shifting toward mutual cooperation in green technology transfer, renewable energy infrastructure, and climate crisis adaptation. By supporting East Timor’s clean energy transition and maritime infrastructure, South Korea can strengthen its standing as a middle-power partner in Southeast Asia while opening new avenues for green tech firms.

The High-Stakes Test of "Sales Diplomacy": Beyond MOUs and Photo-Ops

Both Turkey and East Timor differ vastly in economic size, military capacity, and regional focus. Yet, both summits serve a singular goal for President Lee Jae-myung: proving that South Korean foreign policy can yield measurable economic benefits for domestic industries and citizens.

A recurring pitfall in international summitry is the reliance on non-binding Memorandums of Understanding (MOUs)—agreements that make for impressive press releases but frequently dissolve without government follow-through. To ensure these November summits deliver lasting value, the South Korean government must back them up with detailed implementation roadmaps, tariff reductions, investment guarantee frameworks, and active working-level committees.

South Korea’s export-driven economy remains highly vulnerable to global trade fragmentation and rising geopolitical tensions. In this context, presidential summits must serve as functional business platforms. By actively dismantling regulatory bottlenecks and opening foreign doors for domestic enterprises, state diplomacy directly impacts commercial survival and national economic health.

Key Takeaway: Foreign diplomacy should be evaluated not by ceremonial headlines, but by executable contracts, industrial risk management, and the long-term economic security delivered to domestic businesses and workers.

What to Watch Next in Seoul’s Pragmatic Foreign Policy Push

As South Korea navigates this intensive week of state-level meetings, several concrete indicators will reveal whether this pragmatic diplomatic strategy is delivering results:

  • Nuclear and Defense Follow-Through: Watch for formal joint working groups or technical task forces established between South Korea and Turkey to follow up on nuclear power construction and defense material exports.
  • Commercial Frameworks and Tariffs: Look for tangible progress on trade agreements, tariff adjustments, and mutual investment guarantees with Ankara to help South Korean firms expand across regional supply chains.
  • ASEAN Integration Commitments: Track specific agreements with East Timor regarding climate change adaptation, green energy technology transfers, and joint maritime resource development ahead of Dili’s full ASEAN integration.
  • Institutional Normalization: Observe how efficiently the Lee Jae-myung administration rebuilds career diplomatic channels and institutional processes that were disrupted during the recent period of political turmoil.

For international readers and market observers, South Korea’s November summit diplomacy offers a clear look into the country's post-impeachment direction. If President Lee Jae-myung can successfully translate these bilateral engagements into concrete industrial deals and broader geopolitical alliances, Seoul will take a major step toward establishing a stable, interest-driven foreign policy built to withstand global economic shifts.
