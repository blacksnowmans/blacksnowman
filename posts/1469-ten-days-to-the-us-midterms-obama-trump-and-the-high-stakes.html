Ten Days to the U.S. Midterms: Obama, Trump, and the High-Stakes Shift Facing Seoul

October 26, 2026

Ten days before the American electorate casts its ballots, the atmosphere across battleground states is charged with an intensity that extends far beyond Washington. The upcoming midterms are not merely a contest for legislative majorities; they represent a fundamental referendum on the viability of Donald Trump’s brand of populism and a critical juncture for international security and global supply chains. For foreign observers—and particularly for political strategists in Seoul—the dramatic shifts unfolding in American politics carry immense implications for trade, security, and diplomatic strategy.

Inside South Korea, the administration of President Lee Jae-myung is watching these developments with meticulous attention. As a trade-dependent nation navigating an increasingly fractionated world, South Korea recognizes that changes in Capitol Hill’s leadership directly impact foreign trade tariffs, alliance commitments, and Middle Eastern stability. To understand why this midterm election has captured such intense interest in Seoul, one must look closely at the political heavyweights clashing on the trail, the volatile polling numbers, and the geopolitical wildcards threatening to disrupt the final stretch of the campaign.

A Battle of Political Legacies: Obama’s Return vs. Trump’s Counter-Attacks

The campaign entered a new phase when former President Barack Obama formally broke his silence and stepped directly onto the rally stage. Framing the midterms as a decisive moment to bring an end to "corruption and incompetence," Obama delivered a sharp critique aimed straight at Donald Trump’s governing record and moral standing. By framing Trump’s tenure in the White House as a period where public office was leveraged for personal and family gain, Obama sought to energize the Democratic base and re-engage moderate swing voters who have grown weary of political volatility.

Obama’s return is far more than a routine endorsement tour. For the Democratic Party, his presence provides symbolic authority, framing the choice before voters as a defense of democratic institutions and political decency. His message—succinctly warning voters that "enough is enough"—is designed to stoke anxieties about the prospect of a renewed Trump era and mobilize voters who might otherwise sit out a midterm cycle.

Trump’s response to Obama’s offensive was swift and characteristically aggressive. Rather than engaging in detailed policy debates, Trump focused his counterattacks on personal mockery and crowd-size comparisons. Dismissing Obama as a "terrible president," Trump repeatedly claimed that the former president’s rally venues were virtually empty. This focus on optics and personal insult highlights Trump’s reliance on populist mobilization, where maintaining an aura of rally dominance takes precedence over structured policy arguments.

Adding a paradoxical twist to the closing days of the campaign was Trump’s own participation in early voting. For months, Trump had publicly denounced early and mail-in voting, describing the process as a "stupid system" vulnerable to widespread fraud and urging his supporters to vote strictly on election day. Yet, accompanied by his wife, Trump cast his ballot early, creating a stark contradiction between his public rhetoric and personal actions. For critics, this inconsistency underscores a transactional approach to election rules, where institutional processes are vilified when convenient but utilized when individual votes are on the line.

A Poll Crash, Pre-Emptive Lawsuits, and an October Security Surprise

The political rhetoric comes against the backdrop of troubling numbers for the Trump camp. Recent opinion polls show Trump’s approval rating dropping to 31%, with Democrats building a lead of up to 12 percentage points in key generic congressional matchups. A decline of this magnitude so close to election day has sent shockwaves through Republican circles, casting serious doubt on earlier projections of a decisive conservative sweep of both the Senate and the House of Representatives.

This steep decline reflects persistent public anxiety over transparent governance and ongoing legal controversies from Trump's previous term. In a telling sign of the internal alarm within Trump’s inner circle, key aides and advisers have reportedly begun retaining defense attorneys in anticipation of aggressive congressional investigations should Democrats capture or retain legislative control. Winning or maintaining political influence has transformed from a matter of policy execution into an urgent strategy for legal self-preservation.

Faced with mounting headwinds, Trump’s campaign appears to be preparing the groundwork to challenge the legitimacy of the election results if the outcome proves unfavorable. In battleground areas such as Alaska, legal filings have already been initiated to delay or halt the certification of election tallies. By raising pre-emptive allegations of voting irregularities, the campaign is setting up a narrative of election denialism similar to previous cycles, raising severe concerns about post-election stability and institutional trust.

Compounding these domestic tensions is an unprecedented national security development. Reports indicate that the U.S. Central Command has submitted a detailed military strike plan targeting Iran to the Secretary of Defense, which now sits on the White House desk awaiting final executive approval. Intelligence reports have escalated warning levels to the highest tier regarding alleged Iranian assassination threats against American figures. Should military action be authorized in the final days before the election, a sudden conflict in the Middle East would drastically redefine the campaign landscape, forcing voters to weigh high-stakes foreign policy emergencies alongside domestic concerns.

Protectionist Tariffs vs. Economic Reality: The Trade Dilemma for Export Economies

Economic policy remains another sharp point of divergence between the two political camps. Trump has vigorously championed his protectionist platform, pointing to a 5.6 trillion won (around $4.2 billion) investment decision by German automaker Audi as proof that his tariff threats force foreign manufacturers to bring capital and production into the United States. In contrast, Obama and Democratic economists argue that broad, indiscriminate tariffs act as a regressive tax on domestic consumers and disrupt global value chains, ultimately inflicting long-term harm on the broader automotive sector.

This economic debate carries massive ramifications for export-driven nations like South Korea. Under the leadership of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea’s government has emphasized pragmatic, national-interest-driven diplomacy designed to protect domestic manufacturers while maintaining strong trade ties with major global powers. South Korean business conglomerates in key sectors—such as electric vehicles, semiconductors, and batteries—are highly vulnerable to abrupt shifts in U.S. trade policy.

For international readers, understanding South Korea's current political context adds essential clarity to how Seoul views Washington. Following the chaotic end of the previous presidential period—when former leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was impeached, removed from office, and subsequently imprisoned—South Korea under President Lee Jae-myung has sought stability, institutional integrity, and predictable economic strategy. The main opposition party, the conservative People Power Party, continues to debate foreign policy direction, but the ruling Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung is focused on ensuring that South Korea's economic interests remain protected regardless of which party controls the U.S. Congress.

While economic models suggest that inflation trends under a hypothetical second Trump term might eventually cool faster than during the Biden era, public sentiment toward Trump’s economic messaging remains lukewarm. Voters appear increasingly concerned that the policy unpredictability and trade friction associated with his approach could induce market volatility rather than lasting stability. For trade partners like South Korea, navigating American protectionism requires preparing for both tariff pressures and supply chain shifts.

What to Watch Next as Washington Approaches the Finish Line

As the final countdown to election day proceeds, several key indicators will determine the trajectory of both U.S. domestic politics and international policy:

  • Congressional Control and Judicial Vulnerability: Watch whether Democrats maintain or expand their margin in the House and Senate. A Democratic victory will likely lead to immediate congressional subpoenas and renewed investigations into Trump's executive actions, while a Republican majority could usher in immediate legislative pushes for aggressive protectionist trade bills.
  • White House Decision on the Iran Strike Plan: The status of the U.S. Central Command's proposal regarding Iran is the most critical geopolitical variable. Any executive order approving military action prior to election day would immediately surge global oil prices, destabilize energy markets, and reorder voter priorities.
  • Post-Election Legal Challenges: Pay close attention to early litigation filed in contested states like Alaska. The speed and scope of these lawsuits will signal whether the post-election period will feature prolonged legal battles over certification or a standard transition of legislative power.
  • South Korean Trade Scenario Planning: In Seoul, monitor how President Lee Jae-myung’s foreign ministry and trade negotiators adjust their contingency plans. Key focus areas include potential adjustments to automotive tariffs, clean energy subsidies, and bilateral alliance cost-sharing discussions once the makeup of the new U.S. Congress is finalized.

The final ten days of this midterm campaign will test the resilience of American democratic norms and send ripples across global financial and diplomatic capitals. Whether through high-stakes economic debates, historic former presidential clashes, or sudden national security crises, the outcome in Washington will reverberate far beyond America's borders.
