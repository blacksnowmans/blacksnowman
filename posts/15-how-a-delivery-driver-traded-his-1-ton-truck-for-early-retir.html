How a Delivery Driver Traded His 1-Ton Truck for Early Retirement and a Steady Dividend Stream

August 30, 2026

For fourteen years, the daily routine began the same way: gripping the steering wheel of a one-ton delivery truck while painkillers fought a quiet, losing battle against throbbing ankles, knees, and a chronic lower back. Working as a logistics delivery driver for a major supermarket chain in South Korea is a grueling test of endurance. Instead of rewarding diligence with security, the job wore down the body into a fragile, unpredictable structure. Taking a single day off meant scrambling to find a replacement vehicle and paying out of pocket anywhere from 200,000 to 300,000 won (about $150 to $220) in daily rental fees—meaning even rest came at the cost of going into debt. Trapped in a cycle where taking a break was a financial penalty, the question was no longer how to succeed, but how much longer the body could survive inside that cramped cab.

That breaking point—the realization that staying meant physical collapse—sparked a radical exit strategy. For workers outside Korea, the FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) movement is often associated with tech professionals or corporate climbers banking millions. But in South Korea's high-pressure labor market, early retirement is increasingly pursued by blue-collar workers reclaiming bodily autonomy. This is the story of how one former delivery driver built a hybrid lifestyle of dividend investing and flexible gig work to walk away from physical burnout, capturing a sustainable monthly cash flow without a massive corporate nest egg.

Building a Psychological Safety Net on Blue-Collar Wages

Before handing in a resignation letter, the math had to be mercilessly calculated. Romantic notions of quitting without a plan are a fast track to financial ruin, particularly in an economy facing persistent inflation. The first psychological milestone was hitting 200 million won (about $145,000) in liquid financial assets. To critics in Seoul's hyper-inflated real estate market, that sum might not even cover a standard apartment lease deposit. Yet, seeing that number in a bank account fundamentally altered the power dynamic of daily labor.

Armed with the quiet confidence that he could walk away at any moment, the author found himself immune to petty workplace grievances and unreasonable demands. Money ceased to be merely a medium for consumption and became a boundary defending personal dignity. Still, a safety net requires rigorous simulation. Before quitting permanently, test runs were conducted using bicycle delivery gigs to gauge hourly earnings and physical fatigue. The blueprint crystallized: 1 million won (about $730) per month in dividends from a 100-million-won stock portfolio, combined with another 1 million won earned by pedaling a bicycle for three hours a day, yielding a baseline cash flow of roughly 2 million won (about $1,460) per month. Only after countless spreadsheet simulations did the resignation letter leave his pocket.

Taming the High-Yield Covered Call Jungle

High-yield covered call exchange-traded funds (ETFs) have a controversial reputation in financial circles, often criticized for eroding principal value during sharp market corrections. Yet, integrated carefully into a practical retirement strategy, they can serve a distinct purpose. Utilizing funds like NVDY—an ETF tied to U.S. tech giant Nvidia that generates high option premiums—the investor collected enough monthly payouts over a two-year span to fully recover his original capital.

Once the initial principal is recouped, the risk equation shifts entirely, transforming the asset into a high-yielding, infinite-return cash machine. While mainstream financial textbooks warn of the capped upside and downside exposure inherent to covered calls, retirees prioritizing immediate monthly liquidity view portfolio evaluation differently. When market downturns strike, panic-selling is replaced by a stoic philosophy: even if dividend payouts are sliced in half by market volatility, the physical capacity to pick up bicycle delivery shifts provides an infallible backup engine. Resilience, in this view, is rooted less in complex financial engineering and more in physical elasticity.

Redefining Daily Rhythms After Leaving the Rat Race

Retirement often conjures images of sleeping in until noon, but true post-work stability requires structure. Mornings now begin at 5:30 AM with economic broadcasts and a warm send-off for his working wife. Rather than fleeing an unwanted workplace, the day is deliberately engineered through physical and mental discipline: a rigorous three-hour bicycle ride along the Han River to manage blood sugar and cardiovascular health, followed by coursework toward a degree in English Language and Literature at the Korea National Open University.

Without structured habits, early retirement risks slipping into isolation and lethargy. Solo explorations on the subway to unfamiliar neighborhoods, spontaneous meals at quiet local diners, and quiet evenings sharing a coffee with his wife anchor a lifestyle that proves true freedom is not about doing nothing, but about mastering one's own schedule.

Scaling Toward a Dual-Income Retirement Blueprint

The long-term vision now extends to a coordinated couple's retirement, targeting a total net asset baseline of 600 to 700 million won (about $440,000 to $510,000). To support his wife's upcoming exit from the workforce, a four-pillar pension fortress has been designed:

  • Personal Pension Accounts: Systematically built up to maximize tax advantages upon withdrawal.
  • Housing Pensions: Utilizing their primary residence to secure a lifelong government-backed reverse mortgage annuity.
  • Early National Pension: Claiming state pension benefits slightly ahead of schedule to bridge early cash-flow gaps.
  • Dividend Pipelines: Reinvesting in high-yield assets to generate continuous passive income.

When these four gears turn in sync, the household expects a combined cash flow of roughly 4 million won (about $2,900) per month—a modest sum by metropolitan standards, but ample for a peaceful life once the trap of social comparison is dropped. For those standing at the threshold of early retirement, the advice remains intensely practical: start small, buy a tiny slice of a dividend stock, and actually use those first few dollars of payout to buy a cup of coffee. Experiencing capital work on one's behalf for the first time is the only way to truly decode the rules of modern capitalism.
