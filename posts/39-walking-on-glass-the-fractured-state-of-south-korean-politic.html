Walking on Glass: The Fractured State of South Korean Politics

September 24, 2026

In the digital age, politics in South Korea often feels like a high-stakes performance played out on smartphone screens. A single social media post can trigger a national firestorm, and a stray comment from a public official can leave deep scars on the electorate. It is exhausting, precarious, and—to the average observer—increasingly volatile. From the recent social media controversies surrounding Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the Democratic Party (the main liberal opposition), to the ongoing legal battles involving Han Dong-hoon, a prominent figure in the ruling People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party), the current climate reveals a society struggling with profound, festering divisions.

The Politics of Retweets: Why Words Still Matter

The recent controversy involving Lee Jae-myung’s social media activity highlights a modern political paradox: the heavy burden of digital silence. When Lee, a major political leader, retweeted a post, he faced immediate backlash. His defense was simple: he cannot vet the entire history of every account he chooses to amplify, and sharing a sentiment does not mean endorsing the author’s entire character. Yet, in the eyes of the public, the nuance was lost. When the original author of the post was identified as someone known for toxic partisan rhetoric, the backlash was swift. Critics argued that such actions invite unnecessary scrutiny and damage the leader's credibility.

This incident reflects a deeper, more cynical reality in Korean politics. The public often assumes that everything a politician does is calculated. When a leader claims they were "unaware" of a post's context, the public reaction is rarely one of forgiveness. Instead, they ask, "How could they not have known?" This creates a trap for public figures; even a moment of casual interaction on social media is treated as a strategic manifesto. As observers have noted, for a public figure today, social media is an "apple with no antidote." It promises connection but frequently delivers political poison.

The underlying issue, however, goes beyond mere social media etiquette. It is a symptom of a society where the wounds of long-term conflict have not healed. When politicians use "exclusionary language"—demonizing the "other" to solidify their own base—they are not stitching up societal wounds; they are rubbing salt into them. True healing will require, at minimum, a move away from the politics of hate and toward a more tempered, respectful discourse. Until public figures prioritize the quality of their language, they will continue to find themselves caught in the very traps they set for their opponents.

The Han Dong-hoon Saga: Justice or Political Warfare?

If social media represents the superficial friction of Korean politics, the legal battles surrounding Han Dong-hoon, the former Minister of Justice, represent its structural breakdown. The political landscape was recently shaken when Kim Seung-won, a Democratic Party candidate, resigned just a day after being embroiled in a controversy regarding "public standards."

Critics of the ruling party have framed this moment as a turning point in the push for a special counsel investigation into what they call "fabricated prosecutions." They argue that Han Dong-hoon stands at the center of a web of controversial investigations—ranging from the high-profile case involving a South Korean official killed near the maritime border to allegations of data manipulation within government agencies. Proponents of the special counsel argue that Han was not merely a supervisor but a "field commander" for these politically charged investigations, using the power of the state to target political rivals.

The stakes are high. While some cases have resulted in acquittals, the political fallout remains immense. For instance, a recent case involving a business transaction where nearly $700,000 (1 billion KRW) was at the center saw an acquittal in the second trial—a result the opposition claims proves the prosecution’s initial narrative was flawed. Despite these judicial setbacks, observers note that figures like Han often refuse to acknowledge the court’s findings, preferring instead to characterize any criticism as a political attack. This refusal to respect the judicial process, according to critics, suggests a fundamental disregard for the rule of law.

The controversy is now extending to allegations of online manipulation, specifically involving the ruling party’s internal bulletin board. With police raids on servers already conducted, the results of this investigation could prove to be another major "judicial risk" for the ruling bloc. As the Democratic Party gathers consensus on the necessity of a special counsel, the question is no longer whether they will push back, but how effectively they can navigate the legal and political minefield Han Dong-hoon has constructed around himself.

The "Stone in the Shoe": A Metaphor for Reform

Perhaps the most poignant analysis of the current situation comes from the South Korean intellectual Rhyu Si-min, who recently used the metaphor of a "stone in the shoe." He argued that the current president is walking in a "shoe" chosen by the public—a reality he may not have wanted, but one he must live with. When the shoe is uncomfortable, every step is painful. But the real problem is not just the shoe; it is the stones that have found their way inside.

These "stones"—a metaphor for the toxic political advisors, the baggage of past scandals, and the refusal to address core issues—are what cause the isolation of the leadership. Rhyu suggests that the president is so focused on the pain of walking that he has failed to stop, take off the shoe, and remove the debris. In this view, the president is not merely suffering from bad luck; he is suffering from his own refusal to clear out the obstacles that are preventing him from moving forward.

The lesson here for all of Korean politics is clear: you cannot walk toward the future if you are constantly crippled by the present. Whether it is the need for genuine judicial reform or a cooling of partisan rhetoric, the "stones" in the national shoe must be removed. Ignoring them, or pretending they aren't there, only leads to more limping, more pain, and eventually, a total halt in progress.

What to Watch Next

  • The Special Counsel Push: Watch for the Democratic Party’s formal move to launch an investigation into the "fabricated prosecutions." The consensus within the party is solidifying, and the next legislative session will be critical.
  • The Bulletin Board Investigation: Keep an eye on the results of the police investigation into the ruling party's internal online forums. If evidence of organized manipulation by leadership surfaces, it could trigger a new, explosive round of political upheaval.
  • Judicial Outcomes: Upcoming court rulings related to bribery and abuse of power allegations against various political figures will act as a barometer for whether the "judicial risk" narrative holds water or if the current administration can successfully shift the blame.
