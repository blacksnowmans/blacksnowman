The Truth Behind South Korea’s Cooling Property Market

September 24, 2026

If you have been following the news in South Korea lately, you are likely familiar with the "head-spinning" state of the real estate market. For many, the dream of owning a home feels increasingly like a fantasy, and the frequent maps showing fluctuating apartment prices have become a source of daily anxiety. A recent expert analysis, featuring economic commentator Lee Bong-woo, breaks down the current "housing crisis" to reveal that the market's recent shifts are not the result of natural cooling, but of a specific, high-stakes game of cat-and-mouse between the government and property owners.

The "Ma-Yong-Seong" Phenomenon and the Tax Sting

The conversation centers on the so-called "Ma-Yong-Seong" districts: Mapo, Yongsan, and Seongdong. These three areas in Seoul have long been the gold standard for real estate, witnessing explosive growth that effectively priced out the average citizen. Recently, however, the momentum in these areas has stalled. While home prices are still listed at record highs, the actual number of completed sales has plummeted. It is a stalemate: sellers are holding onto high prices, but buyers have stopped signing the contracts.

Contrary to popular belief, this cooling is not because people suddenly stopped wanting to live in these prime neighborhoods. Instead, it is a direct consequence of government tax policy. Lee Bong-woo argues that the stagnation in "Ma-Yong-Seong" is a direct result of aggressive tax hikes targeting luxury apartments—specifically those valued at over 3 billion won (about $2.2 million)—in the traditional elite strongholds of Gangnam, Songpa, and Seocho.

When the government increases holding taxes (property taxes) and restricts capital gains tax exemptions, the math for investors changes immediately. If holding a property becomes a "tax bomb" and selling it yields little profit due to high capital gains taxes, the motivation to hoard luxury housing evaporates. For many, the decision to "dump" their properties onto the market is not a choice, but a defensive move against mounting financial pressure. This proves that the market does respond to policy—but only when the measures are punitive enough to force a change in behavior.

The Gap Between Presidential Optimism and Reality

While the data shows a forced cooling in certain sectors, there is a dangerous disconnect between this reality and the narrative pushed by the South Korean presidential office. The current administration appears to operate under the optimistic belief that stabilizing the high-end Gangnam market will naturally cause a "trickle-down" effect, cooling the rest of the country. History, however, has repeatedly proven this theory wrong.

This outlook is viewed by critics as either dangerously naive or a fundamental misunderstanding of market mechanics. Whenever the government signals that "stability is coming soon," it risks lulling the public into a false sense of security while failing to address the underlying causes of the crisis. In reality, while the center of Seoul faces regulatory pressure, the surrounding regions and the broader capital area continue to see prices rise—a classic "balloon effect," where squeezing one part of the market causes the pressure to bulge elsewhere.

The reliance on short-term fixes rather than structural reform leaves young people and the middle class in a state of perpetual instability. By failing to acknowledge the severity of the situation, the government loses its credibility, and the market, which moves based on liquidity and profit logic rather than political rhetoric, continues to operate in its own chaotic orbit.

The Missing Link: Sustainable Supply and Structural Reform

The core issue, as noted by Lee, is that the government’s approach to supply remains vague. While officials frequently announce plans to increase housing supply, they rarely specify where, how, or for whom these homes are being built. Providing incentives for developers often leads to speculative, low-quality construction rather than the stable, public-led housing options that low-income citizens and youth actually need.

To break the "invincible real estate" myth, South Korea requires more than just stop-gap tax adjustments. It needs:

  • A Concrete Supply Roadmap: Moving away from developer-friendly projects toward long-term, public-sector housing.
  • Consistent Tax Enforcement: Moving beyond oscillating tax rates that change with political tides to a firm, permanent structure that makes property hoarding economically non-viable.
  • A Shift in Economic Philosophy: Re-establishing the principle that homes are for living, not for financial speculation.

What to Watch Next

Moving forward, the critical factor will be the consistency of government policy. If the administration continues to waver under the pressure of wealthy property owners or reverts to loosening regulations at the first sign of economic friction, the market will remain a "ticking time bomb."

Observers should watch for the government’s next specific land-use and supply announcements. If these policies continue to favor private profit over public necessity, we can expect the current stalemate to break in favor of further price volatility. True leadership in this sector would mean abandoning the "wait-and-see" approach and taking the politically difficult steps necessary to curb speculation once and for all. Until then, the housing market will remain a place where statistics reflect the health of the economy, but individual stories reflect the deep, unresolved frustrations of the public.
