Decoding Modern Stress: Brain Science, Control Obsession, and Simple Ways to Reset Your Mind

September 14, 2026

We live surrounded by a relentless daily barrage of stimulation. The moment we wake up, smartphone notifications begin to flash, followed by the endless pressure of work, and that quiet, lingering anxiety about the future that presses down on a corner of our minds. Dealing with neural stimulation and psychological stress is no longer just a matter of medical trivia—it may be the single most essential survival skill for anyone navigating the modern world. Why on earth do our hearts race over the smallest trifles, driving ourselves deeper into traps of our own compulsive worry?

At the intersection of cutting-edge brain science and mental health medicine, experts offer a fascinating analysis of what stress actually is. When we examine insights ranging from physical stimulation techniques suggested by professor Moon Keun-shik to patterns of obsessive thinking diagnosed by psychiatrist Choi Myung-ki, we begin to realize just how much we have been pushed around by vague, formless anxieties. It is worth examining these complex psychological mechanisms together and pondering concrete ways to anchor ourselves in our everyday lives.

Awakening the Brain Through Physical Stimulation: The Optic Nerve Connection

The method proposed by Moon Keun-shik is remarkably intuitive and immediate: take your thumb and firmly press down on a specific hollow spot on your skull. Applying strong pressure for 10 seconds—until the skin turns pale—activates the optic nerve, producing a noticeable brightening of the vision. This is not merely a placebo effect; it is rooted in human anatomy. That specific region sits right along the pathway of major blood vessels supplying vital nutrients and oxygen to the optic nerves leading to the eyes.

When we stare at computer monitors or smartphone screens for hours and feel our vision blurring, what we actually need may not be expensive supplements, but this kind of physical improvement in blood flow. Considering that the brain is a massive organ ultimately dependent on a delicate, sensitive network of blood vessels, this 10-second habit can serve as a highly efficient break for busy modern people. For those who are far too busy to care for themselves, such a brief window of time paradoxically becomes the greatest form of liberation.

Of course, this method will not cure every form of failing eyesight. However, it functions capably as a "reset button" when the brain has slipped into overload and the nervous system has grown dull. Experiencing it firsthand, the fatigue around the eyes noticeably recedes. Before hunting for grandiose wellness regimens, perhaps we should start with the most basic physical stimuli our bodies are asking for.

The True Nature of Stress: Between Control and Compulsion

Psychiatrist Choi Myung-ki identifies "predictability" and "control" as the core criteria determining the scale of our stress. Stress derived from predictable events is relatively manageable because we can prepare for the blow to some degree. The real trouble arises when an entirely unexpected crisis hits, or when we feel utterly powerless to fix it.

Uncertainty and Anxiety: Even in states of "boredom and emptiness" where supposedly nothing is happening, human beings experience stress. The human brain possesses an instinctive drive to constantly predict and control its environment. Ultimately, stress is not a problem of the external environment, but the manifestation of our brain's desperate hunger to control that environment.

Many people ask, "What should I do to avoid feeling stressed?" Yet by trying to wrestle with every single unpredictable event beyond our reach, we sink deeper into the quagmire. Separating what is within our control from what is not sits at the very heart of mental health. We need to practice accepting that the unexpected is simply a natural part of life.

In fact, well over 90 percent of our lives are filled with unpredictable variables. When we deny this and attempt to control everything perfectly according to plan, tragedy begins. As Choi's analysis suggests, our vulnerability to stress stems precisely from our obsession with absolute control. We must pause and ask ourselves whether the issues weighing most heavily on our minds today reside squarely in the realm of the uncontrollable.

Astonishingly, about 90 percent of human stress responses tie directly back to obsessive tendencies. Choi's remark that "nine out of ten people suffer from obsession" is hardly an exaggeration. Fretting over trivial matters in advance, writing out catastrophic future scenarios that never actually happen, and losing sleep over them—all of these processes stem from compulsive mental traits.

Concrete Tools to Melt Away Anxiety

The tools introduced to manage these states are remarkably practical. Items like squeeze toys, textured sensory balls, and prayer beads are far more than mere toys. They act as vital "channels" through which individuals grappling with obsessive anxiety can vent surplus energy and relax their frayed nerves.

Moving one's fingers continuously or performing repetitive physical actions helps distribute the cognitive overload hitting the brain's frontal lobe. Through meaningless repetition, the brain sends a quiet signal to itself: "I am safe right now."

Outsiders might mock these items, asking why adults are playing with such things. The reality is quite the opposite. Those who successfully manage their anxiety are capable. The act of actively seeking out tools to calm an overheating brain is an exceptionally sophisticated form of mental self-care. For the moments one is squeezing a fidget toy, worries about the future temporarily halt, allowing full immersion in the present sensation.

The reason prayer beads and rosaries survive beyond religious utility is precisely because of this dynamic. Repetitive sensory stimulation quickly guides the human brain back to a state of equilibrium. There is no need to seek out an elaborate meditation center. Keeping a small fidget object on your desk, or developing the habit of holding a comforting item in your hand, can serve as an exceptional shield protecting your mental health.

Conclusion: Cultivating Healthy Habits to Soothe Yourself

Reflecting on brain science and mental well-being reveals a fundamental truth: the intense stress we experience largely stems from two pillars—brain fatigue and the relentless desire for control. While physical blood-flow stimulation relieves the brain's physiological exhaustion, management of compulsive thinking addresses psychological fatigue. Both approaches ultimately converge on a single objective: self-care.

Our generation exists under the constant pressure to be evaluated, to compete, and to achieve. Yet sometimes, the act of pressing your fingers into your skull for 10 seconds or rolling a stress ball in your palm can represent a truly revolutionary form of resistance. Rejecting the relentless efficiency demanded by society and tuning into the natural rhythm of your own body and mind—this may well be the ultimate survival tactic in an age of stress.

Coping with stress is ultimately about changing how we view ourselves, not changing the world. Are we pouring so much energy into uncontrollable things that we neglect our own vulnerable blood vessels and nerves? Right now, try finding that hollow spot near your temples with your thumb. Within the brief, refreshing relief of that 10-second press, your brain will undoubtedly feel a deep sense of reassurance.

If you feel anxious, let yourself be anxious; if you feel compulsive, let yourself be compulsive. After all, it proves you are alive. Just try not to sink too deep. Keep a soothing object in your hand and a bit of breathing room in your heart. We are far more resilient creatures than we realize. So tonight, set tomorrow's worries aside for a while, and trust that simply resting is enough. Tomorrow's brain will be just a little bit clearer than today's.

What to Watch Next

  • Notice your physical warning signs: When your eyes grow dry or your temples tighten during the workday, resist the urge to push through and test a 10-second physical reset instead.
  • Audit your control issues: Whenever anxiety spikes, pause to write down whether the source of your stress falls strictly within your circle of control.
  • Find a physical anchor: Experiment with simple sensory objects—like a small tactile toy or smooth stones—to keep on your desk for moments when mental overload threatens to take over.
