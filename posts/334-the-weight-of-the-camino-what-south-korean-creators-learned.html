The Weight of the Camino: What South Korean Creators Learned Packing for Santiago

September 07, 2026

Before you ever set foot on the historic trails of northern Spain, a far more immediate and brutal trial awaits on your living room floor: packing the backpack. For anyone dreaming of tackling the more than 800-kilometer (approx. 500-mile) journey across the Camino de Santiago, every single gram dropped into that main compartment becomes a matter of survival. Recent deep-dive videos from South Korean travel creators breaking down their actual gear lists have sparked a widespread online conversation among outdoor enthusiasts back home, laying bare the painful gap between what you think you need for a month-long trek and what your body will actually tolerate.

Watching these veteran hikers dissect their choices highlights a universal dilemma for modern travelers. When you absorb every packing tip on YouTube, the temptation is to over-engineer your loadout, transforming a simple walking trip into a portable storage unit. Stripping away the excess isn't just about physical comfort; it is a confrontation with the anxieties we carry from modern life into the wilderness. Here is a look at what South Korean travelers discovered works, what fails miserably, and why the hardest part of the Camino is deciding what to leave behind.

Footwear and Backpacks: The Foundation of Survival

The journey begins with the two items that will either make your pilgrimage a spiritual awakening or a month-long exercise in orthopedic torture: the backpack and the boots. Popular Korean creator breakdowns emphasize that your pack—such as the Osprey Kestrel 48L (scored second-hand via Korea's popular peer-to-peer marketplace, Danggeun Market, for about $110, down from its retail price of around $200)—must be fitted in person. Among hikers, brands like Osprey, Deuter, and Gregory dominate, but a hip belt that fails to match your torso length will turn long-distance walking into a slow-motion catastrophe.

Footwear demands the same uncompromising pragmatism. High-end trail shoes, like Hoka models retailing between $230 and $250, earn high praise for comfort, but proper waterproofing is non-negotiable, particularly for damp spring conditions. Statistical averages show pilgrims walking 20 to 25 kilometers (12 to 15 miles) a day; shoes that haven't been properly broken in at home will shred your feet by week one. Experienced voices emphasize a golden rule of long-distance trekking: your total pack weight should never exceed 10% of your body weight. If you weigh 60 kilograms (132 pounds), your absolute limit is 6 kilograms (13 pounds). Break that rule, and the Camino stops being a challenging walk and becomes active penal servitude.

The Ghost Cargo: Overpacking and Digital Dead Weight

The human instinct to pack for "just in case" scenarios produces some remarkably heavy regrets. Creators looking back on their luggage inventories often laugh at the items they dragged across Europe out of sheer anxiety. Among the casualties of common sense are oversized plastic garment bags originally brought to shield backpacks, and sets of six heavy padlocks and steel cables hauled under the mistaken impression that Spanish hostels are hotbeds of brazen thievery. Neither was used even once.

Equally burdensome are the artifacts of modern convenience. Bringing a pink hair dryer or a heavy 13-inch laptop weighing over 1.2 kilograms (nearly 3 pounds)—intended for video editing on the road—added massive, dead weight to daily marches. Electronics, too, often overshoot reality. Purchasing hefty cellular data packages proved unnecessary when hostel Wi-Fi and basic navigation apps easily covered daily needs. The takeaway from seasoned Korean travelers is stark: three changes of clothes are plenty. Albergues—the traditional pilgrim hostels—feature washers and dryers, making multiple clothing sets redundant. If you try to avoid the minor chore of daily laundry, your lower back and knees will pay the physical tax.

Protecting the Engine: Blisters, Knees, and Friction

When the trail turns hostile, the enemy is rarely the topography; it is friction. The relentless repetition of tens of thousands of steps creates blistering heat between toes and heels. Korean travel veterans swear by a simple, low-tech hack: ordinary petroleum jelly, picked up cheaply at local discount stores like Daiso, transferred into a twist-up lip balm tube. Applying it to hotspots mid-walk without getting greasy fingers on your hands saves skin without slowing your pace.

For deeper structural protection, trekking poles are non-negotiable. Even hikers with iron knees find that descending steep, rocky Pyrenean slopes destroys joint cartilage over time. Poles transfer the downward load to your upper body, dramatically sparing your knees. Paired with blister-specific hydrocolloid bandages like Compeed—widely available in Spanish pharmacies—these tools keep minor discomforts from metastasizing into trip-ending injuries. Skipping poles to look rugged is a beginner's mistake that guarantees an early exit.

Coping with Albergue Life and Shared Spaces

Surviving the physical trail is only half the battle; surviving your roommates in communal dormitories requires a different set of skills. Albergues pack dozens of tired strangers into shared bunk rooms where mornings begin well before dawn. Headlamps with a red-light mode become essential gear here; switching on a blinding white LED at 5:00 AM while packing your gear is a fast way to alienate every exhausted pilgrim in the room.

Sleep disruption is the silent killer of long-distance hiking, driven largely by the inescapable symphony of communal snoring. While traditional foam earplugs work for some, desperate creators have even resorted to using wireless earbuds like AirPods to block out the noise—an expensive luxury, but one that preserves sanity when sleep is at a premium. Along the way, unexpected sensory details—like a companion dabbing a single drop of perfume under their nose to mask the subterranean funk of a converted basement hostel—remind travelers that grace often arrives in micro-doses.

The Camino de Santiago does not test your endurance with grand, cinematic trials; it wears you down with a thousand tiny choices about what you are willing to carry.

Ultimately, packing for a pilgrimage forces an honest audit of your daily attachments. Every superfluous gadget, heavy cosmetic bag, and paranoid security measure exacts payment in sweat and joint pain. By stripping the load down to essentials, travelers find their attention shifting away from illuminated screens and toward the rhythm of their breathing and the landscape rolling past. What to watch next isn't another gear review video, but your own luggage scale: if the number makes you wince, keep cutting until it doesn't.
