South Korea’s "Fabricated Prosecution" Bill: Turning the Tables on the Investigators

November 07, 2026

The air inside the National Assembly chamber on November 6 was thick with a tension that has become the new normal in South Korean politics. For hours, members of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, had held the podium in a desperate filibuster, attempting to block a piece of legislation they claim is designed to dismantle the very foundations of the country’s rule of law. But in the end, the numbers were simply not on their side. Under the leadership of the Democratic Party (DP), the nation’s ruling party, the "Special Counsel Act for Fabricated Prosecution" was brought to a final vote and passed, marking a seismic shift in the ongoing struggle between the current administration and the remnants of the previous judicial establishment.

As the electronic tally confirmed the bill’s passage, the opposition benches were already empty. In a choreographed show of defiance, the PPP lawmakers had marched out of the hall, leaving the red nameplates on their desks as silent witnesses to a session they refused to legitimize. For President Lee Jae-myung and the DP, this is a moment of long-awaited "judicial rectification." For the opposition, it is the birth of a "mammoth-sized" political weapon designed to protect the president by rewriting the history of his own legal battles. This is not just another bill; it is a full-scale investigation into the investigators who once pursued the man who now holds the highest office in the land.

The Seventh Special Counsel of the Lee Jae-myung Era

To understand why this bill matters, one must understand the unique political landscape of contemporary South Korea. This marks the seventh time a Special Counsel—an independent prosecutor appointed outside the standard hierarchy—has been established since the Lee Jae-myung administration took office. In the history of the Republic of Korea, such frequent use of the special counsel system is unprecedented. It signals a profound lack of trust in the permanent institutions of justice: the Prosecution Service and the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO).

The core objective of this new law is to investigate allegations of "targeted investigations" and "fabricated indictments" carried out during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. For foreign observers, the context is vital: Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, a former prosecutor-general who rose to the presidency, was removed from office via impeachment and is currently imprisoned. During his tenure, his administration launched a series of aggressive probes into Lee Jae-myung (then the opposition leader) and figures from the previous Moon Jae-in administration. The DP has long maintained that these investigations were not about justice, but about political elimination through the abuse of prosecutorial power.

The passage of this bill is the DP’s formal response to those years. It seeks to turn the lens back onto the prosecutors themselves. Did they coerce witnesses? Did they hide or manipulate evidence? Did they selectively edit interview transcripts to create a narrative of guilt? These are the questions the new Special Counsel is tasked with answering. However, the frequency of these special probes has led to a sense of "institutional fatigue" among the public. Critics argue that when the "extraordinary" becomes "ordinary," the stability of the state’s legal framework begins to erode.

A Mammoth Investigation into Twelve High-Profile Cases

The scale of this Special Counsel is, by any definition, "mammoth." The law authorizes a team of up to 309 personnel, including dispatched prosecutors and special investigators. This is essentially the size of a large regional prosecutor’s office, dedicated entirely to a single mandate for a period of up to 200 days. The sheer volume of resources reflects the breadth of the investigation, which covers 12 distinct cases.

Six of these cases involve President Lee Jae-myung’s past legal troubles, including the Daejang-dong land development scandal—a complex real estate project in Seongnam that led to allegations of preferential treatment for private investors—and the Ssangyong North Korea remittance case, which involved allegations of illegal funds being sent to Pyongyang. The other six cases involve high-ranking officials from the former Moon Jae-in administration. The investigation will not focus on whether the original defendants were "innocent" or "guilty" in the traditional sense, but rather on the conduct of the prosecutors who handled the files.

  • Coercion of Testimony: Investigating whether prosecutors pressured associates of the President to give false statements under the threat of expanded investigations into their families.
  • Evidence Manipulation: Looking for instances where exculpatory evidence was intentionally omitted from court filings.
  • Political Coordination: Examining whether the timing and scope of indictments were coordinated with the political needs of the then-Yoon Suk-yeol administration.

In a notable concession during the legislative process, the DP removed a controversial clause that would have given the Special Counsel the power to withdraw existing indictments. This was done to head off immediate "unconstitutional" challenges, focusing the fire instead on the criminal prosecution of the investigators themselves. By the time this probe concludes, the "star prosecutors" of the previous era may find themselves sitting in the very witness boxes they once used to secure convictions.

The Opposition’s "Bulletproof" Narrative and Street Politics

The response from the People Power Party has been one of total war. Immediately after the vote, the party held a massive rally in the Rotunda Hall of the National Assembly, declaring it the "day the rule of law died." Their argument is straightforward: they view the bill as a "bulletproof" shield (방탄) designed to erase the President’s "judicial risk" by intimidating the judiciary and punishing anyone who dared to investigate him.

The PPP has announced a shift to "extra-parliamentary struggle," meaning they intend to take their grievances to the streets. Over the coming weekends, the party plans to hold nationwide rallies to denounce the law. This is a high-stakes gamble. By abandoning the halls of parliament for the asphalt of the streets, the PPP is attempting to galvanize its conservative base. However, for the middle-of-the-road voters, there is a risk that this will look like obstructionism. Many Koreans are still reeling from the revelations of prosecutorial overreach that came to light during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment, and a reflexive defense of those same prosecutors may not resonate as widely as the PPP hopes.

The immediate casualty of this clash is the national budget. With the opposition in full boycott mode, the review of the next fiscal year’s budget—essential for addressing the country’s economic challenges—has ground to a halt. In South Korea, it is common for political disputes to take the budget hostage, but the intensity of the "Fabricated Prosecution" fight suggests a particularly long and cold winter for legislative productivity. When politics consumes the judiciary, the first thing to be neglected is usually the livelihood of the citizens.

Fractures Within the Ruling Camp and Constitutional Hurdles

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of this legislative battle is that the dissent was not limited to the opposition. Even within the broader liberal coalition, voices of caution have emerged. Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party and himself a victim of a high-profile prosecutorial probe, raised concerns about potential unconstitutional elements in the bill’s supplementary provisions. Similarly, veteran DP lawmaker Jeong Seong-ho and Kwak Sang-eon expressed regret over what they saw as a lack of "procedural deliberation."

The core of their concern is a fundamental constitutional dilemma: The Conflict of Interest. We are entering a situation where a sitting President will appoint a Special Counsel to investigate the very prosecutors who once indicted him. Even if the investigation is morally justified in the eyes of his supporters, the structural optics are challenging. If the Special Counsel produces a report that exonerates the President’s past actions and jails his former pursuers, will that report be seen as "justice" or "victor’s justice"?

"The goal of prosecutorial reform was never to prove the infallibility of our side, but to ensure the fairness of the system for everyone. If we sacrifice procedural legitimacy in the name of a 'correct' outcome, we risk becoming what we sought to replace."

This sentiment, shared by some on the liberal wing, suggests that the DP leadership is walking a fine line. To maintain the moral high ground, the Special Counsel must operate with a level of transparency and independence that exceeds even the highest standards. If the probe is perceived as a partisan "hit job," it could trigger a cycle of political revenge that will haunt South Korean politics for decades to come, with every change of government resulting in a new Special Counsel to investigate the last one.

What to Watch Next

The passage of the bill is only the beginning. The next few weeks will be critical as the machinery of the Special Counsel begins to turn. Here are the specific developments to monitor:

  • The Appointment Process: Watch how the candidates for the Special Counsel are selected. If the appointees are seen as overly partisan "warriors" for the DP, the opposition’s "bulletproof" narrative will gain momentum. If they are respected, neutral legal veterans, the PPP’s protests may lose steam.
  • The Budget Stand-off: Keep an eye on whether the PPP returns to the negotiating table for the budget or if the DP attempts to pass the national spending plan unilaterally—a move that would be historically aggressive.
  • The Prosecution’s Reaction: The current leadership of the Prosecution Service is now in a corner. Will they cooperate with the Special Counsel, or will we see a "rebellion of the robes" where rank-and-file prosecutors protest the investigation into their colleagues?
  • Public Opinion Polls: The ultimate arbiter will be the public. If the majority of Koreans view the Special Counsel as a necessary cleaning of the "prosecutorial stables," the administration will have the wind at its back. If the public grows weary of the constant focus on the past, the political cost for the DP could be high.

South Korea is currently engaged in a profound experiment: can a democracy use its legislative power to retroactively "correct" the judicial actions of a previous regime without destroying the independence of the judiciary itself? The answer will determine the legacy of the Lee Jae-myung presidency and the future of the Korean legal system.
