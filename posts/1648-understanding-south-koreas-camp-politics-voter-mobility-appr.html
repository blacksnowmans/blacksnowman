Understanding South Korea’s Camp Politics: Voter Mobility, Approval Ratings, and the Lessons for the Lee Jae-myung Era

October 29, 2026

Walk into any political office in Yeouido—Seoul’s legislative hub—during an election cycle, and you will hear endless strategy sessions about "swing voters" and factional loyalty. To an outside observer, South Korea’s political landscape often looks frozen in a bitter, unyielding duel between two gigantic blocks. Yet beneath this polarized surface, a far more subtle and dynamic realignment is constantly taking place. Understanding how South Korean voters actually move, how presidential approval ratings function like an underground water table, and how ruling coalitions collapse from within is essential for anyone tracking the country's trajectory under President Lee Jae-myung.

Beyond Party Labels: The Deep Value Divide in Korean Politics

In South Korean political analysis, the term camp politics (진영 정치) refers to something much deeper than mere party affiliation or individual fandom. A "camp" represents a shared set of core social values and vision for the nation's future. The two major forces that dominate South Korean democracy—the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), a progressive party, and the main opposition People Power Party (PPP), a conservative party—embody fundamentally different philosophies.

The conservative People Power Party has traditionally centered its platform on market-driven economic growth, private competition, material wealth creation, and strict social order. Conversely, the ruling Democratic Party prioritizes shared prosperity, expanded welfare, social equity, fairness, equality, and diversity. Democratic governance in South Korea is built on the principle that these competing worldviews duel during elections, and the victor earns a temporary mandate from the public to put its philosophy into practice.

However, winning a majority does not grant absolute authority. Democratic legitimacy hinges on two critical guardrails: respecting the minority opposition and remaining faithful to campaign promises. When a ruling party succumbs to arrogance, ignores procedural fairness, or abandons the core values that earned it power, public cynicism sets in quickly. Progressive presidents like Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in repeatedly emphasized that dialogue, inclusion, and procedural legitimacy are necessary to maintain public trust. When a party betrays its foundational promises, even its most loyal supporters quietly begin searching for alternatives.

The Myth of Frozen Electorates: Intra-Camp Mobility and Alternative Parties

Foreign commentators often assume South Korean voters are rigidly partisan, with almost no movement between left and right. It is true that direct "cross-ideological voting"—a progressive voter switching directly to the conservative flagship party or vice versa—is rare. Just as in the United States, where loyal partisan bases leave only a narrow band of true swing voters, South Korea's inter-camp swing voter demographic is relatively small, hovering at around 10% of the electorate.

These 10% are crucial, as their independent evaluation of government performance, ethics, and policy competence ultimately decides tight national elections. But focusing solely on this 10% misses where the real dynamic movement happens in Korean politics: intra-camp mobility (진영 내 이동).

While only 10% of voters jump across the ideological fence, an estimated 20% to 30% of potential voters actively migrate *within* their respective camps. When voters become frustrated with their primary party’s incompetence, corruption, or tactical failures, emotional and ideological barriers prevent them from voting for the opposing camp. Instead, they seek alternative political forces that align with their broader ideological family. South Korean political history is rich with such third-party experiments, from historic entities like the United Liberal Democrats and the People's Party to more recent formations.

Today, this intra-camp volatility explains the rise of newer political entities. On the progressive side, former Justice Minister Cho Kuk launched the Rebuilding Korea Party, capturing progressives frustrated with established party politics. On the conservative side, Lee Jun-seok established the Reform Party, appealing to conservative voters seeking a break from the traditional establishment. These secondary parties serve as vital warning signals to the major parties: if a dominant party takes its base for granted, its voters will readily migrate to alternative homes within the same ideological camp.

The "Groundwater" Effect: Why Approval Ratings Break Ruling Parties

To understand why South Korean political parties fracture so easily, one must grasp how presidential approval ratings interact with party dynamics. Political analysts often compare executive approval ratings to a groundwater level. When invisible underground water tables drop dangerously low, the soil weakens, bridges crack, and the surface above becomes prone to sudden sinkholes. Similarly, when a president's approval rating gets stuck at a low level, the entire foundation of the ruling coalition erodes.

This dynamic is driven by an unavoidable axiom of political science: politicians prioritize their own political survival. When presidential or party approval ratings plummet, ruling party lawmakers panic about their prospects in the next general election. To preserve their seats, backbenchers and rival factions immediately demand emergency party restructurings, publicly distance themselves from the executive, or break away to form new factions.

"Every politician seeks power. Those who hold power want more of it, want it stronger, want it longer, and want it verified. This is the fundamental axiom of politics."

This dynamic was vividly demonstrated during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, his administration systematically dismantled the broad conservative coalition that had originally brought him to power. He forcibly ousted party leader Lee Jun-seok, alienated key coalition partners like Ahn Cheol-soo, and attempted to reduce the People Power Party under figures like Kim Gi-hyeon into a compliant subservience to Yongsan (the presidential office).

Rather than acting as a check on executive overreach or preserving the coalition, the party leadership functioned as a mere rubber stamp. The resulting collapse in public trust ultimately triggered severe political crisis, leading to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment. For the current Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party, this history offers a stark lesson: if a ruling party surrenders its independence and transforms into a single-faction monolith focused purely on pleasing the executive, public alienation will follow swiftly.

Media Dynamics, Strategic Leadership, and What to Watch Next

Navigating this complex political terrain is further complicated by South Korea's media landscape. Large, conglomerate-owned news outlets and traditional conservative media outlets have historically exerted strong influence, often attempting to stoke internal divisions within the progressive camp. During his years in opposition, these outlets constantly pushed "judicial risk" narratives framing President Lee Jae-myung as a liability to his party.

Even with the Democratic Party now in power, established media institutions continue to highlight internal party friction while downplaying administrative achievements. Ruling party leaders who fail to recognize these structural biases risk falling into media-driven narratives that divide their coalition.

At the same time, ruling party leadership cannot rely on grandstanding. In Korean political history, party leaders have occasionally made dramatic pledges—such as boasting they would raise party approval ratings by 10 percentage points within 100 days. Such artificial targets almost always backfire. Sustainable public support cannot be manufactured through public relations stunts; it requires delivering tangible improvements in ordinary citizens' economic livelihoods while safeguarding the core values of the coalition.

What to Watch Next

  • DPK Intra-Party Diversity: Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party maintains an open, broad coalition under President Lee Jae-myung, or whether it narrows into a monolithic single-faction structure that alienates moderate allies.
  • Third-Party Traction: Track the polling performance of minor parties like the Rebuilding Korea Party and the Reform Party. Their growth or decline serves as the primary indicator of voter dissatisfaction within the progressive and conservative camps, respectively.
  • Legislative-Executive Balance: Pay close attention to how the DPK leadership in the National Assembly handles executive initiatives. A healthy ruling party must constructively support President Lee Jae-myung's agenda while retaining enough independence to absorb public criticism and prevent administrative overreach.
  • Livelihood Policy Execution: Observe whether the administration's economic policies deliver measurable improvements for middle- and lower-income citizens, as economic tangible performance remains the single most decisive factor for keeping the 10% swing voter block aligned with the government.
