The "Pro-Lee vs. Anti-Lee" Trap: Why South Korea’s Reforms Are Stalling

October 12, 2026

Whenever you open a news site in South Korea, the screen is flooded with talk of "pro-Lee" versus "anti-Lee" factions. On the surface, it looks like standard political maneuvering—the typical jockeying for position within the ruling party. But for many who spent years demanding deep systemic change, these labels are not just annoying; they are a dangerous distraction from the reality of the country's current political trajectory.

The fixation on these factions is a symptom of an outdated, elitist view of governance. It treats modern politics as if we were still living in a Joseon Dynasty court, where political identity is dictated entirely by one’s proximity to the "King." But the movement that brought President Lee Jae-myung to power was never about installing a hero on a throne. It was about dismantling a broken system and returning sovereignty to the people.

The Fallacy of Elitist Political Frames

The current political discourse revolves around dividing politicians based on their loyalty to President Lee Jae-myung. This is a profound misunderstanding of why the current administration exists. When we talk about "pro-Lee" or "anti-Lee" factions, we are viewing the world through an elitist lens that suggests the only thing that matters is who sits at the top. This approach ignores the thousands of citizens who pushed for a "sovereign government" in the aftermath of the collapse of the previous regime under the now-imprisoned Yun Suk-yeol.

The "Revolution of Light"—a term used to describe the massive public movement that eventually led to this political transition—was made up of three distinct groups: radical revolutionaries, structural reformers, and pragmatists focused on efficiency. These groups successfully forced a regime change, but they are now pulling in different directions. The mainstream political establishment wants to reduce this tension to a mere "communication gap" or a personality clash. In reality, it is a fundamental clash of values: those who wanted a root-and-branch reconstruction of the state versus those who merely want to manage the existing system more efficiently.

The Trap of Gradualism and the Absence of Real Reform

A growing sense of disillusionment is setting in among the base. There is a widespread perception that the current government has fallen into the trap of "gradualism" (often called gaeryangjuui or reformism-lite). The logic of the gradualist is seductive: "Why tear down the whole house when we can just renovate it and make it more efficient?"

But this misses the lesson of the Yun Suk-yeol era. The catastrophic failures of that period were not caused by a simple lack of bureaucratic efficiency; they were caused by the people in charge. You can polish a system as much as you want, but if the people running it are fundamentally corrupt or ill-intentioned, the system will still fail. True reform requires the total removal of the power cartels—particularly within the prosecution and the judiciary—that allowed such figures to thrive. Currently, the administration is prioritizing "stability" and "efficiency" over the painful but necessary work of purging those who sustained the previous, corrupt regime.

Reform without the clearing out of personnel is a lie. The moment a government chooses to embrace past collaborators in the name of stability, it abandons its core identity. If you leave the old guard in place, the knife of reform will eventually be turned against those who wielded it.

The "Livelihood" Excuse

Whenever the presidential office faces a crisis of popularity, they turn to the word "livelihood" (minsaeng). They argue that the priority is the economy and the daily lives of citizens. While these issues are undoubtedly important, they are built on a foundation of sand if the state itself is not functioning normally. The mandate given to President Lee was not just to boost GDP by a few percentage points; it was to ensure that a disaster like the Yun Suk-yeol administration never happens again. By ignoring the structural and human cleanup in favor of "quick wins," the government is alienating the very people who fought to bring it to power.

The End of "Boss Politics" and the Rise of Citizen Democracy

A disturbing trend has emerged: attempts by some in the political sphere to silence critics or intellectuals who demand accountability. For example, the pressure placed on figures like Rhyu Si-min—a prominent public intellectual who articulates the frustrations of the reformist base—reveals a dangerous arrogance. When a power structure starts telling its supporters to "be quiet" or "stay in line," it is a sign that it has lost the ability to accept criticism.

The political establishment is clinging to a model of "boss politics" that died with the fall of the old regime. We no longer live in an era where a single leader’s word is law. We are in an era of "deliberative democracy," where hundreds of thousands of citizens observe, analyze, and evaluate every move the government makes in real-time through digital platforms. The citizens did not grant power to President Lee to create a new master-servant relationship; they granted it as a partnership to complete a specific mission.

What to Watch Next

The path forward for the administration is narrow, but clear. The government must decide if it will continue to drift toward a comfortable, status-quo-preserving gradualism or if it will return to the original promise of radical reform.

  • Personnel Accountability: Watch for whether the government initiates a genuine, structural purge of the prosecutorial and judicial cartels that remain from the previous era. Anything less will be viewed as a failure of the reform mandate.
  • Foreign Policy Stance: The administration’s recent, precarious maneuvers regarding foreign military deployments have deeply unsettled the base. Any shift that sacrifices principle for uncertain diplomatic "gains" will further erode core support.
  • Reconnecting with the Base: If the administration continues to ignore the voices of the reformist core, their approval ratings will continue to suffer. The only way to reverse this trend is not through better PR, but by pivoting back to the original, aggressive reform agenda that voters initially supported.

The citizens are watching. They are not waiting for more promises; they are waiting for the structure of the state to be fundamentally fixed. If the administration fails to act, the same massive public energy that swept them into power will be the force that eventually holds them to account.
