The Reality Check Behind Saudi Arabia's Megacity Dreams: Inside the Trouble at NEOM

October 12, 2026

Imagine standing in the middle of a baking, empty desert, told that a gleaming mirror-walled skyscraper stretching over 100 miles long will soon rise around you, complete with a ski resort on mountain peaks that rarely see snow. For a few brief years, this wasn't just science fiction—it was the official pitch of Saudi Arabia’s NEOM project, a staggering attempt to reshape the geography and economy of the Middle East.

Today, that vision is colliding hard with the laws of economics and engineering. What started as a $500 billion gamble to prepare for a post-oil future is now scaling back ambitions, canceling high-profile contracts, and facing ballooning costs that threaten to run into the trillions. To understand why Saudi Arabia’s grandest bet is wobbling, we have to look past the glossy promotional videos and examine the intersection of authoritarian governance, regional rivalry, and financial gravity.

The Grand Ambition of Vision 2030

To foreign observers, NEOM often looks like an isolated vanity project, but it is actually the crown jewel of Vision 2030, a sweeping national blueprint championed by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. The core logic of the plan is simple survival: Saudi Arabia has relied on oil exports for generations, a classic rentier state model that leaves its economy vulnerable to every swing in global energy markets.

To break this addiction, Riyadh launched a series of massive economic overhauls. Alongside NEOM, the kingdom pushed the Red Sea Project to build ultra-luxury tourism hubs along its coastline, and the Saudi Landbridge, a railway designed to connect the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea and turn the kingdom into a major logistics hub. NEOM was meant to be the technological apex of this strategy—a clean-slate metropolis powered by renewable energy and driven by futuristic innovation, proving that the kingdom could invent industries rather than just pump them out of the ground.

Yet these overlapping mega-projects immediately created a staggering capital requirement. Tying the nation's economic future to so many simultaneous, ultra-expensive bets meant that any global economic tremor would inevitably test whether the kingdom's ambitions were writing checks its treasury couldn't cash.

When Reality Hits: The Trouble at The Line and Trojena

The cracks in the foundation became impossible to ignore when reality caught up with the project’s two most famous components: The Line and Trojena.

“The Line” was designed as a 500-meter-tall, 200-meter-wide, and 170-kilometer-long linear city cutting straight across desert and mountain terrain. South Korea’s top builders, Samsung C&T and Hyundai Engineering & Construction, famously jumped into the fray, securing major contracts to tunnel through the difficult terrain and showcase Korean engineering prowess. But by early 2024, those major contracts were abruptly altered or wrapped up as the sheer physical and financial hurdles became apparent. The dazzling digital renderings hit a wall of hard engineering limits.

Meanwhile, “Trojena”—the planned mountain resort meant to feature an artificial lake, a dam, and outdoor skiing in the middle of the desert—faced an even stranger fate. As costs mounted and the economics of maintaining a ski resort in an arid climate were scrutinized, contracts for the infrastructure were scaled back or canceled. Compounding the embarrassment, plans to host the 2029 Asian Winter Games at the site were upended when the event's venue was shifted to Kazakhstan.

The Trillion-Dollar Cost Explosion

How did a $500 billion project become an unsustainable financial burden? According to reporting by Reuters, initial estimates for the entire NEOM endeavor have ballooned to the point where The Line alone is projected to cost over a trillion dollars.

Experts point to a mix of engineering complexity and unchecked optimism. As Noda-sol, a research professor at Dankook University’s GCC Research Institute, has noted, running utilities, high-speed rail, and logistics networks through a single, razor-thin linear space spanning the distance from Seoul to Daejeon is an unprecedented challenge. Planners simply underestimated the difficulty and cost of the subterranean infrastructure required.

Add to this the post-pandemic spikes in global raw material costs, supply chain bottlenecks, and persistent Middle Eastern geopolitical tensions, and the budget pressures snowballed. Financial publications like the Wall Street Journal reported that Trojena’s expected construction costs jumped by over $10 billion, causing its internal rate of return to plunge from an initial target of 9% down to 7%. For international private investors looking for a return, the math simply stopped making sense.

Top-Down Power and the Crown Prince’s Dilemma

Part of the reason these unfeasible plans survived so long on the drawing board lies in Saudi Arabia's rigid, top-down governance structure. Kang Moon-soo, a senior research fellow at the Korea Institute for International Economic Policy, points out that Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman acts simultaneously as prime minister, chairman of the Public Investment Fund (PIF), and chairman of the NEOM board. When the ultimate decision-maker holds every key lever of power, lower-level managers and experts find it nearly impossible to raise red flags.

Furthermore, these megaprojects are deeply tied to regime legitimacy and the transition of power within the royal family. Baek Seung-hoon, a professor at Myongji University’s Middle East Research Institute, notes that as the succession model shifted from traditional sibling-to-sibling transitions to father-to-son, the Crown Prince tied his political survival and reformist credentials directly to the success of NEOM. Pulling back on the project is not just a policy adjustment; it risks looking like a personal setback for the nation's most powerful leader.

Fiscal Deficits and Crowded Calendars

The timing could hardly be worse. While NEOM demands endless capital, global oil prices have slumped into the $60 range—well below the $90-plus per barrel Saudi Arabia needs to balance its national budget. An IMF report recently warned that the kingdom's fiscal deficit widened to 5.8% of GDP, driven heavily by slipping oil revenues.

At the same time, Riyadh has committed to a staggering calendar of global events, including the 2030 Riyadh Expo and the 2034 FIFA World Cup. These events require billions more in tangible, immediate urban infrastructure—subways, stadiums, and airports—leaving less fiscal oxygen for speculative projects like NEOM that promise returns only in the distant future.

Pivoting to Reality: The Rise of Oxagon

Despite the cutbacks, Saudi Arabia is not abandoning NEOM entirely. Instead, planners are executing a quiet pivot: moving away from speculative sci-fi marvels and toward projects with clear, immediate economic utility.

Enter “Oxagon,” a planned industrial and maritime hub on the Red Sea coast. Focused on ports, advanced manufacturing, green energy, and AI data centers, Oxagon is designed to actually generate revenue rather than just capture headlines. Chang Ji-hyang, a senior research fellow at the Asan Institute for Policy Studies, notes that the strategy has shifted toward dialing back ambitions on residential oddities like The Line while doubling down on industrial zones that can deliver faster economic returns.

This pragmatic shift suggests that while the brand name NEOM will survive, its contents are undergoing a heavy dose of reality-driven editing.

The Regional Power Struggle and Real Economic Shifts

Much of this high-stakes economic engineering is driven by fierce regional competition with the United Arab Emirates. Analysts note that Saudi Arabia’s urge to build bigger, flashier, and more transformative projects than its neighbors is part of a broader battle for the economic leadership of the Middle East.

Yet it would be a mistake to write off Vision 2030 as a total failure. Away from the desert mirages, structural economic reform is genuinely happening. Recent government data shows that the non-oil sector's share of Saudi Arabia's GDP hit a record-breaking 54.8%, proving that efforts to diversify the economy away from crude oil are bearing real fruit.

The missteps at NEOM offer a harsh, expensive lesson: no amount of absolute political will can bend economic gravity forever. As Riyadh adjusts its sights from fantasy to fiscal reality, the world is watching to see whether the kingdom can salvage a sustainable future out of the desert sand.

What to Watch Next: Keep an eye on international private investment inflows into Oxagon and upcoming revisions to NEOM’s construction timelines. If Saudi Arabia continues quietly trimming its most extravagant blueprints while hitting its non-oil GDP targets, it will signal a maturing economic strategy that trades theatrical scale for hard-nosed financial survival.
