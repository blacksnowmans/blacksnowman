Inside South Korea's Ruling Party, a 100-Day Milestone Triggers a Multi-Front Power Struggle

November 02, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, the honeymoon phase for South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has come to an abrupt end. When President Lee Jae-myung assumed the presidency, party members expected a period of disciplined support from the legislative branch to anchor the new administration's reform agenda. Instead, as Party Leader Kim Min-seok approaches his 100th day at the helm, the ruling bloc finds itself embroiled in an escalating battle for internal control.

For observers outside South Korea, understanding the inner workings of the country's ruling party requires looking beyond official statements of unity. South Korean political parties operate as complex alliances of competing factions, where senior lawmakers constantly test the strength of party leadership ahead of election cycles. Today, Kim Min-seok faces a confluence of political threats: open challenges from senior lawmakers, a high-stakes proxy battle in internal elections, a explosive investigation into alleged cult infiltration, and a troubling slide in public approval ratings. What began as minor policy disagreements is rapidly expanding into a contest over the party's future direction.

A Veteran’s Ultimatum and the Battle Over Party Direction

The immediate spark igniting tension within the ruling party came from Representative Jung Chung-rae, a influential senior lawmaker within the DPK. As Kim Min-seok’s 100th day as party leader drew near, Jung issued an unambiguous public warning, declaring that Kim’s leadership "will be judged at the 100-day mark" and revealing that he is deciding "what flag to raise" after that milestone passes. In the coded language of South Korean politics, such a statement from a heavyweight politician is a direct challenge to the sitting leader’s authority, signaling an intention to organize an independent factional stance if the leadership fails to satisfy party critics.

Jung further stoked internal friction by unilaterally reviving a proposal to merge the DPK with the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, firebrand progressive outfit led by politician Cho Kuk—setting an explicit target deadline of January for the merger. This move drew immediate and sharp pushback from party leadership and mainstream lawmakers aligned with President Lee Jae-myung. Representative Lee Hae-sik openly rebuked Jung, asking how acting like a prophet and arbitrarily declaring a merger timetable without prior consultation could possibly benefit the party. Within party ranks, concerns are mounting that a premature merger could compromise the DPK's broad appeal, especially as Cho Kuk’s vocal public presence puts pressure on both President Lee Jae-myung and Party Leader Kim Min-seok.

The rift has been widened by commentary from prominent outside voices. Defenses of political commentator Rhyu Si-min by Representative Jung, alongside reports of pollster Park Si-young suggesting at a retreat that Kim Min-seok might be unfit to lead the party into the next general election, have heightened paranoia among party leadership. While younger and newly elected lawmakers have mostly stayed quiet, senior figures are actively jockeying for position. Beneath the philosophical arguments over progressive unity lies a classic struggle for leverage over future parliamentary nominations and party governance.

The Women’s Committee Election: A Proxy War in Plain Sight

While senior figures trade barbs in public, a parallel battle is unfolding in an internal election concluding on November 3. The election for the chairperson of the DPK’s National Women’s Committee might sound like a routine organizational matter to foreign observers, but in South Korean party politics, it is anything but. The head of this committee exercises substantial influence over candidate selection rules, gender quotas, and regional network mobilization for upcoming nationwide elections.

The contest has narrowed to a head-to-head race between candidates Kim Nam-hee and Moon Jeong-bok. Far from remaining a contest of policy platforms, the race has transformed into a high-stakes "mini national convention," framed by political insiders as a proxy war between mainstream pro-Lee Jae-myung loyalists and factional allies of Jung Chung-rae. Because female party members constitute a vital voting bloc within the DPK, the outcome will serve as a direct referendum on the leadership's grip over the party apparatus.

The intensity of the contest forced the party’s official Election Management Committee to step in, issuing a formal warning to candidate Moon Jeong-bok for violating party election rules. The disciplinary measure sparked intense debate between supporting camps, with Moon's supporters protesting the ruling as overly harsh while rivals called for strict adherence to regulations. For Kim Min-seok, the election presents a lose-lose scenario: regardless of who wins, the bitterness generated by the campaign threatens to leave deep factional scars that the party leadership will be forced to mend.

The Cult Membership Scandal Nears Its Reckoning

Compounding the political maneuvering is a scandal involving potential infiltration of the party by external religious groups. This week, the DPK leadership is scheduled to present the results of a comprehensive internal audit to a general assembly of its parliamentary lawmakers, addressing explosive allegations that members of Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious group often described as a cult—joined the ruling party en masse.

In South Korean political history, allegations of secret, organized block enrollments by religious groups carry massive weight. Critics allege that such groups attempt to manipulate primary votes and internal party decisions by registering thousands of dedicated followers as dues-paying party members. The upcoming report will reveal the actual scale of enrollment, whether these individuals obtained voting rights, and whether they exerted influence in past leadership conventions or candidate primaries.

The presentation places Kim Min-seok in an awkward tightrope walk:

  • Transparency versus exposure: Revealing significant vulnerabilities in the party registry risks alienating grass-roots members and handing ammunition to political opponents.
  • Factional weaponization: If the report reveals systemic flaws, critics could accuse party leadership of administrative incompetence or demand accountability for past registration oversight.
  • Systemic reform: The leadership must introduce strict verification safeguards without weakening the party's core principle of expanding participatory rights for regular members.

If the report confirms widespread infiltration, the fallout could trigger calls for a complete overhaul of the party's member management system, putting further strain on leadership stability.

Plunging Polls and a Blocked Legislative Agenda

Underpinning all of these internal troubles is a sobering reality: public support for the Democratic Party of Korea is slipping. Recent opinion polls show the ruling party's lead narrowing significantly, with analysts warning that support could drop into the 20% range. What worries party strategists most is that the DPK is failing to capitalize on political troubles within the main opposition People Power Party (PPP), currently led by Jang Dong-hyeok, which has been bogged down by internal recording controversies and issues involving Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon. Instead of drawing dissatisfied voters, the ruling party is seeing its own core base grow disillusioned by internal bickering.

This internal friction comes at a crucial juncture for the country's governance. The ruling party faces a heavy legislative agenda that requires total party discipline. Key priorities include launching a parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de regarding the judiciary's conduct during the short-lived December 3 emergency martial law declared prior to the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The party is also pushing a special prosecutor bill targeting past "fabricated prosecutions" conducted by political prosecutors during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure—who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is currently serving a prison sentence.

An Editorial Perspective: For voters who supported the transition to a new administration, the current spectacle is frustrating. Voters did not put the Democratic Party in power to watch senior figures engage in factional warfare over future nomination rights. They expected structural reform of state institutions and tangible improvements in economic wellbeing. If the ruling leadership and its internal critics remain focused on internal dominance rather than delivering on key legislative promises, they risk squandering their mandate.

What to Watch Next

As Kim Min-seok approaches his 100th day in office, the coming days will test whether the current leadership can reassert authority or if the party will fracture into open rivalries. Key developments to monitor include:

  • The November 3 Women’s Committee Election Result: The outcome will immediately indicate which faction holds momentum inside the party's grassroots organization.
  • The General Assembly Audit Report: How lawmakers react to the findings on Shincheonji enrollment will reveal whether the issue can be managed quietly or will escalate into a debate over leadership accountability.
  • Kim Min-seok’s 100-Day Address: The party leader's upcoming milestone speech will show whether he opts for a conciliatory approach toward internal critics like Jung Chung-rae or takes a harder line to enforce party discipline.
  • Legislative Execution: Progress on the parliamentary probe into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de and the special prosecutor bill will serve as a practical test of whether the party can unite behind its core legislative agenda despite internal noise.
