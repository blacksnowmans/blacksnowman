The Trump Meltdown: Why America’s Midterm Chaos is a Warning for Seoul

November 01, 2026

With just three days left before the U.S. midterm elections, the political atmosphere in Washington has descended into a volatile spectacle of ego and instability. Donald Trump, the former U.S. president, has abruptly revived campaign rallies he had just canceled, driven by reports questioning his stamina. As the U.S. navigates its most contentious electoral cycle in recent memory, the ripples of this chaos are being felt far beyond American borders—reaching directly into the boardrooms and government offices of South Korea.

For those watching from Seoul, this is no longer just a matter of American internal politics. It is a preview of the protectionist headwinds and diplomatic turbulence that the administration of President Lee Jae-myung will need to navigate in the coming years.

The Internal Collapse of the Trump Campaign

The recent decision by Trump to reverse his cancellation of rallies in Republican strongholds like Kansas and Iowa was sparked by media reports suggesting he was suffering from “fatigue.” For a political leader, such reports are often mundane, but for Trump, they served as a catalyst for a public explosion. His frantic return to the campaign trail suggests a camp teetering on the edge of exhaustion and desperation.

What we are witnessing is the transformation of a major political campaign into an exercise in personal ego. When a leader of a global superpower prioritizes the refutation of a “tiredness” narrative over consistent strategy, it raises uncomfortable questions about stability. Furthermore, Trump’s verbal lashing of journalists—calling them “disgusting” and telling them to “get in shape”—highlights a deeper psychological unease. When a candidate views legitimate media scrutiny as a personal assault, the democratic process itself becomes the casualty.

Even more concerning is Trump’s rhetoric regarding the election results. As polls show his traditional base in states like Texas becoming competitive, he has preemptively labeled the results as “rigged.” This refusal to accept the possibility of defeat—and the subsequent sowing of distrust in the electoral system—is a dangerous precedent that threatens the bedrock of American democracy.

The $5,000 Promise and the Protectionist Pivot

Amidst the shouting, Trump has doubled down on a radical populist pledge: if the Republican Party wins, he promises to provide every American adult with $5,000 (about $3,600 USD) in direct cash payments. While this serves as a potent tool to grab headlines, it is essentially a reckless form of "vote-buying" that ignores the fundamental realities of national fiscal health.

Crucially for South Korea, Trump explicitly tied this promise to his "America First" agenda. He warned recipients, “Do not spend this money in Korea or overseas; it must be spent in the U.S.” By framing the nation’s closest ally as a culprit for capital flight, Trump is signaling a transactional and hostile approach to future trade relations. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, this is a clear warning that any future trade negotiations will be met with intense protectionist pressure. South Korea’s major conglomerates, heavily invested in the U.S. market, now face an environment of deep uncertainty regarding tariffs and investment subsidies.

Fear Politics: From Air Force One to Mar-a-Lago

The theater of the absurd reached new heights during a recent Halloween incident where Air Force One was used for a low-altitude flight over Washington, causing alarm among citizens who feared a repeat of the 9/11 tragedy. Using the presidential aircraft for such a display of power—at the expense of public safety and air traffic control—is a disturbing example of the privatization of state assets.

Simultaneously, reports have emerged that "Avenger" air defense missile systems have been deployed at Trump’s Mar-a-Lago resort in Florida. The fact that a former president’s private residence must be fortified like a military bunker speaks volumes about the extreme polarization and security threats currently plaguing American society. When a political divide leads to missile batteries on private property, it is a sign that the era of peaceful discourse has been replaced by a climate of fear and mutual suspicion.

What to Watch Next

As the U.S. midterm elections approach, the record-breaking turnout in early voting suggests that the American electorate is acutely aware of the stakes. While the Democratic Party currently holds a lead in some polls, the unpredictability of the Trump campaign remains the primary variable.

For South Korea, the path forward requires a shift in diplomatic strategy. The "Lee Jae-myung doctrine" will likely need to be more agile than ever, balancing the necessity of strong ties with the U.S. against the reality of a political landscape that is increasingly prone to volatility and protectionism.

  • Monitor Tariff Adjustments: Keep a close watch on potential shifts in U.S. trade policy immediately following the election, particularly regarding steel, semiconductors, and electric vehicle subsidies.
  • Economic Resilience: The government should look to diversify trade partners to mitigate the risks associated with an "America First" policy that may penalize foreign investment.
  • Diplomatic Prudence: Seoul must maintain a firm, principle-based diplomatic stance that emphasizes the mutual benefits of the ROK-U.S. alliance, even as the rhetoric from Washington grows increasingly erratic.

The upcoming vote will be more than just a referendum on the current U.S. administration; it will serve as the opening act for the 2028 presidential race. For the rest of the world, including South Korea, the outcome will determine whether the U.S. continues to operate as a predictable ally or pivots further toward an isolationist, unpredictable power. The coming days will be a test of whether the collective wisdom of the American people can pull their political system back from the brink.
