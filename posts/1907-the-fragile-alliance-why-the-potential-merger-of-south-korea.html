The Fragile Alliance: Why the Potential Merger of South Korea’s Liberal Parties is Stalling

November 03, 2026

The South Korean political landscape is currently dominated by the efforts of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration to restore democratic values following the turbulent and dark era of the now-imprisoned former president, Yoon Suk-yeol. Yet, beneath this push for reform, a concerning discord is emerging within the pro-democracy camp. The discussions regarding a potential merger between the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the nation's largest liberal party and the current ruling force—and the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP), a smaller but highly influential liberal party, have transformed from a logistical challenge into a public display of broken trust.

To the outside observer, the merger talks might appear to be a straightforward consolidation of power. In reality, they have devolved into a hollow game of political theater, lacking the substance of genuine cooperation. The recent disclosures by Chung Chun-seng, the Secretary-General of the RKP, regarding failed meetings with his DPK counterparts, reveal a dynamic defined by the arrogance of a dominant party and the growing sense of betrayal felt by its smaller partner.

A Breakdown of Trust: Behind the Closed Doors

The meetings between Chung Chun-seng of the RKP and Lee Hae-sik of the DPK were intended to bridge a growing divide. During their first sit-down on September 22, Chung offered a blunt warning: stop the public maneuvering and "media play," and instead engage in honest, private dialogue that can lead to concrete results for the public. However, by the second meeting on October 17, the DPK still lacked a coherent roadmap. When the RKP proposed a specific timeline for legislative cooperation during the National Assembly’s annual audit, the DPK responded with silence.

This communication blackout is not merely a scheduling issue; it is a signal. The DPK’s retreat into silence suggests an unwillingness to recognize the RKP as a legitimate partner, despite both parties being essential to the success of the Lee Jae-myung administration. The irony is palpable: the very entities meant to act as the vanguard of democratic reform are failing to practice the basic tenets of democratic communication.

The subsequent media reporting only exacerbated the situation. Several outlets painted the RKP as the "uncooperative" party, implying they were stonewalling the DPK. In reality, the RKP was merely rejecting performative meetings that served as photo opportunities rather than substantive negotiations. This strategy of leaking favorable narratives to the press—or "media play"—is a classic political tactic that, in this instance, has only succeeded in deepening the distrust between the two parties’ respective voter bases.

The Battle Over "Negotiating Group" Status

A major point of contention lies in the proposed reforms to the "Negotiating Group" status within the National Assembly. Currently, a party needs 20 seats to be recognized as a formal negotiating group, which grants them significant privileges, including a share of state subsidies and the right to appoint policy researchers. The DPK’s sudden proposal to lower this threshold to 15 seats has been met with skepticism by the RKP, which currently holds 12 seats.

The RKP views the 15-seat threshold as a calculated move to keep them under the DPK’s thumb, effectively denying them the status of an independent negotiating group. If the threshold were lowered to 10 seats—a return to the standard that existed before the authoritarian Yushin era under Park Chung-hee—the RKP could operate with genuine independence. By insisting on 15, the DPK appears to be trying to maintain its gatekeeper status, ensuring that smaller liberal parties remain beholden to the ruling party's whims. This is a far cry from the democratic diversity the DPK claims to champion.

Internationally, South Korea’s 20-seat requirement is an outlier. In Germany, the threshold is 5% of the total seats; in France, it is 15. The 20-seat barrier, originally tightened during the Yushin dictatorship to stifle opposition, remains a relic of an era the current administration claims to have moved past. If the DPK is serious about reform, it must consider that true strength lies in a decentralized legislature, not in monopolizing parliamentary power.

The Specter of "Absorption" vs. "Solidarity"

The rhetoric surrounding a potential merger has also been a flashpoint. Kim Min-seok, a prominent DPK leader, famously used the term "absorption merger" earlier this year. To the members of the RKP, this was not a invitation to partner—it was an insult. The RKP was formed by citizens seeking a more principled, professional, and distinct brand of liberalism. Being treated as a mere "appendage" to the DPK ignores the mandate the RKP received from its voters.

When leadership from one party speaks of "absorbing" another, it signals a desire for conquest rather than coalition. The RKP leadership has been clear: they are willing to discuss solidarity, but they will not be liquidated. The DPK’s demand that the RKP resolve "dual-party membership" issues—where some members hold ties to both parties—before any formal merger talks can proceed is seen by many in the RKP as a transparent attempt to poach members and hollow out the RKP from within.

The Road Ahead: Building a "Crisis-Recovery Coalition"

As Shin Jang-sik, the RKP’s representative, has argued, the stakes are far higher than petty seat counts. With the 2028 parliamentary elections and the 2030 presidential election on the horizon, the liberal camp must avoid the trap of internal division. The goal should be a "Crisis-Recovery Coalition"—a broad, tent-like alliance that includes the DPK, the RKP, and various progressive civil society groups. This alliance is necessary to ensure that the remnants of the Yoon Suk-yeol era never regain power.

For the DPK, the lesson is clear: if they wish to lead, they must learn to respect their allies. The RKP is not an enemy, nor is it a subordinate. The popularity of the RKP in recent polling—which shows a strong public preference for the RKP to maintain its independent path rather than rushing into a poorly conceived merger—reflects a public that values the RKP’s distinct, professional voice.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the upcoming parliamentary audit and whether the DPK moves to officially lower the negotiating group threshold to 10 seats. Should the DPK continue to stall or insist on the 15-seat compromise, the RKP is likely to suspend merger discussions indefinitely, signaling a shift toward a future defined by "healthy competition" rather than a forced, fragile alliance. True unity cannot be forced through bureaucratic maneuvering; it must be built on the foundation of mutual respect and a shared commitment to the democratic project.
