Why South Koreans Still Want Marriage—and Why They Cannot Afford It

November 19, 2026

On paper, South Korea’s demographic crisis often looks like a simple rejection of family life. Outside the country, headlines tend to frame the world’s lowest birthrate as a story of young people turning their backs on marriage altogether. Yet a fresh wave of surveys and reporting released in Seoul on November 18 points to a far more uncomfortable reality: most South Koreans still want to get married. They just look at their bank balances, housing costs, and employment contracts and decide they cannot.

According to figures published on November 18, 55% of respondents in South Korea said people should get married—the highest level of positive sentiment recorded in 12 years. Rather than embracing permanent singlehood as an ideal, a solid majority of the public still holds onto the idea of building a family. The problem is not a sudden collapse in desire. It is the widening gulf between what young adults want and what South Korea’s economy and social institutions allow them to build.

Digging beneath that headline figure reveals just how sharply the pressures fall along lines of wealth, job security, and gender. For policymakers trying to reverse a demographic spiral, the data delivers a blunt message: exhorting young people to marry and have children rings hollow when the basic rungs of adulthood—a stable paycheck and an affordable place to live—feel out of reach.

The 14-Point Gender Gap Behind a 12-Year High

While the overall 55% approval figure suggests a resilient cultural attachment to marriage, a closer look at unmarried South Koreans reveals a striking divide between men and women. According to figures reported by South Korean outlets Asia Economy and Segye Ilbo, 43.9% of unmarried men said people should marry, compared with just 29.9% of unmarried women—a 14 percentage point gap.

To understand why fewer than three in ten unmarried women express enthusiasm for marriage, it helps to understand what marriage has historically demanded in South Korea. Even as South Korean women have matched or surpassed men in higher education and entered the workforce in huge numbers, family expectations and workplace norms have been far slower to evolve. Marriage and childbirth still carry disproportionate career penalties and domestic burdens for women, creating a very different risk calculation than the one men face.

In that light, the 14-point gap is not an abstract cultural quirk. It is a barometer of how differently young men and young women experience the weight of South Korean society. When marriage feels like an institution built on unequal sacrifice, positive sentiment in a national poll cannot translate into trips down the aisle.

Locked Out by Seoul Housing Prices and High-Interest Debt

When unmarried South Koreans are asked directly why they are delaying or giving up on marriage, the number one answer across multiple media reports—including Etoday and Newsis—is plain and material: a severe shortage of marriage funds.

In South Korea, getting married is rarely a small private ceremony followed by a modest rental apartment. Weddings themselves carry heavy expenses, and securing a home has become an almost insurmountable barrier for people starting out on their own. In Seoul, housing prices have climbed to 11.7 times annual income. Even for couples who do manage to tie the knot, the financial strain does not ease afterward: reporting by BabyNews shows that newlyweds spend one-fifth of their entire income on housing costs alone.

The financing structure of South Korean housing compounds the squeeze. Many young couples who cannot buy a home outright rely on jeonse, South Korea’s distinctive lease system in which tenants hand over a massive lump-sum deposit—often hundreds of thousands of dollars—instead of paying monthly rent, with the landlord returning the deposit at the end of the contract. Most young adults have to borrow heavily from banks to assemble that deposit. With interest rates remaining elevated, monthly principal and interest repayments on those housing loans tighten the financial vise around young workers’ necks.

For anyone entering adulthood without existing property or substantial financial help from parents, marriage begins to look less like a personal milestone and more like a high-stakes financial gamble. Telling young people to start families for the good of the nation while leaving housing affordability unresolved is not a policy—it is wishful thinking.

Why Cash Handouts Are Losing Ground to Jobs

If money to secure a home is the first wall blocking marriage, the second is the instability of how young South Koreans earn that money in the first place. Highlighting employment insecurity as the second most cited reason unmarried adults avoid marriage, the daily newspaper Hankyoreh pointed to a shifting mood in public expectations around demographic policy. Alongside reporting from the JoongAng Ilbo, the coverage underscores a sharp pivot in what voters actually want from the government: demands for one-off cash subsidies are giving way to urgent calls for quality youth jobs and employment stability.

The logic is straightforward. When workers are cycling through temporary contracts and worried about whether their jobs will exist next year, long-term commitments like marriage and child-rearing feel reckless. Statutory benefits such as parental leave exist on paper, yet for precarious workers afraid of losing their footing, taking extended time off remains out of reach. The OECD has reached a similar diagnosis, attributing South Korea’s ultra-low birthrate to the combined squeeze of employment insecurity, housing expenses, and education costs. When job quality erodes, marriage and childbirth follow it down in a domino effect.

Still, faced with shrinking populations, local governments across South Korea are scrambling to use whatever levers they control—mostly cash and settlement packages. Reporting from NewsPim and Jeolla Ilbo shows that Wanju County has aggressively expanded settlement, marriage, and childbirth subsidies in an effort to hold its population above 100,000. Gangseo District has taken a similarly bold step, putting a 10 million won (roughly $7,000) grant for a first child into next year’s budget plan.

On the surface, these local initiatives offer welcome breathing room to young families dealing with immediate expenses. Yet there is an obvious danger. Without tackling the overwhelming concentration of industries and opportunities in the greater Seoul area—and without creating stable, long-term employment in regional communities—local subsidies risk devolving into an exhausting, zero-sum cash race between municipalities. Writing a check may persuade a couple to register their address in a town for a year or two, but only a durable local job ecosystem can convince them to stay and build a life there.

Challenging Patriarchal Rules at the Birth Registry

Economic barriers are only part of the story. Young South Koreans are also running up against legal and social rules that reflect an older, far more rigid era. In an exclusive report, BabyNews revealed that a bill has been introduced to abolish South Korea’s paternal surname priority rule—a move aimed squarely at one of the country’s most entrenched patriarchal defaults.

Under existing rules governing birth registration in South Korea, a newborn is required by default to take the father’s surname and ancestral clan origin, a traditional lineage marker known as bon-gwan. The proposed legislation would end that automatic requirement and allow parents to decide a child’s surname and lineage through mutual agreement.

To readers outside Korea, a change in birth-registration paperwork might sound symbolic. Inside the country, it speaks directly to the generational disconnect driving down marriage rates—especially among young women. While older statutes still enforce traditional family hierarchy, younger South Koreans increasingly expect horizontal, equal partnerships. Small institutional reforms will not pay a mortgage or stabilize a short-term labor contract, yet dismantling outdated legal defaults sends a clear signal that family life does not have to mean surrendering equality at the door. Until South Korea’s laws and social norms catch up with the values of the people expected to start families, government blueprints for reversing the birthrate will keep falling short.

What to Watch Next

The gap between South Korea’s 55% approval for marriage and its stubbornly low marriage and birth numbers sets up several concrete tests in the months ahead:

  • Movement on the paternal surname bill: Watch whether the proposed legislation to let parents jointly choose a child’s surname and ancestral origin gains traction in the National Assembly, and how fiercely conservative lawmakers or traditionalist groups push back against altering family registration rules.
  • The Lee Jae-myung government’s policy mix: As public skepticism toward one-off cash handouts grows, watch whether central government spending shifts meaningfully away from fragmented cash incentives toward structural youth employment programs, contract-worker protections, and housing relief.
  • Outcomes of municipal cash races: Track whether aggressive local packages—such as Wanju County’s population-defense subsidies and Gangseo District’s 10 million won ($7,000) first-child grant—actually retain young households over time, or merely pull residents temporarily from neighboring districts.
  • Borrowing costs and Seoul housing ratios: Keep an eye on interest rates and housing-price-to-income ratios in the capital. Until the cost of securing a home drops from 11.7 times annual income to something ordinary paychecks can reach, positive attitudes toward marriage will remain trapped on paper.

South Korea does not suffer from a lack of people who want a partner and a home. It suffers from an economy and an institutional framework that have priced those ordinary hopes out of reach. Whether the country’s political leadership confronts the real obstacles—jobs, housing, and outdated family norms—or keeps reaching for easier short-term handouts will determine whether that 12-year high in marriage sentiment turns into real families or fades into frustration.
