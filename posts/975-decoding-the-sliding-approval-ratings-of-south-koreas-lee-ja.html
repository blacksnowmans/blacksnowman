Decoding the Sliding Approval Ratings of South Korea's Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 11, 2026

Checking the daily tracking polls has become a grim ritual for anyone following South Korean politics these days. Watching deep-dive analyses of President Lee Jae-myung’s plunging approval numbers brings a familiar mix of unease and quiet frustration. This is not the future the progressive camp envisioned when power changed hands, yet warning lights are flashing across every major data set. Something fundamental is going off track.

Six months into the Lee Jae-myung administration, the slide in public support demands a cold, hard look. Beyond the raw numbers, we have to examine what the media and polling agencies are actually capturing—and where the line between statistical illusion and political reality begins to blur.

The Divergent Numbers Staring Down the Administration

A Shocking 61.4% Negative Rating

According to a poll by Straight News, President Lee’s approval rating sits at 37.1%, while his disapproval rating has climbed to a striking 61.4%. This is the highest negative mark since he took office, with critics noting that the disapproval figure has effectively doubled from 31% in just half a year. Outside of the Jeolla provinces—the liberal stronghold in the country's southwest—disapproval outpaces approval in every single region. Even during the tumultuous tenure of impeached former leader Yoon Seok-yeol, currently serving a prison sentence, such uniform hostility was rarely seen.

Reports from the presidential office suggest that President Lee is losing sleep, personally monitoring social media and trying to engage directly with the public pulse. Yet, despite these earnest efforts from the top, the negative numbers keep climbing while positive evaluations flatline. On the surface, the government appears to be drifting, entirely disconnected from the electorate. But before taking these numbers at face value, we must ask whether they represent the pure mood of the entire South Korean electorate or if they are a trap of skewed sampling.

When Highly Engaged Partisans Shape the Narrative

A closer look suggests these survey results are less a random sample of the general public and more a reflection of hyper-engaged political partisans. It is statistically implausible that 100% of ordinary citizens are tuned into every political micro-development, yet respondents seem uniformly eager to rebuke the president. During the three years of Yoon Seok-yeol's presidency, weekly polls rarely produced such relentlessly hostile polarization.

The reality is that moderate voters and ordinary citizens fatigued by constant political noise tend to hang up the phone or refuse to participate in automated surveys. The people who stay on the line to complete these questionnaires are overwhelmingly angry political junkies. Meanwhile, even traditional progressive voters are fracturing. In this climate, motivated critics are jumping at the chance to press the button for "very poorly." These polls measure the concentrated fury of a mobilized opposition and a disappointed base, not necessarily the country as a whole.

💡 Key Takeaway
  • Straight News polling recorded a historic 61.4% disapproval rating for President Lee Jae-myung.
  • Rather than a balanced cross-section of the public, the data largely captures the outsized anger of politically active respondents.
  • Internal fissures and voter fatigue within the progressive base are bleeding directly into survey response rates.

Stalling Out at the Margins: Polls and the Coming Parliamentary Audits

The Temporary Floor and the Threat of Stagnation

Data from polling firm Jowon C&I shows a similar split: 37% approval and 61.4% disapproval. While the president’s recent press conference managed to deploy a political parachute, halting the freefall, it has failed to spark a dramatic rebound. For now, the administration's numbers have entered a long, exhausting plateau.

Relief that the bleeding has stopped is premature when the grassroots mood remains volatile. Voters want to see proven competence and a clear vision, neither of which is currently being reflected in the indexes. A prolonged plateau is not stability; it is a precarious holding pattern that leaves the government vulnerable to another plunge.

The Danger of Alienating the Core Base

As South Korea approaches the high-stakes parliamentary audit season—where government ministries face intense scrutiny from the conservative opposition party, the People Power Party—the administration desperately needs its core supporters to form a defensive shield. Instead, policy missteps and tone-deaf communication are alienating even loyalists.

Within the progressive bloc, support from the Rebuilding Korea Party base has largely drained away, signaling cracks in the wider reformist coalition. Meanwhile, the conservative People Power Party base has zero intention of ever backing Lee Jae-myung. Desperately chasing elusive moderate or conservative voters while bleeding your own base is a classic political error. A government that loses its friends while failing to win over its adversaries is heading toward total isolation.

🔥 Alienating Your Base is Political Suicide

Conservative voters were never going to support the Lee administration. If the government fails to consolidate the progressive reform coalition and instead persists with policies that demoralize its own supporters, it risks governing in a vacuum.

Gallup Numbers and the Democratic Party's Sharp Drop

The Illusion of a "Concrete Floor"

Gallup Korea’s tracking puts the president’s approval rating at a static 37%, a figure some optimists label as the administration's "concrete support floor." This is a misreading of reality. At this stage, 37% is not a badge of stability; it is the final line of defense. The government is not resting on a solid foundation; it is barely keeping its head above water.

Concrete floors crack under enough pressure. The erosion of these numbers stems not just from presidential communication miscues, but from the perceived listlessness of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. The swift, decisive reforms voters expected have slowed to a crawl, replaced by a daily drip of disappointing news.

A 5% Percentage Point Drop for the Democratic Party

The sliding numbers are not confined to the executive branch. The Democratic Party saw its own party approval rating drop by 5 percentage points in a single cycle. The driver is clear: party morale has hit rock bottom. In areas that directly impact daily survival—such as real estate policy and cost-of-living measures—the government and ruling party have offered few comforting answers.

While citizens grapple with economic anxiety, the ruling party has often appeared consumed by internal factionalism and optics. Chasing centrist swing voters while ignoring your base is a recipe for losing both.

📊 What the Numbers Are Telling Us

An approval rating of 37% and a sharp 5-point drop for the ruling party are more than routine mid-term dips. They serve as a harsh warning that ignoring the material realities of citizens' lives will erode the foundation of any progressive administration.

Closing the Gap Between Rhetoric and Reality

President Lee’s late-night social media outreach shows genuine determination to connect with the public. But a leader's messaging cannot substitute for structural reform across a bureaucratic and complacent party apparatus. If the presidential megaphone shouts reform while the cabinet and ruling party stumble on basic governance, public fatigue will only deepen.

The core values that brought this government to power—fairness, justice, and protection for society's vulnerable—must be visibly realized in daily life. Excuses will no longer suffice. To survive the upcoming political battles, the administration must use this window to deliver tangible results rather than rhetorical appeals.

The numbers are not merely a report card; they are an early warning signal from a public that expected better. Listening to the muffled frustration behind the data, rather than dismissing it as noise, is the only way forward for South Korea's progressive leadership.
