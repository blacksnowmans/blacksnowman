South Korea’s Legislative Crunch Time: Media Battles, Budget Wars, and Reform Bills Surge in National Assembly

November 27, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul, the atmosphere on November 26 was nothing short of chaotic. With the statutory deadline for the regular parliamentary session rapidly approaching, committee rooms across the complex became battlegrounds. Lawmakers wrestled simultaneously over a sprawling 821 trillion won (approximately $590 billion) national budget, controversial media reform bills, sweeping overhauls to family law, and bitter disputes over local education funding.

The high-stakes showdown reflects the intense friction between South Korea's major political camps. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), aligning with the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, is pressing hard to push through core legislative priorities before the session expires. Meanwhile, the main opposition conservative group, the People Power Party (PPP), is fighting back aggressively, accusing the government of fiscal recklessness and authoritarian overreach. For observers outside Korea, this collision offers a clear window into how policy, regulatory power, and state spending are being reshaped in real time.

Regulating the Press: The Controversy Over the "Four Media Laws"

The sharpest political clash of the day took place within the National Assembly’s Culture, Sports, and Tourism Committee. In a single-party vote, DPK lawmakers unilaterally cleared a package of amendments collectively known as the "Four Media Laws" through the legislation review subcommittee, overriding vocal protests from opposition members.

At the heart of the approved package is a punitive mechanism targeting fake news and distorted reporting. Under the new provisions, media outlets determined to have published intentionally false or malicious reports can be barred from receiving state advertising contracts for up to one year. Ruling party lawmakers framed the measure as an essential tool to enforce journalistic accountability and protect the public from disinformation, arguing that financial penalties tied to government ad spending act as a necessary deterrent.

The opposition PPP vehemently rejected the move, walking out of the committee room in protest. Opposition leaders labeled the package a rushed legislative effort designed to "shackle the press" and tame outlets critical of the administration. They warned that using government ad budgets as a leverage point creates a dangerous mechanism for arbitrary state administrative punishment, which could severely chill investigative reporting.

Industry insiders and media freedom advocacy groups have raised similar alarms. Critics point out that South Korea already possesses civil defamation laws and a dedicated arbitration body for press disputes. Adding government advertisement cutoffs creates a system of "double penalty" that risks penalizing press freedom. Adding further fuel to the debate, DPK Representative Choi Min-hee introduced a separate amendment to the Press Arbitration Act. Her bill proposes expanding the Press Arbitration Commission to 120 members and granting the President of the Republic the power to directly appoint its chairperson. Opposition lawmakers argue that Presidential appointment power over the commission's leadership directly threatens its political neutrality, setting up another fierce battle as the bills move toward a plenary vote.

The $590 Billion Budget Battle and the Future Response Fund

While media regulations dominated the cultural committee, economic policy hit a standstill in the Special Committee on Budget and Accounts. Lawmakers clashed over the government's proposed 821 trillion won ($590 billion) national budget for the upcoming fiscal year, alongside a newly proposed "Future Response Fund."

The ruling DPK argues that expanding state spending beyond the baseline figure is vital to secure new national growth engines. The proposed Future Response Fund is designed to inject public capital directly into high-tech strategic sectors, particularly artificial intelligence and next-generation manufacturing. According to the ruling party, aggressive fiscal expansion is necessary to maintain competitiveness in global tech markets.

The opposition PPP has taken a starkly different stance, warning that the DPK’s spending plan relies on unsustainable national debt and weakens fiscal discipline. During the subpanel review, details of the Future Response Fund were put on hold after the two sides failed to agree even on its underlying legal foundation.

To counter the government's proposal, the PPP unveiled its own alternative 11.6 trillion won (about $8.3 billion) budget proposal titled the "Livelihood Resolution Budget." Rather than funding broad state-led investment funds, the PPP's plan calls for sharp cuts to spending it deems wasteful or politically motivated. Instead, the opposition advocates redirecting funds into targeted relief for small business owners, expanded welfare for low-income households, and housing stability measures.

The Budget Standoff in Numbers:
• 821 Trillion Won (~$590 Billion): Government and ruling party proposal targeting fiscal expansion and high-tech industrial funds.
• 11.6 Trillion Won (~$8.3 Billion): Opposition PPP alternative proposal focusing on targeted welfare, small business support, and fiscal restraint.

Education Chiefs Stand Ground Against Funding Cuts

Fiscal disputes spilled out of committee rooms and onto the front steps of the National Assembly on November 26, when regional education leaders staged a high-profile collective action. Sixteen superintendents of education from across South Korea's provinces and major cities held an emergency joint press conference to oppose proposed changes to the Local Education Fiscal Grant Act.

Under South Korea's education finance system, the central government allocates a fixed portion of internal tax revenues directly to regional education offices to fund primary and secondary schooling. Fearing that proposed revisions would reduce their budgetary allocations, the superintendents demanded that the National Assembly legally guarantee a minimum baseline grant of 84.1 trillion won (approximately $60 billion).

Gathering in front of the main Assembly building with just four days remaining in the legislative session, the superintendents warned that any reduction in state grants would directly hurt students. They argued that budget cuts would force regional offices to slash funding for essential programs, including school facility modernization, free meal plans, and expanding public childcare services.

The education chiefs expressed particular frustration over rumors that the grant restructuring might be attached to broader budget spending bills to force a quick vote. They urged lawmakers not to bypass public discussion, stressing that weakening local education finances puts the basic quality of public schooling at risk.

From 35-Year-Old Family Law Reforms to Tech Regulation Friction

Despite the broader political gridlock, several significant non-budget bills managed to make progress through parliamentary subcommittees.

Most notably, the Legislation and Judiciary Committee passed a comprehensive overhaul of the Family Litigation Act—the first major revision of the statute in 35 years. A key focus of the reform is tightening child support enforcement. Under the new text, courts can issue detention or custody orders (감치 처분) against delinquent parents who fail to pay child support for more than 30 days. Previously, legal loopholes and lengthy non-payment thresholds allowed non-custodial parents to evade obligations for extended periods. Additionally, the amendment establishes formal procedures allowing family courts to hear testimony directly from children under the age of 13 during custody and divorce proceedings, prioritizing child welfare.

In the technology and financial sectors, legislative outcomes were mixed:

  • Digital Assets Basic Act: DPK Representative Yoo Dong-soo introduced a comprehensive framework aimed at institutionalizing cryptocurrency markets, establishing clear token issuance guidelines, and enhancing investor protections.
  • Online Platform Fairness Act: The long-delayed bill aimed at regulating dominant tech platforms remains stuck in committee for its sixth consecutive year. The legislation faces renewed international friction following formal concerns raised by U.S. Republican lawmakers, who warned that the proposed rules could unfairly target American tech giants and trigger trade friction.
  • Healthcare & Regional Bills: Proposals to grant special judicial police powers to the National Health Insurance Service faced fierce resistance from medical groups and were set aside. A controversial bill allowing traditional Korean medicine doctors to use diagnostic X-ray devices was deferred for further review, while convenience store sales of basic over-the-counter drugs were kept capped at 20 items. On regional issues, lawmakers jointly introduced an amendment for the Gadeokdo New Airport project, while five separate bills aimed at relocating the head office of the National Agricultural Cooperative Federation (NongHyup) remained stalled due to competing regional interests.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly enters the final countdown of its regular session, several critical milestones will determine the political direction of the coming year:

  • Plenary Action on Media Laws: Watch whether the ruling DPK uses its parliamentary majority to push the "Four Media Laws" and the Press Arbitration Act through the full Assembly floor, or if opposition pushback forces procedural delays.
  • Budget Deadline Negotiations: Key leaders will attempt to reach a late-night compromise on the 821 trillion won budget. Pay close attention to whether the proposed Future Response Fund survives intact or gets scaled back in exchange for opposition-backed livelihood spending.
  • Education Grant Baseline: Keep an eye on whether lawmakers accept the superintendents' demand for an 84.1 trillion won baseline grant, or if local school budgets will face structural trimming in next year's final spending plan.
