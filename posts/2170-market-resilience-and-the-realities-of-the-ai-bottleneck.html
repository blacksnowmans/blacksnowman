Market Resilience and the Realities of the AI Bottleneck

November 10, 2026

For many investors waking up to the latest market data, the news that the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) has reclaimed the 10,000-point mark felt like a reprieve from a long, harsh winter. After a brutal stretch that left many questioning whether the AI narrative had finally hit a brick wall, the index’s two-day surge suggests that the market is beginning to find its footing. But this rebound is not just a technical bounce; it is a complex reaction to shifting energy prices and a softening of the volatile VIX index, which measures market fear.

However, beneath this veneer of recovery, a more profound, structural challenge remains. We are moving beyond the era of blind optimism and entering a phase where the market is demanding answers to a cold, hard question: Who is actually making money from all this hardware? As the dust settles following the recent U.S. election, the market is bracing for a new reality where AI data centers—the bedrock of the current tech boom—face significant regulatory headwinds and severe physical constraints. Understanding this shift is essential for anyone trying to navigate the current, volatile investment landscape.

The Resurrection of the Semiconductor Index and the Test of AI Substance

The recapture of the 10,000-point level by the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index is a necessary first step in restoring investor confidence, but the "quality" of this recovery matters more than the raw numbers. The market is shifting its focus from speculative growth to tangible financial health. We are no longer asking "who is building more chips," but "how are the end-users of these chips generating profit?"

Key to this transition will be the upcoming stock performance of companies like Anthropic, an AI startup and major competitor to OpenAI (the creators of ChatGPT). Investors are scrutinizing the revenue growth rates of these large language model developers. If these firms cannot prove that their massive capital expenditures—specifically on Nvidia’s GPUs—are translating into scalable revenue, the current market rally risks being a mere temporary correction. The companies that survive the next round of market volatility will be those that prove their AI infrastructure has real-world, profit-generating utility.

Oil Prices and the VIX: Signals of a Psychological Pivot

The commodity markets have offered a much-needed cooling effect on global anxiety. The price of West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil, a benchmark for U.S. oil prices, recently dipped below $90, settling around $86.70 per barrel. This drop is largely attributed to signs of easing geopolitical tensions in the Middle East, with reports of renewed negotiations involving mediators like Qatar and Pakistan. Should these talks succeed, the potential release of roughly $6 billion to $9 billion (about 8 trillion to 12 trillion won) in frozen Iranian assets could significantly alter the regional landscape.

For the South Korean economy, which is heavily dependent on energy imports, this decline in oil prices is a vital tailwind, easing inflationary pressures. Simultaneously, the VIX index, known as the "fear gauge," has retreated below 20, dropping from its recent highs of 28. Historically, when the VIX hits such extremes, it often signals a bottom in the stock market. While caution remains necessary, the market is clearly betting on a return to relative stability.

The Regulatory "Bomb" and the Physical Limits of AI

Even as market sentiment improves, a new danger is emerging: political and environmental regulation. Senator Bernie Sanders, a prominent figure in the U.S. progressive movement, has called for a national moratorium on large-scale data centers, citing their massive electricity consumption. Furthermore, the BAAJAA Act (Bipartisan American Affordable Living and Jobs Act), currently under discussion, threatens to curb tax incentives for data center operators and increase their energy costs.

The problem is not just policy; it is physics. AI development has outpaced the development of the power grids required to sustain it. A single Small Modular Reactor (SMR) requires roughly 56.8 million liters of water daily just for cooling—an amount equivalent to the daily consumption of 23,000 people. With the average wait time for power grid connection now exceeding four years, the "AI Revolution" is hitting a physical bottleneck. Paradoxically, this creates a massive opportunity for South Korean firms in the power infrastructure sector, such as HD Hyundai Electric and LS Electric, which have seen their order backlogs stretch well into 2029.

Nvidia and the Risks of "Hero-Dependent" Leadership

The valuation of Nvidia, the poster child of the AI boom, has become a point of intense debate. At roughly $180, many analysts argue that the stock is entering deep value territory; if it dips below $160, it becomes historically inexpensive relative to expected earnings. Yet, the stock remains sluggish.

A growing concern among investors is "leadership risk." Nvidia’s meteoric rise has been tied so closely to the charisma and strategic vision of its CEO, Jensen Huang, that the market is struggling to imagine the company without his singular direction. The market is beginning to price in the "post-Huang" uncertainty. When you invest in a company that relies on a single iconic figure, you are essentially betting on that individual’s invincibility. As the market enters a more mature, regulatory-heavy phase, Nvidia must prove that its systems—not just its CEO—are sustainable.

The Structural Flaws of the Korean Capital Market

Finally, we must address the persistent lethargy of the South Korean stock market. Critics often point to an "all-in" bank-centric culture, where capital is evaluated through the lens of collateral and tax law rather than creative, future-oriented business potential. This rigid structure makes it difficult for new, software-driven industries to thrive.

Furthermore, there is a fundamental issue with the independence of the Korea Exchange. When the leadership of the exchange is staffed by political appointees and the government maintains a tight grip on market policy, it stifles the market’s ability to self-correct. The recent attempts to regulate "short-selling" or "stock-price suppression" through government mandate mirror the top-down management styles seen in China rather than the free-market dynamics required for a world-class financial hub. For the South Korean retail investor—the "Donghak Ants"—the current system often feels like a rigged game where they are the primary victims of mismanagement and outdated bureaucracy.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the actual quarterly earnings reports of AI infrastructure companies. The narrative of "potential" is over; the era of "proof" has begun. If the data centers currently under construction cannot show concrete revenue growth by mid-2026, the current AI premium will likely evaporate, regardless of how much power they consume.
