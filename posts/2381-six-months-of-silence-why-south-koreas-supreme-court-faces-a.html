Six Months of Silence: Why South Korea’s Supreme Court Faces an Impeachment Threat Over Han Duck-soo

November 16, 2026

Outside South Korea’s Supreme Court in Seoul’s Seocho-dong legal district, a statue of the Goddess of Justice holds up a set of scales. Inside the courthouse, however, critics argue that time itself has become a thumb on the scale. For six months, the nation’s highest court has held off on issuing a ruling in the case of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, who stands accused of complicity in the emergency martial law and insurrection crisis involving Yoon Suk Yeol.

With Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol already removed from office through impeachment and held in prison, the prolonged silence from the Supreme Court has fueled a volatile debate across South Korea. As the six-month delay drags on, legal observers warn that the expiration of Han’s detention period could soon open the door for his release—a prospect that has collided head-on with public anger and prompted an extraordinary ultimatum from lawmakers to impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae.

According to a public opinion survey conducted by South Korean polling firm Realmeter, 56.9% of respondents view the potential release of the former prime minister as problematic. In domestic political commentary—most notably in a sharp critique published by the Korean political blog Black Snowman—observers contend that the court’s hesitation looks less like careful legal deliberation and more like political calculation by an entrenched judicial elite. Whether the court’s delay stems from procedural caution or political timidity, the standoff has pushed South Korea into a high-stakes confrontation between judicial independence and democratic accountability.

The Martial Law Fallout and a 56.9% Public Rebuke

To understand why a procedural question over one former official’s detention has ignited calls to impeach the head of the judiciary, it helps to look at the gravity of the underlying case. During the emergency martial law crisis centered on Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol, Han Duck-soo held the post of prime minister—the highest-ranking cabinet official responsible for overseeing state affairs. Rather than serving as a constitutional check against an unlawful bid to seize power, critics and investigators allege that Han’s actions went beyond mere dereliction of duty, placing him at the center of suspicions that he helped lend procedural cover to the insurrection incident.

Realmeter’s finding that 56.9% of South Koreans oppose releasing Han underscores how little appetite the public has for leniency. In assessing the polling data, Korean political commentators noted that the numbers cut across ordinary partisan lines and reflect a broad social consensus.

"The public holds a very strict standard regarding the release of insurrection offenders."

From the perspective articulated in Black Snowman’s commentary, that strict standard is rooted in everyday frustration over inequality before the law. Ordinary South Koreans grappling with high living costs and a tight job market face severe consequences for minor infractions or subsistence-related offenses. Critics argue that if high-ranking officials accused of undermining the constitutional order are quietly released on technical grounds—such as invoking defendants’ rights or citing health concerns—it confirms a widespread suspicion of judicial privilege. In that view, extending leniency to a key figure in an insurrection case does not uphold the rule of law; it erodes public faith in the courts altogether.

Six Months on Hold at Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae’s Supreme Court

At the center of the storm is the Supreme Court led by Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae. Why has a case tied to the most severe constitutional crisis in decades remained unresolved for half a year? In South Korea’s criminal justice system, the calendar carries concrete legal consequences: if final rulings are delayed long enough, a defendant’s statutory detention period expires, creating grounds for release pending further proceedings.

Critics inside and outside the legal community have begun to ask whether this six-month freeze is truly coincidental. As cited in the Korean commentary surrounding the case, analysts have observed that even as the Supreme Court appears to hesitate after delaying a ruling for six months, public opinion remains firmly negative toward any release.

Opponents of the court’s handling argue that a judiciary committed to defending the Constitution should have prioritized insurrection-related trials with speed and clarity. Instead, Black Snowman’s analysis characterizes the bench’s silence as a form of bureaucratic self-preservation—waiting for public outrage to cool before issuing a favorable ruling or canceling detention. Critics invoke the legal maxim that justice delayed is justice denied, warning that prolonged uncertainty harms social cohesion and deepens skepticism, particularly among younger South Koreans watching how power is treated by the courts.

Of course, courts are legally required to weigh due process, defense rights, and procedural standards rather than bow to popular pressure. Yet for those watching the clock tick down in Seocho-dong, the court’s failure to deliver a timely verdict has left it vulnerable to charges of opportunism regardless of how it eventually rules.

An Unprecedented Ultimatum: Impeaching the Chief Justice

Into this vacuum stepped the Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive party in South Korea’s National Assembly. Led by party leader Shin Jang-sik and floor leader Kim Jun-hyung, the party issued a blunt warning: if the Supreme Court moves to release Han Duck-soo, lawmakers will immediately push to impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae.

The threat marks a dramatic escalation, pulling the legislature and the judiciary toward a direct constitutional clash. Within South Korean political circles, the proposal has drawn immediate pushback from critics who warn that threatening to impeach a sitting Chief Justice over an ongoing case is an excessive measure that undermines judicial independence. Protecting judges from legislative retaliation, they argue, is essential to keeping courts free from political intimidation.

Supporters of the Rebuilding Korea Party’s stance reject that framing. As argued in the Korean commentary backing the ultimatum, judicial independence exists to uphold the Constitution and rests on public trust—not to function as a shield for officials suspected of attacking the constitutional order. From this viewpoint, when the judiciary abdicates its responsibility, the National Assembly’s constitutional power of impeachment serves as a necessary democratic check.

Still, political strategists acknowledge that pulling the trigger on impeachment carries steep risks for lawmakers. Both Park Si-young, a political commentator who runs the YouTube channel Park Si-young TV, and Realmeter CEO Lee Taek-soo have highlighted the delicate balancing act facing party leaders.

"While going as far as impeachment carries a heavy political burden, given that public opinion is negative toward a release, it remains to be seen how the party leadership will make use of it."

An impeachment drive could trigger legislative gridlock and rally South Korea’s conservative bloc around the defense of the courts. At the same time, the Rebuilding Korea Party and South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party face pressure from voters who oppose any compromise on accountability. Navigating between strong public hostility toward Han’s release and the institutional fallout of impeaching a Chief Justice will test the tactical judgment of party leaders in the weeks ahead.

Double Standards, Civic Pressure, and What to Watch Next

Behind the immediate fight over Han Duck-soo lies a deeper wound in South Korea’s modern political memory. For decades, critics have pointed to a recurring pattern in which powerful figures involved in military rebellions or insurrections were pardoned and restored to prominence within years, while ordinary citizens faced rigid enforcement of the law. Commentators warn that allowing high-ranking officials to retain top-tier defense attorneys—often former senior judges or prosecutors whose influence is known in Korea as jeongwan privilege—and secure leniency based on age or past government service perpetuates a damaging double standard.

In South Korea, civil society has repeatedly stepped in when formal institutions appear deadlocked. Commentators credit the 56.9% polling opposition not to passive sentiment, but to sustained civic engagement—from the citizens who rallied in the winter cold to defend the constitutional order to ongoing scrutiny across news outlets, YouTube channels, and social media platforms. Whether that pressure proves decisive now hinges on three concrete developments:

  • The Supreme Court’s next move on detention: Watch whether Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae and the bench finally issue a verdict in Han Duck-soo’s case, or allow the six-month delay to reach the expiration of his detention period and trigger his release.
  • Follow-through on the impeachment threat: If Han is released, watch whether Shin Jang-sik, Kim Jun-hyung, and the Rebuilding Korea Party formally file an impeachment motion against Jo Hee-dae—and whether the ruling Democratic Party joins an unprecedented confrontation with the judiciary or seeks a more cautious path.
  • Shifts in public and conservative reaction: Track upcoming polling from agencies like Realmeter to see whether public opposition remains firmly above 50%, and how South Korea’s conservative bloc responds if lawmakers move forward against the Chief Justice.

Ultimately, the standoff over Han Duck-soo is about far more than one former prime minister’s legal fate. It is a test of whether South Korea’s courts and parliament can resolve the legacy of the martial law crisis without shattering public trust in the rule of law itself.
