The Rise of JOMO in South Korea: Why Young Koreans Are Choosing to Miss Out

October 25, 2026

Late at night on a Seoul subway car, the scene is almost universally the same: rows of passengers hunched over glowing screens, thumbs flicking rhythmically through social media feeds, investment charts, and short-form video clips. For years, this hyper-connected rhythm was considered simply the cost of doing business in one of the world's most wired societies. Falling behind on the latest trending cafe in Seongsu-dong, missing a viral streaming drama, or failing to catch a sudden rally in retail stocks was not merely an inconvenience; it felt like a personal failing. The pressure to know everything, consume everything, and display everything created an ambient, chronic dread: FOMO, or the Fear of Missing Out.

Yet across South Korea, an unmistakable counter-reaction is taking root among young adults. Rather than scrambling to keep pace with an algorithmic conveyor belt, a growing number of people are deliberately pulling the plug. They are embracing what has come to be known as JOMO—the "Joy of Missing Out"—turning away from relentless digital competition in pursuit of psychological quiet. What looks at first glance like simple exhaustion is evolving into an intentional cultural stance: reclaiming personal sovereignty by choosing to be left behind.

From FOMO to Burnout: The Toll of Hyper-Connectivity

The anxiety that JOMO seeks to cure did not emerge in a vacuum. South Korea’s digital landscape is notoriously intense, characterized by ultra-fast infrastructure, pervasive mobile platforms, and dense social networks where consumption habits and lifestyle markers are shared and judged in real time. When someone else struck it rich in volatile stock or cryptocurrency swings, or documented flawless weekend getaways, the news landed directly on the retinas of peers who were already stretched thin by demanding work routines.

This dynamic fueled severe psychological strain. In recent years, psychiatric clinics across the country witnessed a notable influx of patients suffering from acute stress tied directly to investment obsessions and constant upward social comparison. When every price fluctuation and friend's luxury purchase triggers a spike in dopamine or a wave of relative deprivation, the nervous system remains in a permanent state of high alert. Many found themselves attending gatherings they had no interest in attending and memorizing cultural trivia they did not care about, purely out of terror that not knowing would render them socially obsolete.

The inevitable endpoint of this treadmill was pervasive burnout. By allowing external algorithms and peer surveillance to dictate how time and attention were spent, individuals found their mental reserves stripped clean. The constant chase after an illusion of connection was, paradoxically, leaving them entirely isolated and exhausted.

The Rise of JOMO and the Art of ‘Happy Abandonment’

Against this backdrop, JOMO is being reframed by South Korea's Gen Z not as a passive surrender, but as an assertive declaration of autonomy. Instead of asking how to absorb more information faster, young Koreans are beginning to ask: What can I afford to cut out?

This mindset has given rise to the phrase "happy abandonment" (haengbokhan pogi). In a society that has long treated relentless striving as a foundational virtue, quitting or missing out has historically carried the stigma of defeat. But as younger Koreans realize that endless consumption yields only emptiness, letting go has become a strategic necessity. Happy abandonment means consciously recognizing that human energy is finite, deliberately choosing not to chase every trend, and preserving the mental space that remains.

The concrete manifestations of this shift are striking in their deliberate simplicity:

  • Trading Streaming Video for the Radio: Rather than spending hours paralyzed by endless catalogs on streaming video platforms, individuals are tuning back into traditional FM radio broadcasts. By stripping away visual overstimulation and algorithmic recommendations, the warm cadence of a radio host's voice allows the mind’s internal pace to slow down.
  • Choosing Stoves over Delivery Apps: In a country famous for instant food delivery at any hour of the night, some are intentionally walking to local neighborhood markets, buying raw ingredients, and cooking meals from scratch. While vastly less "efficient" in modern terms, the tactile process of chopping, simmering, and waiting restores a foundational sense of agency.
  • Reviving the Written Word: Fast, transient instant messaging is being supplemented by handwritten letters. The deliberate friction of pen on paper forces a level of emotional depth that a quick reply on a mobile messenger cannot replicate.
  • Embracing Aimlessness: Sitting by a window in a cafe in Daehangno—Seoul’s historic theater and university district—silently watching rain fall while a phone sits turned upside down on silent, has become an intentional practice rather than wasted time.
"To fill a vessel with something new, you must first keep emptying it. Recognizing that happiness comes across a broad spectrum means letting go of standardized metrics of success."

A Luxury of the Connected: The Divide Between Choice and Exclusion

While the turn toward intentional disconnection provides relief for stressed professionals and students, it also surfaces an uncomfortable structural reality: the ability to walk away from digital systems is itself a privilege. Voluntary disconnection assumes that one possesses both the high-level literacy and the material security required to log off safely.

For large segments of Korean society, the challenge is not digital saturation, but involuntary exclusion. As daily life has migrated into automated touchscreens and mobile software, vulnerable groups are often left behind against their will. Elderly citizens regularly find themselves stranded before automated self-service kiosks in fast-food outlets, transport hubs, and neighborhood cafes, unable to navigate confusing digital interfaces. Similarly, when vital public welfare initiatives—such as emergency government support funds—are administered primarily through web portals and mobile applications, those lacking digital literacy face formidable bureaucratic walls.

This contrast is crucial for understanding the wider social landscape. For an exhausted white-collar worker or university student, putting a smartphone in a drawer is a healthy psychological reboot. For an elderly resident in a regional district, being cut off from the screen means being cut off from basic services and social participation. As public discussions around mental well-being champion the virtues of offline living, government initiatives focused on digital inclusion and digital literacy training remain essential so that "unplugging" remains a personal choice rather than an imposed hardship.

What to Watch Next

South Korea's experiment with JOMO is still taking shape, caught between deeply ingrained cultural habits of rapid adaptation and a newfound craving for rest. Over the coming months and years, several developments will signal whether this movement marks a permanent cultural pivot or merely a passing coping mechanism.

First, observe how personal boundary-setting evolves in workplaces and daily life. Track whether young workers push for clearer separations between professional messaging and off-hours rest, and whether offline social rituals—such as device-free dining or phone-free study spaces—move from niche novelties into mainstream social norms.

Second, watch the commercial response. As consumer tech companies and retail platforms face user fatigue, monitor whether digital wellness features, screen-time management tools, and simplified "dumb-phone" alternatives gain commercial traction in the Korean market.

Finally, keep an eye on how state policy negotiates the digital divide. As the administration continues to manage public service delivery, the balance between expanding digital efficiency and maintaining accessible offline access points for seniors and low-literacy citizens will test whether South Korea can foster a society where staying connected is easy, but stepping away remains a viable, healthy choice.
