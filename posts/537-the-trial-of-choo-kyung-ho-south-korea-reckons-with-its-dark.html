The Trial of Choo Kyung-ho: South Korea Reckons with its Darkest Night

October 01, 2026

The events of December 3—the night of the emergency martial law declaration—remain etched in the collective memory of South Koreans as the darkest moment in the nation’s constitutional history. While the immediate chaos of that night has passed, the legal reckoning is now moving toward its climax. At the center of this storm is Choo Kyung-ho, the current Mayor of Daegu, who, while serving as the floor leader of the People Power Party (the PPP, South Korea’s main conservative party) at the time, played a pivotal role in the attempt to paralyze the National Assembly. Prosecutors have requested a 20-year prison sentence, a figure that signals not just the potential end of a high-profile political career, but a profound judgment on the survival of South Korean democracy itself.

The Core of the Insurrection Charges

Blocking the Will of the People

The special prosecutor’s office, led by Cho Eun-seok, has painted a damning portrait of Choo’s actions. By seeking a 20-year sentence, the prosecution is arguing that Choo did not merely act out of misguided political loyalty; he actively sabotaged the constitutional functions of the National Assembly. As the PPP floor leader, Choo is accused of orchestrating maneuvers—such as repeatedly shifting the location of party meetings—to prevent lawmakers from gathering to vote on a motion to lift the martial law decree. This was not a disagreement over policy; it was an attempt to neuter a constitutional institution.

The trial has turned into a frame-by-frame dissection of security camera footage from the night of the incident. The footage shows the frantic, desperate defense of the National Assembly as elite martial law troops attempted to storm the main chamber. It was only through the physical resistance of parliamentary staff—who barricaded doors and deployed fire extinguishers—that the assembly was able to hold the vote that ultimately ended the martial law decree. The prosecution is now forcing the court to consider whether Choo’s efforts to stall that vote made him an accomplice to insurrection.

The Testimony of Ahn Cheol-soo

Perhaps the most revealing moment in the trial came with the testimony of lawmaker Ahn Cheol-soo. Ahn, a prominent figure in the conservative bloc, confirmed the internal paralysis within the PPP during the martial law crisis. While Ahn openly called for the resignation of then-President Yoon Suk Yeol, Choo remained steadfast in his commitment to "party-government unity."

Choo’s defense has been largely dismissive, claiming he was merely managing party affairs in a time of crisis. However, his frustration in court—where he complained about the level of detail in the questioning—suggests an ongoing refusal to engage with the gravity of the charges. The court is now tasked with separating the mundane duties of a party leader from the criminal act of facilitating a state of exception that, had it succeeded, would have destroyed the country's democratic framework.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • High Stakes: Daegu Mayor Choo Kyung-ho faces a potential 20-year prison sentence for his role in obstructing the National Assembly during the December 3 emergency martial law.
  • The Evidence: Prosecutors have used CCTV footage to argue that Choo coordinated legislative delays specifically to protect the martial law decree from being overturned.
  • The Defense: Choo maintains his actions were standard political maneuvers under the banner of "party-government unity."
  • Public Fallout: The crisis triggered a massive backlash, with PPP membership cancellations surging six-fold following the incident.

The Anatomy of Subservience: The "Party-Government-Office" Relationship

Command and Control in the Modern Era

The trial has exposed the unhealthy, top-down relationship between the presidential office (known as "Yongsan") and the ruling party. Choo’s testimony inadvertently revealed a structure where the party functioned less as an independent legislative body and more as an extension of presidential will. Text messages from presidential aides referring to "commands" from the President—and Choo’s subsequent compliance—highlight a culture of subservience that replaces democratic debate with a "command and obey" structure.

When the ruling party leadership feels compelled to answer to the President’s "commands" on holidays, the space for representing the public’s voice vanishes. This is the antithesis of the deliberative democracy that South Korea has fought to build over the last few decades. The trial is forcing the public to ask: If the ruling party is merely a tool for the executive branch, who is left to hold power accountable?

A Massive Exodus of Supporters

The political cost of these actions has been severe. In the ten days following the martial law decree, over 7,745 party members left the People Power Party. This mass exodus was not limited to the party’s fringe; it included the "responsible members"—the core base that forms the bedrock of any conservative political movement in Korea. Daily membership cancellations, which usually averaged around 100, surged to over 600 per day. This data tells a clear story: the party’s base felt betrayed by their leaders' disregard for constitutional norms.

Editor’s Perspective: Why We Must Remember

Reflecting on the events of December 3, it is easy to succumb to the temptation of apathy or the desire to move on. However, we must remain vigilant. We saw what happened when democracy was left vulnerable: some were wielding fire extinguishers to guard the doors of the National Assembly, while others—like the defendant in this case—were reportedly moving mountains to keep those doors locked.

The 20-year sentence requested by the prosecution is not just a punishment for one individual; it is a declaration that the constitution is not a plaything for politicians. If the legislative process can be silenced by executive "commands," then the very foundation of the Republic is at risk. As we await the verdict, the lesson remains clear: power must always be directed toward the people, never toward the preservation of a regime that seeks to override their voice. The struggle for democracy is never truly "over"—it is sustained by the memory of those who stood their ground when the lights began to go out.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on the upcoming sentencing hearing. Beyond the specific prison term, the court's written opinion will likely define the legal boundary for what constitutes "cooperation with insurrection," setting a vital precedent for future political crises in South Korea.
