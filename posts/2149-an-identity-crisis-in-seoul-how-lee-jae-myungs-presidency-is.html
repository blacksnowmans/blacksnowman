An Identity Crisis in Seoul: How Lee Jae-myung’s Presidency Is Alienating the Democratic Base

November 09, 2026

For the millions of South Korean progressives who marched through winter nights to defend democracy and pave the way for a transfer of power, the return of the Democratic Party of Korea to the Blue House was supposed to herald an era of restored civic dignity. Following the dramatic impeachment, removal, and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the liberal Democratic Party took the reins under President Lee Jae-myung. The party’s rank-and-file envisioned a government that would champion social welfare, defend the marginalized, and build upon the institutional milestones of the Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in eras.

Instead, a profound sense of ideological vertigo has gripped the progressive camp. Speeches and policy platforms emerging from the Lee administration increasingly feature harsh, unfiltered rhetoric directed at foreign migrant workers and multicultural households. Meanwhile, state messaging around social benefits has taken an aggressively punitive tone, casting recipients of unemployment insurance as freeloaders. For long-time supporters of South Korea's center-left, the unsettling question can no longer be avoided: Is this administration still governing as a Democratic Party government in anything but name?

A Sharp Right Turn: Attacking Welfare and Exploiting Anti-Immigrant Sentiment

The traditional identity of South Korea’s Democratic Party has long rested on defending human rights, expanding labor protections, and gradually constructing a modern welfare state. Yet recent broadsides from the presidential office have sounded remarkably like talking points borrowed from the conservative People Power Party or even further to the right.

The administration’s sudden willingness to single out migrant workers and frame multicultural families as societal burdens marks an unprecedented departure from mainstream progressive governance. In South Korea, where rapid demographic aging has created severe labor shortages in manufacturing and agriculture, multicultural policy has historically been approached by progressives as a vital matter of human rights and social integration. To discard that consensus in favor of inflammatory, exclusionary rhetoric is seen by party loyalists not as an accidental slip of the tongue, but as a deliberate attempt to court populist sentiment at the expense of vulnerable populations.

Equally jarring has been the administration’s aggressive crusade against alleged fraud in unemployment benefits and employment insurance. Rather than strengthening social safety nets during a punishing economic downturn, the government’s public framing mirrors the playbook used during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, treating everyday workers navigating job loss as moral hazards or latent criminals. When a Democratic president belittles the value of labor and attacks the baseline survival tools of the working poor, the sense of betrayal among the party’s traditional base cuts deep.

Political observers inside the camp suspect this sharp maneuver to the right is the byproduct of hidden political debts. Securing control over the Democratic Party apparatus and subsequently the presidency required enormous political capital. The suspicion is growing that President Lee’s administration is now repaying backroom IOUs to major corporate conglomerates and conservative establishment figures. In pursuing what the administration labels as "pragmatism," the president appears to be sacrificing the party’s core DNA to appease conservative media and business elites, undermining the very foundation of his governing coalition.

The Ghost of Roh Moo-hyun and the Fractured Legacy of Moon Jae-in

To understand why this shift feels so jarring, one must understand the emotional and moral benchmark set by past Democratic presidents. During his political ascent, the late President Roh Moo-hyun famously silenced skeptics by pointing to his closest confidant, Moon Jae-in, declaring that a candidate’s fitness for the presidency is proven by the character of the friends he keeps. That philosophy—that leadership is defined by the integrity, humility, and dedication of one’s inner circle—became a guiding ethos for South Korean progressives.

During the Moon Jae-in administration, that ethos translated into stable crisis management. Even as the COVID-19 pandemic threw the global economy into chaos, South Korea gained international praise from outlets like Bloomberg for balancing public health with economic resilience, maintaining top-tier innovation rankings and shielding vulnerable citizens through functional administrative systems. While Moon’s tenure had its share of fierce political battles, it maintained a baseline of decorum and institutional respect.

The contrast today could not be starker. Ordinary citizens report feeling an economic squeeze far more suffocating than during the height of the pandemic, yet the current leadership offers grievance and blame-shifting rather than reassuring statecraft. Furthermore, the figures who populate President Lee’s political orbit—political operatives and controversial surrogates such as Lee Dong-hyung, Kim Yong, Jeong Jin-sang, and Kim Hyun-ji—inspire unending legal scrutiny and factional division rather than broad public respect. The progressive public once rationalized Lee’s combative temperament as the sharp edge needed for bold reforms; today, many fear that edge has been turned inward to dismantle the Democratic Party’s own historical legacy.

Online Populism, Factional Loyalty, and the Politics of Envy

The internal contradictions of the Lee administration are perhaps most visible in its digital public relations. President Lee recently took to X (formerly Twitter) to deliver a philosophical sermon on civic responsibility:

"Praise, but do not glorify; support, but do not worship. Democracy does not come on its own, nor is it preserved on its own."

While the quote ostensibly borrows from the democratic wisdom of Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun, critics within the progressive movement greeted the post with derision. The high-minded rhetoric stands in direct contradiction to how power is exercised behind the scenes. While publicly lecturing citizens against personality cults, the administration has elevated fanatical loyalists who openly speak of eternal leadership and party continuity, even installing sycophantic content creators to helm digital broadcasts for the Blue House. Extreme fandom networks, including aggressive online contingents like the "Gukgudan" and "Mingmingdan," are routinely leveraged to intimidate internal party dissidents.

Beneath the surface lies an intense personal rivalry with former President Moon Jae-in. Rather than building on his predecessor’s accomplishments, Lee’s administration has shown a persistent desire to marginalize the pro-Moon faction. Key government appointments, such as retaining Choi Dong-seok—a vocal and persistent critic of the former president—at the head of the Ministry of Personnel Management, reflect a deliberate effort to dilute Moon’s legacy. President Lee has also repeatedly raised eyebrows by sharing and amplifying hostile social media clips produced by polarizing online figures targeting Moon and liberal intellectuals like writer Rhyu Si-min.

For veteran political watchers, this dynamic carries an unsettling echo of conservative President Lee Myung-bak’s obsession with Roh Moo-hyun after the latter retired to Bongha Village. Seeing crowds of ordinary citizens visit Moon’s quiet rural bookstore in retirement appears to provoke a profound insecurity in a sitting president whose own popularity remains fraught. When state apparatuses and aggressive public audits are weaponized against figures from the prior administration, personal resentment risks superseding the rule of law.

The Crackdown on Internal Dissent and What Comes Next

The friction between the Blue House and the broader progressive civil society has escalated into open confrontation. Independent media figures and movement leaders who command grassroots followings, such as Lee Jong-won, the head of the influential political commentary platform Sisa Tapa, have found themselves subjected to search warrants and mounting state pressure. Attempts to suppress voices that refuse to toe the party line have backfired, sparking pushback from civic groups who view these raids as an authoritarian overreach.

Critics argue that the driving force behind this hardline governing posture stems from the insular factional networks that propelled Lee’s rise—specifically ties linked to the hard-left Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance and early militant fan clubs like the "Sohn Gahyeok" (Fingers Revolutionary Army). Rather than governing as a broad church representing the diverse strands of South Korean liberalism, the administration has often prioritized factional survival and score-settling.

What should observers watch for in the months ahead?

  • The Widening Rift in the Progressive Camp: Watch whether traditional pro-Roh and pro-Moon lawmakers and grassroots activists mount an organized resistance against the Blue House’s policy shifts, especially if conservative social rhetoric continues to feature in executive statements.
  • Backlash from Organized Labor and Welfare Groups: As crackdowns on unemployment benefits deepen and anti-migrant discourse festers, expect South Korea's vocal civil society organizations and labor unions to distance themselves from a ruling party they once considered an ally.
  • The Battle Over Political Accountability: The intensifying legal and investigative scrutiny directed at independent progressive commentators like Sisa Tapa’s Lee Jong-won will serve as a key barometer of whether the administration plans to bridge the divide with its base—or double down on silencing critics from within.

Power acquired by abandoning foundational principles rarely produces long-term stability. If the Lee Jae-myung administration continues to govern by imitating the conservative playbook and settling personal grievances with the legacy of its predecessors, it risks alienating the very citizens who made its existence possible.
