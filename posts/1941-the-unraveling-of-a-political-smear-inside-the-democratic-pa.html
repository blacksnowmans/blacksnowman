The Unraveling of a Political Smear: Inside the Democratic Party's "Shincheonji" Controversy

November 04, 2026

For weeks, the halls of the National Assembly have been echoing with a bitter dispute that has left the ruling party, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), in a state of internal turmoil. At the heart of the storm is Kim Min-seok, a prominent figure within the DPK, who stands accused of orchestrating a baseless smear campaign against his own party colleagues. The controversy centers on allegations that certain party members were part of a "secret three-party alliance" involving the Shincheonji Church of Jesus—a fringe religious group often viewed with deep suspicion in South Korean society.

The situation has moved beyond mere political maneuvering. It has sparked a reckoning over how parties handle internal dissent and whether a closed-door apology can ever heal the damage caused by public accusations of "treason" or "secret cult affiliations." As the evidence—or lack thereof—comes to light, the incident has exposed a dangerous culture of "political labeling" that threatens to alienate the very base that propelled the DPK to power.

The Anatomy of a Baseless Accusation

During the party convention last August, Kim Min-seok alleged that a group of "anti-Lee (Jae-myung)" factions had formed a "secret three-party alliance" with the Shincheonji Church to sabotage the party's leadership. This accusation was not merely a critique; it was an incendiary label in a political climate where religious influence is a volatile subject.

Prominent figures such as senior party members Jung Chung-rae, Choi Min-hee, and Lee Sung-yoon were caught in the crosshairs, subjected to intense online vitriol and social stigma. When a subsequent internal party task force investigation concluded that there was absolutely no evidence to support these claims, the fallout was immediate. Critics, including fellow lawmakers Han Min-soo and Lee Sung-yoon, have publicly challenged Kim, arguing that a quiet, private apology in a closed-door session is a woefully insufficient response to what amounts to a character assassination of party members.

The controversy now touches upon the Political Parties Act, specifically Article 52, which prohibits the circulation of false information during party leadership elections to disadvantage rivals. Violators face up to five years in prison or fines of up to 10 million won (about $7,000). The push for transparency is not just about personal apologies; it is a legal demand for accountability. Party members are calling for the full disclosure of the task force’s findings, arguing that if the party is to maintain its moral authority, it must treat this internal "witch hunt" with the same severity it applies to its political opponents.

The Judiciary Under Scrutiny

While the DPK deals with its internal strife, the broader South Korean judicial system is facing a crisis of confidence under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Critics argue that the current judiciary has become increasingly politicized, operating under a double standard that favors the establishment over constitutional order.

One of the most glaring points of contention is the speed of justice. Observers have noted a stark contrast in how the courts handle cases: when the legal proceedings involved then-candidate Lee Jae-myung before the presidential election, the Supreme Court moved with unprecedented speed, reaching a verdict in just nine days. In contrast, cases involving Kim Keon-hee (the wife of imprisoned former leader Yoon Seok-yeol) have faced months of delays and procedural foot-dragging. This disparity has led to widespread public outcry and calls from within the legal community for Chief Justice Cho’s resignation or impeachment, with many accusing him of prioritizing political protectionism over judicial independence.

The tension has been exacerbated by the judiciary's refusal to accept appointments from President Lee Jae-myung, effectively challenging the separation of powers. By acting as if it is an autonomous political entity rather than a branch of government, the Supreme Court has alienated a public that is increasingly losing faith in the neutrality of the bench.

A Turning Point in Constitutional History

Amidst the domestic political infighting, a major milestone was reached in the courtroom. The Seoul Central District Court recently handed down heavy prison sentences to former military commanders involved in the "December 3rd Emergency Martial Law" incident. Yeo In-hyung, former head of the Defense Security Command, was sentenced to 18 years in prison, while other high-ranking officers received sentences ranging from 9 to 15 years.

The court’s ruling was unequivocal: the martial law declaration was illegal, and the military’s attempt to physically paralyze the National Assembly was an act of treason. The verdict serves as a stern warning that the military must never again be used as a tool for political ambition. It also dismantled the "just following orders" defense, establishing a legal precedent that soldiers have a duty to disobey illegal directives that threaten the constitutional order.

This ruling is more than just a punishment for individuals; it is a clarion call for institutional reform. Legal experts and human rights advocates are now urging the National Assembly to codify the "right to refuse illegal orders" into the Military Service Act. The message is clear: the only way to prevent the nightmare of illegal martial law from recurring is to strip away the culture of blind obedience and replace it with a firm commitment to the rule of law.

What to Watch Next

  • Public Apology Demands: Watch whether Kim Min-seok moves from private explanations to a formal, public apology to the members he maligned. Continued silence may lead to formal legal challenges under the Political Parties Act.
  • Judicial Reform: With growing public pressure, the discourse around the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae will likely intensify. The outcome will signal whether the judiciary is prepared to undergo a significant personnel and systemic overhaul.
  • Legislative Changes: Keep an eye on new bills aimed at strengthening the military's duty to resist illegal orders. If passed, this would represent a historic shift in the relationship between the military and the constitutional government of South Korea.
