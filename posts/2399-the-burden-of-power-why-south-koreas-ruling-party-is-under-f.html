The Burden of Power: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Under Fire from Its Own Supporters

November 16, 2026

Imagine a late-night YouTube live stream, the chat window scrolling so fast it becomes a blur of neon text. Thousands of viewers are tuned in, not for entertainment, but for a post-mortem on the day’s political maneuvers. The atmosphere is thick with a specific kind of Korean political frustration—a mixture of deep loyalty and growing impatience. For the supporters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, the honeymoon period of power has given way to a demanding scrutiny of their own leaders.

In a recent broadcast of the influential YouTube political program Kim Seung-hyun’s Thesis, produced by ANK Media Lab, this tension boiled over. The host, Kim Seung-hyun, spent a significant portion of a nearly two-hour broadcast addressing what he characterized as a vacuum of leadership within the DPK. Specifically, the program focused on the perceived silence of the party’s "Supreme Council"—the top-tier executive body that steers party strategy—and the need for a high-profile press conference from one of its key members, Park Sun-won.

To an outside observer, this might look like a minor internal squabble. However, in the context of South Korean politics, where the ruling party is navigating a complex post-impeachment landscape and a shift in executive power, these demands represent a fundamental struggle over the meaning of "responsible politics." The DPK, having ascended to the presidency under Lee Jae-myung while the former leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol remains imprisoned following his removal from office, now faces the heavy crown of governance. Its supporters are no longer content with the party acting like an opposition force; they want the authority they voted for to be exercised with total transparency.

The Institutional Weight of the Sitting Lawmaker

The primary critique leveled during the Kim Seung-hyun’s Thesis broadcast—specifically between the 90 and 111-minute marks—was directed at the perceived passivity of sitting lawmakers. In the Korean political system, a "sitting lawmaker" (hyeonjik uiwon) holds immense legal and symbolic power. They are the ones who can propose bills, manage the national budget within parliamentary committees, and command the "official mic" of the National Assembly.

"You are a sitting lawmaker. We need sitting lawmakers to act," Kim Seung-hyun emphasized during the broadcast. This plea highlights a recurring grievance among the DPK base: the feeling that while independent commentators and YouTubers are fighting on the front lines of public opinion, the actual elected officials—those with the constitutional power to enact change—are hovering in the background. This is particularly sensitive regarding economic issues. In a period where the Lee Jae-myung administration is tasked with stabilizing the national economy, supporters expect the party's leadership to be the face of these solutions, not just silent supporters of the presidential office.

The frustration stems from the gap between "street politics" and "institutional politics." To a supporter, a lawmaker’s caution can look like cowardice. To the lawmaker, however, the reality is often more constrained. They must balance the demands of the party’s core base with the broader interests of their local constituencies and the necessity of negotiating with the main opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP). This friction is the first major hurdle for the DPK as it attempts to consolidate its role as the stable ruling party.

The Messaging Crisis and Internal Discord

Beyond mere silence, the YouTube broadcast took aim at the "discordant messages" coming out of the DPK. When different lawmakers offer conflicting views on the party’s official stance, it creates a fog of confusion that demoralizes the base. For a ruling party, message discipline is not just about optics; it is about the ability to govern effectively. If the public—and the party’s own supporters—cannot discern the official line on a major economic or social policy, the administration’s authority begins to erode.

The broadcast argued that these stray comments from individual members give ammunition to political rivals and dilute the impact of the leadership’s primary agenda. From the perspective of the party faithful, it feels like "friendly fire." However, this raises a difficult question for any democratic party: how much dissent is healthy? The DPK is a "big tent" party representing a wide array of regional and ideological interests. Forcing total uniformity can stifle the very internal checks and balances that prevent a party from becoming a monolith.

"The core issue isn't the existence of different opinions, but whether those opinions are being reconciled through the party's official channels or scattered across the media to create unnecessary fatigue,"
the broadcast noted. For the viewers of Kim Seung-hyun’s Thesis, the solution is clear: the leadership needs to step up, discipline the narrative, and lead from the front.

The Call for Park Sun-won’s Press Conference

The most specific and perhaps most explosive demand in the broadcast was directed at Park Sun-won, a member of the DPK’s Supreme Council. The Supreme Council is the party’s highest decision-making body, and its members are expected to be the vanguard of the party’s political battles. Specifically, Kim Seung-hyun called for Park to hold a formal press conference regarding what was termed the "Secret Three-Party Alliance" (ban-bunsin bimil sam-ja-yeondae).

The program argued that Park Sun-won has been remarkably consistent in his stance regarding this alliance behind the scenes. Because of this consistency, the host argued, Park has a moral and political obligation to bring the matter into the light of a formal press conference. Why a press conference? In the Korean political hierarchy, a YouTube appearance or a social media post is ephemeral. A press conference held at the National Assembly’s communication hub is a matter of public record. It involves taking direct questions from the press corps and assuming a level of legal and political accountability that informal channels simply do not provide.

The nature of this "secret alliance" remains a point of intense speculation among the DPK’s active supporters. By demanding a formal disclosure, the broadcast is pushing for a "moment of truth" that could either clear the air or set off a political firestorm. For Park Sun-won, the dilemma is acute. Holding such a conference requires not just conviction, but a stockpile of verifiable evidence. In the high-stakes world of the ruling party, a premature or poorly evidenced disclosure can lead to devastating political blowback, not just for the individual, but for the entire Lee Jae-myung administration.

Bridging the Gap Between Supporters and Leadership

The emotional core of the broadcast was captured in a message from a donor that was read aloud: "Why must the people I support always be isolated and harassed? When I think of these days, politics feels so dirty." This sentiment of isolation and exhaustion is common among the most dedicated "active" supporters (often referred to as gaeddari in certain contexts, though the term has various connotations). They feel that the politicians they fought to put into power are being bullied by the media or the opposition, and that the party leadership isn't doing enough to protect them or their shared principles.

This highlights a significant challenge for the DPK. The party’s strength lies in its highly mobilized, digitally savvy base. However, this base often operates on a different timeline than the institutional leadership. The base wants immediate transparency and aggressive counter-attacks; the leadership, managing the gears of government, often prioritizes "situation management" and long-term stability.

To bridge this gap, the DPK must move toward a model of "responsible communication." This doesn't necessarily mean doing everything the YouTube comments demand, but it does mean explaining *why* certain actions are being taken—or why they are being delayed. Silence in the face of supporter anxiety is often interpreted as a lack of will or, worse, a betrayal of the cause.

What to Watch Next

The tension described in Kim Seung-hyun’s Thesis is a microcosm of the DPK’s broader struggle as South Korea’s ruling party. As we move forward, there are several concrete developments that will signal which way the wind is blowing:

  • Park Sun-won’s Decision: Will the Supreme Council member actually hold the requested press conference? If he does, the details of the "Secret Three-Party Alliance" could redefine the internal power dynamics of the party. If he doesn't, the pressure from the YouTube-based fringe of the party is likely to intensify.
  • Economic Policy Proactivity: Watch for whether DPK lawmakers begin to take a more visible, leading role in announcing economic relief measures or legislative reforms. This will be the litmus test for whether the party is shifting from "defense" to "governance."
  • Message Discipline: Keep an eye on the official briefings from the DPK. Are the "stray voices" being brought back into the fold, or is the party showing signs of internal fragmentation?
  • The Role of Alternative Media: The influence of channels like Kim Seung-hyun’s Thesis is not waning. How the Lee Jae-myung administration and the DPK leadership choose to engage—or not engage—with these digital power brokers will determine how stable their base remains.

Ultimately, the demand for a press conference is a demand for the basic currency of democracy: accountability. Whether the DPK leadership can provide that while also managing the complex realities of running a country remains the defining question of the current political era in South Korea. In a landscape where the former leader remains behind bars and the current ruling party holds a decisive mandate, the only real threat to their power may come from the very people who gave it to them.
