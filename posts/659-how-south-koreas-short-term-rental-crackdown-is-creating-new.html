Black Snowman

How South Korea's Short-Term Rental Crackdown Is Creating New Property Winners

September 11, 2026

For years, scrolling through social media in South Korea meant running into a familiar boast: a side hustle running an Airbnb was supposedly printing easy money every month. All it took was a spare room or two, and passive income would roll straight into your bank account. Today, that narrative has completely unraveled. As regulatory pressure tightens across Seoul and other major hubs, the golden age of casual short-term hosting is colliding with hard reality, prompting many owners to panic-sell their properties at a discount.

To the casual observer, the writing is on the wall: the short-term rental boom is dead. But seasoned real estate investors are watching the exact same panic and seeing an opening. While fearful landlords dump assets below market value, opportunistic operators are stepping in to snap them up, proving once again that regulatory chaos rarely destroys a market—it merely redistributes the profits.

The Airbnb Exemption Trap and the 2027 Cliff

For a long time, South Korea’s short-term rental regulations maintained a deeply conservative stance on older buildings. Properties over thirty years old, or semi-basement units—famous globally after the success of the film Parasite—faced blanket bans. Government officials argued that older structures posed severe safety risks for foreign tourists, and that any resulting accidents would become an administrative headache for regulators. It was classic bureaucratic risk aversion.

Yet the numbers on the ground refused to cooperate with government caution. Fueled by the unending global wave of the Korean Wave, foreign arrivals surged. Between January and November 2025 alone, tourist arrivals topped 17.4 million, making an annual total of 18.5 million all but guaranteed. In Seoul, April arrivals jumped 19% year-on-year, injecting roughly 1.15 trillion won (about $850 million) into the local economy. Traditional hotels quickly found themselves overwhelmed, forcing the government to open a pressure valve.

On October 10, 2025, authorities introduced a special deregulation trial scrapping the 30-year age limit for urban guesthouses catering to foreign tourists. Yet the concession came with a bitter pill: the exemption was explicitly designated as a temporary pilot program set to expire permanently on December 31, 2027. Owners of aging buildings realized they were holding ticking time bombs. With legality guaranteed for only a few more years, a wave of anxious property owners began dumping their units onto the market.

Smart operators saw blood in the water. Near Seoul’s Ttukseom Station, one entrepreneurial tenant approached the owner of a multi-family building containing four units that could no longer operate legally under standard long-term rules. Negotiating directly, the operator secured the remaining lease span leading up to the 2027 deadline at a deep discount, sidestepping the steep key money deposits typically required for commercial spaces. Before renovations were even complete, all four units were fully booked, instantly recovering the initial outlay.

Data Blunders and the Bureaucratic Housing Crunch

Behind these sudden market shifts lies a trail of administrative miscalculations. According to a regular audit by the Board of Audit and Inspection—South Korea’s state watchdog—the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism instructed local governments to analyze lodging supply and demand back in 2022, but failed to provide coherent metrics. Seoul officials responded by measuring the growth rate of businesses rather than actual hotel rooms, generating a profoundly flawed projection.

The Ministry approved these nonsensical forecasts without verification, leaving Seoul’s municipal government convinced that the city was hurtling toward an oversupply of tourist lodging, even as tourists flooded the streets. When auditors recalculated the figures, they concluded that if inbound tourism surpasses 19.1 million, Seoul will face a severe hotel room shortage. In short, government agencies manufactured a policy crisis through bureaucratic incompetence, leaving small business owners and tenants to absorb the resulting shockwaves.

The Rise of Alternative Platforms Like Enkostay

As traditional short-term rentals face narrowing horizons, alternative models are filling the vacuum. Chief among them is Enkostay, a short-term rental platform built specifically for foreign students and long-term exchange visitors rather than typical tourists.

The structural advantages are significant. Standard Airbnb operations require complex business registrations tied to specific residency documents. Enkostay, by contrast, allows hosts to list properties almost immediately after lease execution. With South Korea home to over 160,000 international students—a figure the government aims to expand to 300,000 by 2027—platforms catering to mid-term stays are tapping into an explosive, legally stable demographic.

The operational differences between traditional short-term rentals and mid-term leasing are stark. Airbnb hosts face a grueling physical grind, turning over rooms, washing linens, and replacing decorative items every single day. Mid-term leases running two to six months, however, can be managed remotely, even from abroad. While daily turnover can yield higher per-night revenues, expanding a portfolio across multiple units makes the systematized, lower-labor model of mid-term leasing far more sustainable.

Scaling Through Networks and Remote Management

A common trap for newcomers to the space is trying to handle every operational detail alone. Attempting to micromanage daily cleanings and marketing for multiple units quickly leads to burnout. Successful operators survive by leveraging longer lease durations and cooperative local networks.

For instance, an operator managing multiple units in Seoul who receives an inquiry from an Italian exchange student doesn't simply turn them away if their own rooms are full. Instead, they pass the lead to a trusted peer within an informal network, securing a six-month booking and creating a reciprocal pipeline. Furthermore, physical location is no longer a barrier: operators living in places like Jeju Island successfully manage Seoul portfolios by combining automated smart locks with third-party cleaning contractors.

What to Watch Next

Navigating South Korea's changing accommodation landscape requires watching several concrete indicators over the coming months:

  • The 2027 Cliff: Monitor how aggressively landlords holding pre-1995 buildings offload assets as the December 2027 regulatory expiration approaches.
  • International Student Targets: Track immigration and Ministry of Education data regarding the 300,000 foreign student target, which underpins the viability of platforms like Enkostay.
  • Domestic Sharing Legislation: Watch for parliamentary movement on proposed bills to legalize home-sharing for domestic travelers, keeping in mind that fierce opposition from traditional hotels has historically derailed similar initiatives.

Regulatory shifts will always terrify the unprepared, but they also systematically clear the field for those willing to read the underlying data. In South Korea's evolving property market, fortune consistently favors the operator who looks past the panic and builds a better system.
