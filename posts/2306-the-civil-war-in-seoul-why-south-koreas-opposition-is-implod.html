The Civil War in Seoul: Why South Korea’s Opposition is Imploding While a Legal Clock Ticks

November 14, 2026

In the quiet, early morning hours at a cafe near Gwanghwamun, the historic heart of Seoul, two men sat down for a breakfast that was ostensibly about bus routes. To a casual observer, Lee Jun-seok, a representative of the minor Reform Party, and Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul, were simply discussing the logistical headache of expanding commuter lines between the capital and the satellite city of Hwaseong. But in the hyper-sensitive ecosystem of South Korean politics, there is no such thing as a "simple" breakfast between a former conservative party leader and a sitting mayor who remains a top-tier presidential hopeful.

Outside the cafe, the political landscape is in a state of violent flux. As of November 13, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, is not just divided; it is undergoing a public, messy, and potentially terminal internal schism. Meanwhile, the judiciary is facing an unprecedented ultimatum over the fate of Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister who currently sits in a detention center. With his detention set to expire in just seven days, the Supreme Court finds itself caught between legal procedure and the threat of political impeachment from the ruling party.

For those watching from outside the peninsula, the current chaos can seem like a dizzying array of names and acronyms. However, what is happening in Seoul right now is a fundamental battle over the future of the conservative movement and the independence of the judiciary under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party (DP). At stake is whether the opposition can survive its own internal purging or if the country is headed toward a period of unchecked dominance by the current government.

The Internal Implosion of the People Power Party

The People Power Party, which should be serving as the primary check on President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, is currently consumed by a scandal involving allegations of "manipulated disciplinary actions." The conflict pits the current leadership, led by Party Representative Jang Dong-hyeok, against a growing "anti-leadership" faction that claims the party is being run like a private fiefdom.

The core of the controversy involves whistleblowers claiming that the party’s leadership fabricated or manipulated internal disciplinary proceedings to sideline political rivals. This has led to a "mud-slinging" match where the leadership is tightening the gates against returning members, while the opposition within the party is demanding a full-scale investigation committee. Jang Dong-hyeok’s leadership has dismissed these demands as "political maneuvering" designed to destabilize the party, refusing to even consider an internal probe.

This internal war is most visible in the battle over Han Dong-hoon, the former head of the party’s emergency response committee. Han, once a close ally of the previous conservative establishment, is now a lightning rod for division. Representative Jang has gone as far as to describe Han’s attempts to rejoin the party's core as "the politics of immorality," using a term in Korean (paeryun) that usually refers to a child betraying a parent. This vitriol highlights a "pro-Jang" versus "anti-Jang" divide that has paralyzed the party's ability to engage in actual policy-making.

While the leadership fights to keep Han Dong-hoon out, a minority faction argues that the party must expand its tent to survive. They see the exclusion of key figures as a path to "self-destruction." For the average voter, the sight of the main opposition party spending its energy on internal purges while the cost of living rises is a source of profound fatigue. The party is no longer debating how to govern; it is debating who gets to keep the keys to a house that is currently on fire.

Lee Jun-seok, Oh Se-hoon, and the Reshaping of the Conservative Base

While the PPP leadership is busy locking doors, others are looking for a way to build a new house entirely. This is why the Gwanghwamun breakfast between Lee Jun-seok and Mayor Oh Se-hoon is so significant. Lee Jun-seok, the young, Harvard-educated former chairman of the PPP who was famously ousted during the turbulent era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, now leads the Reform Party, a "third zone" group that seeks to attract younger, more moderate conservatives.

By meeting with Oh Se-hoon, a heavyweight within the conservative movement but one who maintains a degree of distance from the PPP’s internal bickering, Lee is signaling a shift toward "policy-centric" alliances. They discussed the Dongtan commuter bus routes—a pragmatic issue for thousands of workers—but the subtext was clear: while the PPP leadership screams about "immorality," we are talking about how to get people to work.

This "Third Zone" movement is gaining traction as PPP heavyweights like Ahn Cheol-soo and Shin Dong-wook also begin to explore alternatives. Shin Dong-wook recently suggested in an interview that integrating with Lee Jun-seok might be more beneficial for the conservative cause than the return of Han Dong-hoon. This highlights a complex calculation: is the PPP salvageable, or is the future of Korean conservatism found in a coalition between metropolitan administrators like Oh Se-hoon and younger reformers like Lee Jun-seok?

However, these movements are not without skepticism. Critics argue that these "Third Zone" maneuvers are just as much about political survival as the PPP's infighting. If the goal is merely to secure a seat at the table for the next election rather than offering a genuine alternative to the Democratic Party's current dominance, these alliances may prove just as fragile as the parties they seek to replace.

Seven Days to Freedom: The Han Duck-soo Legal Standoff

Perhaps the most explosive issue in Seoul this week is not in the halls of the National Assembly, but in the chambers of the Supreme Court. Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister who served during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, is currently facing charges related to alleged "insurrectionary" activities. He is being held in pre-trial detention, but that legal clock is about to run out on November 20.

Under South Korean law, there are strict limits on how long a suspect can be detained without a final conviction. If the Supreme Court does not issue a final ruling by next Wednesday, Han Duck-soo must be released to continue his trial as a free man. This has sparked a furious battle between the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition.

The ruling DP is applying immense pressure on the judiciary. Some party members have even revived the threat of impeaching Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae if the court allows Han to be released. From the DP's perspective, Han Duck-soo is a primary figure in what they describe as "the disruption of constitutional order," and his release would be an affront to "national sentiment."

"The release of a key suspect in a case of constitutional disruption would be a failure of the judiciary to fulfill its role," a ruling party spokesperson recently suggested.

On the other side, the PPP and legal scholars argue that the judiciary must not be bullied by political threats. They maintain that the 6-month detention limit is a fundamental human right and a procedural necessity. To threaten a Chief Justice with impeachment over a procedural release is, in their view, an attack on the separation of powers. The Supreme Court has remained cautious, asking the media to refrain from speculative reporting, but the tension in the Seocho-dong legal district is palpable. The judiciary is effectively being told: "Rule our way, or face the consequences."

Presidential Diplomacy and the Fragile Progressive Alliance

Amidst this backdrop, President Lee Jae-myung is attempting to project an image of "national management." He has invited the leaders of the various political parties to a luncheon at the Blue House this Sunday. The agenda is heavy: a proposed supplementary budget (often reaching into the tens of trillions of won, or tens of billions of dollars), the confirmation of a new Prime Minister, and the perennial headache of committee chair assignments in the National Assembly.

The President’s office says the goal is "cooperation for the people’s livelihood." However, the main opposition is preparing for a "surgical verification" of the government's policies. The PPP is particularly focused on the government’s capital market policies and its recent personnel appointments, which the opposition claims are biased. The fight over the chair of the Legislation and Judiciary Committee—a powerful gatekeeper for all bills—remains a total stalemate.

Furthermore, the progressive camp is not as unified as it seems. A rift has opened between the ruling Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Cho Kuk. Allegations of a "secret pact" regarding future party leadership and presidential ambitions have led to a public falling out. Cho Kuk recently demanded a formal apology from DP representatives who suggested he was involved in backroom deals, citing his past experience with "conspiracy theorists" during his own legal battles.

This internal friction within the pro-government camp suggests that even with a weakened opposition, President Lee Jae-myung faces significant hurdles in maintaining a stable legislative coalition. When the groups responsible for governing spend their time debunking "secret pact" rumors, the actual business of the state—addressing inflation and navigating a cooling global economy—tends to fall by the wayside.

What to Watch Next

  • November 20 - The Han Duck-soo Deadline: If the Supreme Court does not rule, Han will be released. Watch for the ruling party’s reaction—will they actually move forward with impeachment proceedings against the Chief Justice?
  • Sunday’s Presidential Luncheon: Look for whether any agreement is reached on the supplementary budget. If the meeting ends without a joint statement, expect a winter of legislative gridlock.
  • PPP Disciplinary Investigation: Watch if the "anti-leadership" faction in the PPP can force an independent probe into the "manipulated discipline" allegations. If they fail, we may see a wave of defections to the "Third Zone."
  • The Oh-Lee Alliance: Monitor whether Mayor Oh Se-hoon and Lee Jun-seok continue their "policy meetings." A formal partnership between them would signal a massive realignment of the Korean right, potentially bypassing the PPP entirely.

The current state of South Korean politics is a sobering reminder of what happens when partisan survival outweighs public service. With the opposition tearing itself apart and the ruling coalition distracted by internal rivalries and judicial pressure, the "people's livelihood" remains a secondary concern. The coming week will determine whether the country moves toward a fragile stability or a deeper constitutional crisis.
