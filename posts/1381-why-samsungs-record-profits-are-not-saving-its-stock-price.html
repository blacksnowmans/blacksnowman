Why Samsung's Record Profits Are Not Saving Its Stock Price

October 22, 2026

Walking through the financial district in Seoul these days, you can feel the quiet anxiety gripping millions of retail investors. Known locally as "ant investors," ordinary people who poured their savings into South Korea’s stock market—often taking on debt during the pandemic-era boom—are staring at glowing blue numbers on their trading apps, wondering where everything went wrong. Their screens show a relentless slide, even as the corporate headlines tell a completely different story of record-breaking profits and technological dominance.

To understand this bizarre disconnect, we have to look past the hysterical news cycles and examine the actual plumbing of South Korea’s tech economy. The core reality is striking: more than 80% of Samsung Electronics’ projected memory chip production for next year is already locked into long-term supply agreements. The factories in Pyeongtaek and Giheung are running flat out. The market panic is not a reflection of damaged fundamentals; it is a distortion driven by macroeconomic anxiety, temporary sentiment shifts, and global financial weariness.

For foreign observers trying to understand South Korea's heavyweights, the current moment offers a masterclass in how market psychology can decouple from industrial reality. Let us unpack the data behind the headlines.

The Great Disconnect: Record Earnings Meet Market Panic

For a young South Korean office worker navigating high inflation, soaring real estate prices in Seoul, and a brutal job market, buying shares of Samsung Electronics—South Korea’s undisputed corporate titan—has long felt like the only reliable path to building a future. Yet recent charts have been nothing short of a tragedy.

Quarterly earnings reports regularly show Samsung raking in astronomical profits from its semiconductor division, yet the stock price on the Home Trading System (HTS) continues to slip. Investors are left crying out in frustration: "Just how good do the earnings have to be for the stock to go up?"

This abnormal decoupling stems from external pressures rather than corporate weakness. Confusion over the U.S. Federal Reserve's rate cuts, volatile currency exchange rates, and Wall Street fatigue toward tech stocks have given institutional investors an excuse to dump shares. During the previous conservative administration under the impeached former leader Yoon Seok-yeol—now imprisoned following his downfall—loose economic policies and delayed restructuring left the broader market vulnerable. As global supply chain uncertainties smooth out, the stock market has once again made heavy-duty blue chips the primary scapegoat.

Market research firm TrendForce recently projected that quarter-over-quarter price gains for standard DRAM chips will slow from 90% in the first quarter and 60% in the second to around 10% in the back half of the year. Financial institutions immediately jumped to the conclusion that profits have "peaked" and rushed for the exits. But a slowing pace of price growth does not mean earnings are collapsing. The market is simply transitioning from an extreme price surge into a stable, high-yield plateau—yet Wall Street-style panic continues to drive excessive sell-offs.

Locked-In Certainty: The Power of Long-Term Memory Contracts

If you look past the daily stock charts and examine actual trade data, a very different picture emerges. Analysis from KB Securities reveals a decisive fact that completely undercuts the market's anxiety: Samsung has already secured long-term supply agreements (LTAs) with global big tech companies for roughly 80% of its total planned memory production for next year.

Breaking that down further, about 70% of standard DRAM and NAND flash production is already contracted. More importantly, High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—the critical hardware powering the artificial intelligence boom—is completely sold out for the entire year. This means the goods leaving the assembly lines next year already have their buyers lined up before the machines even turn on.

In past memory chip cycles, brutal "chicken games" of overproduction often left warehouses overflowing with unsellable inventory, plunging chipmakers into the red. Today's cycle is entirely different. Hyperscalers like Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are pouring capital expenditures into building proprietary AI data centers, practically begging memory suppliers to allocate volume.

Even for the remaining 20% of uncontracted capacity, global tech giants are engaged in fierce underground bidding wars. In a notoriously volatile stock market, having 80% of next year's revenue and profit already stamped and signed into corporate ledgers provides a concrete safety net that few companies in the world can match.

The Market's Core Contradiction: Corporate value is simply the sum of future cash flows. If a company has locked down 80% of its production via long-term contracts and completely sold out its advanced AI memory lines, a falling stock price is not a sign of broken fundamentals. It is merely a mirage generated by impatient financial capital.

Financial industry estimates suggest Samsung's annual operating profit next year could reach an astonishing 555 trillion won (about $400 billion). Compared to this year's projected 367 trillion won (about $265 billion), that represents a staggering 51% vertical leap in just 12 months. Driving this surge is the unprecedented explosion in the average selling price (ASP) of HBM—particularly as the industry transitions to the sixth-generation HBM4 standard, which requires custom base dies and advanced packaging.

Foundry Turnaround: 4-Nanometer Lines Hit Full Capacity

For years, Samsung’s non-memory foundry business—which manufactures custom chips designed by fabless firms—has been a persistent sore spot, bleeding trillions of won every quarter. But a major reversal is underway. The advanced 4-nanometer (nm) manufacturing lines have recently hit effectively 100% full capacity due to a surge of orders from global clients.

Just a year or two ago, critics claimed Samsung had lost all major clients to its dominant Taiwanese rival, TSMC, due to yield rate controversies. But Samsung has successfully dragged its 4-nanometer yield past the critical 80% threshold. With production now stable, fabless designers looking to maximize performance-to-cost ratios have returned to Samsung's doorstep, spilling excess demand over into its 5-nanometer lines.

In the foundry business, fixed costs for cleanrooms and extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines are astronomical. If factory utilization drops below 50%, multi-trillion-won losses pile up instantly. But once the lines fill up, fixed costs dilute rapidly, turning operations profitable. Samsung's strategy to slash near-term losses and build a runway for foundry black ink through 4nm full capacity and 5nm expansion is proving highly pragmatic.

At the same time, Samsung is using its own hardware to prove its technology. The company has kicked off wafer production for its next-generation mobile application processor (AP), the Exynos 2700, with production scale up more than 10% over its predecessor. Crucially, this chip utilizes Samsung Foundry's second-generation 2-nanometer process, known as SF2P, which boosts computing performance by 12% while cutting power consumption by 25%. If the Exynos 2700 delivers stellar efficiency in upcoming flagship smartphones, it will serve as the ultimate showcase to win back external fabless giants.

Navigating the TSMC Shadow and the AI Supply Squeeze

To be realistic, the path forward is not entirely clear of hurdles. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC)—the undisputed titan controlling roughly 72.5% of the global foundry market—recently announced plans to build an additional 3-nanometer plant in Tainan to begin mass production by 2028. Against TSMC's dominance, Samsung's global foundry market share sits at a modest 5.9%, creating a tenfold gap.

However, TSMC’s near-monopoly has created a critical vulnerability: severe supply bottlenecks. As Apple and Nvidia sweep up all of TSMC's advanced capacity, other major tech firms like Qualcomm, AMD, and MediaTek are left waiting in line. Worse, TSMC has aggressively raised wafer prices, testing clients' patience.

This creates an opening. As the world's only other foundry capable of executing cutting-edge gate-all-around (GAA) manufacturing, Samsung stands as the natural alternative. If supply shortages push desperate fabless clients toward Samsung's 4nm, 5nm, and eventually 2nm lines, the company is uniquely positioned to capture the spillover demand from the global AI boom.

What to Watch Next

For international investors tracking South Korea's macroeconomic and tech pulse over the coming months, three concrete milestones will determine whether Samsung's stock can shake off its macro-induced slump:

  • HBM4 Customer Qualification: Watch for announcements regarding Samsung's formal delivery schedules for its sixth-generation HBM4 memory, particularly tie-ins with major AI accelerator ecosystems. Securing volume commitments here validates the 555 trillion won profit projections.
  • Foundry Yield and Client Announcements: Keep an eye on whether expanded 4nm utilization and the 5nm pivot successfully translate into operating margin improvements on upcoming quarterly balance sheets, as well as any official wins for the SF2P 2nm process.
  • The Exynos 2700 Launch: Track the performance reviews and thermal efficiency metrics of Samsung's next flagship mobile devices powered by the in-house Exynos 2700 chip, which serves as the live stress test for the company's advanced logic manufacturing.

The market is currently pricing in fear, but the factories are printing data. When the macroeconomic fog finally clears, the underlying numbers will force the rest of the world to catch up.
