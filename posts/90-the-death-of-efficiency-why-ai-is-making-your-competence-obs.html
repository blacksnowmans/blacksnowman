The Death of Efficiency: Why AI Is Making Your "Competence" Obsolete

September 03, 2026

We are standing at a massive inflection point where the definition of "competence" is being flipped 180 degrees. Until very recently, the ideal employee was the one who could master Excel functions, read a boss's mind, and churn out hyper-efficient, polished reports. Being "good at work" meant being a high-functioning cog in the machine.

But the arrival of generative AI has shattered that paradigm. If you have been honing your skills purely for the sake of efficiency—writing emails faster, summarizing data more quickly, or organizing schedules with clinical precision—you may have inadvertently spent your career building the very skills that are now the first to be rendered obsolete. If you feel a lingering, quiet anxiety about your job security, you are sensing the shift correctly.

We are trapped in the myth of efficiency, repeating tasks that machines are fundamentally better at performing. The most frustrating irony? The more we use AI to boost our efficiency, the more our uniquely human capacity for deep, original thought seems to atrophy. It is time to stop obsessing over technical proficiency and start asking fundamental questions: Why are we working, and what is the purpose of our effort?

The Trap of Being a Slave to Efficiency

In modern offices, there is a collective joy in using AI prompts to generate first drafts in ten minutes. It is undeniably convenient. But that convenience comes at a harsh price. Efficiency is the native language of machines; the moment a human becomes obsessed with it, they enter into a competition they are guaranteed to lose.

The true human virtue—the value of trial and error—is being gutted in the name of efficiency. Those "pathetic", painful experiences where you struggled, got lost, and eventually stumbled onto a solution are, in fact, your only irreplaceable assets. When you outsource your thinking to an algorithm, you are not just saving time; you are surrendering your agency. If you feel comfortable using the same tools every day, you might not be "working smart"—you might be slowly training your brain to function as a machine component.

Key Takeaways:

  • Efficiency is a machine’s language. The human language is one of struggle, pain, and eventual realization.
  • The moment you grant AI the right to think for you, you relegate yourself to a replaceable part.
  • True competence isn't about providing the "right" answer; it’s about posing the questions that no one else can see.

Knowing When to Quit: The Intelligence of "Cutting"

We have long been taught that "perseverance" is the ultimate key to success. We are told to never give up, to hang on until the bitter end. But the era has changed. In the face of modern obsolescence, we need the "intelligence to cut"—the ability to boldly abandon a dead-end strategy or a project that no longer ignites your passion.

Perseverance in a failing direction is not a virtue; it is a poison that will ruin your career. Why are we so afraid of failure? Because our education system has historically equated "getting the right answer" with success. But in the AI era, success no longer belongs to the people who get the right answers; it belongs to the people who change the game by asking the wrong—or rather, the most unconventional—questions. Perseverance is just a measure of time; courage is the ability to change the direction of that time.

From "Number One" to "Only One"

The quest to be "Number One"—to be slightly better than the person sitting next to you at the same tasks—is a doomed endeavor. AI will eventually surpass all of us at being "Number One." Instead, we must aim to be "Only One."

Your unique tastes, your lived experiences, and your stubborn personal idiosyncrasies form a brand of originality that cannot be trained into a dataset. Looking at the data, experts estimate that 92 million jobs may be lost to automation by 2030. That is a terrifying figure, but it is also a map of opportunity. For every area where AI strips away the "shallow" work, there is a corresponding depth of human-centric work that must be excavated. To survive, you must stop competing for the jobs that are disappearing and start creating the value that only you can provide.

Good Questions Are an Act of Love

The ability to ask a great question is not a technical skill—it is a matter of affection. When you love something, you are naturally curious about it, and that curiosity inevitably leads to insight. A question asked out of a mundane business obligation is miles apart from a question that springs from a place of genuine passion for a craft. When the world is swept up in the giant, impersonal tide of AI, the only thing that will keep you afloat is your own personal obsession.

What do you love enough to keep questioning? Your answer to that question is your survival strategy.

What to Watch Next

If you want to move from being a machine-managed worker to an irreplaceable professional, look for these three things in the coming months:

  • Physical Displacement: Stop trying to "think" your way through problems at your desk. AI does that better. Start moving. Go to new places, meet people outside your industry, and try work that isn't on your job description. Your brain’s creative spark is tied to physical tension and new stimuli.
  • The "Why" Audit: The next time you use an AI tool to finish a task, ask yourself, "What part of this process am I giving up, and do I actually want to lose that skill?" Reclaim the parts of your work that require struggle.
  • The Pursuit of Idiosyncrasy: Stop trying to standardize your workflow. Start documenting your own unique way of doing things—your personal rules, your distinct "scars" from past failures, and your specific outlook on your industry.

Ultimately, the person who remains standing won't be the most "efficient" one. It will be the one who was most unapologetically themselves. Stop looking for the right answer, and start finding your own path.
