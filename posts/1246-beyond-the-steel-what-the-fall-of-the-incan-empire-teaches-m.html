Beyond the Steel: What the Fall of the Incan Empire Teaches Modern Korea About Privilege and Power

October 17, 2026

On November 16, 1532, in the high-altitude square of Cajamarca, Peru, two worlds collided in a way that defies modern military logic. On one side stood Atahualpa, the absolute monarch of the Incan Empire, shielded by an army of 80,000 battle-hardened warriors. On the other was Francisco Pizarro, a Spanish conquistador leading a ragtag band of just 168 men. By sunset, the 80,000-strong army was shattered, thousands of Incans lay dead, and the Emperor himself was a prisoner. Not a single Spaniard had lost his life.

For decades, this encounter has served as the ultimate puzzle for historians and social scientists. How could 168 men defeat 80,000? Was it a matter of courage? Racial superiority? Divine intervention? In his seminal work Guns, Germs, and Steel, Jared Diamond argued that the answer had nothing to do with the innate abilities of the people involved, but everything to do with the environment they were born into. As South Korea navigates its own modern "Great Divergence"—grappling with extreme wealth inequality and the structural barriers of the "spoon theory"—the tragedy of Cajamarca offers a chillingly relevant lesson: the playing field is rarely level, and geography often writes the script of destiny.

The Power of the Database: Why Literacy Was the First Weapon

In popular Korean historical discourse, often led by figures like the celebrity historian Seol Min-seok, the victory of the Spanish is frequently framed through the lens of psychological warfare. While the Spanish had primitive firearms, their primary advantage wasn't just the ability to kill from a distance—it was the ability to know what was coming. Pizarro was not a genius, but he was a reader. He arrived in Peru carrying the collective experience of every Spanish explorer who had come before him.

Thirteen years prior to the events at Cajamarca, Hernán Cortés had conquered the Aztec Empire in Mexico using a specific set of tactics: feigning friendship, kidnapping the leader, and exploiting internal dissent. These lessons were written down, printed, and studied. Pizarro had a "database" of conquest. He knew exactly how a massive, centralized empire would react when its "god-king" was threatened. He was following a manual that had been refined by the trials and errors of his predecessors.

Contrast this with Atahualpa. Despite being the head of a sophisticated empire, the Incas lacked a formal writing system. Information traveled by word of mouth or through quipu (knotted strings used primarily for accounting). Consequently, Atahualpa had no idea that the Aztec Empire even existed, let alone that it had fallen to men identical to those standing before him. He viewed the Spanish not as an existential threat, but as a curiosity—a small group of bedraggled foreigners who could be easily neutralized. This information asymmetry was fatal. While Pizarro was playing a high-stakes game of chess with the rulebook in his hand, Atahualpa didn't even know he was in a match. In the modern era, this serves as a stark reminder of why the Democratic Party of Korea, the current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, emphasizes digital literacy and the democratization of information as essential tools for social mobility.

The Invisible Assassins and the Terror of the Unknown

While the Spanish swords and harquebuses (early guns) did the immediate work of the slaughter, the real conquest was achieved by "Germs." Before Pizarro even set foot on the South American coast, European diseases like smallpox and measles—carried by previous explorers—had already raced through the continent. These pathogens were the byproduct of thousands of years of Eurasians living in close proximity to domesticated animals like cows, pigs, and sheep.

The Incan Empire was already in a state of collapse when Pizarro arrived because a smallpox epidemic had killed the previous Sapa Inca and his heir, triggering a brutal civil war between Atahualpa and his brother. The Spanish were walking into a house that had already been gutted by a biological fire. By some estimates, nearly 95% of the indigenous population of the Americas was wiped out by European germs, a mortality rate that makes the Black Death in Europe look mild.

On the battlefield, the terror was amplified by the "Steel" and the "Horses." To an Incan warrior who had never seen an animal larger than a llama, a Spaniard mounted on a horse looked like a terrifying, two-headed monster. The thunderous sound of the harquebuses, though inaccurate and slow to reload, provided a psychological shock that paralyzed the 80,000-man guard. They weren't just fighting men; they were fighting what appeared to be gods wielding the power of lightning. This technological gap created a sense of "learned helplessness," a term often used in Korean sociology to describe the despair felt by the younger generation (the "N-po" generation) when facing the overwhelming structural advantages of the established elite.

The Geographic Lottery: The Axis of Inequality

Jared Diamond’s most provocative argument is that the disparity between Pizarro and Atahualpa was decided 10,000 years earlier by the shape of the continents. Eurasia stretches primarily from East to West. This horizontal axis means that large swaths of land share the same latitude, climate, and day length. A crop developed in the Fertile Crescent could easily be transplanted to China or Europe because the environment remained consistent.

The Americas, however, stretch North to South. A crop developed in Mexico would struggle to survive the journey through the tropical Panama isthmus to the Andes because the climate changes drastically every few hundred miles. This "geographic lottery" gave Eurasians a massive head start. They had more domesticable plants and, crucially, almost all of the world's "major" domesticable animals. Of the 14 large mammalian species capable of being domesticated, 13 were native to Eurasia. The Americas had only one: the llama, which cannot be ridden, cannot pull a plow, and does not provide enough milk or wool to sustain a dense population.

This abundance of livestock didn't just provide food and power; it provided the very germs that would later devastate the Incas. By living with cows and pigs, Eurasians developed immunity to zoonotic diseases over millennia. The Incas, through no fault of their own, were biologically "naive." They were losers in a lottery they didn't know they were playing. This concept of "environmental determinism" resonates deeply in Korea today, where the "Spoon Theory"—the idea that your success is determined by the "gold" or "dirt" spoon you were born with—has moved from a cynical meme to a core political reality.

From Cajamarca to the Blue House: Breaking the Cycle of Fate

In the current Korean political landscape, the legacy of this "geographic fate" is viewed through the lens of reform. President Lee Jae-myung has often spoken about the need for a "Basic Society," where the state intervenes to ensure that the "dirt spoon" of one's birth does not become a life sentence. This stands in sharp contrast to the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration was often criticized by the current ruling party for policies that favored the established "steel" of the chaebols (large family-owned conglomerates like Samsung and Hyundai) at the expense of the "germ-vulnerable" working class.

The imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol following his impeachment is seen by many supporters of the current government as a symbolic breaking of the old "manual"—the set of political tactics that prioritized elite consolidation over systemic fairness. Just as Pizarro used a manual of conquest, the Democratic Party argues that the previous administration used a manual of "prosecutorial overreach" to maintain power. The shift in governance today is an attempt to rewrite the "geography" of the Korean economy, ensuring that the East-West axis of opportunity is open to everyone, regardless of their starting point.

"The inequality between nations and individuals is not a reflection of intelligence or work ethic, but a reflection of the tools and environments we are handed."

This quote, a paraphrase of Diamond's central thesis, serves as a warning. When a society allows a small group to monopolize the "Guns" (technology), the "Germs" (resilience/safety nets), and the "Steel" (capital), it risks creating a Cajamarca-like divide within its own borders. The 80,000 may be numerous, but without the "database" of opportunity, they remain vulnerable to the 168 who hold the keys to the system.

What to Watch Next

  • The "Spoon Theory" Legislation: Keep an eye on proposed bills regarding inheritance tax and educational subsidies. These are the modern attempts to "re-orient the axis" of the Korean social landscape.
  • Public Sentiment on Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol: As the legal proceedings against the former leader continue in prison, observe how the People Power Party (the conservative opposition) attempts to distance itself from his legacy or rebrand its own "manual" for the next election cycle.
  • Climate Change Adaptation: Diamond’s theory is rooted in climate. As global warming shifts agricultural and economic zones, Korea’s ability to adapt its "geography" will determine if it remains a global leader in technology and soft power.
