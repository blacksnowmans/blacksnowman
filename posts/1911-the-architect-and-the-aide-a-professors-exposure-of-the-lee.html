The Architect and the Aide: A Professor’s Exposure of the Lee Jae-myung Inner Circle

November 03, 2026

Late at night in a quiet Chinese restaurant in Gyeonggi Province, a bottle of kaoliang liquor was shared between an economics professor and a man who, at the time, held no significant public title but possessed immense gravitational pull within the camp of then-candidate Lee Jae-myung. The man was Jeong Jin-sang, a long-time confidant who had been at Lee’s side since his days as a human rights lawyer and the mayor of Seongnam. The professor was Choi Bae-geun, a high-profile academic who had become one of the most vocal intellectual defenders of Lee’s "Basic Income" and "Income-Led Growth" platforms.

What transpired that evening, and in subsequent meetings, has recently been brought to light by Professor Choi himself. In a series of explosive YouTube broadcasts, Choi detailed how he was courted by the inner sanctum of what is now the Lee Jae-myung administration. His testimony offers a rare, unfiltered look into the "patronage politics" that operate behind the scenes of South Korea’s executive power. For those outside Korea, this isn't just a story of political gossip; it is a window into the structural tensions between revolutionary political vision and the old-school machinery of favors, factions, and bureaucratic inertia that continues to define the Blue House—the South Korean presidential office.

The Offer of the "Architect" and the Currency of Patronage

During the presidential campaign, Choi Bae-geun was more than just a supporter; he was an ideological vanguard. It was only natural that Lee’s inner circle would want to formalize his role. According to Choi, the pitch made by Jeong Jin-sang was steeped in historical grandeur. Jeong reportedly likened Choi to Jeong Do-jeon, the 14th-century scholar-official who was the "architect" of the Joseon Dynasty, the kingdom that ruled the Korean peninsula for five centuries. The message was clear: Choi was being asked to draw the blueprint for a new Korea.

However, the "blueprint" came with a price tag of a different sort. Choi alleges that the camp’s overtures were accompanied by specific, transactional "carrots." These included promises to connect Choi’s research to lucrative projects within the Gyeonggi provincial government and its various public agencies. More disturbingly, Choi claims he was told that a position as a civil servant could be "found" or "created" for one of his students.

"It was presented as a way to ensure the administration’s philosophy was implemented, but to me, it looked like private interference in public systems," Choi remarked during his broadcast.

This reveals a persistent feature of Korean political culture: the use of public appointments and government contracts as a form of currency to secure the loyalty of the intelligentsia. While the Lee administration has publicly championed "fairness" and "meritocracy," Choi’s account suggests that in the backrooms, the old rules of "gongsin"—the rewards given to those who help a king ascend the throne—are still very much in play. Choi says he refused the offers, citing the risk of being seen as a "mercenary" rather than a scholar.

The Shift from Campaigners to Career Bureaucrats

As the Lee Jae-myung administration entered its second year, a significant shift occurred in the hallways of the presidential office. Data shows that the proportion of "inner circle" political appointees—those who were with Lee during the campaign or his time as Gyeonggi Governor—dropped from 42.5% at the start of the term to 25.6%. In their place, career bureaucrats from central government ministries have seen their numbers quintuple, now making up over a quarter of the senior staff.

This transition is often a necessity for any new government. The Lee administration took office under intense pressure, following the impeachment and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving a prison sentence. Without a traditional transition period, the administration had to rely on the "professionalism" of the existing technocratic class to keep the wheels of government turning.

However, for critics like Professor Choi, this influx of career bureaucrats represents a "dilution" of the administration’s reformist energy. Choi argues that the "economic bureaucrats"—often referred to in Korea as the "Mofia" (a portmanteau of the Ministry of Finance and Mafia)—are inherently resistant to the radical redistribution policies Lee promised. He points to the previous Moon Jae-in administration, where progressive goals like "Income-Led Growth" were often blunted by a cautious and conservative Ministry of Economy and Finance. By filling the Blue House with these same bureaucrats, Choi fears the Lee administration is effectively surrendering its vision to the very system it promised to overhaul.

The Ghost of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance

Perhaps the most controversial aspect of Choi’s exposure is his mention of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance. For many foreign observers, this name carries little weight, but in South Korean politics, it is a lightning rod. This faction has its roots in the radical student and labor movements of the 1980s and 90s in the Seongnam and eastern Gyeonggi region. They were the dominant force in the now-defunct Unified Progressive Party (UPP), which was forcibly dissolved by the Constitutional Court in 2014 for its alleged pro-North Korean leanings.

The alliance has long been rumored to be the "hidden hand" behind Lee Jae-myung’s political rise. The connection dates back to 2010, when Lee first ran for Mayor of Seongnam. A strategic withdrawal by the UPP candidate allowed Lee to consolidate the progressive vote and win. Since then, accusations have persisted that members of this faction were rewarded with positions in Seongnam’s municipal government and its affiliated NGOs.

Choi Bae-geun’s testimony breathes new life into these concerns. He described a culture within the camp where certain figures were treated with a level of deference that suggested a hidden hierarchy. He warned that when a political movement becomes dominated by a single, insular faction, it stops being a "party" and starts becoming a "cult of obedience."

"When a party cannot tolerate different voices, what remains is not unity, but submission," Choi stated.

This "politics of obedience" is increasingly visible in the rise of "fandom politics" and what some call "digital Red Guards"—aggressive online supporters who launch coordinated attacks (often called "coordinate spotting" or ja-pyo-ggig-gi) against any public figure or scholar who criticizes the President. Choi himself, once a hero to these fans, has become a target since his recent disclosures.

The "Presidential Risk" and the Intellectual’s Remorse

The most poignant moment of Choi’s exposure was his public apology to the voters. As a man who traveled the country during the campaign urging people to trust Lee Jae-myung, he expressed deep shame. He admitted that he had misjudged the character of the leadership, citing the "Presidential Risk" that now looms over the administration due to these internal scandals and the perceived lack of transparency.

The presidential office has attempted to dismiss Choi’s claims as "mere rumors" or "unsubstantiated gossip." However, the gravity of the situation is hard to ignore. Choi is not an opposition politician; he was a founding member of the intellectual circle that gave the administration its policy teeth. When someone of his stature risks his reputation to speak out, it suggests a profound rupture in the government’s base of support.

What is at stake here is the very definition of the Lee Jae-myung presidency. Lee campaigned on a platform of "Pragmatism" and "Fairness," promising to break the "old politics" of the past. Yet, the allegations of private job offers, the reliance on an insular faction like the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance, and the aggressive silencing of internal dissent suggest that the "old politics" may have simply found a new home.

What to Watch Next

As this controversy continues to unfold, there are three key areas to monitor:

  • The Administrative Reform Bill: The government is currently pushing a massive reorganization, including the abolition of the Prosecution Service (to be replaced by separate investigative and prosecutorial agencies) and the splitting of the Ministry of Economy and Finance. Watch to see if these reforms are used to genuinely decentralize power or if they are used to install loyalists in newly created "independent" agencies.
  • Internal Dissent within the Democratic Party: Professor Choi is likely not the only one feeling "intellectual remorse." Watch for other high-profile supporters or party elders who might break ranks. If more "architects" of the administration begin to speak out, the President’s "fandom" will find it increasingly difficult to dismiss the criticism as mere opposition propaganda.
  • The Legal Fallout: While these offers were made during the campaign or early in the term, the promise of government jobs in exchange for political support can teeter on the edge of "abuse of power" or "violation of election laws." Whether the newly formed investigative bodies will have the appetite to look into their own benefactors remains the ultimate test of the administration’s commitment to the rule of law.

The Lee Jae-myung administration stands at a crossroads. It can choose to listen to the "bitter medicine" offered by scholars like Choi Bae-geun and move toward a more transparent, meritocratic system. Or, it can retreat further into the "politics of obedience," relying on its digital vanguard to silence the very people who helped build its foundation. For the international community watching Korea's vibrant but volatile democracy, the outcome will determine whether the "architects" of the new Korea are building a house of glass or a house of cards.
