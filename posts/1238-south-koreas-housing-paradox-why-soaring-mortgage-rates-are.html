South Korea’s Housing Paradox: Why Soaring Mortgage Rates Are Forcing Renters to Buy

October 16, 2026

In mid-2024, an ordinary home hunter in Seoul faces a financial equation that defies conventional economic logic. The Bank of Korea, South Korea’s central bank, announced that average bank mortgage interest rates reached 4.66% in August—marking their highest level in three years and nine months. At major commercial lenders, variable borrowing rates have drifted into the low-5% territory, while some long-term fixed products have climbed toward an eye-watering 7% to 8%. Standard economic textbooks would suggest an obvious outcome: as the price of borrowing spikes, property transactions should dry up and home prices should collapse under the weight of evaporating demand.

Yet on the ground in Seoul and the surrounding Gyeonggi Province, something entirely different is unfolding. Rather than driving buyers away, the tightening credit environment, paired with a severe structural drought in the rental market, has created a bizarre and painful phenomenon known locally as "coerced buying" (gangje maemae). Cut off from affordable rental renewals and watching lease listings vanish, ordinary Koreans are concluding that taking on 5% debt is no longer a speculative wager—it is the only way to avoid residential eviction.

The Anatomy of "Coerced Buying" in a 5% Rate World

To understand why Koreans are buying homes in a high-rate climate, one must look at the rental market. South Korea operates a unique lease system called jeonse, where tenants lodge a massive lump-sum deposit—often 50% to 70% of a property’s market value—with the landlord for two years, paying no monthly rent, before recovering the deposit upon departure. Alongside jeonse runs standard monthly rent (wolse).

Over the past year, supply in both jeonse and wolse has virtually dried up across Seoul. Landlords, wary of tax rules and market volatility, are locking up properties, while new construction starts have slowed significantly. The few lease listings that do appear come with astronomical price tags. For a middle-class household, renewing a lease or finding a new apartment to rent has become a logistical nightmare.

Faced with either moving their families far outside the capital’s commuter belt or scrambling to find scarce rental housing, prospective tenants find themselves cornered. In this environment, paying an extra 0.5 percentage point in mortgage interest to purchase an apartment feels less hazardous than facing the street. The phrase "coerced buying" captures this grim reality: families are not buying out of greed or exuberance, but out of sheer exhaustion with an unforgiving tenancy market. For them, a 5% borrowing cost has ceased to be an investment metric; it has become a necessary fee for basic family survival.

From 2022 Panic to 2024 Resignation: The Shifting Psychology of Debt

The current market looks radically distinct from the sharp downturn seen in 2022. When global central banks embarked on rapid "giant step" interest rate hikes two years ago, Korean homeowners were caught completely off-guard. Families that had taken on cheap 2% to 3% debt during the pandemic saw their monthly obligations double almost overnight. Panic ensued, distress sales flooded the market, and apartment valuations plunged.

By 2024, the psychological makeup of the market has altered dramatically. The prevailing sentiment is no longer panic among existing sellers; it is cold resignation among new buyers. Today's entrants have developed what market observers call an "immunity" to elevated rates. Anyone stepping into an agency office today already assumes that current 4% to 5% rates could eventually push toward 7% or 8%—levels last seen in the wake of the 2008 global financial crisis. Financial stress tests are conducted before contracts are signed.

Consider the cold mathematics of debt service. Borrowing 100 million won (roughly $75,000) under an amortizing loan at 5% entails monthly principal and interest repayments of approximately 600,000 to 700,000 won (about $450 to $525). If benchmark rates surge and mortgage costs reach 8%, that monthly burden leaps to roughly 1 million won (about $750). For a household with a disposable monthly housing budget capped at 700,000 won, taking on even 100 million won in leverage is dangerous. But for those with 1 million to 1.5 million won in cash flow slack, borrowing between 100 million and 150 million won (around $75,000 to $112,000) remains a manageable calculation.

Because buyers are entering the market with these worst-case scenarios already priced into their household budgets, the market has formed a rigid floor. The sudden wave of panic selling that characterized 2022 is largely absent. Instead, an anxiety of a different sort dominates first-time buyers: the fear that if they do not lock in a mortgage now, persistent inflation will leave them permanently behind.

Policy Loans and Regulatory Irony: Squeezing the Under-1.5-Billion Won Market

In response to stubborn household debt figures, the authorities have tightened administrative screws. Regulatory institutions, along with state-backed bodies such as the Korea Land and Housing Corporation (LH)—whose chief, Lee Seong-hun, recently pointed to the need for tighter credit caps—have sought to suppress aggregate loan totals. Stricter Debt Service Ratio (DSR) limits, which restrict total borrowing to a proportion of verified annual income, have been progressively expanded.

Yet the policy implementation has sparked bitter resentment among working-class families. State-backed lending programs aimed at lower- and middle-income first-time buyers, such as the Bogeumjari ("Nest") and Didimdol ("Stepping Stone") loans, are facing depleted reserves. Rather than expanding these funds, financial authorities have responded by raising interest rates on these subsidized products by 0.1 percentage point increments and narrowing eligibility criteria. For younger workers and newly married couples who depend on these programs as their sole bridge to homeownership, the moves feel like pulling up the ladder.

Compounding this anxiety are growing discussions in political and financial circles about tightening credit restrictions specifically on apartments valued under 1.5 billion won (approximately $1.1 million). During the time in office of former president Yoon Suk-yeol—who was later impeached, removed from office, and subsequently imprisoned—the government moved to roll back lending restrictions across broad swathes of the housing sector. Now, under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party, regulators are confronting the lingering macroeconomic strains left in the wake of those moves.

The dilemma is that the under-1.5-billion-won segment constitutes the primary entry point for actual owner-occupiers in Seoul and its neighboring satellite cities. Imposing blanket credit curbs on this specific tier does little to deter cash-rich high-net-worth buyers targeting luxury properties in Gangnam. Instead, it directly penalizes salaried professionals who require leverage to secure standard housing near their workplaces. The perverse result has been a rush to beat regulatory deadlines, inadvertently accelerating transactions before credit windows shut.

The Macro Dilemma: 820 Trillion Won Budgets and Stubborn Inflation

Why cannot South Korea simply cut borrowing rates and relieve the pressure on ordinary citizens? The answer lies in stubborn consumer inflation and an encroaching threat of stagflation.

Official figures from the Bank of Korea place headline consumer price inflation at 2.9%, persistently above the central bank’s 2.0% medium-term target, while projected economic growth lingers at an anemic 1.1%. On the street, everyday price pressures feel significantly steeper than official data suggest. Grocery baskets illustrate the squeeze: a modest 300-gram pack of pork neck (hangjeongsal) now costs as much as 24,000 won (about $18), rivaling restaurant dining prices. Independent restaurant owners and small businesses are shuttering under the twin burdens of soaring wholesale ingredient costs and commercial interest payments.

Lowering interest rates in an economy where everyday inflation remains elevated risks debasing the currency and sparking a renewed cost-of-living spiral. The monetary dilemma is further aggravated by fiscal policy. South Korea’s upcoming national budget is projected to reach a massive 820 trillion won (approximately $615 billion), with roughly 92 trillion won ($69 billion) structured as direct liquidity injections into the economy. While designed to stimulate domestic activity and cushion vulnerable sectors, such immense state expenditures inevitably flood the broader financial system with liquidity, reinforcing inflationary pressures. With fiscal taps wide open, the Bank of Korea has little room to ease monetary conditions, locking borrowing rates at elevated levels for the foreseeable future.

The Calendar Crunch: What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters the final months of the year, all eyes in the property sector are focused on a critical administrative turning point: the January 1 quota reset.

  • The Year-End Credit Freeze: Commercial banks face strict annual quotas on aggregate household lending mandated by financial regulators. By the fourth quarter, many lenders reach their regulatory ceilings, leading them to quietly hike lending spreads or decline mortgage applications entirely. Many buyers who signed contracts in late autumn are deliberately pushing their settlement and deed-transfer dates into early next year.
  • The Early-Year Surge: On January 1, bank loan quotas reset for the new accounting year. Historically, this creates an immediate release valve, producing a burst of loan approvals and transaction settlements in January and February. Observers are watching whether this predictable seasonal shift will trigger an abrupt jump in transaction volumes for mid-market apartments.
  • Subsidized Fund Allocations: Whether the Lee Jae-myung administration replenishes low-interest policy vehicles like the Didimdol loan will serve as a crucial barometer for domestic politics. If subsidized credit remains scarce, middle-class frustration could build heading into local political debates.
  • The Seoul Construction Bottleneck: With construction material costs staying high and housing starts subdued, the persistent deficit in new apartment completions will continue to dictate the rental market through next year.

For South Korea's aspiring homeowners, the calculus has moved past waiting for cheap borrowing costs to return. Caught between sticky consumer inflation, a ballooning state budget, and a bone-dry rental market, many are deciding that the risk of servicing a 5% mortgage is far less daunting than the certainty of having nowhere left to rent.
