Beyond the 99-Cent E-Book: The Amazon Publishing Dispute Surrounding Han Dong-hoon and South Korea’s Reluctant Police

October 13, 2026

Every autumn, South Korea’s National Assembly convenes for its annual audit of state affairs, known locally as the gukgam. Over several grueling weeks, lawmakers summon civil servants, corporate executives, and law enforcement chiefs to testify under bright television lights. While these hearings often degenerate into partisan grandstanding, they also serve an essential constitutional function: prying open internal files that government agencies would much rather keep buried.

During a recent session of the National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee, the spotlight turned unexpectedly toward Amazon’s Kindle Direct Publishing platform. Representative Park Joo-min of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea took the microphone to revive a lingering controversy involving conservative lawmaker Han Dong-hoon, a pivotal figure in South Korea's main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. At the heart of Park’s questioning was not a multi-million-dollar financial maneuver, but a seemingly mundane 99-cent digital workbook sold on an overseas e-commerce site—and the remarkably clumsy legal rationale the police previously used to shelve the case.

A 99-Cent Defense: How Investigators Dismissed the Case

The controversy stems from educational workbooks and materials tied to Han Dong-hoon that were uploaded and sold via Amazon’s international platform. Critics had raised suspicions of potential copyright violations regarding the contents. Yet when South Korean police previously examined the matter, they chose not to pursue the allegations, determining that the case lacked legal merit to warrant an active investigation.

The reasoning police offered at the time sounded straightforward on paper, if bizarrely superficial in substance. Investigators asserted that because the digital books were priced at a nominal $0.99, and because Amazon charged an identical $0.99 distribution and platform fee, the author effectively walked away with zero net income. Because there was no financial gain, police concluded, the publication lacked "commercial intent" (영리 목적). Claiming that obtaining further cross-border verification through international legal mutual assistance was unfeasible, the agency quietly closed the file.

During the committee hearing, Representative Park systematically dismantled that defense. Brandishing standard pricing schedules from Amazon’s global self-publishing portal, Park pointed out that anyone with a web browser could verify the actual payout structure within half an hour. Under Kindle’s self-publishing terms, even a title listed at $0.99 falls into a clear royalty tier, yielding an author payout of roughly 35 percent. Far from an entirely cost-absorbed enterprise where the platform absorbs the entire dollar, the framework allows the creator to collect royalties systematically with each download.

The revelation transformed a seemingly minor technical dispute into a biting critique of police incompetence—or worse, deliberate dereliction of duty. That professional investigators failed to perform a routine check of a publicly accessible e-commerce payout table and instead hid behind the excuse of stalled cross-border paperwork suggested to critics that law enforcement was simply searching for an exit ramp to avoid scrutinizing a powerful conservative figure.

The Legal Pivot: Why "Commercial Profit" Decides South Korean Copyright Cases

To understand why a 35-cent royalty margin matters so much to South Korean politicians, one must unpack the specific mechanics of the country's Copyright Act. Under standard South Korean criminal law, most copyright infringement offenses operate strictly as offenses subject to complaint, known as chingo-joe (친고죄). Under this principle, the state cannot initiate an indictment unless the direct copyright holder files an official criminal complaint.

Because the alleged victims had not filed formal legal actions against Han or the publishers, police initially appeared constrained from opening an inquiry. However, the statute contains a critical exception: if a copyright violation is committed habitually or for "commercial purposes," the complaint requirement drops away entirely. In cases involving commercial profit, police and prosecutors are authorized—and mandated—to launch investigations ex officio (인지수사), regardless of whether an aggrieved party stepped forward.

The determination of commercial intent is not an academic footnote in Korean copyright law; it is the sole trigger that allows the state to bypass the requirement of a formal victim complaint and prosecute unilaterally.

By declaring that the $0.99 price point left zero net profit, the police had neatly insulated the matter from this statutory exception. If there were no profits, there could be no commercial intent, meaning the case could remain classified as an unprosecutable matter absent an external complaint. Once the 35 percent royalty structure was formally introduced into the parliamentary record, however, that entire defensive architecture buckled. If royalties were generated under a commercial distribution platform, the claim that the venture was devoid of commercial ambition ceases to hold water, reopening the legal threshold for an official state-led inquiry.

Institutional Inertia and the Double Standards of Enforcement

The friction surrounding the Amazon publications reflects a much deeper skepticism among South Korean voters regarding the neutrality of the country's investigative apparatus. For decades, the public has watched law enforcement navigate politically sensitive cases with extraordinary caution, often exhibiting sharp legal acuity against private citizens while suddenly turning clumsy and incurious when approaching the upper echelons of power.

This dynamic remains a burning issue even under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung. Although the political landscape shifted dramatically following the impeachment and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving time in prison, the underlying habits of the judicial and investigative bureaucracies do not vanish overnight. Law enforcement agencies retain institutional instincts cultivated through successive regimes, leading critics to argue that an entrenched legal cartel still resists holding high-profile political figures to the exact same standards applied to the general public.

The audit hearing laid bare this double standard. Had an ordinary citizen, an independent business owner, or a minor content creator been accused of circulating copyrighted intellectual property across an online marketplace, it is difficult to imagine investigators accepting an unverified claim that the platform fees canceled out liability without ever checking the website terms. The fact that police cited an inability to coordinate international criminal assistance over an inquiry that required little more than reading an English-language FAQ page struck lawmakers across the aisle as an indefensible institutional failure.

What to Watch Next

The immediate political aftermath now shifts from parliamentary grandstanding to administrative action. As this dispute continues to develop, observers should keep an eye on several concrete pivot points:

  • Police Responses on Reopening the Case: The National Police Agency faces heightened public and legislative pressure to formally review its prior findings. Observers will be watching whether the agency's leadership issues an order for a supplemental investigation or refers the material to a specialized economic crimes unit.
  • Han Dong-hoon’s Counter-Evidence: Representatives for Han and his camp will likely need to produce comprehensive accounting records from Amazon Direct Publishing to prove whether any royalties were actually received, waived, or redirected, moving past broad verbal denials to documentary verification.
  • Legislative Follow-Through: Lawmakers on the Public Administration and Security Committee from the ruling Democratic Party may demand access to the original police case records to determine which individual investigators approved the closure of the file and whether external pressure influenced that conclusion.

At first glance, an argument over a fractional dollar royalty on an American digital platform might appear trivial against the broader backdrop of South Korean statecraft. Yet in modern Korean politics, the smallest procedural irregularities frequently serve as barometers for something much larger: whether the state's legal machinery applies the rule of law equally, or whether it bends its own basic rules to accommodate the powerful.
