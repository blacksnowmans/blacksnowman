Cash Envelopes at the Top, Unpaid Public Defenders at the Bottom: Inside South Korea's Judicial Spending Scandal

October 16, 2026

Under the harsh lights of the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee hearing, a quiet exchange shattered a long-standing defense by South Korea's legal elite. Kim Yong-min, a lawmaker from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, pressed Oh Min-seok, the chief judge of the Seoul Central District Court. The topic was a secretive slush-fund-like cash pool distributed monthly to the country's Supreme Court justices. For years, judicial administrative officials had insisted that these monthly cash envelopes—amounting to millions of won per justice—were not personal perks, but mere pass-through payments meant to reward hard-working judicial research fellows drafting complex legal opinions.

When asked directly whether he, as the former head of those very research fellows, had received these funds, Judge Oh faltered before admitting the truth. He recalled receiving a cash envelope of about 1 million won (roughly $720) just once a year through the financial office. That admission completely dismantled the judiciary's official narrative, revealing a stark reality: while top justices pocketed untraceable cash, lower-tier staff lacked budget for late-night meals and public defenders serving low-income citizens went unpaid by tens of millions of dollars.

A "Pass-Through" Allowance That Never Reached the Staff

To understand why this exchange caused such a stir, one must look at how South Korea's Supreme Court manages its administrative budget. Below the Supreme Court justices sits an elite squad of judicial research fellows—senior judges tasked with conducting deep legal research and drafting judgment opinions. Because the caseload at the highest court is crushing, these fellows work grueling hours.

To justify regular monthly payments of 2 million to 3 million won (about $1,400 to $2,200) paid in cash to each Supreme Court justice, the National Court Administration—the administrative branch of the judicial system—had long claimed that justices were merely conduits. The official story was that justices handed this money down to research fellows during court sessions as "encouragement allowances" (gyeoryeogeum).

However, ledger entries presented during the parliamentary audit told a vastly different story. Between 2022 and September 2025, Supreme Court justices routinely drew average monthly cash sums ranging from 2.29 million to 3.13 million won. In total, the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), South Korea’s state auditor, uncovered that 1.299 billion won (approximately $930,000) in cash had been distributed without legal foundation or receipts under the ambiguous budget category of "Other Operational Expenses."

If the senior-most research fellow—Judge Oh, who led the research division from 2022 to early 2025—received only 1 million won once a year, where did the rest of the 1.3 billion won go? The audit established strong circumstantial evidence that the money remained at the very top of the judicial pyramid, serving as a tax-free personal perk for justices who already earn well over 200 million won ($145,000) annually, alongside 5.12 million won ($3,700) in official monthly business expenses.

Unpaid Public Defenders and Missing Coffee Funds

The scandal over these executive envelopes becomes far more damning when contrasted with the severe fiscal austerity imposed on the lower levels of South Korea’s legal system. At the same hearing, lawmakers highlighted a deepening crisis in public defense funding.

South Korea's public defender system relies heavily on private attorneys who take court-appointed cases to represent indigent defendants, receiving a modest fee of 400,000 to 500,000 won (about $290 to $360) per case. Yet, due to severe budget shortfalls, the court system has accumulated roughly 30 billion won ($21.5 million) in delayed payments owed to these court-appointed lawyers. Unpaid fees soared from 500 million won in 2021 to 9.4 billion won in 2023, reaching over 22.5 billion won in 2024 before hitting the 30 billion won mark. While young public defense lawyers waited months to be reimbursed for representing vulnerable citizens, Supreme Court justices continued to collect off-the-books monthly cash envelopes.

The fiscal disparity reaches down into the daily operations of local courthouses. At the Seoul High Court, internal complaints revealed that basic operational funds had completely dried up. By late October, the budget for late-night meal allowances for research fellows working past midnight was exhausted, forcing staff to pay out of pocket. In some offices, departments could no longer afford basic pantry supplies, leaving staff to buy their own instant coffee.

"While those doing real work for vulnerable citizens wait months for basic fees, Supreme Court justices collect encouragement allowances like a secondary monthly salary," lawmaker Kim Yong-min noted during the hearing. "This is a system operated completely out of touch with public expectations."

Lawmakers seized on these details to argue that the judiciary operates under a double standard. While top figures maintained access to discretionary cash without receipts, the essential machinery of public justice was left starved of basic resources.

Shielding Elite Figures: The Redacted Judgments Scandal

The audit also laid bare institutional opacity regarding judicial transparency. Lawmakers grilled Judge Oh over the Seoul Central District Court's decision to appeal a court ruling regarding public access to historical trial records.

Following the failed martial law declaration and subsequent impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently imprisoned—civic groups filed lawsuits demanding that full, unredacted names of key figures involved in the insurrection trials be made public. The Seoul Administrative Court ruled in favor of the public, stating that under current criminal procedure rules, there was no legal basis to anonymize key figures in a case of paramount historical and public interest.

Instead of complying, the Seoul Central District Court filed an appeal to keep the names blacked out. Lawmakers criticized the court for spending public resources to challenge its own administrative bench's ruling, accusing the court administration of turning historical judgments into a game of "find the hidden name."

The episode underscored a broader pattern of institutional self-preservation: an establishment reluctant to expose its top figures to public scrutiny, whether in matters of finance or historical accountability.

What to Watch Next

The revelations from this year's parliamentary audit have galvanized demands for fundamental reform in how South Korea's judicial branch manages public money and public records. As legislative scrutiny intensifies, several key developments will determine whether these exposures lead to structural changes:

  • Legislative Budget Cuts: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea, holding a firm majority in the National Assembly, is expected to leverage upcoming budget review sessions to slash or eliminate non-vouched items like "Other Operational Expenses" within the Supreme Court's annual budget.
  • Systemic Rules on Cash Discretion: Following the Board of Audit and Inspection's formal warnings regarding unvouched cash distributions, eyes are on the court system to see if it will formally codify a ban on receiptless cash payments across all judicial administrative branches.
  • Public Defender Back-Pay Allocations: With arrears reaching 30 billion won ($21.5 million), the National Assembly faces immediate pressure to pass supplementary budget provisions to clear back pay owed to court-appointed defense attorneys.
  • Appellate Rulings on Court Transparency: As the appeal over the anonymization of trial records in cases stemming from Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure moves forward, the outcome will signal whether the judiciary intends to prioritize public right-to-know over institutional protectionism.
