The Architect and the Candidate: Inside the Ideological Bargain That Built Lee Jae-myung’s Presidency

October 18, 2026

In July 2021, inside a stifling office in Yeouido—Seoul’s political and financial island—a campaign crisis was brewing. Lee Jae-myung, then the governor of Gyeonggi Province vying for the presidential nomination of the progressive Democratic Party of Korea, was stumbling in televised debates. To his core supporters, he seemed uncharacteristically muted and defensive. Korean voters often describe bold, punchy politicians as providing a refreshing gulp of "cider," while overly cautious speakers are likened to choking on a dry "sweet potato." In the mid-summer heat, Lee was acting like a sweet potato.

Desperate campaign aides turned to an outsider: Professor Choi Bae-keun, an iconoclastic progressive economist. Pushing past anxious staff at the campaign’s headquarters in Yeouido’s Far East VIP Building, Choi was granted a brief 30-minute window before Lee’s next media engagement. Choi did not offer polite pleasantries. Instead, he delivered a blunt warning that reverberated through the hallway: attempting to appease the political establishment or earn the approval of Korea’s entrenched conservative media cartels was a fatal illusion.

To understand the current trajectory of South Korea under President Lee Jae-myung, one must look back at this intense ideological bargain struck between the reformist intellectual and the populist politician during the 2021 campaign trail. It was a relationship built not on political transactionalism, but on a shared determination to dismantle old economic paradigms—a pact whose original promises now face their ultimate test in government.

From Sajik Park to 'Basic Finance': The Scholar-Politician Pact

The alliance between Choi and Lee was forged months before the Yeouido crisis, in late spring 2021, near Sajik Park in central Seoul. Lee arrived with a entourage of aides, but the meeting quickly dispensed with ceremonial protocol. Choi laid down a strict condition: any backing would require Lee to commit to sweeping structural overhauls, starting with a comprehensive "education revolution" and a redesign of the nation's financial architecture.

In Korean political history, intellectuals and presidential contenders often align for superficial image management. Choi viewed their dynamic differently, invoking the historical precedent of Jeong Do-jeon—the 14th-century Neo-Confucian scholar who partnered with General Yi Seong-gye to overthrow a corrupt dynasty and design the institutional framework of the Joseon Kingdom (1392–1910). For Choi, Lee was the political vehicle capable of smashing entrenched interests, provided he was equipped with the right economic machinery.

Out of these early sessions emerged the theoretical foundation of "Basic Finance"—one of Lee's signature policy proposals. Designed to bypass traditional financial gatekeepers, the policy aimed to provide state-backed, low-interest long-term loans and financial access as a basic right to all citizens, particularly younger and lower-income demographics excluded by commercial banks.

Recognizing the risk of reform plans being diluted by career bureaucrats, Lee made an unusual request: he asked Choi to directly compile a talent pool and think tank of like-minded experts to execute the agenda post-election. Rather than viewing the request as overreaching, Choi saw it as a pragmatic move to avoid the trap that had snared previous progressive administrations—surrendering policy execution to conservative mandarin bureaucrats and politically opportunistic academics.

The Summer Crisis: Confronting the Ghost of Roh Moo-hyun

By mid-summer 2021, the primary race had intensified. Lee was facing formidable challenges from established Democratic Party heavyweights, including former Prime Ministers Lee Nak-yon and Chung Sye-kyun. Attempting to preserve party unity for the general election, Lee began pulling his punches in debates, resulting in the dreaded "sweet potato" moniker and a drop in grass-roots enthusiasm.

During their 30-minute confrontation in Yeouido, Choi pulled no punches. He berated Lee for playing on his rivals' terms and losing the sharp, uncompromising reformist edge that had made him a political force.

"The privileged establishment and the legacy conservative media will never accept you as president, even if you win," Choi warned him, referring specifically to the powerful trio of conservative daily newspapers often called Cho-Joong-Dong (the Chosun Ilbo, JoongAng Ilbo, and Dong-A Ilbo). "Do you not remember what happened to Roh Moo-hyun?"

The reference to former President Roh Moo-hyun (who served from 2003 to 2008) carried immense emotional and strategic weight. Roh’s presidency was defined by relentless battles with conservative media and opposition institutions, a struggle that ended tragically after he left office. Choi’s message was clear: attempting to court conservative favor through moderate compromise was a trap. Victory required mobilizing the electorate through unwavering, radical clarity.

Lee took the critique to heart. He recalibrated his debate strategy, abandoned his defensive posture, and reclaimed his assertive political identity. The campaign regained its momentum.

The Honam Gambit and the Price of Power

By September 2021, the campaign reached its decisive turning point: the primary in the southwestern Honam region, encompassing Gwangju and South Jeolla Province. Honam is the traditional political heartland of South Korea's progressive movement; winning a decisive victory there is essential for securing the party's nomination.

Lee’s camp aimed for an ambitious target: securing an outright majority of 58% in the first round to avoid a risky runoff election. The key lay in capturing the public sentiment over the crucial Chuseok holiday (the autumn harvest festival), when families gather and consolidate political consensus across the region.

To tip the scales, campaign leaders urged Choi to make a formal, televised endorsement. Though initially reluctant to take center stage in partisan electoral politics, Choi staged a public press conference at the Far East VIP Building. Lee publicly embraced the endorsement, declaring that he had gained "an army of ten thousand troops." The public backing from a trusted reformist intellectual reassured skeptical voters in Honam, propelling Lee to a decisive primary victory and ultimately the presidential nomination.

Yet when Lee eventually won the presidency—following the dramatic impeachment, removal, and imprisonment of his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—Choi made a deliberate choice to step back. Rather than accepting a cabinet position or senior presidential governorship, Choi declined all public office.

Choi sarcastically offered one condition under which he would accept a role: guaranteed full authority for the entire five-year presidential term, untouchable by political maneuvering—a demand he knew no political administration could grant. By remaining outside the government, Choi sought to avoid becoming bogged down by bureaucratic inertia or compromising his role as an independent critic.

What to Watch Next: Semiconductor Illusions and Macroeconomic Risks

Today, President Lee Jae-myung sits in the Blue House, backed by a strong Democratic Party majority in the National Assembly against the conservative opposition People Power Party. However, the intellectual architects who helped construct Lee's political ascent are issuing sharp warnings from the sidelines.

While the administration currently points to a rallying KOSPI stock index and robust export figures driven by global demand for memory chips, critical observers view these indicators as a dangerous optical illusion. South Korea's recent economic uptick is largely a byproduct of foreign capital expenditure in global artificial intelligence infrastructure, rather than a reflection of underlying domestic economic strength.

As Lee’s term progresses, several critical indicators will reveal whether the administration remains faithful to its founding reformist vision or succumbs to short-term economic complacency:

  • Third-Quarter Macroeconomic Vulnerability: External demand for semiconductors is highly cyclical. If global tech capital expenditures slow down in the second half of the year, South Korea's export momentum could drop sharply, exposing deep vulnerabilities in household debt and domestic consumption.
  • Implementation of 'Basic Finance': Watch whether the Lee administration pushes through genuine legislative frameworks for universal credit access, or allows mainstream financial regulators and traditional banking interest groups to neuter the initiative.
  • Education and Institutional Reforms: The administration’s early promise of an "education revolution" remains its most complex policy challenge. Observers will be watching whether the Ministry of Education introduces concrete structural changes to university governance and regional development before political capital ebbs away.

For President Lee, the mandate given by his supporters was not simply to manage the state apparatus smoothly, but to execute the structural transformations promised in those intense strategy sessions of 2021. Whether his administration can push past temporary economic cushions and tackle deep-seated institutional reforms will define the legacy of his presidency.
