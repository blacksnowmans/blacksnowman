A Four-Year Sentence, a Courtroom Outburst, and South Korea’s Fight Over Media Framing

November 23, 2026

Inside Criminal Collegiate Division 27 of the Seoul Central District Court, the atmosphere was anything but subdued. Just one day earlier, South Korea’s Supreme Court, sitting en banc—meaning a full panel of justices reserved for major cases—had finalized a four-year prison sentence against Kim Keon-hee on charges including stock manipulation involving Deutsch Motors. Now, back in a lower courtroom to face a separate trial over alleged violations of the Political Parties Act, the defendant did not offer the quiet contrition often expected after a final conviction. Instead, she raised her voice at the bench, questioning the special counsel, the judges, and the South Korean public.

What followed outside the courtroom quickly became almost as controversial as the outburst itself. Several South Korean news outlets summarized Kim’s remarks using the Korean word ulbun—a loaded term that translates roughly to righteous indignation or the pent-up anguish of someone suffering an injustice. According to a widely circulated Korean political commentary by the blogger Blacksnowman, that single editorial choice revealed as much about the state of South Korea’s press as Kim’s remarks revealed about the country’s political and legal reckoning.

For international observers trying to make sense of post-impeachment South Korean politics, the episode offers a window into two overlapping battles: whether the country’s judicial system can consistently hold powerful figures accountable, and whether its newsrooms can report on those proceedings without softening the reality for their readers.

Inside the Courtroom After the Supreme Court Verdict

In South Korea’s legal system, an en banc Supreme Court ruling leaves no further avenue for ordinary appeal. When the full bench upheld Kim Keon-hee’s four-year prison term in the Deutsch Motors stock manipulation case, it brought years of investigations, special counsel disputes, and political maneuvering to a definitive legal conclusion.

Yet when Kim appeared the very next day at the Seoul Central District Court for her ongoing Political Parties Act hearing, she flatly rejected the legitimacy of that ruling. According to accounts cited in Blacksnowman’s commentary, she directed sharp criticism at both the judiciary and the broader public:

"I will never, ever understand why I am being convicted over the Deutsch stock manipulation. How long are you going to keep looking at the political winds? The special counsel, the judges, and the public are all being influenced politically. I even doubt whether the Supreme Court justices actually looked into my case."

Kim and her supporters have long argued that the special counsel probe and subsequent prosecutions amounted to political targeting rather than neutral law enforcement. From her perspective, the courtroom remarks reflected a belief that political pressure had tainted the legal process from start to finish.

Critics, however, saw the confrontation very differently. In his analysis of the hearing, Blacksnowman argued that dismissing a full-bench Supreme Court verdict as a political conspiracy ignores the evidentiary record behind the conviction. As the commentary notes, the four-year sentence was not based on political rhetoric, but on physical evidence, bank account transaction records, and witness testimony. In that view, challenging whether the nation’s highest judges had even read the file amounted to a direct rejection of the rule of law by someone long accustomed to operating near the center of state power.

The Battle Over a Single Word: Why "Ulbun" Sparked Backlash

Immediately after the hearing, headlines across a swath of South Korean media framed Kim’s courtroom statements by reporting that she had "poured out ulbun." To a foreign reader, the controversy over a single noun might seem minor. In Korean, however, word choice carries heavy moral framing.

As Blacksnowman’s commentary explains, ulbun is not a neutral synonym for anger. It carries a sense of nobility and moral grievance—the word typically used to describe the despair of workers unfairly dismissed from their jobs or ordinary citizens harmed by abuses of state power. By applying that label to a defendant newly convicted of disrupting the financial markets, critics argue, news outlets blurred the distinction between victim and perpetrator.

The backlash centered on two main points raised by media critics:

  • The erasure of market victims: Stock manipulation is not a victimless offense. In South Korea, individual retail investors—colloquially known as gaemi, or "ants," because they are small players swimming alongside institutional giants—often bear the heaviest losses when share prices are artificially distorted. Critics argued that framing a convicted defendant’s anger as righteous indignation amounted to a secondary injury against ordinary investors who lost money.
  • The distortion of courtroom conduct: Rather than describing the episode plainly as a "courtroom disturbance" or a "refusal to accept a judicial verdict," sympathetic headlines subtly nudged readers toward viewing Kim as a tragic figure wronged by the system. Blacksnowman characterized this pattern as a lingering habit among parts of the Korean press to defer to powerful figures even after they fall from grace.

In South Korea’s polarized media environment, debates over vocabulary are rarely just semantic. Critics contend that unless newsrooms establish clearer editorial standards for covering high-profile trials, public trust in journalism will continue to erode alongside trust in public institutions.

From Home Videos to Prison Time: The Political Context

To understand why Kim Keon-hee’s four-year sentence carries such symbolic weight inside South Korea, it helps to step back and look at the political shifts of recent years.

During Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, allegations surrounding Kim—including the Deutsch Motors affair—remained among the most explosive issues in domestic politics. Critics repeatedly accused prosecutors of conducting passive investigations, delaying inquiries, and issuing non-indictment decisions that effectively shielded the ruling circle from scrutiny. Supporters of Yoon Suk-yeol and the conservative People Power Party, meanwhile, dismissed opposition calls for a special counsel as partisan warfare.

South Korea’s political landscape has since changed dramatically. Yoon Suk-yeol was impeached, removed from office, and is currently serving time in prison. Today, the country is governed by President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea, now the ruling party, while the People Power Party serves as the main opposition. Under this altered political reality, special counsel proceedings and judicial rulings have moved forward against figures previously seen as untouchable.

Even the run-up to the Supreme Court verdict featured an unusual public-relations subplot. Shortly before the sentencing was finalized, casual personal videos of Kim—showing her jumping rope and mopping floors at home—were abruptly made public. In Blacksnowman’s reading, these clips were far from innocent daily updates. Instead, the commentary describes them as a calculated "image-making" campaign designed to project modesty, soften her public persona, and build sympathy ahead of an anticipated release.

When the Supreme Court instead handed down a final four-year prison term, that carefully managed strategy collapsed overnight. To critics, the contrast between the domestic choreography of the home videos and the combative outburst inside the Seoul Central District Court laid bare the gap between curated public relations and the reality of facing prison time.

What to Watch Next in South Korea’s Courts and Newsrooms

Although the Supreme Court’s four-year sentence closes the chapter on the Deutsch Motors stock manipulation case, several concrete developments remain in motion and will shape South Korea’s political and institutional landscape in the months ahead:

  • The Political Parties Act trial: Proceedings at the Seoul Central District Court’s Criminal Collegiate Division 27 are still underway. Observers will be watching how Kim Keon-hee’s legal team adjusts its strategy now that her Supreme Court conviction is final, and whether her courtroom conduct affects how judges evaluate the remaining charges.
  • Judicial and prosecutorial reform under the Lee Jae-myung government: With the Democratic Party of Korea holding the presidency, supporters of institutional reform view the Supreme Court verdict not as a finish line, but as a starting point. Key debates center on ensuring that prosecutors and courts apply the law consistently regardless of who holds power, preventing future accusations of selective delays or political shielding.
  • Accountability in the South Korean press: The controversy over the ulbun headlines has renewed calls from media critics and civic groups for stricter journalistic guidelines. Whether major outlets adjust how they report on courtroom defiance by formerly powerful figures—or continue to rely on sympathetic framing—will be a revealing test for South Korea’s media ecosystem.

For decades, South Korea’s democracy has wrestled with a recurring question: whether the law reaches all the way to the top. The Supreme Court’s four-year ruling delivered an emphatic answer in one landmark case. How the country’s courts handle the remaining trials, and how its newsrooms choose to describe them, will determine whether that principle takes lasting root.
