Why Your Child’s Brain Needs Less Structure This Summer

September 03, 2026

As summer vacation approaches, a familiar wave of anxiety washes over parents in South Korea. The pressure is palpable: schedules are packed with English camps, premium enrichment programs, and "educational" trips to famous landmarks. Parents spend hours debating which experiences will give their child a competitive edge, often losing sight of what the child actually enjoys. But according to Dr. Jang Dong-sun, a prominent South Korean brain scientist, this frantic quest for "good experiences" might be doing more harm than good. In reality, these curated schedules may not be stimulating a child’s brain at all—they might simply be a form of "retail therapy" for anxious parents.

Modern neuroscience suggests that the brain does not grow simply by having information injected into it. In fact, the opposite is true. True neural development occurs when the brain is forced to predict outcomes, fail, test hypotheses, and correct its course. If you want to cultivate a sharper, more resilient mind in your child this summer, it might be time to tear up the rigid itinerary.

The Trap of the Perfectly Planned Vacation

Every vacation, families spend small fortunes (often thousands of dollars) flying to Jeju Island or traveling abroad, yet the memories that stick with a child are rarely the view from the plane or the luxury hotel room. They are the simple, unscripted moments: eating a piece of roasted corn on a dusty street corner or finding a unique rock on a hiking trail. The brain learns through what scientists call "prediction errors."

For a brain to "mark" an experience as significant, a child must be allowed to ask, "What will happen here?" and then witness the result. A parent-curated roadmap leaves no room for the child to make predictions. When a child is dragged through a perfectly organized tour, they aren't having an experience; they are performing labor. When a parent shouts, "Look at this! Isn't it amazing?" the moment the child arrives at a landmark, they are effectively killing the child’s curiosity. Instead, try handing the map to the child. Let them decide what to eat or where to go next. These minor, self-chosen moments provide far more neurological stimulation than a multi-million won package tour. A vacation should not be a checklist to conquer, but a laboratory where a child’s agency is tested.

The Necessity of 'Doing Nothing'

In South Korea, where the culture of high-intensity academic competition permeates even the youngest age groups, parents often view a child sitting still as a sign of laziness. However, if a child spends their vacation glued to a smartphone or watching "Shorts" (short-form, algorithmically-driven videos), they aren't resting—they are overtaxing their brain. Constant sensory input keeps the brain saturated with dopamine, effectively shutting down the ability to think independently.

The brain requires the "Default Mode Network" (DMN) to be active in order to process information and recharge. Modern children are rarely given even a single minute for this to happen. When a child complains of being bored, handing them a tablet is like throwing gasoline on a fire. Instead, encourage them to do absolutely nothing—even if it means lying flat on the floor for ten minutes. It may be tedious at first, but this silence is the only way to quiet the brain and lower stress hormones. Deep, diaphragmatic breathing is, in scientific terms, the most effective "brake" for a hyper-stimulated brain.

Learning Through Physicality and Patience

The physical environment plays a critical role in brain development. A child who only walks on flat, paved asphalt has a different neural profile than one who navigates the uneven, unpredictable terrain of a mountain trail. Hiking demands the constant engagement of the vestibular system and proprioception, providing the brain with a sophisticated physical workout. Reaching the summit doesn't just build muscles; it leaves an imprint on the prefrontal cortex, reinforcing the belief that "I can overcome obstacles."

Fishing offers a similar, albeit different, lesson. It is an exercise in patience. In a digital world where gratification is instantaneous, the long wait for a bite trains the brain to maintain a high level of focused attention. Recent studies suggest that children who engage in regular nature-based activities show faster development in the hippocampus, the area of the brain responsible for spatial memory and learning. The brain is hard-wired to respond to the physical world, not just the digital one.

The Human Connection

While Virtual Reality (VR) and other technologies now allow for "simulated" experiences, no high-tech tool can replace the quality of a conversation between a parent and a child. Technology is merely a facilitator; the core of any meaningful experience must be human interaction. A child’s brain feels most secure when they are with their parents, and it learns best when it feels safe.

My own journey as a parent has been a process of unlearning. Like many others, I used to start every trip by creating a list of "must-do" activities, believing it was my duty. But I have come to realize that my obsession with efficiency was fostering passivity in my child. We are conditioned to search for "the right answer"—the right summer project, the right academy progress, the right report. But a child’s brain does not grow on answers; it grows on questions.

This summer, I intend to throw away the plan and simply ask my child, "Where should we go?" They might suggest somewhere absurd, but that absurdity is proof that their brain is sparking and expanding. As we look ahead, the challenge for parents is to move away from being the "architects" of their children's lives and start being their partners in discovery. Being less "efficient" and more curious is perhaps the most intelligent educational choice a parent can make.

What to watch next: Observe your child during their "bored" moments. Instead of reaching for a digital device, introduce a low-stakes task—like navigating a new neighborhood or planning a simple dinner—and see how their decision-making skills develop without your interference.
