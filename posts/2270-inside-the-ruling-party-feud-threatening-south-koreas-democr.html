Inside the Ruling Party Feud Threatening South Korea’s Democratic Leadership

November 12, 2026

"Regain your sanity." "Reflect on your responsibility."

These harsh warnings were not fired across the aisle by South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. Instead, they were hurled internally between high-profile figures within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. When Representative Choi Min-hee publicly characterized a section of her own party colleagues as "hyenas," Representative Kim Woo-young—a key loyalist aligned with President Lee Jae-myung—wasted no time firing back, calling her remarks an unpardonable mistake and demanding immediate self-reflection.

What might look like a routine exchange of political insults is actually the visible tip of a much deeper, more dangerous rift within the ruling party. As South Korea navigates the early tenure of President Lee Jae-myung, the Democratic Party is fracturing along factional lines. On one side stands the "Pro-Seok" faction, aligned with party leader Kim Min-seok; on the other stands the "Pro-Cheong" faction, coalescing around senior figure Jung Chung-rae. With party leader Kim’s approval ratings tumbling and control over candidate nominations for upcoming elections on the line, an internal power struggle threatens to drain the momentum from the Lee administration's legislative agenda.

The War of Words: Factional Tension Reaches a Boiling Point

To understand why this public spat has alarmed ruling party supporters, one must look at the political weight of the players involved. Kim Woo-young is no ordinary backbencher. A former head of Seoul’s Eunpyeong District, a presidential secretary during the former Moon Jae-in administration, and the former standing head of the Democratic Party's Innovation Committee, Kim is widely viewed as a core voice within President Lee Jae-myung's inner circle. When a figure of his stature publicly reprimands a colleague and demands accountability from opposing internal camps, it reflects a calculated strategic assessment from the party's core power brokers.

The collapse of civil discourse within the party highlights how completely internal communication channels have broken down. Rather than engaging in policy debates or constructive criticism, prominent party members are trading public barbs that dominate news headlines. For grass-roots supporters who expected the ruling party to focus on tackling cost-of-living issues and pursuing judicial reforms in Seoul's legal district of Seocho-dong, this descent into factional mudslinging feels like a betrayal.

The Pro-Seok faction’s apparent push to solidify absolute dominance over party operations alongside the presidential office risks alienating diverse voices within the coalition. While party-government alignment is critical in the early stages of a presidency, enforcing factional conformity and framing internal critics as adversaries threatens the pluralism that has historically anchored the Democratic Party.

The $210,000 YouTube Scandal and the Battle Over Influencers

The dispute escalated dramatically when Kim Woo-young turned his attention to influential political commentators outside the National Assembly. Kim publicly highlighted reports that 279 million won (approximately $210,000) had been paid in consulting fees to Park Si-young, head of the political consulting firm Corp. Park Si-young and host of the popular YouTube channel Park Si-young TV.

Kim framed these payments as deceptive advertising, accusing Park of acting as a factional mouthpiece for the Pro-Cheong camp and undermining party primaries through biased political coverage. The revelation that hundreds of thousands of dollars flowed to a prominent political media creator sent shockwaves through the party's base. Members are now demanding full transparency regarding how these funds were disbursed and whether corporate bank accounts were used to sway internal opinion.

Key Detail: At the center of the financial controversy is 279 million won (approx. $210,000) in consulting fees allegedly paid to political media platforms. Critics contend these funds crossed the line from public relations into factional primary manipulation.

Park Si-young and his allies forcefully rejected the accusations, dismissing Kim’s commentary as petty framing. They argue that featuring reform-minded politicians during past general elections was part of legitimate media activity aimed at party renewal, completely separate from any consulting contracts. They pointedly noted that Kim Woo-young himself had appeared on the channel multiple times in the past to build his own political profile.

This clash highlights a growing dilemma in South Korean politics: the outsized influence of political YouTube channels and partisan "speakers." Commentary from figures like Park Si-young, alongside media commentators such as Lee Ji-eun, Kim Seung-hyun, Kim Dae-ho, and Jang Youn-sun, can swing thousands of party votes. However, when big money and political commentary become entangled, it invites accusations of media manipulation. It also provides easy ammunition for conservative opposition figures, such as People Power Party lawmaker Jang Dong-hyeok, to attack the ruling party's moral credibility.

Plunging Approval Ratings and Demands for Leadership Reform

The friction inside the party is made far more urgent by a sharp decline in public confidence. Recent opinion polls show party leader Kim Min-seok’s personal approval rating plummeting to 23.7%. This stands in stark contrast to President Lee Jae-myung’s national job approval rating, which holds steady around 44%, and the Democratic Party's overall support level of 35%. In Automated Response System (ARS) polling, party leadership approval suffered a steep 10-percentage-point drop, signaling deep dissatisfaction among the party's core base.

Compounding these political headwinds is a series of unaddressed internal controversies. Jung Chung-rae took to Facebook to issue three explicit demands to Kim Min-seok's leadership team:

  • Acknowledge specific missteps: Clearly state where the party leadership erred.
  • Direct apology: Offer an explicit apology to affected victims.
  • Preventive measures: Present concrete structural safeguards to prevent repeat occurrences.

Jung’s public challenge stems from lingering anger over how the leadership handled allegations involving the controversial religious group Shincheonji, as well as friction surrounding warnings issued by local committee head Park Gyu-hwan regarding secondary harm against internal whistleblowers. While the Pro-Seok faction sought to minimize public discussion of these issues to maintain party unity, the Pro-Cheong faction demanded thorough investigations and public accountability.

This double standard has drawn fierce criticism. The Democratic Party built its political identity on relentlessly attacking scandals during the tenure of former president Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—including investigations into his wife, Kim Keon-hee. If the ruling party now brushes aside its own internal scandals, it risks looking hypocritical to voters who demanded higher ethical standards following Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's impeachment and removal from office.

The Real Stake: Candidate Nominations and the Road Ahead

At its core, this bitter struggle is not just about words or YouTube commentators—it is a battle over candidate nominations (공천권) for upcoming local and legislative elections. Factional battles in South Korea almost always boil down to who gets to place their loyalists on the ballot.

Reformers within the party fear that Kim Min-seok’s leadership is dismantling the established "system nomination" framework—a standardized, rules-based process meant to prevent party bosses from handpicking candidates. Recent organizational shifts have fueled these anxieties. For instance, the appointment of former lawmaker Jung Bong-ju as a special advisor in a role previously connected to Representative Han Min-soo, alongside former lawmaker Choi Se-sung taking over responsibilities tied to Choi Min-hee, has been interpreted by critics as an effort to sidelined internal opponents and reward factional loyalists.

If the Democratic Party allows factional infighting to replace transparent nomination rules, it risks fracturing the coalition that brought President Lee Jae-myung to power. The priority for the ruling party should be delivering on its legislative promises: advancing judicial reforms in Seocho-dong, dismantling institutional entrenched interests, and stabilizing the national economy. Entrenching internal power grabs at the expense of reform risks turning the ruling party into its own worst enemy.

What to watch next: Pay close attention to whether party leader Kim Min-seok offers a formal response or reform package to address his plunging 23.7% approval rating. Furthermore, monitor whether the party leadership moves to institutionalize fair "system nomination" rules or continues replacing key committee figures with factional advisors ahead of the next election cycle.
