Inside South Korea’s Supreme Court Split Over the 2024 Martial Law Cabinet Meeting

November 20, 2026

On the night of December 3, 2024, cabinet ministers filed one by one into the grand reception room of South Korea’s executive compound in Yongsan, Seoul. Outside, the country was minutes away from an emergency martial law declaration—an event that stunned a democracy that had spent decades building institutional guardrails against authoritarian rule. Inside, according to timelines cited in South Korean court records, then-Prime Minister Han Duck-soo checked off arrivals and walked back and forth between the reception room and the inner office of Yoon Suk Yeol, who led the martial law declaration before later being impeached, removed from office, and imprisoned.

How the law should judge Han’s actions during those tense hours has become one of the sharpest legal disputes in South Korea’s post-martial law reckoning. In an en banc ruling—a full-panel decision reserved by South Korea’s Supreme Court for cases of major constitutional weight—a majority of justices sought to narrow Han’s liability for convening the pre-declaration Cabinet meeting, finding that prosecutors had failed to prove an “intent to subvert the constitutional order.” Yet Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyung-mi, joined by fellow dissenters, delivered a blunt rebuttal. In a dissenting opinion championed by Korean legal commentators critical of the majority’s formalism, Justice Oh argued that lending the formal trappings of a constitutional Cabinet meeting to an unlawful martial law plan was itself deep participation in insurrection.

Two Readings of Insurrection at the Supreme Court

To understand why a single procedural meeting triggered such a fierce split on South Korea’s highest bench, foreign observers must first look at how Korean criminal law defines insurrection. Under South Korean statute, proving insurrection requires more than showing that a defendant knew an order was unlawful; prosecutors must establish a specific subjective purpose to subvert the constitutional order. Under South Korea’s constitution, major executive acts—including declaring emergency martial law—must go through deliberation by the State Council, commonly known as the Cabinet, which is chaired by the chief executive with the prime minister serving as vice chair.

According to the Supreme Court majority opinion, that high evidentiary bar was not cleared regarding Han’s involvement before martial law was announced. The majority reasoned that recommending and convening a Cabinet meeting did not automatically prove Han intended to destroy constitutional governance, concluding that “the Cabinet meeting recommendation does not prove intent to subvert the constitution” and pointing toward partial acquittal on that aspect of the charges.

Justice Oh’s dissent rejected both the majority’s framing and its conclusion. In her dissenting opinion, she wrote that she could not agree with the refusal to recognize insurrectionary intent, arguing that Han’s conduct could not be sliced into isolated fragments before and after the declaration. Rather, his behavior had to be judged as a continuous sequence. According to Justice Oh, Han received the martial law proclamation and instructions directly from Mr. Yoon earlier that evening, immediately recognized that the measure lacked both substantive and procedural legal grounds, and saw that Mr. Yoon’s resolve was irreversible.

Korean commentators backing Justice Oh’s analysis emphasize the criminal law doctrine of willful acceptance: when a person foresees a criminal outcome and proceeds anyway to lay the groundwork for it, legal intent is established. By working to assemble the Cabinet despite knowing the meeting would serve only to manufacture formal legitimacy for an unconstitutional decree, Justice Oh argued, Han accepted and facilitated the destruction of constitutional order.

At the heart of her dissent is a distinction between classic military coups and what political scientists and Korean jurists call a “self-coup.” Unlike historical coups staged by military officers seizing power from outside the government, a self-coup occurs when an elected leader abuses emergency powers to coerce the legislature and neutralize judicial checks. Because a self-coup operates through existing state organs and official paperwork, Justice Oh warned, procedural compliance cannot be treated as neutral administration. Instead, giving unlawful power the appearance of legal regularity becomes one of the primary tools used to entrench a coup after the fact.

Ninety Minutes in Yongsan and a Two-Minute Cabinet Session

The factual timeline emphasized in Justice Oh’s dissent—and detailed in Korean commentary reviewing the court record—centers on what Han did, and failed to do, during roughly 90 minutes at the Yongsan compound. After expressing only passive concern during his initial meeting with Mr. Yoon, Han spent over an hour waiting for enough ministers to arrive to meet the Cabinet’s legal quorum. In her dissent, Justice Oh highlighted that critical gap:

“For more than an hour that was given to him, the defendant cared only about filling the quorum and did nothing.”

According to the dissent, Han made no effort to brief arriving ministers on the unconstitutionality of the proclamation or organize collective opposition inside the Cabinet. Instead, court timelines record the following sequence on the night of December 3, 2024:

  • 9:29 p.m. (21:29): After checking arrivals in the grand reception room and meeting privately with Mr. Yoon and then-Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun, Han entered Mr. Yoon’s office a second time to report that the Cabinet quorum had been satisfied.
  • 9:35 p.m. (21:35): Han returned to the grand reception room to update the waiting ministers on the status of arrivals.
  • 9:37 p.m. (21:37): Han personally telephoned Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs Minister Song Mi-ryung, who was still in transit, urging her to get there quickly without giving any warning or mention of martial law.
  • 10:16 p.m. to 10:18 p.m. (22:16–22:18): The Cabinet session convened for just two minutes. Marked by unilateral statements from Mr. Yoon with no substantive debate or deliberation, it ended immediately before martial law was publicly declared.

What followed the announcement, Korean legal analysts note, further undermined claims that Han was merely a passive bystander. Right after the declaration speech—as troops were moving toward the National Assembly—Han attempted to collect post-hoc signatures from the Cabinet members present on martial law documents. After the meeting broke up, Han remained alone in the reception room for about 16 minutes with then-Interior and Safety Minister Lee Sang-min. According to the record cited in the commentary, Han shared details from a secret document Mr. Yoon had given him earlier—covering plans to paralyze key institutions, including cutting off electricity and water—and discussed next steps.

Han’s defense team offered a starkly different interpretation. His lawyers argued that he was simply processing statutory procedures under a superior’s directive, no different from a politically neutral civil servant arranging a meeting and handling paperwork required by law. In her dissent, Justice Oh dismissed that comparison as a fundamental misunderstanding of constitutional rank. Even low-level bureaucrats are under no legal duty to obey manifestly unlawful orders, she noted. For a prime minister—the nation’s second-highest executive official and vice chair of the Cabinet, tasked specifically with advising and checking executive power—citing a superior’s orders cannot excuse assembling a constitutional body to rubber-stamp an insurrection.

How Lower Courts and Global Precedents Framed the Self-Coup

The divide on the Supreme Court also reflects differing judicial emphases seen across four separate trial panels at the Seoul Central District Court, South Korea’s main trial court handling the December 3 cases. Criminal Agreement Division 25, presided over by Judge Ji Gwi-yeon, found Yoon Suk Yeol guilty as the ringleader of an insurrection, though it took a cautious view on whether there had been a pre-planned blueprint for long-term dictatorship, pointing to insufficient evidence and a loosely formed plan after neutralizing the National Assembly.

By contrast, Criminal Agreement Division 33, led by Judge Lee Jin-gwan, convicted Han Duck-soo of performing essential duties in an insurrection and explicitly defined the December 3 episode as a “self-coup aimed at long-term dictatorship.” Judge Lee drew a sharp comparison to South Korea’s own authoritarian past, including the military seizure of power by former dictator Chun Doo-hwan. Because a top-down self-coup in a modern democracy inflicts immense institutional damage when successful, Judge Lee reasoned, its participants warrant even stricter punishment than those involved in classic bottom-up military coups. Two other panels—Division 32 under Judge Ryu Kyung-jin, which convicted former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min by noting that even an ordinary citizen could see martial law was unconstitutional, and Division 35 under Judge Baek Dae-hyun, which resolved investigative jurisdiction disputes and issued a conviction—similarly treated the December 3 actions as clear insurrection.

Korean commentators supporting Justice Oh’s dissent have situated these rulings alongside international precedents where democracies confronted self-coups or attacks on constitutional transfers of power. They point to the United States’ prosecution of participants in the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack as insurrectionists alongside constitutional debates over barring them from office; Germany’s 2022 prosecution of the far-right Reichsbürger plot to storm parliament under the principle of defensive democracy; and Brazil’s criminal prosecutions and political bans targeting senior figures linked to the 2023 storming of Congress and the presidential palace by supporters of former President Jair Bolsonaro. Across these systems, legal commentators argue, elected officials who betray constitutional trust face heavier, not lighter, scrutiny.

What This Legal Divide Means Going Forward

Beyond the fate of a single former prime minister, the clash between the Supreme Court majority and Justice Oh Kyung-mi exposes a core vulnerability in South Korea’s constitutional architecture. The Cabinet’s mandate to deliberate on emergency powers exists to prevent unilateral executive overreach. Yet on the night of December 3, 2024, that safeguard was compressed into a two-minute formality designed to satisfy a quorum checklist.

Moving forward, legal observers will be watching three concrete developments closely. First is how South Korean trial and appellate courts navigate the tension between the Supreme Court majority’s strict threshold for proving subjective intent and Justice Oh’s holistic, context-driven standard when judging other senior officials who handled paperwork, communications, or logistical steps around the December 3 declaration. Second is whether the stricter standard articulated by Division 33—treating modern self-coups and their institutional facilitators as uniquely severe threats—gains lasting traction in Korean jurisprudence, or whether future officials accused of enabling unlawful executive actions can rely on procedural compliance as a legal shield. Finally, South Korea’s legal and political institutions face the practical question of how to reinforce Cabinet accountability so that a constitutional check on emergency power can never again be reduced to a race to fill a room.
