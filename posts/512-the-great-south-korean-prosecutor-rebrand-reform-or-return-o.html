The Great South Korean Prosecutor Rebrand: Reform or Return of the Inquisitors?

September 10, 2026

For over seven decades, the South Korean prosecution service held a near-monopoly on both investigating and prosecuting crimes—a concentration of power that critics argued made it a political weapon rather than an instrument of justice. That era was supposed to end this July when the National Assembly passed legislation to completely separate investigation from prosecution, abolishing the 72-year-old Supreme Prosecutors' Office. On October 2, the old nameplates are scheduled to come down, and two new entities will take their place: the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA) to handle probes, and a newly minted Prosecution Service focused solely on indictments and trials.

Yet, instead of popping champagne, lawmakers and legal reformers are sounding alarms. A newly released organizational blueprint from the Ministry of Justice has sparked fury within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's main liberal party. Rather than signaling the completion of historic reform, critics warn that the blueprint looks less like a dismantling of the old guard and more like a Trojan horse designed to sneak the prosecutors back through the back door.

The Staffing Shell Game: Thousands of Investigators with Nowhere to Go

The most glaring flaw in the Ministry of Justice’s reorganization plan lies in its headcount. Under the new arrangement, the Prosecution Service—which is theoretically being stripped of its investigative mandate—is slated to retain a massive staff of 8,412 personnel, including 2,292 prosecutors and 6,120 general staff members. The Ministry has touted this as a 21.4% reduction in its total quota. Look closer, however, and the numbers tell a different story.

Once you subtract the personnel transferring to the new investigative agency, the actual net reduction in staff is a meager 238 people. Crucially, roughly 7,000 career investigative officers who spent their careers running criminal probes are not leaving; they are being absorbed directly into the Prosecution Service. This raises an obvious, uncomfortable question: why does an agency restricted purely to indictments and trial maintenance need thousands of investigators?

Reformers argue that keeping these investigators on board is a transparent loophole. Without an active investigative mandate, these officers will likely be used to review other agencies' case files under the guise of "coordination," effectively allowing prosecutors to maintain a backdoor command over investigations. Unless the staff is drastically reduced and physically reallocated, the structural machinery of the old prosecution service remains completely intact, ready to be flipped back on the moment political winds change.

A New Department of "Judicial Control" Over Other Agencies

Compounding these fears is the creation of a brand-new "Judicial Control Division" within local prosecution offices. The division's stated purpose is to review cases dropped by police or other investigative bodies. But critics have blasted the name and concept as peak audacity. In a standard democratic separation of powers, the judiciary checks the legality of investigations—prosecutors do not act as a quasi-judicial body to police other independent investigative agencies.

The prosecution service is an institution that critics argue has spent decades shielding its own and overstepping its bounds. For such an agency to appoint itself as the "controller" of external bodies strikes many as an institutional power grab. Instead of a streamlined prosecution service focused strictly on courtroom arguments—tasks that legal experts suggest require only a few hundred trial prosecutors—the new blueprint creates a sprawling, super-sized bureaucracy designed to preserve its traditional political leverage.

This duplication of oversight functions mirrors past practices where prosecutors used their authority to meddle in lower-level probes. Without strict, systemic caps on what the new prosecution offices can touch, "judicial control" risks becoming a euphemism for political interference.

Personnel Is Policy: The Return of Old Networks

Structural blueprints are only as good as the people running them, and recent appointments have done little to calm anxieties. The nomination of Kim Ji-yong—a former senior prosecutor who previously signed statements opposing the stripping of investigative powers and fighting disciplinary measures against then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol—as the final candidate to head the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency has sent shockwaves through reformist circles.

When figures tied to the old guard are placed at the helm of the new investigative body, while the prosecution office itself remains staffed by veterans of the old regime, the entire architecture begins to look like a "two roofs, one family" cartel. Shin Jang-kwon, a prominent figure in the minor opposition Rebuilding Korea Party, didn't mince words, calling the overhaul a mere "prosecution name-change" rather than genuine reform.

The DPK leadership, caught off guard by the public backlash, has started scrambling to discuss revisions. Yet, critics note that the government's standard defense—claiming that the statutory quotas for prosecutors are fixed by law and therefore difficult to change—rings hollow. Laws are meant to be amended when a massive constitutional and criminal overhaul takes place. Treating statutory quotas as immutable while gutting investigative powers suggests a reluctance to follow through on the harder, messier work of democratic restructuring.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea approaches the October 2 deadline for the official dismantling of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, the battle lines are drawn over four core demands pushed by progressive lawmakers:

  • Total Headcount Reduction: Whether the government will actually lower the statutory quota for prosecutors rather than grandfathering in legacy staff.
  • Abolition of the Judicial Control Division: Whether the prosecution will be stripped of its power to directly police and meddle in cases handled by other agencies.
  • Elimination of Internal Task Forces: Whether special crime units will be excised from the prosecution side to prevent backdoor joint investigations.
  • Independent Leadership: Whether appointments to the new investigative agencies will be free from the shadow of old-boy networks.

True reform of South Korea's powerful criminal justice system requires more than swapping out letterheads on office doors. It demands irreversible staff reductions, slashed budgets, and institutional firewalls that prevent any single legal caste from regaining dominance. Whether the ruling camp will stand firm against institutional inertia or accept a hollowed-out compromise will determine if October 2 marks the birth of a fair accountability system—or simply the resurrection of the old guard under a new title.
