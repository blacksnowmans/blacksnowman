Cracking at the Seams: Why South Korea's Ruling Democratic Party is Facing a Quiet Revolt

September 03, 2026

Walk through the bustling streets of Seoul on any given evening, and you can feel the low, steady hum of political anxiety. For months, the atmosphere inside South Korea’s political corridors has felt less like a governing party operating with confidence and more like a pressure cooker waiting for the valve to blow. To the outside observer, South Korea’s progressive camp might look secure, steering the levers of power under President Lee Jae-myung. But beneath the surface, a bitter reckoning is underway. The source of the friction isn't just coming from the traditional opposition conservative bloc—it is erupting from within, sparked by plunging approval ratings, tribal infighting, and a growing sense of betrayal among the very people who carried the party to power.

The Inconvenient Truth Behind the Numbers

Political parties love grand slogans, often leaning on sweeping promises like a "100-day miracle" to rally the faithful. When Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), grandly pledged to claw back 10%p in public approval ratings, it was meant to be a lifeline for a weary base. Instead, the reality check has been brutal.

When the latest polling data dropped, the results sent shockwaves through party headquarters. Rather than climbing, the Democratic Party’s overall approval rating slumped by 4.3%p. Worse still, President Lee Jae-myung’s positive job evaluation plummeted by 8.4%p. For seasoned political watchers, these aren't just the normal fluctuations of a midterm slump; they are a glaring, neon-lit warning sign from a exhausted electorate.

The flashing red lights are burning brightest in the capital. In Seoul—historically the ultimate bellwether of South Korean politics—negative evaluations of the administration surged to a staggering 70.2%. Meanwhile, disapproval among moderate, unaffiliated voters climbed to 58.5%. These numbers point to a dangerous reality: the Democratic Party has retreated into an isolated bubble, relying on an increasingly narrow core base while alienating the broader public. It raises a painful question for party strategists: Have they spent so long looking only at the numbers they wanted to see that arrogant overconfidence completely blinded them to the mood on the ground?

Tribal Politics and the Disappearance of Party Unity

One of the most corrosive developments in recent months has been the rise of insular internal factions and exclusionary mentalities—phenomena that critics within the progressive camp have bluntly dubbed the "Gong-chi-mo" dynamic. Instead of directing political energy outward to address national challenges, internal factions have weaponized party processes to sideline internal critics.

What was ostensibly designed as a framework to manage party conventions quickly devolved into a tool for exclusion rather than expansion. True political movements grow by widening their tents; this strategy has instead diced the progressive base into microscopic, warring camps. The tragic irony of modern partisan politics is watching logical debate turn into ritualistic excommunication.

This factionalism reached a boiling point when senior Supreme Council member Park Sun-won publicly attacked former Rebuilding Korea Party leader Cho Kuk, labeling him "ungrateful." Reducing legitimate, commonsense political critique to petty emotional warfare leaves voters shaking their heads. When did the progressive camp become so narrow-minded that anyone outside an inner circle is treated as an existential enemy? While party members tear each other apart on television and social media, their actual political opponents are simply sitting back and watching the self-destruction.

A Broken System and the Silence of Accountability

Every major political party requires an institutional moral compass—an internal ethics and inspection unit designed to keep power honest and transparent. Yet, public trust in the Democratic Party’s ethics apparatus has severely eroded, with growing suspicions that the watchdog has been co-opted into a partisan tool.

The lingering controversies following the resignation of former Ethics Tribunal head Han Dong-su are emblematic of a wider malaise. When fairness vanishes from a political system, the system ceases to be a framework for governance and becomes an instrument of raw enforcement. A double standard where "our allies are innocent, and our rivals are guilty" leaves no room for genuine internal reform.

This underlying frustration is precisely why prominent progressive media figures like PD Lee Jong-won have publicly demanded a special independent counsel investigation—a "convention special probe"—into the party’s internal affairs. This is not mere theatrical outrage; it is a cry of desperation from people who genuinely believe that without cutting out this rot, the party has no viable future. The Democratic Party risks becoming trapped in a prison of its own making.

What to Watch Next

As the dust settles on these internal feuds, the path forward for South Korea's ruling camp hinges on a few critical developments to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • Public Opinion Rebound (or Further Erosion): Watch upcoming weekly polling data closely. If Seoul's negative evaluation remains locked above 70%, pressure on party leadership to enact a dramatic cabinet or leadership reshuffle will become overwhelming.
  • The Ethics Unit Reform: Pay attention to whether independent lawmakers or party reformists successfully push for an overhaul of the internal ethics inspection system to restore its lost neutrality.
  • Inter-Party and Intra-Coalition Dynamics: Keep an eye on how the Democratic Party manages its relationships with smaller progressive allies like Cho Kuk's party. Further public feuding will signal that the leadership has lost control of its message heading into future local or legislative preparations.

Politics remains, at its core, the art of winning human hearts. Right now, the Democratic Party is not just failing to win new minds—it is actively driving away the loyalists who built its foundation. Unless the leadership finds the courage to look in the mirror, empty out the self-serving factions, and embrace genuine self-reflection, the future political winter for South Korea's progressives may prove far colder than they anticipate.
