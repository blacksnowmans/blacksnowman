The Disappearing First Rung: How Generative AI Is Quietly Dismantling South Korea's Entry-Level Job Market

October 26, 2026

Across university campuses in Seoul and the bustling tech hubs of Pangyo, a bitter paradox now dominates every job-search forum: "If every job listing demands three to five years of experience, where on earth is a graduate supposed to get that first year?" For generations of South Koreans, the path from university to the middle class was defined by the entry-level hiring window. Conglomerates and tech firms routinely hired thousands of fresh college graduates every spring and autumn, investing substantial time and money into on-the-job apprenticeship programs to train them from scratch.

Over the past two years, that gateway has snapped shut. Conventional wisdom among older executives and mainstream economists has blamed the freeze on macroeconomic headwinds: prolonged high interest rates, subdued domestic consumption, and a global tech correction. "Wait for the cycle to turn," job seekers were told, "and the hiring doors will reopen."

A comprehensive analysis of ten million job postings published over the last seven years on one of South Korea's largest employment platforms tells an entirely different story. The freeze is not an ordinary cyclical downturn. Instead, it marks a profound structural shift driven by the rapid enterprise adoption of generative artificial intelligence, which has effectively wiped out the bottom rungs of the white-collar corporate ladder.

Ten Million Postings Reveal a Targeted White-Collar Rout

To settle the debate over whether macroeconomics or automation is driving the hiring freeze, researchers analyzed approximately ten million employment listings posted between 2017 and 2024. The study tracked job titles, detailed task descriptions, and corporate experience requirements—specifically whether openings were open to entry-level candidates, required no prior experience, or mandated a strict minimum number of years in the field.

Up until late 2022, the proportion of entry-level openings on the platform had maintained a steady, post-pandemic upward trajectory. The turning point occurred precisely at the end of 2022, coinciding with the public release and rapid commercial rollout of advanced generative AI models such as OpenAI's ChatGPT. What followed was not an economy-wide contraction, but a targeted strike against entry-level knowledge workers.

"Attributing this simply to broader societal changes or the decline of traditional open recruitment leaves far too many suspicious patterns unexplained," noted Kang Tae-young, chief executive of Underscore, a Seoul-based data intelligence firm that spearheaded the analysis.

The sectoral breakdown exposes the precision of this contraction. In software engineering—a field that spent the pandemic years competing aggressively for entry-level talent—the share of listings open to fresh graduates rose from 33.5% in 2020 to 37.1% in 2022. By 2023, it fell to 30.9%, and by 2024 it had plunged to 26.8%. IT infrastructure roles suffered an even steeper drop, falling from a peak of 50.7% in 2022 to 36.9% in 2024.

The contagion quickly spread across the wider white-collar spectrum. Junior openings in digital design slid from 51.8% to 43.9% between 2022 and 2024, multimedia design fell from 47.1% to 34.4%, marketing dropped from 44.5% to 39.9%, and entry-level sales listings dropped from 64.2% to 54.5%.

The control group that disproves the macroeconomic excuse is the blue-collar sector. For college-educated applicants seeking technical and physical field roles—jobs where physical manipulation, on-site presence, and manual dexterity are required—the entry-level hiring share held remarkably steady. It sat at 71.7% in 2020, rose to 77.6% in 2022, and hovered at 75.0% in 2024. Had interest rates and tight monetary policy been the primary culprits, physical and industrial sectors would have contracted alongside the digital economy. Instead, the doorway closed almost exclusively where work consists of text, code, and structured rules.

The Broken Ladder: Trading Junior Salaries for Subscriptions

This micro-level recruitment data is corroborated by official statutory figures from South Korea's Ministry of Employment and Labor. Tracking national employment insurance subscribers reveals that the proportion of insured workers aged 29 and under in the information and communications technology (ICT) sector reversed its upward trend in late 2022 and began a steep, sustained decline. In manufacturing, by contrast, the youth employment ratio remained flat.

Even more telling is the age divergence across the economy. If general business insolvency were stalling hiring, employment would contract across every demographic. Yet while hiring rates for workers in their twenties have plummeted, hiring rates for professionals in their thirties, forties, and fifties have continued to climb. Companies are still staffing up, but they are filtering out novices.

By categorizing some 1,800 micro-tasks according to their level of generative AI exposure, the data uncovered a direct mathematical relationship: the more exposed a job is to generative AI, the higher the minimum years of experience an employer demands in the job posting. Tasks that once formed the core apprenticeship of a junior analyst—drafting boilerplate code, summarizing market research, cleaning spreadsheets, and producing first-draft pitch decks—are now executed in seconds by advanced foundation models. Rather than paying an entry-level worker to learn by performing these administrative routines under supervision, companies are pairing senior practitioners with AI tools to handle multiple workloads, conditioning all new recruitment on an applicant already possessing three to five years of proven execution experience.

The 8% Illusion and the Polarization of Perceived Risk

Despite the severity of this structural shift, broader public and managerial awareness remains strangely muted. A nationwide representative survey of 1,700 South Korean adults sheds light on why the wider public has been slow to grasp what is occurring.

While 46% of respondents reported using generative AI tools on a regular basis, only 8% paid the $20 to $30 monthly subscription fees required to access state-of-the-art enterprise and flagship models, such as Claude 3.5 Sonnet or OpenAI's top-tier engines. The remaining 38% relied exclusively on deprecated, free-tier services.

This divide has generated a deceptive U-shaped perception curve regarding AI competence:

  • The Free-Tier Blindspot: Respondents who had only experimented with basic, free-tier models evaluated AI's workplace competence as lower than those who had never used AI at all. Having witnessed hallucinated answers or awkward phrasing on casual queries, many concluded that the technology was merely an amusing novelty incapable of replacing a human clerk.
  • The Veteran's Panic: Among the 8% who actively use high-tier, paid models in their daily professional workflows, the perception is inverted. In an assessment covering 12 major occupational groups—spanning corporate management, legal, medicine, and engineering—senior professionals who used paid models expressed the deepest dread. Having directed the models through complex code refactoring, structural financial modeling, and contract cross-referencing, these veterans recognized that the analytical output matches or exceeds that of junior and mid-level personnel.

This perceptual gap creates a dangerous lag in corporate governance and education. Senior executives who have only dabbled with free consumer apps reassure themselves that human intuition remains irreplaceable, even as their own department heads quietly automate junior requisitions out of the annual budget.

Structural Reckoning for an Aging Economy

According to projections by the state-run Korea Development Institute (KDI), roughly 38.8% of South Korean jobs currently contain micro-tasks where more than 70% of the workflow is technically automatable. Looking beyond 2030, that threshold will encompass over 90% of tasks across most service professions.

This transition does not imply immediate, mass dismissals of incumbent white-collar workers. Instead, it manifests as a quiet, insidious closure of entry portals. The immediate beneficiaries are established senior professionals, who harness AI to amplify their personal leverage. The losers are the young entrants locked outside the compound gate, unable to acquire the baseline organizational literacy that transforms a college graduate into an experienced specialist.

This dynamic also threatens to worsen educational inequality. Students whose families can comfortably fund expensive AI tool suites, custom plugins, and technical training enter the labor market with productivity profiles far removed from those of peers relying solely on university lectures and standard textbooks. What was once intended as a tool for cognitive democratization is hardening into an engine of credential polarization.

For policymakers under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, confronting this issue requires looking beyond standard macroeconomic relief packages or short-term youth public works internships. When corporations no longer bear the cost of training junior personnel because software does the work for pennies, the social contract of professional development is broken.

In the coming quarters, the critical indicators to watch will not be aggregate headline unemployment figures, but the divergence between youth labor-force participation and middle-age retention in high-tech services. If corporate recruiting continues to mandate multi-year experience for roles that were once the starting line of middle-class careers, South Korea will face an unprecedented generational bottleneck—one where an aging society, already contending with acute demographic headwinds, leaves its most technologically literate generation stranded at the perimeter of the knowledge economy.
