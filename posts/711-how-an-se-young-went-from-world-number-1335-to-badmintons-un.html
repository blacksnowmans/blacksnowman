How An Se-young Went from World Number 1335 to Badminton’s Undisputed Queen

October 05, 2026

The empty gym in Gwangju is quiet long after midnight, save for the rhythmic, blistering crack of a shuttlecock hitting carbon fiber. For a twelve-year-old girl named An Se-young, this was where every day began and ended. Born to a former national gymnast mother and a boxer father, An was a child who cried through brutal training sessions designed for high schoolers, yet refused to quit. Decades later, that same relentless drive would carry her to the absolute summit of global badminton, turning her into an icon of perseverance in a country obsessed with elite athletic success.

Yet, the story of An Se-young is not just a straightforward tale of prodigious talent. It is a grueling epic of physical agony, institutional clashes, and a relentless psychological evolution that transformed an overwhelmed teenager into the most dominant player in the sport. To understand her rise is to understand the immense pressures of South Korea's elite sports machine—and the sheer willpower required to break it.

From a Gwangju Prodigy to the Youngest National Selections in History

Long before she was making global headlines, An’s stubbornness was already legendary among local coaches. At Gwangju Punam Elementary School, she was pushed into the sport by her mother's recreational badminton hobby. Her youth coach later recalled that in 45 years of coaching, he had never seen a child cry through high school-level drills while managing to finish every single one of them out of pure spite and determination.

That stubborn streak paid off rapidly. In December 2017, while still a third-year student at Gwangju Sports Middle School, An shocked the Korean sporting establishment by dominating the senior national team trials with a flawless 7-0 record. It was the first time in South Korean badminton history that a middle schooler earned a senior national team spot purely on merit. When reporters asked the teenage phenom what her ultimate goal was, she did not hesitate: "To win first place at tournaments, of course. And an Olympic gold medal." At the time, it sounded like youthful bravado. It was actually a prophecy.

Hitting the Brutal Reality Check of the Senior International Circuit

The transition from junior dominance to the senior global stage, however, was merciless. When An made her senior international debut in early 2018, the Badminton World Federation (BWF) slapped her with a humbling world ranking of 1335. Her junior accolades meant nothing in the adult jungle.

At her debut international tournament in Osaka that April, she crashed out before the quarterfinals. A few months later, at the 2018 Jakarta Asian Games, she ran face-first into China's Chen Yufei—then one of the world's absolute best—and was unceremoniously bundled out in the 32nd round. Chen would go on to become An's most agonizing nemesis for years to come. Sitting at world number 1335, a seventeen-year-old An was forced to learn the hard way that raw talent is merely a ticket to the waiting room.

Instead of folding, she weaponized her failures. In 2019, she detonated onto the world stage by capturing five international titles, including the French Open—where she stunned Rio Olympic champion Carolina Marín—and the Korea Masters. By year's end, her ranking had rocketed to world number 9, and the BWF named her the Most Promising Player of the Year, a first for a South Korean.

Conquering Adversity: The Pain of Tokyo and the Rise of a New Mentality

The COVID-delayed Tokyo Olympics in 2021 brought another wave of heartbreak. Still only 19, An reached the quarterfinals only to be stopped once again by Chen Yufei in a fierce battle. Cameras caught a tearful An vowing to herself on live television: "Let's work even harder, thinking I might die out there. Let's make the next Olympics shine."

True to her word, she bounced back immediately, sweeping three consecutive tournaments in Bali later that year. But the psychological turning point arrived in 2023 at the prestigious All England Open—badminton's equivalent of Wimbledon. Facing Chen Yufei yet again in a nail-biting final, An finally secured victory, becoming the first Korean women's singles player to win the tournament in 27 years. Afterward, she revealed the secret to her breakthrough: she had simply stopped obsessing over winning and started enjoying the flow of the game.

That mental shift propelled her past Japan's Akane Yamaguchi to the world number 1 ranking in July 2023—just five years and five months after starting her senior career at rank 1335. Soon after, she captured the World Championships title in Copenhagen, cementing her status as the queen of the sport.

The Hangzhou Drama and the Fight for Athlete Rights

The crowning achievement of her career nearly cost her body entirely. At the 2023 Hangzhou Asian Games, playing the singles final against Chen Yufei, An's right patellar tendon tore mid-match. Writhing in pain and heavily taped, she dragged her shattered leg through the final game to secure a historic gold medal for South Korea.

Subsequent medical scans revealed a complete tendon rupture. Yet, the victory was followed by an even bigger shockwave: at the 2024 Paris Olympics, where she captured South Korea's first Olympic singles gold in 28 years by defeating China's He Bingjiao, An used her post-match press conference to openly criticize the national team’s outdated injury management and training systems.

Her blunt public critique sparked a massive national scandal. It triggered a thorough investigation by South Korea's Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, resulting in 26 mandatory institutional reforms aimed at giving national athletes the basic right to choose their own medical treatment and rehabilitative care—a legacy arguably as important as her gold medals.

Staring Down History: The Undisputed Reign of 2026

Far from slowing down, An's recovery only hardened her dominance. By 2025, she was posting a staggering 73-4 record (a 94.8% win rate), surpassing $1 million in seasonal prize money and securing back-to-back All England titles.

By late 2026, sitting comfortably past her 100th consecutive week as world number 1, An added another historic milestone by defending her singles crown at the Aichi-Nagoya Asian Games, defeating Yamaguchi yet again. In a sport known for burning out its prodigies by their early twenties, An Se-young has engineered a masterclass in longevity, sports science, and sheer psychological grit.

"Well done, Se-young. Let's breathe a little now." — An Se-young, reflecting on the grueling road that turned her from a crying child in a Gwangju gym into badminton's peerless monarch.

What to Watch Next: As the international BWF World Tour rolls toward its season finale, watch how An manages her workload and preventative physical therapies. Following the institutional reforms she forced through in Korean sports, her physical health remains the only variable capable of slowing down an otherwise unstoppable athletic machine.
