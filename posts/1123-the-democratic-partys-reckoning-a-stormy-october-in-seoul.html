The Democratic Party’s Reckoning: A Stormy October in Seoul

October 14, 2026

The corridors of the National Assembly have been echoing with more than just policy debates lately. October 13th marked a particularly volatile day in South Korean politics, where the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the liberal party currently holding the presidency under Lee Jae-myung—found itself mired in a multi-front conflict. From high-decibel shouting matches during state audits to internal soul-searching over sagging approval ratings, the political landscape is currently defined by a sense of urgency and deep-seated friction.

The Battleground of the National Audit

The most intense theater of conflict has been the state audit, a crucial annual period where lawmakers review government administration. Recent sessions have devolved into vitriolic exchanges. A notable flashpoint occurred between Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the DPK’s Supreme Council, and Joo Jin-woo of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party. After Joo unleashed a series of harsh public critiques, Kim retorted that he had “never seen anyone enjoy extremist verbal sparring and have a long political career.”

The situation has escalated beyond mere name-calling; the PPP is now threatening legal action, and the DPK is counter-maneuvering by proposing reforms to the National Assembly Act. The ruling party is considering legislation that would allow for the suspension of parliamentary activities for members who engage in excessive verbal abuse. While the DPK frames this as a necessary measure to restore decorum, critics argue it risks deepening the divide between the ruling and opposition blocs.

The rhetoric has become so inflammatory that Han Byung-do, the DPK’s floor leader, publicly likened the behavior of conservative lawmakers to “cyber wreckers”—a Korean term for disparaging social media influencers who chase clicks through scandal-mongering. This collapse of basic political etiquette has led many within the assembly to worry that the legislative body is losing its function as a space for governance, becoming instead a stage for performative outrage.

Pressure on the Judiciary

Simultaneously, the DPK has turned its sights on the judiciary, demanding the resignation of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The party has raised allegations regarding the "improper payment of cash" under the guise of incentive bonuses for Supreme Court justices, accusing the Chief Justice of issuing dishonest explanations.

The DPK’s leadership has made this a central campaign, utilizing visual aids and presentations during meetings to keep the pressure high. They are supported by smaller opposition factions, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive party that often aligns with DPK stances on reform). For the ruling party, these allegations are not merely administrative errors; they are presented as a moral failure that threatens the integrity of the state. However, the aggressive nature of this campaign has sparked concerns that the ruling party is encroaching upon the principle of the separation of powers and "politicizing" the judicial system to advance its own reformist agenda.

Internal Alarms: The 35% Threshold

Perhaps the most significant development is the quiet, internal alarm being sounded regarding public support. Choi Min-hee, a DPK lawmaker, recently voiced a candid assessment: the party’s approval rating has plummeted to around 35%. Choi, who is part of the party's core faction, admitted that the party has lost the trust of its traditional base, including voters in their 40s.

Choi’s warning is clear: the decline cannot be blamed solely on the opposition’s attacks or negative media coverage. Instead, she pointed to a growing sentiment among supporters that the Lee Jae-myung administration has failed to meet the high expectations set during his election. For a government that rose to power on promises of systemic change, the perception that reforms are moving too slowly—or are failing to improve the daily lives of citizens—has created a tangible sense of betrayal among core voters.

Adding to the chaos is an internal investigation into reports that members of the Shincheonji Church of Jesus—a controversial religious movement—may have organized a mass enrollment in the Democratic Party. For the DPK, the prospect of external religious groups influencing party decisions or primaries is an existential threat. Figures like Rep. Jeong Chung-rae have called for a thorough investigation, noting that any external subversion of the party’s democratic process must be met with severe consequences.

The Path Forward: Policy as the Only Remedy

Despite the toxic atmosphere of the audit sessions, there is a push for substantive legislative work. Lawmakers like Lee Hun-ki are spearheading efforts to regulate platform companies, specifically targeting "abductive advertising" tactics used by major e-commerce giants like Coupang. Such efforts to protect consumer rights are critical; the DPK knows that its path to regaining public support lies in delivering visible, concrete improvements to the economy, not in winning shouting matches.

Other initiatives, such as the "Special Act on Farmland"—which seeks to protect non-speculative farmers while punishing real estate speculators—and regional development projects, remain the party's primary tools for rebuilding credibility. The administration must also navigate complex issues like technology theft prevention and diplomatic strategy, all while managing the fallout from controversies like the President’s recent golfing activities, which have been criticized as ill-timed given the current security climate.

What to Watch Next

  • The "Civility" Legislation: Will the DPK actually move to amend the National Assembly Act to punish "verbal abuse," and how will the PPP respond?
  • Judicial Reform Standoff: Watch for the next move from the Supreme Court regarding the incentive bonus scandal. The DPK’s ability to prove wrongdoing will determine if this is seen as legitimate oversight or political overreach.
  • Approval Rating Trends: Whether the 35% floor holds or continues to slide will dictate how much political capital the Lee Jae-myung administration spends on aggressive reform versus moderate, consensus-based policy.
  • Internal Housekeeping: The results of the probe into Shincheonji’s alleged infiltration will be a litmus test for the DPK’s internal security and disciplinary strength.

As the noise of the National Assembly continues to dominate the headlines, the ultimate question for the DPK is whether it can pivot from fighting the opposition to governing for the public. In a political climate that feels increasingly fragile, the party’s survival may depend less on the loudness of its protests and more on the tangible results of its policies.
