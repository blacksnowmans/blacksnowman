Inside the Ruling Party's Crisis Over Alleged Sect Infiltration

October 27, 2026

South Korea’s progressive ruling party is grappling with an acute internal crisis, and the fault lines are running right through the top leadership. At the center of the storm is an explosive, politically sensitive investigation into alleged links between party members and Shincheonji, a controversial religious movement that has long played a volatile role in South Korean politics. For a party that rose to power promising radical transparency, the leadership's instinct to manage the fallout behind closed doors has triggered a furious backlash from within its own ranks.

For observers outside South Korea, political party disputes here often look like opaque theater. But this standoff matters because it directly tests the governing style of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. When a ruling party begins fighting over transparency, cover-ups, and internal accountability, it signals deeper anxieties about governance, factional loyalty, and whether the party can govern without reverting to the backroom politics it was elected to replace.

How a Quiet Inquiry Exploded Into Public View

The controversy broke wide open when senior party figures publicly contradicted each other over how the Shincheonji investigation would be handled. The leadership announced that the findings of the internal inquiry would be presented during a private general meeting of lawmakers slated for November. The official line was straightforward: contain a sensitive issue and prevent the conservative opposition from weaponizing it.

Yet that strategy instantly collapsed when lawmaker Choi Min-hee publicly and flatly denied that any such agreement on a closed-door meeting had actually been reached. For party supporters, this kind of public disunity is deeply frustrating. When senior lawmakers are openly bickering over basic procedural agreements, it raises hard questions about how the ruling camp expects to push through major legislative priorities like judicial and media reforms.

Worse still, the decision to keep the findings under wraps has invited toxic comparisons. Critics inside and outside the party argue that hiding behind closed doors looks uncomfortably like the opaque administration style seen during the scandal-plagued tenure of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol. For a political movement that mobilized millions by demanding accountability from past conservative governments, choosing secrecy over sunlight is proving to be a political liability.

A Clash at the Top and 10-Minute Briefings

The internal rebellion soon moved from whispered complaints to direct confrontation. Supreme Council Member Han Min-soo launched a scathing attack directly in front of party leadership heavyweights, including Senior Supreme Member Kim Min-seok. Han challenged the leadership head-on, demanding to know if the initial claims linking the party to the religious sect were manufactured purely for factional gain during internal leadership races.

The frustration boiled over when details of the investigative process leaked. Han revealed that the inquiry—which had consumed months of political oxygen—was slated to be wrapped up with a brief, unsupported ten-minute presentation in a private room. To critics, attempting to resolve a sprawling controversy with ten minutes of verbal claims is an insult to the party rank and file.

Han’s outburst was not merely a personal grievance; it reflected a wider fear that critical party decisions are being made by a tiny, unaccountable circle rather than through proper institutional channels. If the allegations are baseless, critics argue they should be dismissed with proof; if true, those responsible must face consequences. The refusal to lay out comprehensive evidence has only fueled suspicions that there is something to hide.

The Dilemma Facing Leadership

As the pressure mounts on Senior Supreme Member Kim Min-seok to take responsibility, the leadership finds itself trapped in a classic political trap. Delaying the report until November was ostensibly meant to buy time and cool tempers, but it has achieved the exact opposite. By postponing transparency, the leadership has effectively created a vacuum that is rapidly filling with rumors, speculation, and damaging leaks.

Factional lines are hardening. Lawmakers aligned with figures like lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae are demanding a swift, principled reckoning, arguing that sweeping the issue under the rug will permanently stain the party's moral authority. On the other side, some worry that escalating the fight will only hand ammunition to the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party, which is eager to paint the ruling camp as hypocritical.

Yet history offers a warning. South Korea’s progressive movements have repeatedly fractured over internal factional strife when they fail to police their own ethics. Allowing a religious controversy to fester into a full-scale civil war risks derailing the broader legislative agenda of the Lee Jae-myung government at a time when public attention should be firmly fixed on economic stability and livelihood issues.

What to Watch Next

As this internal battle plays out, several concrete milestones and indicators will determine whether the ruling party can regain control of the narrative:

    The November General Meeting: Watch whether the leadership actually holds the private lawmakers' meeting as scheduled, or if mounting pressure forces them to release the investigative findings publicly beforehand. Disciplinary Actions: Look for whether the party takes formal action against either those who allegedly stoked false claims or those implicated in the sect's reported activities. Real accountability would mean expulsions or official sanctions, not just verbal warnings. Factional Realignment: Monitor statements from key internal heavyweights like Kim Min-seok and Jung Cheong-rae. If the pro-Lee and reformist factions fail to reach a consensus, the rift could spill over into broader legislative gridlock in the National Assembly.

For now, the ruling party's most urgent task is simple: choose between the comfort of a closed door and the risk of total transparency. In South Korean politics, covering up an embarrassing internal mess almost always costs more than cleaning it out in the open.
