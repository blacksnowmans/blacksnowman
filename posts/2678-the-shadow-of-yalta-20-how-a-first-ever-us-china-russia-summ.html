The Shadow of Yalta 2.0: How a First-Ever US-China-Russia Summit Threatens Korean Security

November 24, 2026

For decades, South Koreans have lived with the anxious reality that the fate of the peninsula can sometimes feel like a secondhand discussion held in rooms far away. That long-standing fear has roared back to life following a diplomatic development that has gripped international observers: the very first trilateral summit bringing together the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia. On paper, the meeting was framed as an exercise in managing global friction. In reality, it has all the hallmarks of a classic great-power carving session—a high-stakes game where dominant nations divvy up the global order while smaller states are left waiting outside the door.

To foreign observers, the most alarming takeaway is not just the grand theater of great-power alignment, but what was whispered—and reportedly considered—behind closed doors. Reports indicating that Donald Trump weighed shelving demands for total North Korean denuclearization in exchange for a swift push toward a Korean War-ending peace declaration have sent tremors through Seoul's security establishment. As the world watches these heavyweights maneuver, the specter of a "second Yalta Conference"—where major powers draw spheres of influence over the heads of those most affected—is no longer a theoretical ghost story. It is a very present danger.

The First-Ever US-China-Russia Summit and the Ghost of Yalta

When the leaders of Washington, Beijing, and Moscow sit down at the same table for the first time in history, the geopolitical tectonic plates naturally shift. Yet, rather than a genuine breakthrough for global peace, this summit bore all the traits of a mutual grievance-clearing session. The optics alone told a story: while South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung—representing the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, the current ruling party—sat near the American delegation to underscore the enduring strength of the US-South Korea alliance, Russian President Vladimir Putin was seated directly beside Peng Liyuan, the wife of Chinese President Xi Jinping. The seating arrangement was a vivid, unspoken snapshot of tightening Sino-Russian cohesion.

Xi used the trilateral platform to champion a multipolar world order designed to check American hegemony, while Putin floated the idea of hosting a second trilateral summit, even dangling the prospect of inviting North Korean leader Kim Jong Un as an observer. For Moscow, pulling Pyongyang firmly into its orbit and maximizing its own leverage in Northeast Asia is a clear strategic play. Meanwhile, Trump's transactional approach to foreign policy treats North Korea's nuclear arsenal not as an existential security crisis, but as a potential marquee achievement for his own resume. By dangling the possibility of a liaison office and a formal end-of-war declaration without insisting on upfront denuclearization, Trump risks offering a de facto endorsement of North Korea as a nuclear-armed state.

For South Korea, this is a nightmare scenario. The foundational pillar of regional security is the complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula. The moment that core principle becomes a negotiable bargaining chip, the architecture of peace begins to crumble.

Pyongyang's Multilateral Pivot and the Myth of 'Non-Expansion'

While the superpowers barter, North Korea is executing a sophisticated diplomatic pivot of its own. In a rare move, Kim Jong Un recently greeted foreign ambassadors directly—his first such direct reception in roughly three decades—while proclaiming that Pyongyang "seeks neither hegemony nor expansion."

To an uninitiated observer, the phrase sounds surprisingly moderate. In reality, it is a high-level psychological maneuver. By declaring that North Korea has no intention of attacking others, Kim is attempting to frame his nuclear arsenal as purely defensive, thereby shifting the narrative toward getting international sanctions lifted while preserving his nuclear status quo. State media outlets have heavily publicized the event, signaling to Washington that Pyongyang no longer relies solely on bilateral negotiations with the U.S. Instead, North Korea is actively strengthening its ties with a bloc of anti-Western and friendly nations.

This calculated charm offensive is mirrored by Pyongyang's revival of historic rhetoric, such as a prominent nod to a 73-year-old pact with China celebrating "immortal friendship." With Beijing needing a strategic buffer against the U.S. and Moscow seeking partners amid its war in Ukraine, the traditional socialist alliance is tightening. For the progressive administration of President Lee, this multipolar hedging by Pyongyang complicates enforcement of sanctions and narrows the window for traditional diplomacy. Kim's ultimate goal is not disarmament; it is international acceptance as a legitimate nuclear state.

Trump's Bold Play and the Collapse of Bilateral Talks

The temptation for unconventional diplomacy runs deep in Washington's transactional wing. Reports indicate that plans prepared for a potential return of Trump included offering a formal Korean War-ending peace declaration and a U.S. liaison office in Pyongyang, while shelving the strict demand for "Complete, Verifiable, and Irreversible Dismantlement" (CVID) of North Korea's nuclear program. This represents a staggering departure from traditional American non-proliferation policy.

Such proposals stem from a desire for quick, headline-grabbing diplomatic wins. But they are laced with poison. A peace declaration enacted while North Korea retains its nuclear capabilities risks creating a catastrophic security vacuum. Without a concurrent disarmament framework, a political peace declaration could quickly fuel debates over the status of U.S. forces stationed in South Korea and the United Nations Command, leaving Seoul exposed while Pyongyang enjoys unearned diplomatic absolution.

Intriguingly, Pyongyang has responded with cold indifference. Rather than jumping at Trump's overtures, Kim Jong Un has signaled that the threshold for dialogue has risen dramatically. Backed by renewed economic and military support from Russia and China, North Korea now believes its leverage is at an all-time high. It is no longer asking for minor concessions like a liaison office; it wants total sanctions relief and arms-control negotiations conducted on equal footing as a recognized nuclear power.

Navigating a Rapidly Shifting Northeast Asian Landscape

As great powers maneuver for advantage, South Korea faces a punishing strategic squeeze. The risk of being sidelined—the dreaded "Korea Passing" phenomenon—is acute when Washington, Beijing, and Moscow negotiate over regional stability without meaningful input from Seoul.

President Lee's administration faces a formidable balancing act. On one hand, the ironclad alliance with the United States remains non-negotiable. On the other, Seoul must deploy nimble, multi-directional diplomacy—evidenced by creative soft-power overtures like proposing cultural and informal engagements—to carve out maneuvering room and prevent regional tensions from boiling over.

The bottom line for South Korea is clear: security cannot be outsourced to the transactional whims of foreign leaders, nor can the pursuit of peace be allowed to eclipse the absolute necessity of denuclearization. As the great powers redraw their maps, Seoul must combine unwavering firmness on its core security principles with pragmatic, clear-eyed diplomacy.

What to Watch Next

  • The US Political Landscape: Monitor how potential shifts in Washington's foreign policy leadership translate rhetoric on "peace declarations" into concrete diplomatic initiatives toward Pyongyang.
  • Sino-Russian-North Korean Trilateral Dynamics: Watch for any concrete moves toward Putin's proposed second trilateral summit, specifically whether Kim Jong Un participates as an observer to formalize a new regional bloc.
  • Seoul's Diplomatic Counter-Moves: Observe how President Lee's administration coordinates with Washington while simultaneously managing channels with Beijing and Moscow to prevent diplomatic isolation.
  • Pyongyang's Enforcement of Sanctions Evasion: Keep an eye on trade and military cooperation between North Korea and Russia, which serves as the primary financial and diplomatic oxygen keeping Pyongyang's nuclear posture defiant.
