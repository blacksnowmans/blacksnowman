An Unprecedented Constitutional Clash: South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach the Chief Justice

November 22, 2026

On November 21, the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly in Seoul witnessed a sudden and sharp escalation in rhetoric that pushed the country’s political crisis into uncharted territory. Within hours, parliamentary debates descended into accusations of an "assembly coup," warnings of "political suicide bombs," and accusations that the ruling party was acting like an "insurrectionist organization."

At the center of this firestorm is Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, the head of South Korea’s Supreme Court. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has rapidly built a party-wide consensus to introduce a formal impeachment motion against Chief Justice Jo, targeting December 3 as the launch date. In response, the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP), has declared an all-out political war, accusing the ruling party of mounting an existential attack on the independence of the judiciary.

For international observers watching South Korea's political landscape under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, this clash represents far more than routine partisan fighting. It is a direct institutional collision between the legislative majority and the nation's highest court, threatening to trigger complete gridlock in the National Assembly right as vital year-end legislation and budget negotiations loom.

The December 3 Target: Inside the Ruling Party’s Push to Oust the Chief Justice

Within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, momentum has gathered quickly around December 3 as the symbolic date to submit the impeachment motion against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae. Party leaders and rank-and-file members argue that recent decisions by the Supreme Court represent an unacceptable abuse of judicial authority, accusing the high court of issuing rulings that effectively "exonerate" figures involved in ongoing political and legal controversies.

From the perspective of DPK leadership, moving swiftly before the end of the year is essential to assert parliamentary accountability over a judiciary they view as out of step with constitutional oversight. Key party hardliners have openly demanded an immediate, unhesitating offensive. Representative Choo Mi-ae, a prominent figure within the ruling party, publicly urged her colleagues to stop calculating political gains and losses and execute the impeachment without delay, warning that hesitation would risk losing vital political momentum.

This aggressive posture is mirrored across the broader liberal camp. Representative Shin Jang-sik of the minor liberal opposition Cho Kuk Innovation Party echoed the call for speed, urging the coalition to push the impeachment motion through immediately over the weekend. The prevailing sentiment among party loyalists is that once a judicial decision is perceived as politically biased or flawed, parliament has a duty to act immediately.

However, this high-speed push has revealed notable legal complexities. While much of the initial political anger stemmed from controversial court decisions, including those surrounding former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, reports indicate that the specific grounds related to the Han Duck-soo ruling were actually omitted from the draft impeachment motion currently being crafted by DPK strategists. This discrepancy has raised critical questions about what specific, legally verifiable misconduct will be cited to justify the removal of the head of the judicial branch.

The Conservative Counteroffensive: Charges of an 'Insurrection Party' and Political Dictatorship

The main opposition People Power Party has responded with intense hostility, characterizing the DPK's initiative as a lawless attack designed to cow the nation’s judiciary into submission. PPP leader Han Dong-hoon launched a fierce direct attack against the ruling party’s leadership, framing the move against Chief Justice Jo as an explicit assault on the constitutional order.

"The Democratic Party’s attempt to impeach the Chief Justice is nothing less than a self-confession of insurrection," Han Dong-hoon declared on November 21. "The Democratic Party has now become a party of insurrection. Do not back down—go ahead and introduce the impeachment on December 3. In the end, everyone involved will have to go to prison."

Han’s sharp rhetoric reflects a broader strategy by conservative leadership to frame the ruling party's actions as a parliamentary tyranny that undermines the separation of powers. Conservative lawmakers across the board quickly joined the fray with equally scathing language:

  • Representative Jung Jum-sik called the impeachment attempt "the worst, lowest-grade impeachment in constitutional history" and branded it an "assembly coup" designed to destroy the judicial branch.
  • Representative Jang Dong-hyeok warned the ruling party that pursuing the Chief Justice would ultimately act as a "suicide bomb" for the DPK’s own political standing.
  • Representative Kim Eun-hye asserted that the political backlash would end up engulfing the ruling camp itself long before any judicial removal could take effect.

For the conservative opposition, the fact that the DPK floated an impeachment motion within twenty-four hours of an unfavorable court outcome demonstrates a retaliatory motive. They argue that the ruling party is using its legislative majority to carry out a collective assault on individual judges, eroding the foundational principle of judicial neutrality enshrined in Article 1 of the South Korean Constitution.

The Legal Community Strikes Back: Warnings of a Parliamentary 'Super-Appellate Court'

As political warfare intensifies inside the National Assembly, South Korea's legal establishment has voiced deep concern. Major bar associations and legal analyst groups released official statements calling for an immediate halt to the impeachment proceedings against Chief Justice Jo.

The core of the legal community's objection lies in the dangerous precedent such an impeachment would set for the rule of law. Representatives from legal organizations pointed out that using legislative power to remove a judge over dissatisfaction with court rulings effectively turns the National Assembly into a political super-appellate court sitting above the Supreme Court itself.

Core Objections Raised by the Legal Community:

  • Erosion of Judicial Independence: Subjecting judges to political removal based on the outcome of specific rulings creates a chilling effect across the entire judiciary.
  • Subversion of Appeals Procedures: Disagreeing with a court decision should be addressed through established legal appeals, not through legislative sanctions against the judge.
  • Misuse of Constitutional Remedies: Impeachment was designed as an extraordinary remedy for severe, proven illegalities, not as a routine tool for political retaliation.

Legal experts emphasize that if judges face the threat of impeachment whenever their decisions anger the ruling majority, the independence of individual rulings will be severely compromised. Judges might feel compelled to weigh political consequences rather than strict legal precedent when issuing verdicts in high-profile cases. Consequently, the legal profession is warning that converting impeachment into a weapon of political pressure threatens the structural integrity of South Korea’s constitutional democracy.

What Lies Ahead: Year-End Paralysis and the Future of South Korea’s Judiciary

Whether or not the Democratic Party follows through on its December 3 target to formally table the impeachment motion, the conflict has already inflicted lasting damage on institutional relations in Seoul. The willingness of both sides to employ extreme rhetoric underscores how narrow the middle ground has become in South Korean politics.

In the immediate term, several key developments will determine the direction of this crisis over the coming weeks:

  • The December 3 Motion: The primary issue to monitor is whether the DPK leadership formally introduces the impeachment motion on December 3, and whether they can maintain internal consensus on the exact legal grounds cited in the filing.
  • Year-End Legislative Gridlock: The timing of this judicial battle directly overlaps with mandatory parliamentary deadlines for the annual national budget and major policy bills. An all-out battle over the Chief Justice makes bipartisan compromise on economic and budget issues nearly impossible.
  • Opposition Mobilization: The conservative People Power Party is likely to utilize parliamentary tactics, including filibusters and public rallies, to protest what they view as a parliamentary coup, potentially paralyzing committee hearings through the end of the year.
  • Institutional Fallout in the Courts: The Supreme Court faces a prolonged period of political uncertainty. Even if an impeachment motion fails to pass or is eventually rejected by the Constitutional Court, the political campaign against Chief Justice Jo damages public trust in the judiciary.

The battle over Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae has evolved far beyond a dispute over individual court rulings. It has become a fundamental test of how South Korea balances legislative power against judicial independence. As the December deadline approaches, the confrontation threatens to drag the National Assembly into total paralysis, leaving little room for constitutional compromise.
