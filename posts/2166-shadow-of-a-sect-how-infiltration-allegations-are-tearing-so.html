Shadow of a Sect: How Infiltration Allegations Are Tearing South Korea’s Ruling Party Apart

November 10, 2026

Inside the headquarters of the Democratic Party of Korea, the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, what began as a routine review of party member rolls has erupted into a ferocious ideological and factional civil war. Heated leadership meetings have dissolved into bitter recriminations, with senior figures invoking the darkest chapters of South Korea’s modern history to accuse their own colleagues of orchestrating an unprincipled cover-up. At the heart of the storm is Shincheonji, the controversial religious movement whose alleged covert infiltration of mainstream political parties has long haunted South Korean public life.

The immediate trigger for the crisis is an internal investigation conducted by the party’s Member Roster Clean-up Task Force. Far from settling simmering rumors about mass registrations by sect members, the task force's findings have ignited an explosive confrontation. Lawmakers who viewed the records have arrived at irreconcilable conclusions: senior party veterans argue that the data confirms systematic, coordinated infiltration, while the leadership insists the evidence is inconclusive. With the conservative opposition seizing the opportunity to demand an independent special counsel, the ruling party finds itself caught between an uncontrollable internal revolt and a damaging external offensive.

A Roster Kept Behind Closed Doors: The Task Force Fallout

To understand the panic, an outside observer must understand the political weight of Shincheonji. Formally known as the Shincheonji Church of Jesus the Temple of the Tabernacle of the Testimony, the group is widely viewed by mainstream South Korean society and established Christian denominations as a fringe sect. Outside South Korea, the group became famous in early 2020 as a primary super-spreader during the initial COVID-19 outbreak in Daegu. Domestically, however, its notoriety stems from aggressive proselytizing tactics and allegations that its disciplined followers systematically infiltrate established institutions—including churches, local councils, and political parties—to influence policy and protect sect interests.

In South Korea’s primary election system, registered party members wield decisive influence over candidate nominations. A coordinated voting bloc numbering in the thousands can easily tip a tight internal primary in favor of an allied candidate. For months, whispers swirled that Shincheonji followers had registered en masse as dues-paying members of the Democratic Party.

In response, the party leadership established a dedicated Member Roster Clean-up Task Force to cross-reference membership rolls and determine whether organized infiltration had occurred. However, the leadership's decision to restrict access to the resulting report—allowing lawmakers to inspect the records only on an individual, private basis rather than making the findings public—backfired immediately. Rather than reassuring lawmakers, the non-disclosure policy provoked furious accusations that the leadership was attempting to suppress uncomfortable facts.

Individual lawmakers who viewed the materials emerged with completely contradictory narratives. One camp, led by veteran figures, claimed that the membership patterns showed undeniable, coordinated influxes from specific regional hubs associated with the sect. Conversely, party leadership figures argued that the data did not provide sufficient legal or factual proof to establish deliberate, organized collusion. This fundamental divergence over a single set of documents turned an administrative cleanup into an ideological battleground.

Coup Analogies and Factional Feuds: Lee Sung-yoon vs. Kim Min-seok

The internal frustration reached a boiling point when Lee Sung-yoon, a member of the Democratic Party's Supreme Council, launched a blistering personal assault on party representative Kim Min-seok. In South Korean progressive politics, there are few rhetorical insults more devastating than invoking military dictator Chun Doo-hwan, the general who seized power in a bloody 1979 coup and ruled through brutal authoritarian repression in the 1980s.

Directly confronting Kim, Lee evoked the notorious legal argument once used in South Korea during the 1990s that "a successful coup cannot be punished by law." Turning that logic against the party leadership, Lee asked whether Kim believed the Shincheonji controversy was exempt from accountability simply because it represented a "successful political operation."

"They once claimed that Chun Doo-hwan could not be punished because his coup was successful. Does Representative Kim Min-seok believe that his own Shincheonji controversy is acceptable because it was a successful political scheme?"

For a sitting Supreme Council member to compare his own party leadership to an authoritarian military junta reflects an astonishing breakdown in party discipline. While Kim Min-seok has offered public apologies on three separate occasions in an attempt to de-escalate the controversy, the concessions have done little to pacify critics. Factions aligned against the current leadership argue that repeated apologies without full disclosure and radical purges are merely an attempt to weather the media cycle. The dispute is no longer just about religious infiltration; it has transformed into a high-stakes proxy war over the legitimacy and moral authority of the party’s top decision-makers.

Systematic Infiltration or Overblown Fear? Song Young-gil Demands Emergency Sanctions

Adding substantial weight to the rebel camp is Song Young-gil, an influential party heavyweight and former party leader. After examining the task force's investigation files, Song broke decisively with the leadership's cautious line. He publicly asserted that the files provide concrete evidence of organized, systematic party infiltration designed to manipulate internal decision-making processes.

Characterizing the influx as an existential assault on party democracy, Song laid out an aggressive, uncompromising roadmap for the leadership:

  • A comprehensive party-wide audit: Moving beyond sample surveys to conduct a thorough, forensic review of the entire national membership base.
  • Invocation of emergency disciplinary powers: Utilizing the leadership's extraordinary constitutional authority within the party to immediately strip identified sect members of their membership rights without protracted bureaucratic delays.
  • Legislative reform: Proposing immediate amendments to South Korea’s Political Parties Act and internal party charters to establish explicit legal mechanisms preventing religious cults from orchestrating mass registrations.

Song’s demands highlight the dangerous trap facing the ruling party. If the leadership accedes to a full-scale audit and mass expulsions, it risks validating the opposition's claims that the party has indeed been compromised by a controversial religious group. But if the leadership continues to urge restraint and maintains confidentiality, it will face relentless accusations of cowardice and complicity from within its own ranks.

The Conservative Counter-Offensive: The People Power Party Demands a Special Counsel

While the ruling party fractures from within, South Korea’s conservative opposition has seized the moment to launch a punishing political counter-offensive. The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition, has transformed the Democratic Party’s internal disarray into a central national issue.

Leading the charge is conservative lawmaker Jeong Jeom-sik, who has framed the Democratic Party’s closed-door handling of the report as an outright cover-up. Jeong and the PPP leadership have formally called for an independent special prosecutor (*teukgeom*) to carry out a compulsory criminal investigation into what they characterize as a "secret alliance" between the ruling party and Shincheonji.

By branding the Democratic Party’s private review process as a "smoke-filled room cover-up," the conservative opposition has established an aggressive narrative. If the Democratic Party refuses a special prosecutor, the PPP argues, it is an implicit admission of guilt. If the party accepts, it opens its internal databases, communication records, and nomination processes to prosecutorial scrutiny. For an administration under President Lee Jae-myung that needs legislative stability to advance its broader economic and judicial reform agenda, this escalating religious scandal threatens to drain critical political capital.

What to Watch Next: Transparency, Discipline, or an Escalating Crisis?

The Democratic Party now stands at an uncomfortable crossroads where half-measures are no longer viable. As the controversy deepens, several critical developments will signal whether the ruling party can regain control of the narrative or succumb to deeper factional paralysis.

First, watch whether party representative Kim Min-seok yields to mounting internal pressure to make the task force's full report public. Continued refusal will only fuel suspicions among the party's own lawmakers and provide ongoing ammunition to the opposition. A shift toward full transparency, however, will require the leadership to articulate an unambiguous standard for what constitutes improper religious mobilization versus legitimate political participation by ordinary citizens.

Second, observe whether the party leadership initiates formal disciplinary proceedings or exercises its emergency disciplinary powers against suspected infiltration networks. Failure to act against individuals flagged in the review will alienate senior figures like Song Young-gil and deepen factional rifts ahead of upcoming legislative battles.

Finally, monitor how the National Assembly handles the People Power Party’s push for a special prosecutor. If the opposition succeeds in galvanizing public demand for a compulsory judicial inquiry, the ruling party could find itself subject to an external criminal investigation of its own internal machinery—a scenario that would cast a long, paralyzing shadow over President Lee Jae-myung’s governing agenda.
