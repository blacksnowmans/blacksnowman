The Crack in the Foundation: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Turning on Itself

November 14, 2026

Imagine the inner sanctum of a political party that has recently ascended to the heights of power. The halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul’s political heart, should be buzzing with the business of governance—passing budgets, refining social safety nets, and solidifying the mandate of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Instead, the air is thick with the scent of a brewing civil war. On November 13, the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party and current ruling power, saw its internal tensions boil over into a public spectacle of boycotts, leaked recordings, and demands for expulsion.

For an outside observer, this might look like standard political bickering. But in the context of South Korean politics, where party discipline is usually paramount and the memory of the previous administration’s collapse is fresh, this is an existential crisis. The "Pro-Myung" faction—those loyal to President Lee Jae-myung—and the "Pro-Chung" faction—supporters of Jung Chung-rae, a powerful and outspoken senior member of the party—are no longer just disagreeing; they are engaged in a full-scale battle for the soul and the future leadership of the party. At the center of this firestorm is Choi Min-hee, a member of the party’s Supreme Council, whose leaked private comments have acted as the match in a powder keg.

The Recording that Ripped the Veil

The current crisis was ignited by the release of a private recording from a meeting between Rep. Choi Min-hee and party members. Choi is a member of the Supreme Council, the highest decision-making body of the Democratic Party. In the recording, she reportedly made several incendiary claims that have since dominated the news cycle on major networks like JTBC and KBS. Specifically, she discussed the history of the movement to impeach Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was removed from office and is currently imprisoned. Choi claimed that President Lee Jae-myung had been consistent in his push for impeachment, but she also alleged that the President had once sought to cooperate with Han Duck-soo, who served as Prime Minister during the transition, to induce Mr. Yoon's resignation.

To a foreign reader, this might seem like ancient history, but in Korea, the narrative of the "Candlelight Revolution" and the subsequent impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is the bedrock of the current government’s legitimacy. Any suggestion that there was backroom dealing or inconsistency in the President’s stance is political dynamite. It threatens to alienate the hardcore base that views the impeachment as a pure, uncompromising moral victory.

However, the fallout didn't stop at historical revisionism. Choi’s private comments reportedly included sharp, personal attacks on her own colleagues. She allegedly referred to certain party members as "opportunists," called another a "yes-man," and even ventured into conspiracy territory by claiming that a specific religious group, the Unification Church, was backing certain internal rivals. This breach of decorum has shattered the thin veneer of unity within the party leadership. When a high-ranking official disparages their peers in such visceral terms, it signals a breakdown in the trust required to run a government.

The controversy further spiraled when Choi turned her ire toward the press, reportedly labeling journalists as "hyaenas." This prompted a formal petition to the National Human Rights Commission of Korea (NHRCK), with media organizations arguing that such rhetoric infringes on the dignity of journalists and the freedom of the press. For a ruling party that prides itself on being the champion of democratic values and human rights, having a top leader accused of dehumanizing the media is a significant PR disaster.

A House Divided: The Battle of Factions

The reaction to Choi’s comments was swift and revealed the deep structural rifts within the Democratic Party. Kim Min-seok, another heavyweight on the Supreme Council, took the unprecedented step of ordering an official ethical investigation into Choi. In the hierarchical world of Korean politics, a public move by one Supreme Council member against another is a declaration of war.

This move immediately polarized the party into two camps: the "Pro-Myung" (loyal to President Lee) and the "Pro-Chung" (loyal to Jung Chung-rae). The Pro-Chung faction, which views Choi as an ally, responded by boycotting leadership meetings. They argued that the investigation was a "targeted hit" designed to purge a specific faction. They didn't just defend Choi; they went on the offensive, claiming that Kim Min-seok himself should be the one under investigation.

To understand why this matters, one must understand the "Pro-Chung" faction. Jung Chung-rae is a firebrand politician known for his massive influence over the party’s most active online supporters, often called the "Reform Daughters." These are the grassroots activists who propelled Lee Jae-myung to the presidency. If the Pro-Chung faction feels sidelined, it risks alienating the very base that keeps the party’s engine running. By labeling the ethical probe as a "political tool," they have effectively paralyzed the party's executive functions. The Supreme Council, which should be coordinating national policy with the President’s Blue House (or the current presidential office), is instead bickering over who should be fired.

"The fact that the party's top decision-making body has ceased to function because of factional emotionalism is a dangerous sign for a ruling party. They have taken the leadership’s ability to govern hostage for the sake of internal power struggles."

This internal friction has reached a point where some analysts are using the term "split-level" crisis. It is no longer about ideology; it is about who will hold the most influence when the party eventually looks toward the next election cycle. In the Korean political system, the ruling party must act as a shield for the President. When the shield starts hitting itself, the President’s legislative agenda becomes vulnerable to an emboldened opposition—in this case, the conservative People Power Party.

The Merger Mirage and Public Skepticism

As if the internal fighting weren't enough, a new complication has emerged: the prospect of a merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the smaller liberal faction led by Cho Kuk. Rumors of a merger have been floated by some members of the Democratic Party, likely as a way to "expand the tent" and distract from the internal chaos. However, the reaction from Cho Kuk’s camp was icy, to say the least.

The Rebuilding Korea Party issued a stinging rebuttal, stating that "discussions of a merger have not progressed even 1 millimeter." They accused the Democratic Party of using the merger talk as a "political show" to manipulate public opinion. Cho Kuk himself has been critical, pointing out the irony that the very people now calling for a merger were the ones who most vehemently opposed it during the last election cycle.

This friction highlights a broader strategic dilemma for South Korea’s liberals. While a merger would create a massive legislative bloc, it also brings together two very different types of political energy. The Rebuilding Korea Party is seen as more radical and focused on "judicial reform," while the mainstream Democratic Party must balance those urges with the practicalities of everyday governance.

Public opinion is just as divided as the politicians. A recent poll by Gallup Korea showed that among Democratic Party supporters, 44% favor a merger, while 43% oppose it. This near-perfect split suggests that there is no mandate for unification. If the leadership tries to force a merger to solve their internal problems, they may end up doubling their headaches by bringing in a whole new set of factional rivalries. For now, the merger talk appears to be a mirage—a convenient talking point that lacks any real substance or consensus.

The High Cost of Internal Strife

Why should the average person outside of Korea care about these committee meetings and leaked tapes? Because South Korea is currently navigating a precarious economic and geopolitical landscape. With high inflation and a stagnant housing market, the "bread and butter" issues for Korean citizens are reaching a boiling point. A ruling party that is too busy investigating its own members to pass meaningful economic legislation is a party that is failing its primary duty.

The timing of this crisis is particularly bad for Kim Min-seok, who is approaching his 100th day in his leadership role. Instead of celebrating a successful first term and laying out a vision for the future, he is playing referee in a mud-wrestling match. The "100-day mark" is a significant milestone in Korean politics, often seen as the end of the honeymoon period. For the Democratic Party, the honeymoon didn't just end; the house caught fire.

  • The Factions: Pro-Myung (Lee Jae-myung) vs. Pro-Chung (Jung Chung-rae).
  • The Standoff: Ethical investigation ordered against Choi; Pro-Chung faction responds with a boycott of leadership meetings.
  • The Distraction: Proposed merger with Cho Kuk’s party rejected by the smaller party as "political theater."

The danger for President Lee Jae-myung is that this infighting makes his government look amateurish. The narrative of his presidency was supposed to be one of "competence and action," a direct contrast to the perceived chaos of the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era. If the Democratic Party cannot manage its own Supreme Council, voters will begin to wonder if they can manage the national budget or the complexities of the Korea-U.S. alliance.

Furthermore, the "hyaena" comments and the subsequent Human Rights Commission petition have given the opposition People Power Party an easy target. They can now paint the ruling party as arrogant and anti-press, a label that is hard to shake once it sticks. In politics, perception is often reality, and right now, the reality looks like a party more interested in "bowl-breaking" (a Korean term for factional infighting) than in the welfare of the people.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether this is a temporary flare-up or the beginning of a permanent schism within the Democratic Party. Here are the concrete developments to monitor:

  • The Ethics Committee Ruling: If the party’s ethical arm moves to officially discipline or expel Choi Min-hee, expect the Pro-Chung faction to escalate their boycott, potentially leading to a mass resignation of leadership members.
  • The "100-Day" Address: Kim Min-seok’s upcoming speech marking his first 100 days will be a crucial test. Will he offer an olive branch to the dissenting factions, or will he double down on "cleaning up" the party?
  • Polling Trends: Keep an eye on President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings. If the public starts to blame the President for the party’s mess, he may be forced to intervene directly, which could either settle the matter or further expose the "Pro-Myung" bias.
  • The Rebuilding Korea Party’s Stance: Watch if Cho Kuk’s party begins to court the disgruntled "Pro-Chung" members. If they do, we could be seeing the early stages of a real political realignment on the left.

South Korea’s ruling party is at a crossroads. It can choose to settle its internal grievances through established channels and return to the work of governing, or it can continue down the path of factional warfare. For a nation that has seen its fair share of political upheaval, the stakes couldn't be higher. A ruling party that destroys itself from within leaves the door wide open for the very forces it fought to replace.
