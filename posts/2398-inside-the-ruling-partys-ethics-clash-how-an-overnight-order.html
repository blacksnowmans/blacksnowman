Inside the Ruling Party’s Ethics Clash: How an Overnight Order Sparked a Leadership Crisis in Seoul

November 16, 2026

On a Friday morning in mid-November, three members of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea’s top decision-making body refused to take their seats. The boycott of the Supreme Council meeting was not triggered by a legislative defeat or an opposition ambush, but by an overnight decision from their own party leader. Hours earlier, Democratic Party Leader Kim Min-seok had used his executive authority to order an ethics inspection of Senior Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee—without speaking to her once before issuing the directive.

That account, laid out publicly by fellow Supreme Council member Han Min-soo, has since become the focal point of a widening rift inside President Lee Jae-myung’s governing party. While Kim Min-seok declared on November 16 that the move was a strictly procedural matter handled independently of politics, critics—including Han Min-soo, the political commentary channel Kim Seung-hyeon’s Agenda, commentator Hong Sang-woo, and former Supreme Council member Park Gyu-hwan—have questioned both the fairness of the process and the party’s readiness to govern while consumed by internal score-settling.

For outside observers, the clash offers a revealing look at how South Korean political parties manage factional rivalry after leadership elections. Beneath a dispute over disciplinary paperwork lie deep fractures left over from the party’s August 7 national convention, mutual accusations of rule-breaking, and a newly released poll showing the ruling party dropping to a record low.

An Overnight Order and Unhealed Convention Wounds

To understand why a single disciplinary order fractured the leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the liberal ruling party backing President Lee Jae-myung—it helps to look back at how the current leadership took shape. According to Han Min-soo, the boycott by three Supreme Council members was meant to prevent an embarrassing public spectacle for party members and supporters, who would otherwise have watched leaders sit side by side the morning after one had covertly initiated an ethics probe against the other.

In his public remarks, Han invoked the Korean idiom yeokjisaji—putting oneself in another person’s shoes—to argue that ordering a probe against a senior colleague without a word of prior consultation raised serious questions about whether the Supreme Council still functions as a collective body. He also traced the tension directly back to the party’s August 7 convention, where Kim Min-seok won the leadership against a rival who captured 46 percent of support from dues-paying party members and the general public.

Han recalled an August 27 party workshop where, despite post-convention promises of unity, Kim allegedly named the former party leader six or seven times in remarks that Han perceived as humiliating. Han questioned whether the current leadership had ever once reached out to that rival camp since the convention, and criticized a criminal complaint filed against the head of a media outlet that had supported the rival—a complaint that led to a search-and-seizure operation.

Han also laid out a detailed timeline to push back against accusations involving Shincheonji, a controversial religious sect whose alleged mass sign-ups in political parties frequently trigger scandals in South Korean politics. According to Han:

  • During the convention’s joint speeches, he ignored hecklers behind him accusing him of being anti-leadership and tied to Shincheonji.
  • On August 24, a party membership cleanup task force was announced on the party leader’s orders.
  • On October 2, the head of that task force delivered a verbal briefing regarding Shincheonji members, which leaked to the press on October 13.
  • Only on October 14, after Kim Min-seok himself first raised the issue of mass enrollments on the party’s official YouTube channel, did Han bring the matter up in a leadership meeting.

Han urged the party leader to take responsibility for resolving the mess—citing the proverb that the person who tied the knot must untie it—so the ruling party can return its focus to livelihoods and legislative reform.

Record-Low Polling and the November 16 Standoff

While DPK leaders traded barbs in public, voters delivered a sobering verdict. According to a Realmeter survey conducted for the second week of November 2026, the Democratic Party fell to an all-time low of 34.6 percent support. Meanwhile, the People Power Party—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—stood at 43.0 percent, opening up a gap of more than eight percentage points.

Just as notable for the DPK was movement on its own flank. The Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller progressive-leaning party that acts as both an ally and a competitor to the DPK for reform-minded voters, climbed from 6.9 percent to 8.7 percent. Undecided or non-affiliated voters accounted for 8.8 percent, followed by the Reform Party at 1.9 percent, the Progressive Party at 0.9 percent, and other parties at 2.0 percent.

On the same day those numbers appeared, the DPK’s Ethics Tribunal—the internal disciplinary body responsible for adjudicating party misconduct—abruptly canceled its scheduled review of Choi Min-hee’s case. Speaking on Kim Seung-hyeon’s Agenda, analysts argued that the leadership’s allies had failed to logically refute Han Min-soo’s claims and only pulled back from the Monday disciplinary review out of fear of worsening public backlash following the Realmeter poll.

Party Leader Kim Min-seok, however, framed the situation in entirely different terms. In an official statement on November 16, Kim confirmed that he had ordered the Ethics Inspection Team—the party’s investigative arm—to conduct a probe, received its report, and instructed that the case be transferred to the Ethics Tribunal.

"All subsequent procedures will be carried out strictly and independently in accordance with the party constitution and regulations."

Asking all party members to respect the party’s institutional judgment on ethics matters separate from politics, Kim positioned his actions not as factional retaliation, but as standard enforcement of internal rules.

Allegations of Double Standards and Pressure on the Tribunal

Kim’s appeal to strict rules immediately invited scrutiny over whether those rules are applied evenly across factions. On Kim Seung-hyeon’s Agenda, commentators questioned Kim’s broader record since returning to frontline politics after an 18-year absence—asking whether he had transparently accounted for the General Election Task Force budget or led decisively on real estate reform and other core pledges, rather than reviving controversies over an alleged secret three-way factional alliance.

More pointedly, the broadcast presented a chart listing alleged violations of the party constitution and regulations from the August convention onward by Kim Min-seok himself and lawmakers aligned with him, explicitly naming Hwang Myeong-seon, Jeon Jin-sook, Jeon Jun-hee, and Park Seon-won. Critics argued that pushing swiftly for an ethics probe against a rival Supreme Council member while leaving allegations against the party leader and his allies unaddressed risks feeding the exact charge of hypocrisy that has long plagued major Korean parties once they hold power.

Concerns over institutional independence deepened further when groups of DPK lawmakers entered the fray. Both Kim Seung-hyeon’s Agenda and commentator Hong Sang-woo, speaking on the Hong Sang-woo Show, criticized collective statements issued by internal parliamentary groups such as Deomincho (an association of first-term DPK lawmakers) and Deominhyeok demanding severe punishment in the case.

Hong noted that the Ethics Tribunal is a nine-member independent body that includes external civilian members. Having 20 to 30 sitting lawmakers issue collective demands for harsh penalties after a case has already been referred, he argued, amounts to political pressure aimed at steering the outcome—undermining trust in the tribunal’s decisions just as surely as outside interference would damage a court of law. Critics contended that if Kim Min-seok truly wanted to protect the tribunal’s independence, he should have reined in pressure campaigns from his own party’s lawmakers.

Unwritten Rules, Precedent, and What to Watch Next

To measure how far the current clash departs from standard practice, former DPK Supreme Council member Park Gyu-hwan offered a procedural breakdown. While any rank-and-file member can file a complaint with the Ethics Tribunal, Park explained that an ex-officio inspection ordered directly by the party leader carries extraordinary weight. Historically, leaders have reserved that power for urgent, severe misconduct likely to result in heavy punishment—such as bribery, sexual misconduct, or reckless public remarks right before an election that threaten to tank the party’s vote share.

By that yardstick, ordering an inspection over a weekend, completing the investigation, reporting back to the leader, and referring the matter to a 2 p.m. Monday tribunal session was, in Park’s assessment, highly unusual. He also contrasted the current atmosphere with the tenure of former party leader Jung Chung-rae, recalling that the Ethics Tribunal once operated at such a strict distance that the party leader never received updates on its deliberations or placed a single phone call to its members.

Park highlighted a second, potentially decisive rule: the principle of "first-in, first-out." Monday’s 2 p.m. Ethics Tribunal meeting was not convened specifically for Choi Min-hee; it was a pre-scheduled session with a docket that already included an earlier disciplinary complaint filed against Party Leader Kim Min-seok himself. Because the tribunal meets infrequently and reviews multiple cases in sequence, Park noted that bypassing an existing complaint against the party leader to fast-track a case against Choi alone would break sharply with precedent.

With President Lee Jae-myung’s administration needing steady legislative backing from its ruling party, the DPK’s internal confrontation is unlikely to remain a purely procedural dispute for long. Over the coming weeks, four concrete developments will signal whether the party can contain the damage:

  • The Ethics Tribunal’s docket order: Whether the nine-member body reschedules Choi Min-hee’s review and, if so, whether it follows the first-in, first-out precedent by ruling first on the prior complaint involving Kim Min-seok.
  • Factional restraint by lawmakers: Whether Deomincho and other parliamentary blocs halt public statements demanding specific punishments while cases remain under review.
  • Leadership communication: Whether Kim Min-seok and dissenting Supreme Council members like Han Min-soo resume regular leadership meetings or remain locked in public disputes over the convention’s aftermath.
  • Polling drift toward rivals: Whether the DPK’s 34.6 percent support stabilizes or whether prolonged infighting drives more reform-oriented voters toward the Rebuilding Korea Party and the undecided column.
