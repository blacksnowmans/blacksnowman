Inside South Korea’s Judicial Crisis Over the Han Duck-soo Insurrection Ruling

November 20, 2026

Tens of thousands of pages of trial records sat before South Korea’s highest court in one of the gravest constitutional cases in the country’s modern history. Yet it took Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s Supreme Court just nine days to reach a decision: overturning a lower court ruling and remanding former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo’s insurrection case back down with instructions pointing toward partial acquittal.

For international observers, the speed of that ruling was startling enough. Inside South Korea, it set off a political and legal firestorm. According to a sharply critical Korean political commentary citing legal analysts, journalists, and lawmakers from South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party and the progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, the decision amounts to an unprecedented twist of legal doctrine designed to shield powerful figures and protect the judiciary itself. At the same time, the ruling hinges on the Supreme Court’s insistence on applying a strict legal threshold for proving insurrection intent—a standard on which both the court’s majority and dissenting opinions reportedly agreed in principle.

To understand why a single appellate procedure—known in South Korea as pagihwansong (a reversal and remand for retrial)—has escalated into a campaign to impeach the Chief Justice and dismantle the court system’s central bureaucracy, it helps to look at the legal precedents now in dispute and the high-stakes calendar looming over Seoul.

Breaking With the Chun Doo-hwan Precedent

At the heart of the legal controversy is how South Korean courts define participation in an insurrection. Under South Korea’s judicial system, a remand from the Supreme Court does not immediately end a trial, but its legal reasoning binds the lower court retrying the case. In Han Duck-soo’s appeal, commentators note that the Supreme Court did not dispute that the underlying martial law decree was unconstitutional and illegal. Rather, the bench drew a sharp line between recognizing the illegality of martial law and possessing the specific awareness and intent to commit insurrection.

In the critical commentary circulating in South Korea, lawyer Park Pan-gyu zeroed in on a striking detail from the written opinions:

"On the point of applying a strict legal doctrine regarding the purpose of insurrection, the majority opinion and the dissenting opinion are identical."

On the surface, demanding rigorous proof for the gravest crime against the state might look like textbook judicial caution. However, Park and other critics argue that the Supreme Court elevated the evidentiary bar to an impossibly high level, effectively hunting for legal pathways to classify an attempted subversion of constitutional order as something short of insurrection.

Lawyer Jeong Pil-seung underscored why this approach represents a dramatic departure from established South Korean jurisprudence by pointing to case number 96Do3376—the landmark 1990s Supreme Court ruling that convicted former military leader Chun Doo-hwan over abuses during his rule. In that foundational precedent, the Supreme Court recognized insurrection as an organized, composite enterprise. If a defendant contributed even partially to that broader scheme, the court treated the conduct as part of a single organic crime of insurrection.

According to Jeong, the Cho Hee-dae court effectively abandoned that holistic standard in Han’s case. By slicing a sequence of actions into isolated fragments—an approach critics in the commentary describe as "salami tactics"—each individual step can be downgraded from insurrection to lesser offenses such as abuse of authority or procedural violations. Jeong argued that the ruling amounts to a de facto reversal of Supreme Court precedent, eroding the consistency on which judicial authority depends.

Accusations of Self-Protection and Shifting Standards

Why would the nation’s highest court narrow its own precedent in such a sensitive case? While defenders of strict statutory interpretation generally argue that criminal liability must be proven element by element, political critics see institutional self-interest at work.

Shin Jang-sik, leader of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, argued in the Korean commentary that the ruling reflects a "self-protective perspective" inside the judiciary. In Shin’s view, if the Supreme Court had upheld a sweeping standard of insurrection liability, accountability could have rippled inward toward court officials and judicial administrators accused of passively tolerating the crisis or failing to take required measures at the time. Narrowing the legal threshold, Shin contended, shields the broader judicial establishment from scrutiny.

Shin particularly criticized the court’s reasoning that an official could understand a martial law decree to be unconstitutional and illegal without recognizing it as an act of insurrection. Critics warn that such logic creates a ready-made defense for future powerholders accused of subverting constitutional rule.

The backlash has been amplified by what journalist Hong Sa-hoon described as jarring inconsistencies across recent high-profile court verdicts:

  • Kim Keon-hee: Courts upheld a four-year prison sentence on stock manipulation charges, evaluating her actions before and after the core offense as a single, comprehensive pattern of conduct.
  • Myung Tae-kyun: Despite high-profile allegations of opinion-poll manipulation threatening electoral fairness, the courts issued an acquittal, applying what critics viewed as a markedly lenient standard.
  • Han Duck-soo: Rather than assessing the former prime minister’s conduct comprehensively, the Supreme Court segmented his actions, paving the way for partial acquittal on insurrection charges.

Different criminal statutes naturally involve different legal elements and burdens of proof. Yet Hong and the commentary’s authors argue that the contrast between sweeping liability in one political case and narrow fragmentation in another reinforces a damaging public perception that South Korean justice bends according to who stands before the bench.

The Impeachment Drive Against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

The fury over Han’s remand has now fused with an effort by South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party—which holds power under President Lee Jae-myung—to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Led publicly by Democratic Party lawmaker Park Ju-min, proponents insist the impeachment push is not mere political retaliation over an unfavorable verdict, but a constitutional remedy for institutional failure.

According to Park Ju-min and the sources compiled in the commentary, the impeachment case rests on three primary pillars:

  • Procedural haste in a historic trial: Delivering a remand ruling in just nine days on a massive insurrection record, which critics argue defies common sense and reflects a breakdown of judicial deliberation.
  • Neglect of constitutional duties: Allegations that Cho failed to fulfill his obligations as head of a constitutional organ by delaying the nomination and appointment of Supreme Court justices—an institutional impasse also reflected in a separate appointment-delay case overseen by Judge Lee Jae-kwan.
  • Alleged financial and ethical misconduct: Accusations involving improper "encouragement payments" and the alleged misuse of institutional corporate credit cards for private dining at high-end Japanese omakase restaurants.

For critics of the Supreme Court leadership, the combination of rushed constitutional jurisprudence and personal ethics allegations has stripped the bench of the moral authority needed to demand deference. Democratic Party lawmakers have staged intensive protests on the steps of the National Assembly’s main building to build momentum for a floor vote.

Chilling Effects on Lower Courts and What to Watch Next

Beyond Han Duck-soo’s individual fate, legal observers warn that the Supreme Court’s ruling carries immediate consequences for South Korea’s lower courts. Because trial judges closely track Supreme Court signals to avoid seeing their verdicts overturned on appeal, critics fear the narrow interpretation of insurrection intent will serve as an informal guideline chilling other major prosecutions—specifically pending cases involving Lee Sang-min and Park Sung-jae.

At the same time, reformers are targeting the structural mechanism they blame for enforcing conformity inside the judiciary: the National Court Administration (Beobwonhaengjeongcheo). As the administrative arm of the Supreme Court, the agency wields sweeping control over judicial personnel assignments and budgets. Lawmakers such as Park Ju-min argue that this centralized leverage discourages individual judges from ruling strictly according to law and conscience, prompting calls to abolish the agency altogether.

Three concrete milestones will determine how this constitutional clash unfolds next:

  • The pre-November 30 impeachment vote: Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party succeeds in pushing the impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae through the National Assembly before the end of November, aiming to send an unmistakable warning to the judicial leadership.
  • The National Judges’ Representative Conference on November 30: This nationwide gathering of representative judges will serve as a litmus test for the judiciary’s internal independence—revealing whether rank-and-file judges use the forum to voice dissent against the Supreme Court’s trajectory or close ranks behind institutional hierarchy.
  • The December 3 legislative deadline: Reformers in the National Assembly are pressing to pass legislation dissolving the National Court Administration before December 3, the second anniversary of the martial law crisis. Whether that bill clears the legislature will decide if South Korea’s judicial crisis ends as a passing political confrontation or permanently reshapes the architecture of the nation’s courts.
