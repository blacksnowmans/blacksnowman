South Korea’s Legal Standoff: Why the Ruling Party Is Hesitating Over a Mammoth Special Counsel Law

November 12, 2026

Inside the formal, high-ceilinged courtrooms of Seocho-dong—Seoul’s legal capital where the Supreme Court and public prosecutors' offices cluster—a quiet, calculated reversal is underway. Defendants who were indicted in connection with the unconstitutional emergency martial law decree declared under the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol no longer look defeated. Instead, they command the floor, turning criminal trials into ideological bullhorns. Outside, in the legislative halls of Yeouido, the supermajority Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), now the country's ruling party, sits on a legislative nuclear option: a mammoth, 309-person Special Counsel bill designed to dismantle weaponized prosecutions. Yet, trapped by an obsessive fear of political backlash, the party’s leadership hesitates to pull the trigger.

To understand why this legal and legislative impasse matters, one must look past the daily partisan squabbles and examine the structural stakes. What is unfolding in South Korea today is a high-stakes test of constitutional accountability. On one side, the judiciary appears to be systematically diluting charges against key figures from the previous regime. On the other, the ruling party, paralyzed by internal factional suspicions and flawed polling interpretations, risks squandering its mandate to restore judicial justice.

Judicial Resistance and Stalled Insurrection Trials in Seocho-dong

In South Korea’s legal system, trial court proceedings are expected to adhere to strict statutory timelines, particularly in high-profile constitutional cases. Yet, the ongoing trials surrounding the state of emergency and martial law deployment during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency have hit an unnatural standstill. Defendants charged with core roles in insurrectionary acts are not mounting legal defenses based on statutory principles; instead, they are aggressively pushing political narratives, openly demanding the withdrawal of indictments and railing against the potential impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

This aggressive posture by defense attorneys is fueled by visible signals of leniency from the bench. While prosecutors initially sought stern punishment for top orchestrators, appellate courts and lower tribunals appear inclined to isolate guilt onto two primary figures—Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and his wife, Mrs. Kim Keon-hee—while granting procedural exits for their key accomplices. Strict legal precedents established in first-instance rulings by presiding judges like Senior Judge Lee Jin-gwan are being systematically chipped away at upper court levels.

The most glaring evidence of intentional delay lies in the Supreme Court itself. The highest court has sat on a recusal application (기피 신청)—a formal challenge seeking to remove Senior Judge Lee Jin-gwan from presiding over these critical trials—for 168 days. According to judicial statistics, the average processing time for a recusal challenge in South Korean courts is just 1.1 months (roughly 33 days). A previous recusal challenge filed in the Suwon District Court involving Judge Lee was dismissed in 66 days. Holding onto this motion for 168 days is an unprecedented departure from norm.

This delay is not a mere bureaucratic oversight. The recusal motion was lodged by Choi Sang-mok, the former Deputy Prime Minister for Economy under Mr. Yoon. By refusing to rule on the motion for over five months, the Supreme Court effectively freezes Judge Lee’s courtroom authority and isolates him from enforcing swift judicial rulings. This tactic gives defense teams precious time while placing immense pressure on principled judges on the front lines.

Concurrently, key figures facing charges of executing essential duties during the martial law crisis are poised to walk out of detention centers on procedural grounds. Former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo is just days away from the expiration of his maximum pre-conviction detention period, while former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min—who oversaw police deployment and media control efforts during the crisis—and former Justice Minister Park Seong-jae are scheduled for release next month.

This systematic release of key figures poses a grave threat to South Korea's constitutional jurisprudence. Under Supreme Court Precedent 2014Do10978, the legal standard for incitement to insurrection requires only that actions generate an appetite for execution among a crowd, establishing that early, decisive punishment is required to protect the constitutional order. By allowing key players like former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun, former Defense Intelligence Commander No Sang-won, and senior cabinet officials to dilute their criminal responsibility behind procedural delays, the judiciary risks narrowing the legal definition of insurrection itself. Legal experts warn that such a dilution creates a dangerous precedent, granting historical and legal legitimacy to far-right historical distortions that attempt to whitewash past military crackdowns.

A 309-Member Special Counsel and the Fallacy of "Political Backlash"

To break this judicial paralysis and address weaponized investigations conducted during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure—ranging from the Daejang-dong land development scandal to controversial North Korean corporate remittance cases—reform-minded lawmakers have drafted a sweeping Special Counsel bill. The proposed investigative body is colossal: it calls for 309 personnel, including assistant special prosecutors, dispatched prosecutors, and special investigators. In terms of sheer scale, it rivals the historic 310-member Special Counsel team assembled to investigate the martial law crisis.

Proponents argue that an independent Special Counsel is the only legal tool capable of unearthing fabricated evidence, witness intimidation, and politicized indictments hidden within public prosecutors' internal file cabinets. Yet, despite holding a commanding majority in the National Assembly, the leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea remains stalled, citing fears of alienating moderate voters and triggering a conservative electoral backlash.

A closer look at public opinion data reveals that this fear of a "centrist backlash" is largely a statistical illusion. Overall national polling shows 37.3% support for the Fabricated Prosecution Special Counsel against 45.3% opposition. However, a demographic breakdown exposes deep partisan polarization rather than centrist resistance:

  • Democratic Party Supporters: 75.9% strongly favor the immediate passage of the Special Counsel bill.
  • People Power Party (PPP) Supporters: 77.4% overwhelmingly oppose the measure.

The opposition to the bill is concentrated entirely within the core base of the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition party. By misinterpreting opposition from PPP loyalists as a warning sign from swing voters, the ruling party leadership is effectively betraying three-quarters of its own base. Senior DPK figures, such as Representative Jung Sung-ho, have publicly undermined the bill by claiming that proving prosecutorial fabrication in court is "practically difficult," sending weak signals to the very institutions under scrutiny.

This tactical hesitation creates a striking paradox. On November 11, DPK Supreme Council Member Kim Min-seok issued a stern warning during a leadership meeting, stating that the Supreme Court would bear full responsibility if former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo were released, promising decisive action against judicial overreach by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Yet, behind these tough public statements lies a lack of concrete legislative execution. Words without legislative action have left the ruling party looking passive, while defense teams and judicial figures exploit the delay.

Factional Smears and the Battle Over Party Nominations

Adding to the paralysis is internal factional friction within the Democratic Party of Korea, exacerbated by unverified media reporting. A recent controversy erupted when journalist Jang Yoon-sun reported on an MBC Radio broadcast that reformist lawmakers aligned with former leadership figures had presented three ultimatum-like demands to DPK leader Kim Min-seok’s camp:

  1. The immediate resignation of current leadership and the establishment of an Emergency Response Committee.
  2. Guaranteed party candidate nominations for upcoming local and general elections.
  3. The immediate pursuit of a formal party merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive ally.

The report triggered immediate, fiery pushback from DPK Senior Supreme Council Member Choi Min-hee. In public statements, Choi denounced the claim that lawmakers were demanding "guaranteed nominations" as a complete fabrication that demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of South Korea’s system-based nomination rules.

"We never demanded the first or second points," Choi clarified. "And regarding the third point, merging with the Rebuilding Korea Party is a position shared by many DPK lawmakers, not just a single faction. Nominations in our party belong to the grassroots party members, not faction leaders."

The controversy highlighted significant lapses in journalistic verification. The reporting relied on off-hand remarks made during an informal inquiry with Lawmaker Lee Sung-yoon, without conducting basic cross-checks with the reformist lawmakers directly accused of trading political demands. In response to the unverified broadcast, Choi Min-hee filed a formal complaint with the Press Arbitration Commission (언론중재위원회), a statutory agency that mediates press disputes and handles claims for corrections.

The spread of these nomination rumors damages the party's internal cohesion. The DPK has spent years establishing a standardized, quantitative nomination system designed to prevent party leaders from handing out candidacies in secret rooms. The insinuation that faction heads are negotiating candidate slates behind closed doors breeds deep cynicism among rank-and-file party members.

Worse still, conservative commentators and internal critics have weaponized a dangerous narrative: accusing reformist lawmakers of forming a "three-party secret alliance" involving anti-leadership factions and fringe religious groups like Shincheonji. This narrative is particularly toxic because, if accepted, it could provide conservative legal strategists with political ammunition to argue for the constitutional dissolution of the ruling party before the Constitutional Court. Fabricated internal smears, if left unchecked, threaten to inflict self-referential harm on the ruling coalition.

What to Watch Next in Seoul Politics

As the legal and political showdown in Seoul reaches a boiling point, several concrete milestones will indicate which direction the country is heading:

  • The Supreme Court’s Recusal Ruling: Watch whether the Supreme Court finally issues a decision on the 168-day-old recusal motion against Senior Judge Lee Jin-gwan. A decision to uphold the challenge or continue stalling will signal a complete judicial deadlock in the martial law trials.
  • The Expiration of Pre-Conviction Detention Dates: Key dates are approaching for the release of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo and former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min. Their physical release from detention centers will mark a critical turning point for public sentiment.
  • Floor Action on the 309-Member Special Counsel Bill: Pay attention to whether DPK floor leadership schedules the Special Counsel bill for a binding vote in the National Assembly, or if internal debates defer the bill further.
  • Judicial Impeachment Proceedings: Monitor whether the ruling party translates its verbal warnings against Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae into formal impeachment motions on the Assembly floor.
