Why South Korea's Schools Are Trading Tablets for Paper and Pens

September 27, 2026

Step into a modern South Korean elementary school classroom, and you will likely see a familiar sight: children scrolling on tablets, tapping on keyboards, and struggling to sit still for more than a few minutes. In a hyper-digitized society where high-speed internet is ubiquitous and screens dictate daily life, the ability to sit quietly with a book and write by hand has become a rare commodity. Yet, a recent educational experiment featured on the South Korean public broadcaster EBS's documentary series EBS DocuPrime has sparked a national conversation about the hidden costs of our digital age.

Researchers followed sixth-grade students at Songre Elementary School as they embarked on an intensive, four-week handwriting project. For one hour every day, these 12-year-olds were asked to put down their devices, pick up a pen, and copy out a bestselling children's novel by hand. What started as an exercise in frustration ultimately revealed something profound: when children are forced to slow down and trace words physically, it triggers a remarkable cascade of cognitive, neurological, and emotional benefits that typing simply cannot replicate.

The Agony of Silence: Breaking the Digital Dopamine Loop

The project began with a heavy dose of friction. The students were given The Longest Night, a beloved South Korean children's book about friendship and survival. For children accustomed to short-form video content and instant dopamine hits from smartphone screens, sitting down to transcribe a book was akin to torture. Park Seo-jun, a sixth-grader participating in the project, admitted that he struggled simply because the process felt entirely foreign. Filling even a single page felt like an insurmountable hurdle.

Teachers set a strict rule: this was not a race to finish quickly. Students were instructed to read with comprehension and write deliberately, paying attention to the context of every single sentence. In modern classrooms where speed and efficiency are prized above all else, forcing children to stop and stare at a blank page creates immediate anxiety. The initial weeks were defined by restlessness and complaints, reflecting a generation whose attention spans have been fractured by constant digital stimulation. To keep the students going, producers had them photograph and share their handwritten pages—a tiny anchor of accountability in an otherwise unfamiliar sea of slow, analog labor.

By the second week, however, a subtle shift occurred. Seo-jun, who previously could not sit still for ten minutes, managed to lock in and write continuously for 30 minutes without a wandering thought. He later noted that having a fixed amount of time actually brought a sense of calm. Instead of running away from an overwhelming task, he was learning how to pace himself. More importantly, the act of physical transcription fostered a deep emotional connection with the text. Seo-jun described feeling as though he had become the protagonist, a rhinoceros named Nodden, absorbing the character's resilience with every stroke of his pen. This was no longer passive reading; it was an immersive, therapeutic dialogue with literature.

Numbers Don't Lie: Measuring the Psychological Shift

To move beyond subjective impressions, the documentary team utilized the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM), a standardized evaluation tool used to assess self-efficacy, satisfaction, and performance in daily activities. Students evaluated themselves before and after the four-week project on metrics like handwriting neatly, concentrating in class, and speaking up during lessons.

Neat handwriting emerged as the students' top priority, accounting for nearly 28% of their self-identified goals, followed by classroom concentration. By the end of the four weeks, the data showed significant improvements across the board. The performance score for writing neatly rose, while satisfaction jumped markedly. More strikingly, concentration scores in the classroom saw major gains, with one student's performance score shooting up from 4 out of 10 to a near-perfect 9. Experts noted that these gains were clinically significant, proving that the discipline of handwriting acts as a powerful psychological tool for building self-esteem and self-regulation.

This quantitative leap underscores a vital truth about child development: self-efficacy is built through endurance. When children push through the physical discomfort of writing to complete a tangible goal, their self-image transforms from someone who gives up easily into someone who is capable of sustained focus.

What Happens Inside the Brain: The Neuroscience of Handwriting

Why should handwriting make such a drastic difference compared to typing on a keyboard? To answer this, researchers used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to monitor brain activity in real time. The experiment compared students typing on a laptop versus writing by hand.

The results were decisive. While laptop typing produced minimal brain activation, handwriting triggered a powerful surge of blood flow in the prefrontal cortex—the brain's command center responsible for attention, working memory, and long-term planning. Specifically, the left prefrontal cortex, which governs language expression, lit up intensely. Experts from Seoul National University explained that handwriting requires intricate motor control, spatial planning, and continuous mental projection about what words will come next. Digital typing largely automates these cognitive hurdles, allowing the computer to handle spacing and alignment while the human brain coasts on autopilot.

These findings align with international research. A prominent study from the Norwegian University of Science and Technology found that handwriting vastly increases connectivity across brain regions associated with memory, sensory processing, and motor control. When a child writes by hand, the tactile friction of the pen against paper, the visual feedback of forming letters, and the fine motor adjustments required combine to create a rich neural symphony. In contrast, heavy reliance on screens without analog intervention has been linked in academic literature to declining attention spans and poorer executive function in children and adolescents.

Reclaiming the Classroom in an Age of EdTech

South Korea’s educational authorities have spent years pouring massive budgets into digital infrastructure, outfitting classrooms with smart devices and high-tech tools in the name of future-proofing education. While technological literacy is necessary, the rush to digitize has outpaced our understanding of the collateral damage: the erosion of basic literacy, deep reading skills, and the capacity for sustained concentration.

The Songre Elementary School project serves as a timely reminder that the future of education does not necessarily lie in more expensive gadgets or flashier screens. Sometimes, progress requires stepping backward. By forcing children to grapple with the slow, deliberate pain of holding a pencil, schools can reactivate the neural pathways that screens have lulled to sleep. As South Korea continues to grapple with the fallout of screen-saturated childhoods, the most radical educational reform might be the simplest one: closing the laptop, putting down the tablet, and handing children a stack of paper and a sharp pencil.

What to watch next:

  • Policy shifts in local school boards: Watch whether municipal education offices begin incorporating analog literacy programs alongside their heavy investments in edtech infrastructure.
  • Classroom curriculum changes: Look for upcoming guidelines on whether schools will experiment with dedicated handwriting or "analog detox" hours in primary grades.
  • Long-term cognitive studies: Follow upcoming educational research in South Korea assessing the lingering effects of post-pandemic digital immersion on student attention spans.
