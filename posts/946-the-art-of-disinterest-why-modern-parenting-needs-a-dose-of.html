The Art of Disinterest: Why Modern Parenting Needs a Dose of "Strategic Neglect"

October 10, 2026

Every parent knows the scene: a child collapses in the middle of a grocery store or erupts in tears because they didn't get their way. In South Korea, where the pressure to raise "perfect" children often leads to hyper-involved parenting, many mothers and fathers are finding themselves exhausted and, ultimately, ineffective. Increasingly, child psychology experts are pushing back against the trend of over-empathizing with every flicker of a child’s mood.

Professor Chosun-mi, a prominent figure in South Korean child psychology based at Ajou University Hospital, has been gaining significant attention for her no-nonsense approach to raising children. Her central thesis is as simple as it is difficult for modern parents to swallow: when a child uses tantrums to control their environment, the most effective tool at a parent's disposal is not negotiation, but calculated indifference.

The Power of "Strategic Neglect"

In many Korean households, parents react instinctively to a child’s fussing—offering a toy, a snack, or an immediate apology to silence the noise. According to Chosun-mi, this is exactly what gives the child a "magic switch." When a child realizes that a tantrum or a bout of pouting leads to an immediate change in the parent's behavior, that behavior is reinforced. To the child, the parent’s reaction is a form of power.

The solution, while seemingly harsh, is to cut off the oxygen to the tantrum. When a child acts out, even acknowledging the behavior—asking "Why are you like this?" or "Do you want me to buy you something?"—serves as a reward. By giving the child attention, you are validating their outburst. Instead, the expert suggests that parents simply ignore the behavior, walk away, or offer no emotional response at all. If the child’s tantrum yields no audience, the "performance" naturally loses its utility.

Building Resilience Through "Healthy Deprivation"

Modern South Korean society is characterized by high expectations and intense competition. Paradoxically, the more parents try to cushion their children from every minor frustration, the less equipped those children become to handle the real world. This phenomenon is becoming a macro-level issue in Korea: young adults who have been coddled their entire lives frequently struggle to adjust to entry-level jobs, often quitting at the first sign of professional stress or a salary that doesn't meet their expectations.

Chosun-mi argues that "deprivation" is a necessary component of development. If a child never learns to endure discomfort or disappointment under the guidance of their parents, they lack the "inner durability" required for adulthood. In this light, always acceding to a child's demands isn't a gesture of love; it is an act of sabotage. Parenting, at its best, requires the courage to stand back and allow a child to experience and process frustration on their own.

Restoring Parental Authority

A frequent complaint among parents is that they feel they have lost their authority. The reality, as many experts point out, is that they usually lost that authority years ago. When a parent allows a child to decide whether they "feel like" brushing their teeth or doing their homework, the power dynamic has already shifted.

If an employee told their boss that they refused to do a task, and the boss responded by giving them a bonus just to keep them happy, we would call that dysfunctional management. Yet, this is exactly how many parents treat their children. Reclaiming authority does not mean shouting or constant discipline; it means re-establishing the fundamental boundaries that define the parent-child relationship. If a child refuses to complete a task, the parent should be a steady, unmoving force—calmly waiting for compliance rather than negotiating or pleading.

The Dilemma of "Empathy-First" Parenting

Current parenting trends emphasize validating a child’s every emotion. While empathy is a vital skill, its application has become a trap for many. Parents are burning themselves out trying to emotionally regulate their children, only to find that the more they accommodate the child’s moods, the less respect the child has for the parent’s instructions.

True education involves teaching a child that the world does not revolve around their emotional state. While it is important to be kind, it is equally important to teach that there are responsibilities that must be met regardless of how one "feels" at that moment. The goal is to raise an individual who possesses the capacity for patience and the resilience to navigate a world that will not always cater to their whims.

What to Watch Next

For parents trying to implement these changes, the transition will likely be chaotic at first. Children who have grown accustomed to being the center of household decision-making will push back harder when those boundaries are finally drawn.

  • Consistency: The most important factor in the coming months will be consistency. A parent who attempts "strategic neglect" but caves after ten minutes of crying only teaches the child that they need to scream longer next time to get their way.
  • Professional Assessment: It is worth noting that some persistent, extreme behaviors may point toward developmental or psychological conditions like ADHD. If a child's struggles seem disproportionate, seeking professional help is a sign of responsible, proactive parenting, not a failure of character.
  • Reframing Morning Routines: Watch for parents beginning to trade "perfection" for "functionality." Moving away from the pressure of the "perfect morning" (perfect breakfast, perfect attire) toward a system that prioritizes getting the child out the door on time will be a key indicator of a shift toward more sustainable, realistic parenting.

Ultimately, parenting is a process of unlearning one's own fantasies of the "perfect parent." As South Korea continues to navigate shifting social norms, the move toward fostering independence and resilience over total accommodation may prove to be the most important cultural correction in the home.
