Ninety-Five Minutes in Seoul: Inside the Trial Testing South Korea's Courts and Markets

November 18, 2026

At 10:28 p.m. on the night emergency martial law was declared in South Korea, text alerts began lighting up the phones of ruling-bloc lawmakers in Seoul. Within thirty-five minutes, Choo Kyung-ho—then floor leader of the conservative People Power Party—ordered his colleagues to rush immediately to the National Assembly. Six minutes later, he told them to go to the party's central headquarters instead. A short phone call with Yoon Suk Yeol followed. Then the meeting point shifted back inside the legislature, and just after midnight, moved back to party headquarters once again.

Those ninety-five minutes of contradictory text notices now sit at the center of a high-stakes first-instance criminal trial against Choo Kyung-ho on insurrection-related charges. Speaking on the prominent political YouTube program Kim Ou-joon's Humility Is Difficult News Factory, a panel of legal and media figures—including Rebuilding Korea Party lawmaker Kim Hyung-yeon, attorney Park Pan-gyu, Newstapa investigative journalist Han Sang-jin, and journalists Joo Jin-woo and Bong Ji-wook—dissected what the court's upcoming ruling could mean not only for one politician, but for South Korea's judiciary and rattled financial markets.

For readers outside South Korea, the case is about far more than internal party logistics. Under South Korean law, only a vote by lawmakers inside the National Assembly can lift a declaration of martial law. Whether Choo's rapid venue changes reflected unavoidable confusion on a chaotic night or a deliberate attempt to keep conservative lawmakers away from the chamber floor has become a defining test of how South Korea's courts establish criminal intent—just as the judiciary prepares for another major ruling involving former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo and as President Lee Jae-myung's administration faces mounting criticism over tumbling stock prices.

Ninety-Five Minutes of Shifting Venues and the Question of Intent

To understand why prosecutors and judges are scrutinizing a single hour and a half so closely, it helps to trace the minute-by-minute record laid out by Newstapa journalist Han Sang-jin during the broadcast. According to the timeline presented on the program, the sequence unfolded across late evening on the 3rd and into the first minutes of the 4th:

  • 10:28 p.m.: Yoon Suk Yeol declares emergency martial law.
  • 11:03 p.m.: Choo Kyung-ho sends a text notice summoning an emergency general meeting of People Power Party lawmakers, telling them to come to the National Assembly immediately.
  • 11:09 p.m.: Six minutes later, a revised notice moves the gathering from the National Assembly to the People Power Party's central headquarters.
  • 11:22 p.m.: A phone call lasting roughly one minute takes place between Yoon Suk Yeol and Choo Kyung-ho.
  • 11:33 p.m. to 11:49 p.m.: New notices direct lawmakers back to a budget committee conference room inside the National Assembly.
  • 12:03 a.m.: The meeting site is switched a third time, returning to the People Power Party central headquarters.

On the surface, the rapid back-and-forth looks disorienting. On the broadcast, Han Sang-jin cited an assessment by journalist Hong Sa-won suggesting that some People Power Party lawmakers had already entered the National Assembly compound and that the shifting instructions may have been aimed at drawing them back out. Critics of Choo's actions point to the timing of the 11:22 p.m. call with Mr. Yoon and the subsequent reversals as grounds to suspect an effort to hinder attendance at the repeal vote.

Yet as the panelists and legal observers noted, suspicion is not the same as proof in a criminal courtroom. Defense arguments hold that the venue changes were driven by physical access restrictions around the National Assembly and rapidly evolving confusion on the ground. Under South Korean criminal law, showing that fewer lawmakers ultimately cast ballots is not enough to secure a conviction on insurrection-related or obstruction charges. Prosecutors must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant acted with subjective criminal intent—either actively aiming to block the parliamentary vote to lift martial law or willfully accepting that outcome.

That evidentiary gap is where the first-instance trial will be decided. Judges must weigh the content and context of the one-minute call at 11:22 p.m. alongside the notices sent before and after it. If the court finds that chaotic access controls plausibly forced constant improvisation, proving criminal intent becomes far harder. Conversely, if corroborating evidence shows an effort to pull lawmakers already inside the compound back out, the legal picture shifts dramatically.

Judicial Discretion and Consistency Under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

Debate over Choo's trial quickly widened on the program into a broader argument over whether South Korea's courts—operating under Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—are applying consistent legal standards across politically sensitive cases. The discussion carried added urgency because the courts were only a day away from delivering a verdict in the case of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo.

Journalist Joo Jin-woo questioned the predictability of judicial outcomes by pointing to disparities in past rulings, referencing earlier trial decisions involving President Lee Jae-myung—including an acquittal and a suspended sentence of one year in prison suspended for two years in Public Official Election Act cases—alongside cases overseen by Presiding Judge Han Sung-jin. Reflecting on the difficulty of forecasting verdicts, Joo remarked that courtroom predictions often carry little certainty for defendants currently standing trial.

Attorney Park Pan-gyu voiced an even blunter view of how judicial discretion shapes outcomes, warning that the composition of the bench can outweigh the volume of investigative material presented in court.

"Who the judge is matters," Park Pan-gyu said on the broadcast, adding that no matter how much evidence and reasonable inference a special counsel puts forward—particularly regarding events before midnight—it comes to nothing if the presiding judge refuses to accept it.

At the same time, legal analysts caution against reading every differing verdict as proof of bias. Different cases involve distinct factual records, statutory charges, and evidentiary thresholds, making direct one-to-one comparisons imprecise. Still, as Han Sang-jin emphasized during the broadcast, cases tied to insurrection and martial law carry extraordinary constitutional weight, requiring judges to rule strictly on law and evidence without political calculation. For South Korea's judiciary, maintaining public trust depends on delivering objective, thoroughly reasoned judgments that insulate the courts from accusations of partisanship or outside pressure.

Market Losses and Scrutiny of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

While the courts weigh constitutional accountability, prolonged political and legal uncertainty has spilled directly into South Korea's financial markets. On the broadcast, journalist Bong Ji-wook underscored the scale of recent losses on the KOSPI, South Korea's benchmark stock index, by comparing the vanishing market capitalization to Seoul's housing market.

According to Bong, the combined value of all apartments in Seoul exceeds 2,000 trillion won—roughly $1.4 trillion—and the wealth erased in the equity sell-off has surpassed that same 2,000 trillion won mark. He likened the drop to "every apartment in Seoul being burned down," arguing that despite losses on a scale felt heavily by households and investors, authorities had failed to label the downturn an emergency. Refusing to acknowledge a crisis openly, Bong argued, only deepens investor anxiety.

Panelists also directed sharp criticism at how President Lee Jae-myung's administration—backed by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—has communicated with markets during the slide. Kim Hyung-yeon, the Rebuilding Korea Party lawmaker, argued that addressing technical obstacles and enforcement gaps—including pledges to track down violations over a one-year follow-up period—is fundamentally a test of political will rather than mere technical capacity. Kim also pointed to visible disarray inside the cabinet: although the Prime Minister issued a public statement aimed at steadying investor nerves, an episode the previous day in which the Education Minister appeared visibly rattled exposed a lack of message coordination across ministries.

Joo Jin-woo similarly criticized a cabinet minister for smiling and offering disjointed remarks that disappointed both the public and investors, noting how acutely market sentiment reacts to the tone of senior officials during a downturn. Even so, Joo left open the possibility of a swift turnaround, recalling that the government had previously reversed market momentum within a single day through decisive policy announcements. If officials shift away from defensive explanations and present concrete, proactive measures, he argued, confidence can still be rebuilt. Park Pan-gyu echoed that view, saying authorities need to signal unmistakably—through visible, round-the-clock engagement—that they are deploying every available tool to stabilize the market.

What to Watch Next in South Korea's Courts and Markets

As South Korea navigates overlapping legal and economic tests, several concrete developments will determine whether public and investor confidence stabilizes in the weeks ahead:

  • The reasoning in Choo Kyung-ho's verdict: Watch how the first-instance court interprets the 11:22 p.m. phone call with Yoon Suk Yeol and the three venue changes between 11:03 p.m. and 12:03 a.m. Specifically, look at whether the judges find sufficient objective evidence of intent to obstruct the martial law repeal vote, or conclude that confusion at the National Assembly creates reasonable doubt.
  • Han Duck-soo's ruling and judicial consistency: Watch how lower courts and the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae align their legal standards across parallel martial law and insurrection-related trials, and whether written opinions address public concerns over inconsistent sentencing and evidentiary standards.
  • Policy coordination from the Lee Jae-myung administration: In financial markets, investors will be watching whether President Lee Jae-myung's economic team, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, and the opposition People Power Party can reduce political uncertainty, eliminate conflicting ministerial statements, and pair clear crisis diagnostics with enforceable market-stabilization measures.

Between the courtroom demand for strict proof and the financial market's demand for clarity, South Korea's institutions are being tested on a single underlying principle: whether rules and leadership remain consistent when the pressure is highest.
