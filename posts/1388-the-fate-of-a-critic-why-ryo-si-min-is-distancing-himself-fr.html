The Fate of a Critic: Why Ryo Si-min is Distancing Himself from President Lee Jae-myung

October 22, 2026

Imagine one of the most influential intellectual voices in your country—a man who has spent years defending a political leader through scandals, elections, and trials—suddenly sitting in front of a camera and calmly stating that his relationship with that leader "cannot last forever." In the hyper-connected, emotionally charged world of South Korean politics, such a statement is not merely a philosophical musing; it is a tectonic shift.

Ryo Si-min, a bestselling author, former Minister of Health and Welfare, and arguably the most prominent liberal polemicist in South Korea, recently sent shockwaves through the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. During a YouTube broadcast, Ryo addressed his cooling relationship with President Lee Jae-myung. His message was clear: he values his independence as a critic more than the "comradeship" that defines Korean political life. For a president currently struggling with low approval ratings and internal party fractures, losing the unmitigated support of an intellectual heavyweight like Ryo is a development that signals a deepening crisis within the liberal establishment.

The Duty of Criticism over the Comfort of Comradeship

To understand why this matters, one must understand Ryo Si-min’s unique position. He is not a politician anymore, but he commands an audience that many politicians would envy. For years, he has been the "intellectual shield" for the liberal movement, particularly during the turbulent transition following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Ryo’s support was instrumental in consolidating the base around Lee Jae-myung during his rise to the presidency.

However, Ryo’s recent comments suggest that the "honeymoon phase" of the Lee Jae-myung administration is over, at least for the intellectuals who helped build it. Ryo argued that while social order and human connections are important, they should never supersede one’s honest internal voice. He used the metaphor of fishing—going for crucian carp when it rains or sea bream when the ocean calls—to describe the natural ebb and flow of human desires and interests.

"If expressing my thoughts makes it difficult to maintain a relationship with someone, I choose to give up the relationship," Ryo stated. This is a radical stance in a political culture where loyalty—often referred to as jeon-u-ae or "comradeship forged in battle"—is usually considered the highest virtue. Ryo is effectively arguing that for a critic, self-censorship in the name of friendship is a form of professional suicide. If a critic stops speaking the truth to power because they are friends with the person in power, the criticism loses its life force.

A Presidency Besieged by Numbers and Internal Strife

Ryo’s philosophical detachment comes at a time when the Lee Jae-myung administration can ill afford to lose its defenders. Recent polling data paints a grim picture of the current political landscape. According to the Korea Society Opinion Institute (KSOI), President Lee’s approval rating has dipped to 34.9%, while his negative rating has climbed to 58.8%.

Perhaps most concerning for the ruling Democratic Party is where this dissatisfaction is coming from. While the President maintains a lead in Honam (the Jeolla provinces, a traditional liberal stronghold), his disapproval ratings have crossed the 50% threshold in almost every other region. Even the core "loyalist" demographics—voters in their 40s and 50s who have historically been the bedrock of liberal support—are showing signs of wavering. These voters are feeling the sting of a stagnant economy and are increasingly frustrated with the government’s perceived inability to move past partisan bickering.

The internal reaction to Ryo’s "declaration of independence" has been telling. Within the Democratic Party, the "Pro-Lee" faction—the hardline supporters of the President—reacted with fury, labeling Ryo’s analysis as "bad luck," "a curse," or even comparing it to a shamanic ritual. On the other hand, the "Pro-Chung" faction and other more moderate wings of the party have remained notably more reserved, acknowledging that Ryo is a private citizen and a critic whose job is to provide analysis, not blind loyalty. This split highlights a growing vulnerability: the administration is increasingly surrounded by "yes-men" while the intellectuals who provided its moral and logical framework are heading for the exits.

The SNS Controversy: A Shout-Out to the Enemy

The tension between Ryo and the Blue House (the President's office) reached a boiling point due to a seemingly small interaction on social media. President Lee Jae-myung recently gave a "shout-out" on X (formerly Twitter) to an individual known for relentlessly attacking Ryo Si-min with malicious rumors and false information.

For Ryo’s supporters, this wasn't just a social media mishap; it was a betrayal. It signaled that the President was willing to embrace toxic elements of the online "fandom" even if it meant alienating long-term allies. Ryo himself described the feeling as "devastating." When the leader of a nation appears to validate someone who uses disinformation against an intellectual ally, it creates a "chilling effect" on discourse.

"When you start evaluating a critic’s motives or resorting to character assassination instead of refuting their logic, you close the door on healthy debate," Ryo noted.

This incident has led many to wonder if the President's communication strategy is focused solely on mobilizing a shrinking, radicalized base rather than reaching out to the broader public. In the eyes of critics like Ryo, the presidency is becoming an echo chamber, where any voice that doesn't offer 100% fealty is branded as a "traitor" or an "internal shooter" (a Korean term for someone who sabotages their own side).

Metacognition and the Future of Korean Liberalism

At the heart of Ryo’s argument is the concept of metacognition—the ability to step back and observe one’s own emotions and situation objectively. He argues that even if he feels a sense of loss or hurt, he must accept the reality that the political landscape has changed. "Ah, things have changed. I must accept this," he says, describing his process of self-objectification.

This "cold-headed" approach is a warning to the Lee Jae-myung government. If the administration continues to view every criticism as a legal or political threat to be neutralized, it will eventually find itself completely isolated from the reality of the people. Ryo’s move to distance himself is a litmus test for the administration: Can it tolerate a healthy, tense relationship with its critics, or will it demand total submission?

The legal stakes are also present, though perhaps secondary to the political ones. Many of the attacks against Ryo involve charges of defamation under the Information and Communications Network Act. While the President recently expressed that he does not wish for Ryo to be punished (defamation is a "crime punishable against the victim's will" in Korea, meaning charges can be dropped if the victim agrees), the political pressure remains. Using the judicial system to silence critics is a dangerous precedent that Ryo argues must be resisted at all costs to protect the freedom of expression.

What to Watch Next

The "Ryo Si-min vs. Lee Jae-myung" saga is far from over, and its resolution will tell us much about the future of South Korean democracy. Here is what to watch for in the coming months:

  • The "Exodus of the Intellectuals": Watch if other prominent liberal thinkers follow Ryo's lead. If a broader group of writers, professors, and commentators begins to distance themselves from the Democratic Party, the administration will lose its ability to frame the national narrative.
  • Polling in the 40s and 50s: Keep a close eye on approval ratings among these key demographics. If they continue to drop below 30%, the ruling party may face an internal revolt as members of the National Assembly start worrying about their own re-election prospects.
  • The President’s SNS Strategy: Will President Lee continue to engage with hardline "fandom" accounts, or will he pivot to a more inclusive, "presidential" tone to win back moderates?
  • Legal Precedents: Watch how the courts and the prosecution handle ongoing defamation cases against political critics. Any move toward "judicializing" political speech will likely provoke a strong reaction from civil society.

Ultimately, Ryo Si-min’s decision to prioritize his "inner voice" over his political relationships is a reminder that in a healthy democracy, silence is a luxury the public cannot afford. As Ryo put it, silence in the face of power eventually turns into a "zombie" that eats away at the very foundation of the government.
