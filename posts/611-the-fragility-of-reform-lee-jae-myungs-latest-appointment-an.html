The Fragility of Reform: Lee Jae-myung’s Latest Appointment and a Government in Stagnation

October 02, 2026

October 2nd was meant to be a landmark day for South Korean jurisprudence. It marked the official separation of the nation's investigative and prosecutorial powers, with the launch of two new bodies: the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA), tasked with high-level investigations, and the Prosecution Service, now stripped of its investigative reach. Yet, instead of a celebratory atmosphere, the mood in Seoul was one of grim resignation. President Lee Jae-myung’s decision to force through the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural chief of the SCIA has signaled a direct confrontation not just with the opposition, but with the public sentiment that once fueled his reform agenda.

For those watching from abroad, this moment represents a critical juncture in the maturation of South Korean democracy. The transition is not merely a bureaucratic shift; it is an attempt to address a long-standing grievance: the perceived unchecked power of the traditional prosecutor's office. However, as the government faces mounting criticism for its handling of this transition, one must ask: Why does a long-awaited reform feel like a failure before it has even begun?

A Reform Day That Felt Like a Retreat

The institutional separation of investigation and prosecution is the "skeleton" of the legal reform South Korea has long demanded. However, the government has struggled to find the right people to "flesh out" that skeleton. From the Ministry of Justice to the National Police Agency, and now the SCIA, key positions are being filled by acting officials or controversial appointees. This systemic paralysis suggests that the current administration’s personnel management is effectively collapsing.

The nomination of Kim Ji-yong is the epicenter of this friction. Critics argue that at a time when the SCIA should be asserting its independence as a powerful investigative body, the government is instead installing a figure who mirrors the administration’s own interests. This is not just about political affiliation; it is about the perception that the government is trading one form of centralized power for another. As former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae recently invoked the spirit of Hannah Arendt’s "banality of evil," the warning is clear: blind obedience to executive orders cannot serve as a substitute for judicial justice.

Public dissatisfaction is no longer a fringe sentiment. With disapproval ratings for the Lee administration hitting record lows, the insistence on pushing through controversial appointments without consensus is being viewed as a self-inflicted wound. The administration’s tendency to label internal critics—such as Democratic Party members Park Eun-jung and Kim Yong-min—as "enemies of the state" has turned the political arena into a low-brow shouting match, stifling the very discourse required for meaningful democratic progress.

The Contradiction of "Reform"

President Lee Jae-myung maintains that reform must serve the people, not specific interests. Yet, his administration’s habit of denouncing reform-minded members of his own party as "self-serving" exposes a deep contradiction. By privatizing the mandate of reform and weaponizing it against dissenters, the administration is eroding the very democratic values it claims to uphold.

The Empty Desk: A Symptom of Executive Stagnation

The Chief of Staff is the heartbeat of a South Korean administration, the ultimate insider who manages the flow of state affairs. The fact that the position has remained vacant for 11 days in an administration less than two years into its term is a damning indictment of its health. When potential candidates turn down the job, it is not merely a recruitment difficulty; it is a sign that the administration has entered a state of terminal decline, or what we call "lame duck" territory—often earlier than expected.

There are reports of the President holding a four-hour "solo dinner" with outspoken party figures like Chung Chung-rae. While framed as a consultation, it looks more like a desperate attempt to consolidate a shrinking base of loyalists. By isolating critical voices—like that of public intellectual Rhyu Si-min—and surrounding himself with an echo chamber of hardliners, President Lee is distancing himself from the public pulse. The political class is well aware of the winds of change, and those who prioritize their own survival are already looking for the lifeboats.

The Path Forward: Normalizing the Democratic Process

If there is a silver lining to this stagnation, it is the realization that the democratic process is greater than any single political figure. The reliance on prominent "media influencers" or partisan broadcasters within the liberal camp to shape public opinion has its limits. When these voices remain neutral or evasive on critical issues of state integrity, they fail the public.

The path forward for those concerned with South Korea’s future does not lie in blind support for the current executive. Instead, the focus must shift to the "normalization" of the Democratic Party and the preparation for the next political cycle. The reform of the prosecution system is a noble goal, and the "skeleton" of the law remains intact; it is the political will and the personnel that need to be cleansed and renewed.

What to Watch Next:

  • Legislative Oversight: Keep a close eye on the list of lawmakers who continue to defend the current appointment of Kim Ji-yong. Their political survival may be tied to the administration, but their accountability to voters remains.
  • Internal Party Dynamics: Watch for signs of a "reset" within the Democratic Party. If reform-oriented members begin to distance themselves from the administration, it could signal an internal fracturing that will define the upcoming parliamentary and presidential outlooks.
  • Public Sentiment: The youth vote, particularly those in their 20s and 30s, is shifting rapidly. If their disapproval continues to grow, expect the administration to either double down on populist rhetoric or face a total collapse of its governing authority.

Ultimately, power is transient, but the citizenry is permanent. The current turbulence is a painful rite of passage. If the people remain vigilant and the desire for genuine, transparent reform persists, the current chaos will be remembered not as the end of an era, but as the moment when the public demanded a return to common sense and accountability.
