Mobilize the Base or Chase the Center? Inside South Korea’s Debate Over Progressive Strategy

October 24, 2026

Inside the broadcast studios and political strategy rooms of Seoul, a fierce debate is underway over how South Korea’s ruling progressive bloc should hold power and build a lasting political majority. Following the presidential election that brought President Lee Jae-myung to power, political analysts and lawmakers have converged on talk shows like the Hong Sa-hoon Show to dissect the vote. The central question animating these discussions is fundamental to any democracy: should a governing party expand its coalition by pivoting toward the political center, or by energizing its core supporters and mobilizing those who stopped voting altogether?

To understand the high stakes of this debate, international observers must first grasp South Korea’s rapidly shifted political landscape. The nation is currently governed by President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s main liberal party. This political realignment followed a period of intense instability that culminated in the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), now finds itself recalibrating its own strategies. Yet, despite occupying the presidency, the progressive camp remains divided on how to lock in its electoral mandate and navigate persistent friction with the conservative-leaning judiciary.

The Fallacy of the 'Structural Minority' and the Core-First Strategy

A persistent narrative in South Korean political commentary suggests that progressives are a "structural minority"—that even when all liberal and left-leaning voters unite, they fall short of a permanent majority. Analysts pointing to the recent presidential election note that if you add up the votes cast for conservative candidate Kim Moon-soo of the People Power Party and Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party, their combined total rivaled President Lee’s tally. Only when including votes for minor progressive candidates, such as human rights lawyer Kwon Young-guk, did the progressive-reform bloc achieve a precarious balance.

From these raw numbers, some moderate strategists conclude that the ruling DPK must pivot to the right to capture moderate and rational conservative voters. However, prominent progressive commentators—including Shin Jang-sik, a lawmaker from the ally Rebuilding Korea Party, and former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook—vehemently reject this defeatist framework. They argue that treating progressives as an inherent minority undermines the very supporters who marched, organized, and delivered victory.

Political statistics are rarely a simple matter of addition. A pivot to the center only succeeds if the party’s core base remains completely intact—an assumption that history frequently disproves. When a political party dilutes its primary message to chase uncommitted voters, it risks alienating its most passionate core, losing enthusiasm far faster than it gains new supporters. To abandon core values in pursuit of an elusive center is to risk collapsing the party's primary foundation.

This political reality is captured by the ancient classical idiom Gun-ja-yeol, Won-ja-rae (近者悅 遠자來): "Delight those who are near, and those who are far away will come." Political platforms gain magnetic pull when internal members feel genuine pride and solidarity. A strong, enthusiastic core creates the gravity needed to attract moderate voters naturally. When conservative figures crossed the aisle to support the DPK in recent cycles, they did so not because the DPK had watered down its message, but because it demonstrated a clear, uncompromising drive for reform. A home that grows cold on the inside will attract no guests from the outside.

Defeating the Frame: Targeting Non-Voters over Conversion

Political pollster Park Si-young offers a crucial structural critique of why progressive presidential candidates historically faced tough public perception headwinds. He argues that high negative ratings were not born from inherently unpopular progressive policies, but were heavily shaped during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure. Under that administration, political prosecutors engaged in aggressive targeted investigations, which were then echoed continuously by conservative media outlets to demonize opposition leaders.

Rather than internalizing these media-driven narratives and blaming core voters for being too radical, progressives argue that the government and ruling party must confront the underlying institutional behavior. Blaming the base for being a "minority" succumbs directly to the framing established by conservative establishment cartels.

Furthermore, a major shift in perspective is needed regarding voters who sit on the sidelines. In South Korean elections, voter turnout often hovers around 60%, meaning that roughly 40% of the electorate abstains. Drawing on political insights similar to those framed in Chris Anderson’s How Progressives Become the Majority, analysts emphasize that progressive movements historically achieve dominance not by converting ideological opponents, but by giving non-voters a compelling reason to show up.

Trying to persuade hardline opposition voters to switch sides is often an inefficient political errand. A far more effective strategy is to extend a hand to the 40% of citizens who feel the political system has abandoned them. By delivering tangible economic benefits, strengthening public welfare, and restoring judicial fairness, the government can convince marginalized workers and disillusioned citizens that voting directly improves their daily lives.

Judicial Battles and the Trial of Mayor Oh Se-hoon

The strategic debate within the progressive camp is compounded by ongoing conflict with South Korea's judiciary under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Progressive commentators express deep frustration over recent court decisions that they view as blatantly biased toward conservative figures, contrasting sharply with the severe legal scrutiny applied to progressive politicians.

A flashpoint in this critique is the recent appellate court acquittal of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent figure in the conservative People Power Party. Mayor Oh had been accused of having third-party opinion poll costs paid on his behalf by pollster Myung Tae-kyun during a past campaign. Despite bank records showing that an individual named Kim Han-jung transferred a total of 33 million won (about $24,000) directly to Myung’s firm in five installments—including 10 million won on February 1, 5.5 million won on February 5, 5.5 million won on February 18, 7 million won on February 23, and 5 million won on March 26, 2021—the appellate court ruled the transaction was not an illegal political payment or bribe, labeling it an apparent "normal transaction."

Critics argue this ruling stretches logic to its limits. It asks the public to believe that a private individual paid tens of thousands of dollars, complete with value-added tax, to a political pollster out of pure personal curiosity, without benefiting the political candidate involved. Progressive legal experts argue that while ordinary citizens face strict penalties for minor financial irregularities, high-profile conservative politicians receive selective judicial leniency.

From the perspective of progressive strategists, Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s court is pursuing a calculated political strategy: allow Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached and imprisoned—to serve as a political lightning rod to absorb public anger, while systematically clearing potential future conservative presidential contenders like Mayor Oh Se-hoon or former PPP leader Han Dong-hoon. In response to this judicial posturing, leaders like Cho Kuk, head of the Rebuilding Korea Party, have called for concrete legislative action, including motions to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae for undermining judicial neutrality.

What to Watch Next

As President Lee Jae-myung’s administration pushes forward with its legislative agenda, the strategy chosen by the governing bloc will shape South Korean politics for years to come. Here are the key dynamics to monitor in the coming months:

  • Judicial Reform and Impeachment Motions: Watch whether the DPK aligns with the Rebuilding Korea Party to introduce formal impeachment proceedings against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, or whether the ruling party opts for broader institutional court reforms instead.
  • Legislative Pressure on Prosecution Reform: Track how the DPK-led National Assembly moves to dismantle prosecutorial privileges and restructure investigative powers, testing whether the ruling coalition can maintain its unity against conservative pushback.
  • Policy Targeting the Non-Voter Base: Monitor whether President Lee’s administration prioritizes universal economic measures and labor protections designed to re-engage the 40% non-voting bloc, or shifts toward moderate market deregulational policies aimed at swing voters.
  • DPK Internal Dynamics and Strategy Alignment: Pay close attention to internal debate within the DPK leadership between moderate strategists advocating a center-right policy expansion and reformist factions demanding bold, clear-cut reform initiatives.
