Inside the High-Stakes Battle Over South Korea’s Judiciary: Why the Ruling Party Must Decide Chief Justice Cho Hee-de’s Fate

November 05, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul’s Yeouido district, South Korea’s ruling liberal party is locked in a fierce internal debate over how to confront the nation’s highest judicial authority. Following the historic impeachment and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol from office, the newly inaugurated administration of President Lee Jae-myung faces lingering resistance from conservative strongholds in the judiciary and opposition alignment. At the center of this battle is Chief Justice Cho Hee-de of the Supreme Court of Korea, who faces growing accusations from liberals regarding political bias and neglect of judicial neutrality.

Yet despite holding a commanding parliamentary majority, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) finds itself paralyzed by a tactical dilemma. Should lawmakers launch a legislative parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho, or should they proceed directly to impeachment? For foreign observers watching South Korea’s post-political crisis landscape, this internal struggle reveals a broader question: can a newly empowered ruling party dismantle entrenched institutional power without falling into the traps of legalistic hesitation and political calculation?

The Dilemma of Parliamentary Inquiries Without Impeachment

On paper, a parliamentary investigation (known in Korea as a gukjeong josa) is one of the National Assembly's most visible tools to examine state affairs and expose institutional wrongdoing. However, skepticism is rapidly growing inside the DPK regarding whether an inquiry targeting Chief Justice Cho can yield meaningful results on its own.

The primary flaw of a standalone parliamentary inquiry lies in witness attendance. Figures like Chief Justice Cho and senior judicial leaders are widely expected to refuse summonses to testify, invoking principles of judicial independence and the separation of powers. Without robust mechanisms to enforce attendance, an inquiry risks turning into an empty spectacle. DPK lawmakers such as Yoon Geun-young have publicly questioned the practical utility of an investigation under these conditions, warning that a toothless inquiry where key figures simply decline to appear will accomplish little more than granting the judiciary an unintended political exoneration.

Critics within the liberal camp argue that an investigation stripped of its core target is useless. Without a concurrent push for impeachment—a concrete legal and constitutional mechanism that carries real weight—a legislative inquiry risks lingering indecisively, exhausting public patience while failing to hold senior judicial officials accountable.

Echoes of 2004: How Past Hesitation Haunted the Liberals

To understand why this tactical divide triggers such visceral anxiety among Korean liberals, one must look back 22 years to the presidency of the late liberal icon Roh Moo-hyun. During that era, reformists sought to repeal the controversial National Security Act and overhaul key state institutions. However, lawmakers became bogged down in debates over compromise, legislative inquiries, and fear of public backlash. The reform momentum dissolved, yielding disappointing results that ultimately weakened President Roh's standing and left deep scars on the progressive movement.

DPK lawmaker Yang Bu-nam recently drew a direct line from that historical failure to today’s standoff over Chief Justice Cho. Yang warned that repeating the mistakes of two decades ago—relying on weak compromises and parliamentary inquiries while shying away from decisive political action—invites disastrous long-term consequences. In his view, pushing for a parliamentary investigation without simultaneously initiating impeachment proceedings risks handing conservative judicial figures an opportunity to launch a counteroffensive.

The electoral warnings are equally stark. In the 2004 general elections, the predecessor to today's DPK won a decisive 152-seat majority in the 300-seat National Assembly. Yet after four years of legislative hesitation, poll-watching, and diluted reform measures, the party collapsed to a humiliating 81 seats in the 2008 elections. For today's DPK leadership, the history lesson is clear: temporary approval rating anxieties should not dictate core political battles.

To break the current deadlock inside the party, former lawmaker Jung Bong-ju put forward a radical proposal: force all DPK lawmakers into a closed-door marathon session, confiscate their mobile phones, and keep them locked in a room for two or three days until a single, binding party position is reached. The goal is to eliminate media leaks, private posturing, and endless polling calculations in favor of unified action.

Legal Caution Versus Political Principle Inside the Ruling Party

The primary drag on decisive action comes from the DPK’s sizable contingent of lawyer-turned-politicians. These legal pragmatists argue against impeachment by pointing to political risks and technical hurdles. What if the Constitutional Court of Korea rejects the impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho? Wouldn't the resulting political backlash harm the Lee Jae-myung administration?

However, proponents of forceful action dismiss these concerns as political cowardice masked as legal prudence. In their view, politics is not a courtroom argument; it is a declaration of principles. When a chief justice is accused of severe dereliction of duty and systemic bias, failing to act out of fear of how judges might rule later signals weakness to the public.

Furthermore, time is running out. The legislative calendar imposes a strict cutoff. The DPK initially sought to resolve the issue by December 5, but that date passed without a consensus, pushing the firm decision deadline to December 12. If the party fails to act before the National Assembly transitions into the end-of-year holiday period, the political momentum behind holding Chief Justice Cho accountable will dissipate entirely.

Public Relations and Opposition Countermoves: The Case of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon

Even if judicial figures refuse to testify, proponents of a parliamentary investigation argue that the process holds immense value as a public relations battleground. When televised hearings showcase lawmakers like Seo Young-kyo and Kim Nam-keun of the DPK, or Hwang Un-ha of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, grilling empty chairs or dismantling judicial arguments, the public gains a clear view of the institution's resistance to oversight.

At the same time, the broader political landscape is complicated by maneuvers from the main opposition party, the conservative People Power Party (PPP). A notable example is Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent PPP figure who currently faces growing legal and political pressure over allegations involving monetary gifts ranging from 1 million to 2 million won (approximately $700 to $1,400) as well as controversial municipal initiatives like the Han River Bus project.

As oversight intensified, Mayor Oh hosted an appreciation dinner for PPP members of the National Assembly's Public Administration and Security Committee. Progressive commentators view such gatherings as tactical attempts to shore up party defense lines and blunt legislative scrutiny. Just as with the investigation into Chief Justice Cho, proponents of reform argue that aggressive public exposure—rather than quiet behind-the-scenes maneuvering—is the only way to compel accountability from senior officials.

What to Watch Next

The coming days will determine whether South Korea's ruling party can turn its numerical dominance into concrete institutional reform. As the political calendar moves toward mid-December, several key developments will indicate how this battle will unfold:

  • The December 12 DPK Caucus Decision: Watch whether the Democratic Party adopts a unified party line to simultaneously file an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-de alongside a parliamentary inquiry motion.
  • Chief Justice Cho’s Official Response: Pay attention to whether the Supreme Court chief justice issues a public statement or formally indicates whether he will comply with National Assembly summonses.
  • Constitutional Court Strategy: If an impeachment motion is passed by the National Assembly, track how quickly the Constitutional Court takes up the case and whether the court’s bench composition leads to a prolonged standoff.
  • Opposition Counter-strategy: Observe how the People Power Party responds in parliament, including potential filibusters or committee boycotts aimed at stalling both the judicial inquiry and municipal investigations into figures like Mayor Oh Se-hoon.
