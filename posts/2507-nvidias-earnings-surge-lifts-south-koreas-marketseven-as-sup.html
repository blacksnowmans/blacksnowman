Nvidia’s Earnings Surge Lifts South Korea’s Markets—Even as Supply Chain and China Risks Mount

November 19, 2026

On November 18, a single earnings report from California jolted South Korea’s sluggish stock market back to life. After days of drifting lower, Seoul’s benchmark KOSPI index jumped 1.99% to reclaim the psychologically important 5,000 mark, finishing the session near 5,060. Institutional investors and pension funds, which had been steadily unloading shares, abruptly pivoted back into the market as buyers, joined by returning foreign funds. The spark was Nvidia’s third-quarter earnings surprise—and what it confirmed about the relentless demand for artificial intelligence hardware.

For international investors, the speed of that reversal underscores an essential reality about South Korea’s equity market: few major economies are as tightly wired to the AI capital expenditure cycle. When Nvidia signals that order books remain full ahead of shipments for its next-generation Vera Rubin AI chip, the aftershocks are felt immediately in Seoul, where a handful of semiconductor and component titans drive a massive share of corporate earnings and market capitalization. Yet beneath the surface of November 18’s rally, South Korea’s chipmakers face a far more complicated landscape—one defined by fierce competition over next-generation memory, sagging prices for conventional chips, and widening geopolitical risk around China.

Why Seoul’s Market Moves on Nvidia’s Order Book

To understand why a US chip designer’s quarterly results could rescue Seoul’s stock market in a single afternoon, it helps to look at how South Korea sits inside the global AI supply chain. Nvidia designs the processors that power modern data centers, but it relies on an interlocking manufacturing network stretching across Taiwan, the United States, and South Korea to actually build and feed those systems. South Korea’s particular stronghold is memory—specifically High Bandwidth Memory, or HBM, a specialized form of stacked DRAM placed directly alongside AI processors to prevent data bottlenecks.

When Nvidia reported that AI chip demand continues to outpace supply and outlined plans to deepen partnerships across its manufacturing ecosystem, investors in Seoul treated the news as an earnings forecast for Korea’s largest listed companies. Samsung Electronics, the world’s largest memory maker and South Korea’s most heavily weighted stock, climbed 2.3%. SK Hynix, which has established itself as Nvidia’s primary supplier of advanced HBM chips, surged 4.2%. The optimism spilled beyond semiconductors into Korean robotics and AI-infrastructure stocks, lifting market-wide trading volume as domestic funds halted their recent selloff.

The reaction echoed moves in the United States, where shares of American memory rival Micron jumped 4.7% in after-hours trading to $712. In Seoul, market participants increasingly treat Micron’s stock price as a leading indicator for Samsung and SK Hynix. If Nvidia’s data-center growth is lifting Micron, the reasoning goes, revenue visibility for Korean memory suppliers should strengthen well into next year.

The $390 Billion Lock-In and the Race to HBM4

The most consequential detail for South Korean chipmakers was not simply that Nvidia beat third-quarter expectations, but how it plans to manage a deepening memory bottleneck. According to Nvidia’s disclosures, memory demand is running well ahead of available supply. To prevent future shortages from stalling its hardware rollout, the company is moving away from quarter-by-quarter purchasing toward long-term supply commitments, backed by roughly $390 billion in supply guarantees aimed at locking in capacity from Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

For Korean memory executives, long-term guarantees from Nvidia represent a crucial buffer in a historically boom-and-bust industry. Memory pricing is notoriously cyclical, and manufacturers regularly suffer steep losses when supply overtakes demand. Securing multi-year commitments for high-margin AI memory gives both Korean firms steadier cash flow—though their competitive positions look very different right now.

SK Hynix currently holds the enviable spot as Nvidia’s top memory partner, allowing it to prioritize lucrative AI orders while adjusting allocations for other customers to maximize profitability. Samsung Electronics, by contrast, is playing catch-up. It is aggressively expanding its HBM production capacity to deepen its presence in Nvidia’s supply chain and capture a share of the windfall. With the market already looking past current-generation HBM3E toward the next standard, HBM4, Nvidia’s positive signals regarding future supply from both Korean companies have intensified the race. Who delivers HBM4 first—and with reliable manufacturing yields—will likely determine industry leadership over the next two to three years.

Even as AI memory booms, falling spot prices for everyday chips used in smartphones and PCs are splitting the semiconductor market in two.

That divergence inside the memory market is becoming a central problem for investors. While demand for specialized AI memory remains tight, spot prices for conventional, general-purpose memory have been sliding amid sluggish recovery in consumer electronics like PCs and smartphones. Because Samsung Electronics carries a broader product portfolio exposed to traditional memory markets, its overall earnings face greater drag from weak spot prices than SK Hynix, whose tighter focus on HBM has amplified its upside during the AI buildout. Rising interest rates and broader macroeconomic uncertainty only sharpen that divide: not every chipmaker is rising on the same tide anymore.

Substrates, Governance Rumors, and the China Flashpoint

Nvidia’s expanding footprint in South Korea is also reshaping parts of the supply chain that rarely make international headlines. As AI chips grow larger and more power-hungry, the advanced substrates and packaging technologies that connect silicon to servers have become critical bottlenecks. Ahead of the November 18 market surge, Samsung Electro-Mechanics—an electronic components affiliate of the Samsung Group—revised its substrate supply contract with Nvidia, paving the way for expanded production lines for high-performance FC-BGA substrates. Alongside global packaging partners such as Amkor, Korean component makers are moving up the value chain from commodity suppliers to strategic partners in Nvidia’s hardware ecosystem.

Yet this deeper interdependence cuts both ways, exposing Korean suppliers to governance and geopolitical risks radiating out of Nvidia itself. In the run-up to and aftermath of the earnings release, media reports surfaced questioning Nvidia’s reliance on CEO Jensen Huang, citing personal scandal allegations and speculation over a potential resignation. While such key-man risk added volatility to trading, Nvidia’s leadership sought to reassure markets that the company’s operational trajectory does not hinge on a single executive—and investors in Seoul largely shrugged off the headlines, betting that global AI infrastructure spending is too entrenched to be derailed by executive drama.

A far more dangerous threat lies in Washington and Beijing. Nvidia is facing intensifying scrutiny over allegations—widely dubbed the "Alice Huang" scandal—that it helped connect Chinese tech giant Alibaba with an intermediary in 2022 to bypass US semiconductor export controls. Reports by broadcasters including the BBC and YTN indicate that Alice Huang also established a corporate entity in Japan amid suspicions of chip smuggling, and that China’s People’s Liberation Army has been seeking access to advanced Nvidia processors for AI weapons development. Notably, Nvidia moved to halt shipments of AI semiconductors to China despite holding US government export approvals, a step widely viewed as an effort to contain the political fallout.

For South Korea, this is not merely an American corporate controversy. Korean chipmakers and substrate suppliers remain deeply integrated into both US-led AI supply chains and Asian manufacturing networks, while still holding residual exposure to the Chinese market. If allegations of export-control evasion prompt Washington to impose stricter enforcement rules or widen its compliance net, South Korean firms closely tied to Nvidia could find themselves caught in the crossfire between US national security policy and their own regional supply lines.

What to Watch Next in Seoul’s Chip Trade

The November 18 rebound showed that Nvidia’s earnings power can still ignite a rally in Seoul, but sustaining momentum above KOSPI 5,000 will require more than one strong quarter from Santa Clara. With US market signals turning mixed—the Nasdaq edged up just 0.6% alongside cautious pre-market sentiment—domestic and foreign investors in Korea are facing a more selective environment. Over the coming months, three concrete developments will determine whether South Korea’s semiconductor rally holds or stalls:

  • The gap between HBM and commodity memory prices: Investors should watch whether sliding spot prices for PC and smartphone DRAM stabilize. If general-purpose memory continues to weaken while interest-rate pressures persist, earnings divergence between SK Hynix and broader-portfolio rivals like Samsung Electronics will widen.
  • Execution on HBM4 and substrate capacity: Tracking how quickly Samsung Electronics narrows SK Hynix’s lead on next-generation HBM4 qualification—and how rapidly Samsung Electro-Mechanics scales its FC-BGA substrate lines—will show whether Korea can capture the full value of Nvidia’s $390 billion supply push.
  • Regulatory fallout from the China export scandal and sector legal risks: Any tougher US export restrictions triggered by the Alice Huang allegations could directly complicate supply chains for Korean partners, while ongoing DRAM-related lawsuits involving Micron serve as a reminder that legal and compliance shocks can quickly unsettle an otherwise tight memory market.

For now, AI demand has given South Korea’s markets a powerful second wind. Whether that turns into a durable expansion—or another volatile cycle—depends on how skillfully Seoul’s tech giants navigate the widening split between booming AI silicon, sluggish legacy chips, and tightening US-China export politics.
