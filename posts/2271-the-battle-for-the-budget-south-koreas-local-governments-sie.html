The Battle for the Budget: South Korea’s Local Governments Siege the National Assembly

November 13, 2026

Inside a temporary "comprehensive situation room" set up just steps away from the National Assembly in Seoul, local government officials are working around the clock. This isn't a crisis response center for a natural disaster; it is a war room for the 2025 national budget. As the South Korean government's proposed spending plan moves through the legislative screening phase, the fate of thousands of projects—from high-speed transit lines to local welfare services—hangs in the balance. For mayors and governors, this is the most critical period of the year: if they don't secure the funds now, their regional agendas for the next twelve months are effectively dead on arrival.

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—who advocates for universal welfare and balanced regional development—the stakes are particularly high. Local governments are increasingly struggling with the "trap" of fiscal austerity, making this year's budget cycle not just an accounting exercise, but a survival mission for local economies.

Gyeonggi Province’s "Do or Die" Strategy

Gyeonggi Province, the most populous region surrounding Seoul, has launched a high-stakes campaign to secure over 5.4 trillion won (about $3.9 billion) in central government funding. To ensure this, the province’s First Vice Governor has taken the extraordinary step of taking up residence near the National Assembly. This "battle station" approach is designed to provide real-time responses to the Assembly’s Budget and Accounts Committee as they scrutinize every line item.

For Gyeonggi, this funding is essential for major infrastructure, climate change adaptation, and social safety nets. The province faces a unique political hurdle: the common perception that because it is a metropolitan area, it should be able to "fend for itself." Gyeonggi officials are pushing back hard, arguing that as the home to over one-quarter of South Korea’s population, investments in their region directly boost national competitiveness. They maintain that the 5.4 trillion won is the "minimum survival budget" required to keep the province’s massive population moving and supported.

Shifting the Burden: The Push for Welfare Reform

A major focus of this year's budget negotiations is the restructuring of welfare costs, which have long placed an outsized burden on local coffers. A prime example is the campaign to increase central government funding for long-term elderly care to 80%. Namyangju’s Mayor, Choi Hyun-deok, has been vocal in his support for this bill, noting that if it passes, his city could save approximately 40.8 billion won (about $29 million) in municipal funds—money that could be redirected toward creative local initiatives.

The argument is clear: welfare is a state responsibility, yet the current system forces local governments to drain their own budgets to cover the gaps. In the spirit of the Lee Jae-myung administration’s model of state-led welfare, local leaders are demanding that the central government assume its share. Meanwhile, lawmakers like Jang Jong-tae of the ruling Democratic Party are fighting to ensure that "ethical budgets"—such as support for the impoverished residents of jjokbangchon (tiny, low-rent cubicle rooms)—are not discarded in the name of fiscal efficiency. These small but vital allocations represent the true measure of a government’s priorities.

Regional Hubs and the Fight for Innovation

From the Honam region (Jeolla provinces) to the Yeongnam region (Gyeongsang provinces), local governments are competing for funds to jumpstart future-oriented industries. In Naju, city officials are pushing for an "Energy Cluster" centered around KENTECH (Korea Institute of Energy Technology). This aligns with the current administration's energy transition policy, but without the central government's "seed money," the dream of becoming a global energy hub remains just that—a dream.

However, the process is fraught with peril. A recent report highlighting that Busan had inadvertently omitted the "Southeast Region Investment Corporation" from its priority list served as a wake-up call to other regions. It reminded local administrations that in a landscape where every won is contested, administrative precision is just as important as political lobbying.

Cross-Party Cooperation and the Path Forward

Perhaps the most fascinating aspect of this year’s budget scramble is the pragmatism shown by the Daegu and Gyeongbuk (TK) regions. Despite being strongholds for the opposition People Power Party, local leaders have adopted a "one-team" approach, proactively meeting with the leadership of the ruling Democratic Party to secure bipartisan support for local projects. They recognize that under the current administration, the ruling party holds the keys to the budget, and political antagonism must be set aside for the sake of regional survival.

Similarly, Incheon Mayor Park Chan-dae is leading a high-profile push to secure feasibility approval for the Baengnyeong Airport, a project he argues is vital for both national security and the basic mobility rights of island residents. By hosting forums and engaging directly with the Ministry of Economy and Finance, Incheon is trying to frame their local needs as national imperatives.

As the process moves into the Budget and Accounts Committee’s subcommittee phase, the competition will reach a fever pitch. This is where the real "cutting" begins. With the ruling party aiming to protect welfare-centric spending and the opposition pushing for fiscal discipline, local governments must navigate this minefield without seeing their projects become casualties of partisan warfare.

What should observers watch next? The subcommittee sessions, which often run deep into the night, will determine which projects survive the final cut. The final result will be the 2025 budget "report card." For the residents of these regions, the outcome will decide whether their neighborhood receives funding for essential services or whether the local government will be forced to scale back promises made to its people. In the end, these aren't just figures on a ledger—they are the tangible reality of the lives of citizens across the country.
