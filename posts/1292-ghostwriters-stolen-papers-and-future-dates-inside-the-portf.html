Ghostwriters, Stolen Papers, and Future Dates: Inside the Portfolio Scandal That Shadowed South Korea’s Conservative Elite

October 19, 2026

During the sweltering confirmation hearings of May 2022, the National Assembly floor felt less like a forum for national legal policy and more like an academic integrity tribunal. Facing lawmakers was Han Dong-hoon, a star prosecutor tapped to head the Ministry of Justice under the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Han had been championed by conservatives as an unyielding crusader for the rule of law. Yet across the chamber, lawmakers brandished digital document properties, international journal printouts, and binders retrieved from community childcare facilities. What they laid out was not an argument over judicial philosophy, but an astonishing portfolio of fabricated credentials meticulously assembled for Han’s teenage daughter.

In South Korea, college entrance competition is not merely a milestone; it is an all-consuming meritocratic pressure cooker known as ipshi. The public acceptance of immense academic sacrifice rests on a single pillar: that the system, however brutal, remains fair. That illusion shattered as parliamentary questioning unspooled a dizzying web of ghostwritten academic articles, automated plagiarism exceeding 60 percent, impossible volunteer tallies, and an outsourced mobile application. While Han insisted that none of these credentials had been or would be used in domestic college admissions, dismissing them as mere "practice writings," the disclosures offered a devastating glimpse into how South Korea’s privileged elite manufacture international resumes.

The Kenyan Freelancer and the Metadata Trail

At the center of the initial inquiry was a volume of scholarly output that defied human biology. While attending the eleventh grade at an international school in Songdo, Incheon, Han’s daughter was credited with publishing a battery of English-language scholarly papers spanning medicine, advanced economics, and computer science, alongside multiple self-published electronic books. For a high school sophomore to independently master even one of these specialized domains would be extraordinary; to author across all of them while balancing a standard high school course load bordered on the miraculous.

When pressed on the chamber floor by lawmaker Kim Nam-kook on whether his daughter had actually written these papers, Han’s defense was uncharacteristically passive. "The pieces being referred to as papers are not really of paper quality," Han countered, downplaying them as two-to-six-page reports compiled as writing practice. But when asked point-blank whether his child had personally produced the text, Han retreated into the third person: "I was told that my child wrote them."

Investigative reporting by Kim Ga-yoon of the progressive daily The Hankyoreh revealed why a definitive claim of authorship was impossible. Examining the digital metadata embedded inside one of the research files uploaded under the student’s name, reporters located the document’s original creator tag: "Benson."

Benson was not a classmate or an international school tutor. He was an academic ghostwriter living in Kenya who offered coursework and essay-writing services on the gig-economy platform PeoplePerHour. When contacted directly by The Hankyoreh, Benson confirmed that he had written the entire paper himself. Digital forensics showed the file had been finalized on November 3, 2021; it was published on an academic platform under the student’s name just over three weeks later, on November 27. Confronted with this timeline during the hearing by lawmaker Kim Young-bae, Han stammered that his daughter had "never had any contact with or received help from a person named Benson," leaving the undeniable metadata dangling as an unresolved mystery.

Turnitin Warnings and the 61.9% Plagiarism Cut

Where papers were not outsourced to digital freelancers, they were assembled through wholesale duplication. Several other papers listing Han's daughter as sole or lead author were run through commercial plagiarism screening engines, yielding results that stunned academic ethicists. One paper demonstrated an astonishing 61.9 percent similarity index to published research by Italian and German scholars.

This was not a matter of careless attribution or poorly footnoted citations. Rather, extensive theoretical arguments, syntactic structures, and technical phrasing had been copied directly from foreign academics and repackaged as the original intellectual output of a high school student. These texts were subsequently posted to open-access repositories and predatory academic journals that conduct little to no peer review.

"Generally, if a similarity screening shows less than 20 percent, it passes. If it exceeds 20 percent, many journals will refuse to even send the manuscript out for peer review."
— Lee Hyo-bin, Executive Director, Council for University Research Ethics

Legitimate academic work submitted to global institutions relies on verification networks like Turnitin, while South Korean academia leans on CopyKiller and the Korea Citation Index (KCI) system. Master’s and doctoral theses routinely face immediate disqualification if their similarity indices cross standard thresholds. For a high school portfolio piece to hit 61.9 percent similarity without being flagged exposed a cynical calculation: that predatory journals would publish anything for a processing fee, and that few observers would ever bother to run automated checks on the text beneath an applicant’s glittering resume line.

Time Travel Volunteering and the $1,500 Outsourced App

The academic fabrication extended seamlessly into extracurricular service. Public promotional materials and applications had claimed an eye-popping 20,000 hours of community volunteer work. Simple arithmetic illustrates the absurdity: accumulating 20,000 hours requires ten continuous hours of volunteer labor every single day for roughly five and a half years. Han defended the number by explaining it represented the aggregate sum achieved by an entire student organization rather than his daughter alone. Yet this collective effort had been funneled into prestigious individual accolades, including an award from the Mayor of Seoul, an award from the Incheon Metropolitan Council Chairman, and a commendation from the Youth Activity Promotion Center.

To verify the integrity of the underlying records, investigative journalists from MBC’s flagship current-affairs program PD Note traveled to a community child center in Gyeonggi Province where Han’s daughter was registered as an English tutor. What the crew found in the physical binder was temporal impossibility. The journalists visited the center on June 9, 2022. Yet upon opening the attendance log, they found that dates weeks and months into the future—June 14, June 21, June 28, July 5, and July 26—were already neatly checked off, complete with the student’s signed confirmation that the service had been successfully performed.

The center’s supervisor stumbled through an explanation, offering that dates might have been "scheduled in advance," before pledging to issue an official response via public documentation. That clarification never arrived.

A parallel pattern emerged regarding "SHAREEE," an assistive mobile application designed to connect hearing- and visually impaired individuals with volunteer aides. The project had earned Han’s daughter a semifinalist finish at Technovation, a high-profile international technology contest for young women, framed publicly as an inspiring story of teenage coding for social good. But broadcast journalists noticed a domestic mobile phone number visible in one of the app’s demonstration videos. When called, the programmer who answered confirmed the project was a paid job:

"It was an outsourced contract. The payment was 2 million won (roughly $1,500). I was told it was a vacation homework assignment for the student. The mother kept pressuring me because the delivery was running behind schedule, mentioning that her sibling was a lawyer."

A Family Cartel Built for the Ivy League

The disclosures quickly outgrew a single household, revealing a transatlantic assembly line for elite admissions. Han’s sister-in-law, a woman surnamed Jin residing in Silicon Valley, coordinated closely with the family to construct parallel resumes for her two daughters, Choi Yun-seo and Choi Su-jin (both pseudonyms). Across multiple continents, the cousins traded lead-author spots on papers, co-founded digital journals during the COVID-19 pandemic to publish each other’s articles, and entered identical contests.

The methods employed in California mirrored the corner-cutting in Seoul:

  • Falsified School Affiliation: When entering the Scholastic Writing Awards, one cousin attended Monta Vista High School, a competitive public high school in Cupertino. On her entry, however, she registered her status as "homeschooled" and named her mother as her instructor, circumventing the stiffer regional competition among high-achieving public school peers.
  • Bogus Institutional Ties: For the 2019 American Association of Physics Teachers (AAPT) competition, the sisters listed their institutional affiliation as South Korea's Gachon University. Officials at Gachon later confirmed that while the students had once attended an affiliated youth gifted program years earlier, they held zero formal affiliation with the university in 2019.
  • Brazen Plagiarism of U.S. Faculty: In another instance, the cousins published a paper on South Korea’s candlelight vigil protests using Python data visualization. Sangwon Lee, a professor at New Mexico State University who had published an authoritative 2018 study on the exact same topic, discovered that the introduction of their paper was an almost verbatim duplicate of his own. Tellingly, while the students copied his prose to reach a 46 percent plagiarism match, they entirely omitted the statistical modeling necessary to test the hypotheses, lacking the technical competence to forge the underlying empirical work.

The Residual Cost of Selective Meritocracy

The political context surrounding this scandal has shifted dramatically. The administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, under which Han Dong-hoon rose to the Cabinet and later took the helm of the conservative People Power Party, collapsed under public outcry and constitutional crisis. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and remains imprisoned. Today, South Korea is governed by President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea, while the People Power Party has been relegated to the opposition benches.

Yet the institutional questions raised by the Han portfolio remain unresolved. The defense that these elaborate projects were merely "informal practice" that was "never submitted to domestic Korean colleges" completely ignores the reality of global elite education, where predatory publishing, offshore ghostwriting networks, and outsourced coding are marketed directly to wealthy families seeking admission to prestigious Western universities.

For ordinary South Korean students who endure fourteen-hour study days at neighborhood cram schools (hagwons) to gain a single percentage point on the national college entrance exam, the spectacle left an enduring cynicism. As the National Assembly and educational watchdogs debate future oversights on secondary-school research ethics, the case stands as a permanent case study in how easily the language of merit and fairness can be converted into a high-priced commodity.
