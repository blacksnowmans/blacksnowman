From Itaewon to Yeouido: How Online Toxicity and Factional Wars Are Reshaping South Korean Politics

October 19, 2026

For nearly two years following the October 2022 crowd crush in Seoul’s nightlife district of Itaewon, an anonymous internet user sat behind a keyboard, systematically uploading photographs of young victims who had suffocated to death. Across extreme web forums and open blog posts, this individual posted 3,624 separate times, claiming that the grieving parents were merely paid actors, that the tragedy was a staged hoax, and that the victims had brought death upon themselves. When prosecutors finally tracked down the culprit—a man in his 60s—and hauled him into court, he offered no contrition. Instead, he complained to the judge that facing trial at all was an intolerable injustice against his personal freedom of expression.

That sense of entitlement is meeting an unprecedented judicial wall. South Korean courts, historically notoriously lenient toward online defamation, have begun handing down actual prison terms to cyber-abusers targeting national tragedies. Yet just as the justice system cracks down on this vitriol, the tactics pioneered on the far-right fringe have metastasized. Today, this exact brand of dehumanizing rhetoric has migrated into mainstream political discourse, tearing through the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and threatening the political stability of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

No More Slaps on the Wrist: South Korea Jails Tragedy Trolls

South Korean courts have long treated cyber defamation as a minor infraction, typically dispatched with modest fines or suspended sentences. For malicious creators and aggressive forum users, these financial penalties were simply an operational cost, easily offset by viewer clicks and digital donations. The human cost, however, was borne entirely by bereaved families who, in the deepest throes of grief, were subjected to relentless digital harassment.

That lax era appears to be ending. State prosecutors recently demanded an eighteen-month prison sentence for the man who authored 3,624 malicious posts mocking victims of both the 2022 Itaewon disaster and the 2014 Sewol ferry sinking. This prosecution follows an aggressive initiative launched in July of last year, when the National Police Agency’s National Investigation Headquarters established a dedicated Secondary Victimization Crime Investigation Division. The specialized unit has already produced direct convictions:

  • A man in his 60s, surnamed Cho, was sentenced to six months in prison after publishing 362 posts and videos falsely alleging that Itaewon victims died of narcotics overdoses and comparing the deceased to sex dolls.
  • Another man in his 50s was sentenced to one year behind bars and immediately taken into court custody for sustained, malicious defamation targeting bereaved families from both disasters.

In these rulings, the Seoul Western District Court drew a decisive line between the constitutional guarantee of free speech and actionable criminal violence. Weaponizing falsehoods to destroy the social reputation of the dead and torment the living does not qualify as protected opinion. For the families, who testified to years of debilitating psychological trauma and humiliation, these jail terms set a vital judicial standard: society will no longer subsidize cruelty in the name of free discourse.

The Metastasizing Playbook: From Ilbe to 'New Ilbe' Factions

To understand the depth of this cruelty, one must understand the toxic phrase sichepari—literally "peddling corpses." The slur was popularized by Ilgan Best, or Ilbe, South Korea’s notorious far-right message board. During the aftermath of the Sewol ferry disaster, which killed 304 people, mostly high school students, Ilbe users staged a notorious "binge-eating strike" directly in front of starving parents who were fasting in central Seoul to demand an independent inquiry. The far-right weaponized sichepari to frame any call for public accountability as a cynical financial shakedown.

The tragedy of contemporary South Korean politics is that this vile rhetorical playbook has crossed party lines. Today, online spaces nominally loyal to the progressive establishment—such as the DC Inside discussion boards dedicated to party matters—are mirroring the exact tropes once exclusive to the far right. Observers have begun labeling these radicalized partisan hubs as "New Ilbe."

Inside these digital echo chambers, revered progressive figures are openly subjected to the same reactionary insults. Anonymous commenters attack the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation, named after the iconic late progressive president, accusing former prime ministers and civic leaders of engaging in tax evasion and "corpse-peddling." Former President Moon Jae-in and prominent progressive commentators find themselves routinely vilified with the precise vocabulary engineered by past intelligence agency smear operations.

This dynamic operates on a pervasive inversion of victimhood. Radicalized factions orchestrate digital pile-ons, dispatching coordinated swarms to target anyone inside the party who questions their chosen figures, such as lawmaker Lee Yeon-ju. Yet the moment these agitators face standard political scrutiny or legal accountability, they immediately pivot, crying political persecution and pleading, "Stop bullying me." This weaponized cynicism corrodes genuine intra-party debate, transforming ideological diversity into a scorched-earth tribal contest.

Sectarian Infiltration and the War over Historical Blame

Nowhere is this factional cannibalism clearer than in the explosive controversy over Shincheonji, the secretive, apocalyptic pseudo-Christian sect widely scrutinized for covertly infiltrating mainstream political institutions to manipulate party primaries and public policy.

Historically, the entanglement between Shincheonji and the political establishment belongs squarely to the conservative lineage. Internal Shincheonji youth wing documents dating back to April 2003 explicitly instructed members to covertly support Suh Chung-won, then-leader of the Grand National Party (the direct ancestor of today's main conservative opposition, the People Power Party). The instructions were explicit: infiltrate quietly, disguise your religious identity, and alter public sentiment online. This illicit relationship persisted through the conservative administrations of Lee Myung-bak, Park Geun-hye, and into the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was ultimately impeached, removed from office, and sent to prison.

Yet instead of focusing collective scrutiny on this documented history, warring factions within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea have turned the accusation inward. Certain internal groups have weaponized the sect's name to purge their own internal rivals, tarring veteran figures like Jung Cheong-rae, Choi Min-hee, Lee Sung-yoon, and Han Min-soo with baseless claims of participating in an anti-party "Shincheonji alliance." By utilizing such smear tactics against fellow progressives, these factions not only obscure the real political power players tied to the sect, but also hand an unearned pass to the conservative opposition.

Stagnation at the Helm: The Crisis Facing Party Leader Kim Min-seok

This internal chaos unfolds against a backdrop of collapsing confidence in the Democratic Party’s official leadership. Just fifty days into his tenure as party chairman, Kim Min-seok faces mounting criticism as party approval ratings plummet. Despite an initial pledge to lift the party's numbers by ten percentage points within his first three months, the party has instead plunged by eight points, shedding critical support across its traditional southwestern Honam stronghold, key battlegrounds in metropolitan Seoul, and younger voters.

The party's slide reflects a growing perception of bureaucratic complacency and cultural tone-deafness. A flashpoint occurred recently at a local cultural festival, in what critics have dubbed the "Choco incident." Party leaders arriving to greet the crowd unceremoniously pushed a performing artist off the stage mid-set so that political dignitaries could monopolize the microphone. To the younger demographic and core supporters, the spectacle epitomized an arrogant, dated political culture that treats public art and civic life as mere backdrops for party protocol.

Worse still, friction between the party leadership and the presidential office has broken into the open. Commentators and legal figures, including attorney Noh Young-hee, have reported that President Lee Jae-myung has voiced acute frustration with Chairman Kim's passive leadership style. With the Blue House pressing for ambitious governance reforms and social stability, the ruling party apparatus appears trapped in factional infighting and self-serving maneuvering. The early, ill-timed presidential posturing by veteran figures like Song Young-gil has only reinforced the impression of a leadership apparatus distracted by internal ambition rather than focused on governing.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea balances high-stakes governance with digital fragmentation, several concrete markers will reveal whether the political establishment can arrest this slide:

  • Sentencing Precedents: Watch the Seoul Western District Court's upcoming ruling in the case of the 3,624 defamatory posts. A confirmed, non-suspended prison term will solidify a legal boundary against secondary trauma and set a benchmark for future digital harassment cases.
  • Intra-Party Disciplinary Measures: Observe whether the Democratic Party leadership formally investigates coordinated digital harassment rings operating within its affiliated community boards, or continues to tolerate extremist rhetoric that targets its own historic leadership.
  • The Presidential Office-Party Dynamic: As President Lee Jae-myung pushes his administration's legislative agenda, watch how the Blue House manages its relationship with Chairman Kim Min-seok. With local elections on the horizon, failure to stabilize the party's declining approval ratings will inevitably accelerate calls for leadership reorganization in Yeouido.
