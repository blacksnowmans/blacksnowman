Ideological Debates, Merger Calculations, and Moral Warfare: South Korea’s Ruling Coalition Faces Internal Fractures

November 01, 2026

In the quiet corridors of Yeouido—Seoul’s political epicenter—electoral victory rarely guarantees lasting tranquility. Following the decisive shift in power that installed President Lee Jae-myung in the presidential office and elevated the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DP) to the ruling party, the immediate imperative of opposition unity has given way to internal jockeying. Freed from the immediate crisis of wrestling power away from the previous administration, the broader ruling bloc is confronting a classic dilemma: how to maintain cohesion when ideological factions, ambitious figures, and smaller coalition allies all begin mapping out their own political futures.

The intricate maneuverings within both the ruling majority and the conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), came into sharp focus on October 31. From philosophical spats over party orthodoxy to rigid merger deadlines and sharp ethics battles, South Korea’s political landscape is entering a turbulent phase of internal reorganization. What appears on the surface to be routine daily politics is, in reality, a high-stakes battle over who will control the direction of governance and command the political center ahead of future national elections.

Ideological Squabbles and the 100-Day Clock

The immediate catalyst for intra-party debate was a series of pointed criticisms from Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal author, commentator, and former minister whose opinions carry significant weight among the Democratic Party's core base. Rhyu’s blunt assessment of the party's current posture stirred discomfort within the ranks, leading some commentators, such as political analyst Ha Heon-ki and former lawmaker Park Won-suk, to accuse figures like Rhyu and influential broadcaster Kim Ou-joon of stoking factional divisions at a moment that demands unity.

Recognizing the danger of an open feud among influential opinion-makers, Representative Jung Chung-rae, a senior DP lawmaker and influential party voice, stepped in to calm the waters. Drawing on South Korean historical memory, Jung offered a broad philosophical defense of internal debate: "Even during the independence movement against Japanese colonial rule, there was never just a single line of strategy." By invoking the anti-colonial struggle, Jung argued that constructive critique should not be condemned as factional sabotage or internal betrayal, but embraced as proof of a healthy, pluralistic movement.

Yet Jung’s message was not merely conciliatory; it contained a sharp tactical warning directed at his own party’s executive leadership. Turning his attention to DP leader Kim Min-seok, Jung publicly established a strict timeline for evaluating the current party management. "The leadership structure under Kim Min-seok will be judged once it completes its first 100 days in office," Jung noted. He emphasized that leadership performance must be measured by hard metrics—specifically, raising party approval ratings by 10 percentage points and thoroughly resolving persistent public controversies, including lingering allegations tied to fringe religious groups like Shincheonji.

Most intriguing to Yeouido insiders was Jung’s explicit hint regarding his own future. By stating that he was carefully considering "what banner to raise and what role to play" after the 100-day evaluation period, Jung signaled that while he currently supports the leadership, he is keeping his options open to step forward as an alternative power center should the party stall in public opinion polls.

The Merger Dilemma: Absorption versus Autonomy

Beyond internal party management, the ruling bloc faces a thornier structural question: its relationship with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the progressive satellite party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. During the presidential campaign and its aftermath, the two parties maintained a tight tactical alliance. Now, pressure is mounting to formalize that relationship into a single political party.

Representative Jung Chung-rae put a concrete timetable on the table, declaring that the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party "must complete their merger by January at the very latest." Proponents of a quick merger argue that consolidating the two camps early in the year is essential to present a unified front, streamline parliamentary operations, and prevent unnecessary friction ahead of upcoming local elections.

However, the drive for a rapid fusion is meeting stiff resistance. Critics within progressive intellectual circles warn that a forced, mechanical union risks alienating voters who supported Cho Kuk’s party specifically because it offered a sharper, more unyielding reform agenda than the mainstream Democratic Party. Pushing for an immediate merge based solely on political arithmetic, critics argue, mimics the opportunistic party mergers historically seen in conservative politics.

Cho Kuk himself has signaled that his party will not be easily swallowed whole. Striking a cautious and independent tone, Cho publicly rejected the notion of automatic unity: "Holding a Democratic Party membership card does not automatically make someone a true reform candidate." Cho insisted that before any discussions of formal integration can take place, both parties must clearly define their shared long-term objectives rather than simply calculating seat counts.

To back up his rhetoric with grassroots power, Cho has been actively building an independent regional network. He recently opened the Rebuilding Korea Party’s very first local constituency committee office in Pyeongtaek, an industrial hub south of Seoul where Cho previously suffered a parliamentary election loss. Cho revealed that over 194 days of living in the city, he completed 220 local public engagements, ranging from community volunteering to site visits. Simultaneously, in the traditional progressive stronghold of the Jeonnam and Gwangju regions, his party convened emergency policy roundtables to address regional school meal funding. By establishing local footholds, Cho is signaling to the Democratic Party that his party possesses real organizational depth and will not accept an unconditional absorption.

Bipartisan Respite and the Shadow of Moral Warfare

While intra-bloc rivalries simmered, the legislative floor saw brief, rare flashes of bipartisan cooperation. Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok and Jang Dong-hyeok, head of the main opposition People Power Party, set aside partisan combat to sign a joint pledge supporting the 2027 Seoul World Youth Day (WYD). Recognizing the massive international scope of the Catholic youth gathering, both party leaders promised full parliamentary backing to ensure the event's success. The meeting even featured lighthearted banter, with Jang joking that he had "asked God, who advised him to wait for the Democratic Party," briefly easing the tense atmosphere in the National Assembly.

A similar moment of shared solemnity occurred around the remembrance of the tragic Itaewon crowd crush, where both sides of the aisle reaffirmed their commitment to investigating systemic safety failures and preventing future disasters. But such moments of unity remain fleeting exceptions in a political climate dominated by intense ethics battles.

The fragile peace collapsed as severe moral controversies hit the ruling party. The formal indictment of DP lawmaker Jang Gyeong-tae on sexual harassment charges provided the opposition People Power Party with potent political ammunition. Conservative leaders launched a coordinated offensive, demanding Jang’s immediate resignation and his expulsion from the National Assembly.

Prominent opposition figures escalated their attacks against the administration’s broader ethics record. Former PPP leader Han Dong-hoon publicly questioned whether the Democratic Party leadership took the public for granted, while Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon accused the Lee Jae-myung administration of attempting to bring the judiciary under political control. The moral pressure on the DP was further compounded by internal disciplinary troubles, as the party's own election management committee issued an official warning to lawmaker Moon Jeong-bok for campaign rule violations during internal party elections.

The DP responded aggressively to defend its ground. DP lawmaker Yang Bu-nam took direct aim at recent court rulings favoring conservative figures—such as the acquittal of PPP lawmaker Kim Gi-hyeon—and reignited criticism over past uninvestigated controversies involving high-profile luxury gifts, asking whether multi-million-won luxury items were now considered mere "polite gestures." This cross-fire over judicial fairness highlights a grim reality: with public distrust toward both major parties remaining high, ethics investigations and judicial rulings have become the primary weapons for political leverage.

Opposition Realignment and the Ghost of Martial Law Past

For the opposition People Power Party, capitalizing on the ruling party’s vulnerabilities is a matter of political survival. Facing high public disapproval ratings that reflect lingering public anger over past conservative governance, the PPP is focused heavily on internal re-organization and grassroots consolidation. PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok led party leaders to 충남 (Chungnam, South Chungcheong Province) to host a large-scale training rally for party members in Gongju, Buyeo, and Cheongyang, aiming to secure the vital central region vote.

Concurrently, the PPP is attempting to project policy competence by promoting its rising stars. Proportional representative Lee So-hee was awarded the party's "Outstanding Lawmaker of the Week" for her performance during parliamentary audits, while former provincial assemblyman Go Jun-ho was recruited into the party's central media committee to sharpen its public messaging.

Meanwhile, the Democratic Party is using its executive power and legislative majority to press forward on governance agendas and historical justice. President Lee Jae-myung publicly offered encouragement to Minister of Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs) Lee So-young while emphasizing the need for robust preventive oversight within his cabinet. On the legislative front, the ruling party advanced healthcare safety measures designed to protect both patients and medical professionals during malpractice disputes.

At the same time, the ruling party maintains a firm focus on settling accounts with the previous political era. DP lawmakers launched an aggressive inquiry directed at Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, demanding full transparency regarding internal judicial deliberations on the night of the unconstitutional martial law declaration decreed by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol before his impeachment.

With Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol currently imprisoned following his landmark removal from office, ruling party figures have shown no inclination to soften their rhetoric. Former minister and current DP lawmaker Choomi-ae publicly described Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s attempt to mobilize troops and subvert the National Assembly as "more violent and reckless than the military dictatorship of Chun Doo-hwan," calling for the maximum legal penalties available under the law. By framing the current judicial reform push as a necessary purge of anti-constitutional remnants, the DP seeks to maintain moral high ground even as it navigates its own internal scandals.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea’s political parties recalibrate their strategies, several key indicators will determine whether the ruling coalition can stabilize its power base or if it will devolve into protracted factional warfare:

  • The 100-Day Mark for Kim Min-seok: Watch whether DP leader Kim Min-seok can deliver the 10-point bump in party approval ratings he implicitly promised. If the party's numbers remain stagnant, expect Representative Jung Chung-rae and other party heavyweights to initiate open moves for internal restructuring.
  • Cho Kuk’s January Counter-Strategy: As the proposed January merger deadline approaches, track whether the Rebuilding Korea Party accepts a formal alliance framework or chooses to field independent candidates in local elections to preserve its distinct identity.
  • The Escalation of Judicial Battles: Pay close attention to the court proceedings involving DP lawmaker Jang Gyeong-tae, as well as the DP's ongoing legislative pressure on Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae regarding the actions of the judiciary during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s martial law decree.
