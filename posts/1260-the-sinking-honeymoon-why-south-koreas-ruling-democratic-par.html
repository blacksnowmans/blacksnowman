The Sinking Honeymoon: Why South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party Is Stumbling Into Crisis

October 18, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, the atmosphere surrounding South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) should theoretically be one of triumph. Having reclaimed executive power and installed President Lee Jae-myung in office following the historic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the progressive establishment entered this governing cycle with sweeping reform promises. Yet less than two months into the tenure of party leader Kim Min-seok, whispers of panic are echoing across committee rooms. Talk of an early collapse into an emergency steering committee is no longer just opposition chatter; it is being openly contemplated by the party's own rank and file.

For observers looking at South Korea from abroad, political drama is hardly unusual. However, the current turbulence inside the ruling camp does not stem from external conservative assaults. Instead, it is being fueled by an escalating cycle of unforced procedural errors, paralyzing factional warfare, and an insular bureaucratic culture inside both party headquarters and the presidential office. In a political system where the ruling party must act as the primary engine room for presidential policy, Kim Min-seok’s early stumble offers a case study in how rapid overexpansion and factional hostility can drain a governing mandate before it even gets off the ground.

Fractures at the Core: Gaffes, Feuds, and a Sinking Base

To grasp the depth of the current unease, one must look at how quickly petty managerial missteps have escalated into questions of basic competence. What should have been routine party gatherings have repeatedly devolved into public relations headaches. At a recent flagship party event meant to rally grassroots supporters, organizers abruptly cut off an invited singer's live performance midway through, sparking immediate public embarrassment and intense recriminations among the organizers. While a botched musical stage might seem trivial, in the hyper-online, image-conscious ecosystem of South Korean politics, it symbolized an administration whose leadership appeared unable to manage its own schedule, let alone complex state affairs.

Far more damaging has been a vicious internal legal brawl touching upon the controversial religious sect Shincheonji. Rather than projecting moral authority and unity, senior party figures—including lawmakers Park Sun-won and Choe Kang-wook—became entangled in public accusations and formal complaints regarding the authenticity of leaked phone logs. The spectacle of ruling party figures dragging each other to investigators over fringe factional disputes alienated voters who expected the progressive majority to focus squarely on pressing bread-and-butter economic legislation and judicial overhauls.

The fallout among veteran party elders has been immediate and biting. Senior statesmen who guided the party through the liberal administrations of Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in have gone public with unusually blunt assessments, warning that it is impossible to give Kim’s early leadership a passing grade. Rather than building coalitions and soothing post-election anxieties, the leadership has allowed internal communication channels to freeze over. When senior lawmakers begin distancing themselves from their own party boss within eight weeks of an election, the institutional foundation of the parliamentary majority begins to crack.

Proliferation of Task Forces and the Shadow of the Leadership Race

The structural flaw in Kim Min-seok’s approach, according to party staffers and lawmakers, is a tendency toward overambition without follow-through. Eager to project an image of hyper-efficient reform, Kim launched dozens of ad hoc committees and specialized task forces (TFs) covering virtually every policy domain. Insiders describe this as a "department store" style of party management: glittering storefronts with an overwhelming catalog of initiatives, but almost zero inventory delivered to completion.

Party working-level aides report severe exhaustion as staff members are pulled between multiple under-resourced TFs. Crucial legislative battles—from public welfare support amid stubborn inflation to broader judicial and prosecutorial restructuring—frequently stalled because the leadership shifted focus to the next daily headline. An analysis by public opinion research firm WinGKorea Consulting found that during Kim’s first month in office, negative public mentions of his leadership outnumbered positive ones by roughly six to one. Aside from a single day when Kim delivered a formal keynote address to the National Assembly, party messaging was entirely consumed by historical mudslinging with conservative People Power Party (PPP) lawmaker Han Dong-hoon, controversial remarks referencing former President Roh Moo-hyun’s impeachment, and bitter confirmation hearing squabbles.

Compounding this managerial drift is the unresolved trauma of the Democratic Party's national convention. Kim won the party chairmanship in a fierce contest against rival candidate Chung Chung-rae. The race produced bitter animosity between loyalists of President Lee Jae-myung (the pro-Myung faction) and backers of Chung (the pro-Cheong faction). Although former President Moon Jae-in and President Lee both sent explicit messages warning that unity brings victory while division guarantees defeat, the convention failed to heal the rift.

Crucially, three out of the five elected members of the DPK’s Supreme Council—the supreme decision-making body of the party—are close allies of Chung. Rather than seeking compromise with this formidable intra-party minority, Kim’s inner circle has frequently sought to isolate them. The result is a Supreme Council that resembles a factional trench war, where routine agenda items trigger shouting matches and public posturing, severely hobbling the party’s capacity to coordinate with the executive branch.

A Diplomatic Stumble: How a 'Golf Task Force' Exposed Bureaucratic Paralysis

The vulnerabilities of the ruling bloc are not confined to the National Assembly. Across town at the presidential office (historically referred to as the Blue House, or Cheong Wa Dae), a major strategic communications disaster underscored a shared institutional weakness: the disappearance of candid internal counsel.

As the administration prepared for an upcoming high-stakes bilateral summit with U.S. President Donald Trump, diplomatic planners naturally sought ways to establish personal rapport. Given Trump’s well-known affinity for golf, establishing a working group to prepare logistical and protocol details for potential rounds on the green was standard diplomatic tradecraft. The catastrophe lay in how the presidential office chose to announce it to the public: by literally naming the working team the "Blue House Golf Task Force."

In a domestic environment marked by high inflation, sluggish consumer demand, and deep economic anxiety, publicizing a "Golf TF" read as staggering tone-deafness. Political analysts immediately pounced, questioning how professional aides could fail at the most elementary rule of government communications. A team meant to safeguard South Korea’s national interests in trade and defense negotiations with Washington was instantly caricatured as an executive leisure club.

The blunder landed right as the second presidential secretariat took shape under new Chief of Staff Lim Gwang-hyun, succeeding outgoing chief Kang Hoon-sik. The episode laid bare a toxic bureaucratic culture where civil servants and presidential secretaries focus entirely on reading the mood of top superiors rather than speaking truth to power. In corporate South Korea, market signals force rapid accountability. In public bureaucracies where the leader wields rigid, top-down control, officials often choose self-preservation over candor. No aide felt empowered to step forward and point out the obvious: that branding a state planning team around golf during an economic crunch was public relations suicide.

Echoes of the Past: Why Grip-and-Control Politics Threatens the Lee Administration

This organizational silence has historical parallels that deeply worry progressive veterans. During the Roh Moo-hyun administration, key figures such as former minister Rhyu Si-min came within inches of forging a historic bipartisan compromise to eliminate the most draconian clauses of South Korea's controversial National Security Act. That breakthrough fell apart when rigid factions within the presidential circle, fearful of offending their core base, torpedoed the deal. The ensuing deadlock alienated moderate voters, fueled years of partisan trench warfare, and ultimately crippled that administration’s broader reform agenda.

Today, the polling data suggests the Democratic Party is drifting into a similarly dangerous corridor:

  • Plummeting support: In a recent survey conducted by pollster Realmeter, Democratic Party approval dropped sharply by 7.0 percentage points to 35.5%—the lowest figure recorded since President Lee Jae-myung assumed office.
  • Opposition resurgence: The conservative People Power Party climbed to 41.3%, pulling off an unexpected reversal of fortune against the ruling majority.
  • Defection of the core base: Most alarmingly for party strategists, voters in their 40s (traditionally the progressive movement’s most steadfast demographic anchor) saw a 17 percentage point collapse in support, while self-identified progressive voters dropped by 14 percentage points.

Having witnessed the abuse of state power, eventual impeachment, and subsequent prison sentence handed down to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, progressive voters have little patience for complacency or amateurism from their own side. When a ruling party squanders its mandate on factional infighting and cosmetic task forces while the presidential office trips over basic diplomatic messaging, voters withdraw their enthusiasm rapidly.

For Kim Min-seok and the Blue House secretariat, the path forward requires a fundamental shift from control to consolidation. Kim must stop launching uncoordinated policy initiatives and instead focus legislative capital on two or three decisive reform priorities. More importantly, he must reconcile with the Chung Chung-rae faction inside his own Supreme Council rather than attempting to govern around them. Within the presidential office, Chief of Staff Lim Gwang-hyun must foster an environment where aides can kill poorly framed initiatives before they become public embarrassments. In South Korean politics, where public sentiment shifts with devastating speed, the window to correct course before the electorate delivers an irreversible verdict is closing fast.
