The Battle for Prosecution Reform: Why South Korea’s "Public Prosecution Office" Plan is Sparking Outrage

September 10, 2026

If you have been watching the news in South Korea lately, you might feel a sense of exhaustion. At the heart of the national discourse is a familiar, frustrating cycle: the long-promised "Prosecution Reform"—the effort to limit the immense, unchecked power of the South Korean Public Prosecutors' Office—appears to be collapsing under the weight of backroom maneuvering and bureaucratic sleight-of-hand. Despite decades of pressure to reform, this massive, entrenched organization seems to have a supernatural ability to survive every change of government, protecting its own interests while the public watches, helpless.

Recent revelations regarding the proposed organizational structure for a new "Public Prosecution Office" (a body intended to separate investigation from indictment) have laid bare a calculated effort to maintain the status quo. What was meant to be a step toward curbing prosecutorial overreach is, in practice, turning into a shell game designed to preserve the existing power structure.

The Numbers Game: Preserving Power Under the Guise of Efficiency

The contradiction is stark. Currently, there are about 580 criminal court divisions across South Korea, but only about 300 prosecutors assigned to public trial duties. Simply put, there aren't enough prosecutors in court to handle the workload, yet the prosecution continues to demand more staff. While the actual number of investigators on the ground is decreasing—largely because the mandate for "supplementary investigations" and "special investigations" (often criticized as tools for political targeting) has been scaled back—the number of prosecutors remains bloated or is even increasing.

The concern here is fundamental: if the new Public Prosecution Office is supposed to be an organization without investigative powers, why is it fighting so hard to retain its current staff levels? Critics argue that the prosecution is using the excuse of a 140,000-case backlog to justify keeping its existing personnel. Even as the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee has pushed for a transparent audit of investigative manpower, the prosecution has navigated the cracks in the system to protect its "rice bowl"—the colloquial Korean term for job security and institutional influence.

The logic is simple: if downsizing is legally difficult, at least half of these prosecutors should be reassigned to trial duties in court. Instead, the current plan seems to be a deliberate deception that keeps the investigation-commanding departments fully staffed while ignoring the critical need for trial-focused prosecutors.

The "Cunning Timetable" and the Legislative "Slam Dunk"

The timeline for the proposed structure of the new Public Prosecution Office is equally suspicious. The proposal was designated at a vice-ministerial meeting on September 10, approved by the Cabinet on the 15th, and slated for an October 2 launch. By rushing this process—effectively truncating a 40-day legislative notice period down to just a couple of business days—those behind the plan clearly intended to prevent any meaningful scrutiny or public debate.

The timing was impeccable for the establishment: the President was abroad on a diplomatic tour, the Minister of Justice position was vacant, and the political scene was distracted by other controversies. Even the Minister of the Interior and Safety admitted that the schedule was too tight to allow for proper coordination with the Ministry of Justice, acknowledging that a re-announcement might be necessary. This "hit-and-run" legislative tactic was only possible because of a specific provision inserted by the Ministry of Justice, which allows current prosecutors to be transferred to the new agency while respecting their "personal preferences." This effectively ensures that no one is forced out, rendering the "separation of investigation and indictment" a hollow slogan.

The Prosecution Cartel’s Survival Strategy: By embedding a single clause in the fine print of the new legislation, the legal establishment has effectively neutralized the spirit of reform. They are preserving their organizational 100% while pretending to shift gears, a maneuver that strikes at the very heart of democratic accountability.

The "Pro-Yoon" Influence and the Committee of Conflict

The composition of the Candidate Recommendation Committee for the head of the new investigative agency is another point of contention. Of the nine members, three are former prosecutors. Given that the entire purpose of the reform is to limit the power of former and current prosecutors, this is a clear conflict of interest. Many of these figures were part of the top leadership during the rise of President Yoon Suk-yeol, who is himself a former Prosecutor General.

Opposition lawmakers, including Park Eun-jung and Kim Yong-min, have been vocal in their criticism. They argue that it is absurd to expect a truly neutral leader to emerge from a committee stacked with individuals who have deep ties to the current administration’s prosecutorial inner circle. One potential candidate even has a history of involvement in high-profile cases involving the "Channel A" scandal and the exit-ban controversy surrounding former Vice Minister of Justice Kim Hak-eui. While these cases resulted in acquittals, the candidate’s career path raises significant questions about whether they can conduct an impartial investigation that touches the "sacred" areas of the current power structure.

What to Watch Next

The struggle over the Public Prosecution Office is not merely a bureaucratic dispute; it is a battle over whether South Korea can truly move past a history where the prosecution acts as the final arbiter of political power.

For foreign observers, keep an eye on these three indicators:

  • Legislative Scrutiny: Watch for whether the National Assembly can force a revision of the "supplementary" clauses that allow the mass transfer of investigative staff. If these clauses remain, the "reform" is functionally dead.
  • Appointment Integrity: The nomination of the first head of the new agency will be a litmus test. If the appointee is a known "Yoon-loyalist" with a history of political investigations, it will confirm the critics' worst fears.
  • Public Sentiment vs. Internal Squabbling: The Democratic Party of Korea (the main opposition party) is currently plagued by internal power struggles. The extent to which they can stay focused on the "big picture" of reform—rather than using the issue for internal jockeying—will determine if this movement has any hope of genuine success.

Ultimately, the "Prosecution Reform" has become a litmus test for South Korean democracy. If the system cannot dismantle the self-serving structures of its most powerful legal institution, the path toward a more transparent and equitable society will remain firmly blocked. The reform process is not a chore to be checked off; it is a national imperative that requires constant, vigilant oversight.
