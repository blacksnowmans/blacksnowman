South Korea’s Thirty-Year Itch: Can Deregulating Multi-Unit Housing Fix a Stalled Property Market?

November 04, 2026

For decades, driving through the winding residential alleys of Seoul or its surrounding satellite cities meant dodging a familiar architectural skyline: a forest of low-rise brick buildings capped with orange rooftop water tanks, wedged tightly between gleaming, high-rise apartment complexes. These are South Korea's multi-unit and multi-household houses—known locally as villa or *dagagu* housing—which have long served as the crucial first rung on the housing ladder for young adults, newlyweds, and working-class families priced out of the country's luxury apartment market. But for thirty years, these neighborhoods have been frozen in time, strictly bound by a web of archaic height and floor-area caps designed in an entirely different era of urban planning.

Now, as South Korea wrestles with a stubborn, multi-year housing crisis marked by soaring prices and a paralyzed supply chain, the government is finally reaching for the scissors. With a fresh package of deregulation rolling out, policymakers are betting that loosening the strict limits on multi-unit buildings can inject physical, ground-up supply back into a market overwhelmingly obsessed with towering apartment blocks. Yet, as bulldozers idle and contractors sweat over ballooning construction costs, a fundamental question hangs over the real estate market: is this a genuine turning point for housing accessibility, or merely another paper promise that collides with the harsh economic realities on the ground?

Lifting a Thirty-Year Cap on Multi-Unit Buildings

The core of the recent policy shift targets the restrictive building codes that have strangled the multi-unit housing sector for three decades. Starting this month, the legal height limit for multi-unit houses is being bumped from three stories to four, while maximum floor-area restrictions are expanding significantly from 660 square meters to 1,000 square meters. For an urban housing market where supply has been hyper-concentrated in massive, high-end apartment complexes—often out of reach for average earners—opening the door to taller, larger multi-unit buildings represents a major strategic pivot.

Major media outlets across the country have framed the move as a long-overdue attempt to diversify South Korea's housing stock. When these small-scale buildings were choked off by regulations and battered by a wave of rental scams that terrified renters away from non-apartment housing, a vital shock absorber in the real estate ecosystem simply vanished. By expanding floor-area limits to 1,000 square meters, the government is attempting to hand property owners and builders the regulatory breathing room needed to make development financially viable again.

Yet, enthusiasm on the street is heavily tempered by skepticism. In a climate where construction materials and labor costs have skyrocketed, developers point out that a legal permission slip to build an extra floor does not automatically guarantee project profitability. While the regulatory locks have finally been picked, industry insiders warn that translating these legal changes into actual bricks and mortar will require navigating a maze of stubborn economic headwinds.

The Political Tug-of-War Over Practical Supply

Unsurprisingly, the country's political arena has seized on the housing shortage as a prime battleground. Main opposition figures, including People Power Party leader Chang Dong-hyeok, have taken to visiting youth housing sites to argue that the real estate crisis cannot be solved with political rhetoric or heavy-handed regulations. Instead, the conservative opposition insists that the government must focus relentlessly on delivering physical, customized housing in the exact locations where young people actually want to live.

This political pressure reflects deep public anxiety over housing affordability. Lawmakers from the opposition have aggressively criticized previous and current housing strategies for leaning too heavily on rental-heavy models, arguing that the public desperately wants a clear, dependable pathway to outright homeownership rather than perpetual tenancy.

At the same time, the administration under President Lee Jae-myung is pushing its own aggressive supply timeline. In recent administrative meetings, the president ordered a fast-track approach to land development, pressing agencies to accelerate the creation of new metropolitan greenfield sites meant to supply roughly 80,000 new housing units. While prospective buyers desperate to escape the rental trap welcome any news of large-scale development, critics point out that the sheer volume of a blueprint matters far less than the speed, timing, and geographic placement of the actual construction.

The Metropolitan Dilemma: Transit Before Towers

Translating grand government blueprints into reality in the densely packed Seoul metropolitan area is rarely smooth sailing. Proof of this friction can be found just outside the capital, where local residents and regional officials have pushed back fiercely against newly announced public housing sites. In areas like Gimpo, local communities have argued vociferously that dumping thousands of new housing units into neighborhoods already infamous for their nightmarish daily commutes will only deepen the misery of current residents.

Planners face a classic chicken-and-egg dilemma. Everyone agrees that the broader region needs more housing, but regional associations and local councils are increasingly refusing to accept new developments unless expansive transit infrastructure and local job creation are guaranteed upfront. The old administrative habit of dropping massive housing complexes into a district and telling commuters to figure out the transit later is running into a wall of public backlash. Without a simultaneous expansion of commuter rail and express bus networks, any large-scale housing supply risks triggering a severe political boomerang.

Compounding this infrastructural friction is a harsh physical timeline. As real estate analysts frequently note, even the most ambitious government supply announcement faces a punishing lag time. From land acquisition and administrative approvals to breaking ground and final completion, the physical years required to build a home mean that future inventory does little to soothe the immediate anxieties of a buyer desperate to purchase a property today.

Speed Versus Safety in Urban Renewal

While greenfield developments battle infrastructure bottlenecks, urban renewal projects closer to the city centers are racing ahead at a breakneck pace. Across Seoul and its surrounding cities, maintenance and redevelopment districts are pushing forward aggressively. Neighborhoods are rapidly clearing administrative hurdles, seeking fast-track planning consultations, and securing major construction contracts for mid-to-high-rise residential complexes.

Municipalities are doing their part to grease the wheels. Local governments, such as Incheon, have established dedicated one-stop support centers for redevelopment and reconstruction projects, drastically shortening preliminary consulting periods to help projects move from concept to construction as quickly as possible. Removing these bureaucratic roadblocks is widely viewed as an essential catalyst for bringing fresh housing stock into aging urban neighborhoods.

Yet, this speed run carries its own set of risks. Behind the optimistic press releases of major construction firms securing multi-billion-won contracts lie familiar points of friction: bitter internal disputes among property owners, fierce clashes with contractors over rising construction costs, and mounting fears among residents over steep additional financial levies required to complete projects. Pushing for velocity without robust safeguards risks creating an environment where vulnerable, long-term residents are squeezed out of their own neighborhoods.

Ultimately, the flurry of recent policy announcements—from relaxing multi-unit architectural rules to accelerating new town sites and streamlining urban redevelopment—points to a single, inescapable truth: South Korea's housing strategy is desperately searching for a formula that prioritizes practical stability over political noise.

What to Watch Next

  • Implementation of Multi-Unit Rules: Watch how property owners and small-scale developers respond on the ground as the new height and floor-area limits take effect this month, particularly regarding whether construction costs allow projects to pencil out.
  • Metropolitan Transit Backlash: Monitor local government and resident resistance in designated new housing districts, specifically whether transit and infrastructure commitments are legally locked in before ground is broken.
  • Redvvelopment Cost Disputes: Keep an eye on urban renewal and reconstruction sites in Seoul and Incheon to see if streamlined administrative procedures actually shorten completion timelines or simply accelerate friction over rising construction fees and resident displacement.
