The Shincheonji Ghost: How a Religious Conspiracy Theory is Tearing Apart South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 09, 2026

Inside the high-stakes halls of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) headquarters in Seoul, the air during last August’s national convention was thick with a specific kind of dread. It wasn't just the usual friction of internal power struggles or the pressure of being the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung. It was a whisper that turned into a roar: the claim that a "Triple Secret Alliance" of anti-Lee factions, political "divisionists," and members of the Shincheonji Church of Jesus had infiltrated the party to hijack the leadership vote.

For the uninitiated, Shincheonji is not just any religious group; it is a controversial messianic movement often labeled a "cult" in South Korea, infamous for its secretive proselytizing and its role as a lightning rod during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. To be linked to Shincheonji in Korean politics is often a "kiss of death," a label designed to trigger immediate public revulsion. Yet, months after the convention ended and the leadership was decided, this ghost continues to haunt the DPK. What started as a tactical smear campaign has evolved into a full-blown identity crisis for a party that prides itself on democratic principles and moral superiority over its conservative rivals.

The anatomy of a political frame

The "Triple Alliance" theory was a masterpiece of political framing. It suggested that thousands of Shincheonji members had been ordered to join the DPK as "rightful party members" (those who pay monthly dues and hold voting rights) to sway the election in favor of certain candidates. By late July, what began as unverified "rumor mill" posts on internet forums like Namuwiki and various pro-DPK YouTube channels began to gain legitimacy as high-ranking party officials started echoing the claims.

The timing was surgical. As the leadership race heated up, the rumor served as a convenient weapon to brand any internal opposition as being "in bed with a cult." Representative Park Sun-won, who was appointed as the head of a task force to investigate "party member purification," became a central figure in this drama. Insiders describe a chaotic atmosphere where even senior party members were blindsided by the aggression of the allegations. According to accounts from former lawmakers, the atmosphere was one of "thin ice," where any deviation from the party line could result in being labeled a collaborator with the religious fringe.

The problem, however, was the evidence—or the lack thereof. President Lee Jae-myung himself eventually admitted there was no concrete proof of such a conspiracy. Yet, the damage was done. The DPK, a party that traces its lineage to the pro-democracy movements of Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun, found itself using the same "red-baiting" tactics it once despised. In the past, military dictatorships and right-wing media would smear progressives as "communist sympathizers" without evidence; now, the DPK was using religious "cult" labels to achieve the same chilling effect on internal dissent.

A hundred-page report that no one can read

The most baffling aspect of the scandal is the current behavior of the party leadership. After months of "investigation," the task force led by Representative Park Sun-won reportedly produced a massive 100-page report. Instead of releasing it to clarify the truth and heal the party's divisions, the leadership has placed the document under a bizarre "vow of silence."

On November 6, during a Supreme Council meeting, Park Sun-won effectively shut down inquiries by stating:

"As the head of the Shincheonji investigation, I have much to say. However, unless there is a separate decision by the Supreme Council, I will not say a single word. Why? Because the world is currently in a difficult state, and the people's livelihoods are struggling."

This pivot—using "the people's livelihoods" (minsaeng) as a shield to avoid transparency—has sparked outrage even within the party. Critics argue that if the investigation had found actual criminal interference, the party would be shouting it from the rooftops to purge the bad actors. If it found nothing, it should apologize for the smear campaign. By keeping the report hidden—allowing lawmakers to view it only for 30 minutes in a private office without taking copies—the leadership is fueling suspicions that the document contains proof of their own manipulative tactics.

The numbers, once scrutinized, seem to support the "nothingburger" theory. While some pro-leadership figures claimed up to 14,000 "suspicious" members, more sober analysis from within the party suggests the number of Shincheonji-linked members who actually held voting rights was around 3,300. In a party of over 1.5 million voting members, that represents 0.4%—a statistically insignificant margin that could never have "hijacked" a national convention. The "Triple Alliance" wasn't a threat to democracy; it was a ghost story told to frighten voters into line.

The "Successful Coup" logic and legal peril

The internal backlash reached a fever pitch when Representative Lee Sung-yoon, a member of the DPK’s leadership circle, broke ranks to criticize the "politics of silence." During a meeting on November 9, he compared the current situation to a dark chapter in Korean judicial history. He invoked the infamous 1995 logic used by prosecutors during the investigation into the 1979 military coup: "A successful coup cannot be punished."

Lee’s point was sharp: because the current leadership won the election using these smears, they now feel entitled to bury the truth. In his view, the "Triple Alliance" claim was a "successful political maneuver" based on "manifest lies." But as Lee warned, a victory won through fabrication is a "failed politics" that leaves the party's moral foundation in ruins.

Beyond the moral argument lies a significant legal threat. Article 52 of the Political Parties Act specifically prohibits the spread of false information during internal party elections to prevent a candidate from being elected. This is a serious crime, punishable by up to five years in prison or a fine of 10 million won (about $7,200). Some party members are now arguing that because the "anti-Lee" label was so clearly directed at specific rival candidates, those who spread the rumors could face criminal prosecution.

🔥 The Hypocrisy of "Sweeping it Under the Rug"

Senior party figures like Park Ji-won have urged the party to "move on," arguing that dwelling on the Shincheonji issue is like "burning down the house to catch a flea." But this "good is good" (joeun ge joeun geo) approach is a double standard. If the opposition People Power Party (PPP) had used religious smears during an election, the DPK would be demanding a special prosecutor. By refusing to clean its own house, the DPK loses the right to demand "justice and common sense" from its opponents.

A shift in identity: From reform to real estate

While the party remains bogged down in this internal conspiracy drama, its external policy shifts are raising even more alarms about its core identity. Historically, the DPK has stood for wealth redistribution and "tax justice." However, the leadership is now pushing for a significant easing of the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (Jongbu-se)—a wealth tax on high-value properties that was once a cornerstone of their platform.

The party is proposing to raise the tax deduction threshold for non-resident single-home owners from 1.2 billion won to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million). In reality, a property with a tax assessment of 1.4 billion won often has a market value exceeding 2 billion won ($1.45 million). This means the DPK is effectively fighting to cut taxes for people who own $1.5 million luxury apartments in Seoul’s wealthiest districts (like Gangnam or Seocho) but don't even live in them.

This policy drift suggests a party that is desperate to win over middle-class and wealthy voters in Seoul ahead of future elections, even if it means abandoning its traditional base of renters and the working class. When combined with the "Shincheonji" smear campaign, a troubling picture emerges: a ruling party that is increasingly willing to use far-right political tactics (conspiracy theories and smears) while adopting center-right economic policies (tax cuts for the wealthy).

What to watch next

The DPK is currently in a "pressure cooker" environment. While the leadership hopes the Shincheonji issue will fade away, several factors could force it back into the spotlight:

  • The "Cabinet" Files: The opposition People Power Party (PPP) is currently watching the DPK’s internal strife with quiet satisfaction. Political analysts suggest the PPP is "saving" this issue for the next general election cycle. If the DPK doesn't release the report now, the PPP will likely use the "illegal member list" and "privacy violations" as a cudgel during the campaign.
  • Legal Challenges: If disgruntled party members who were smeared as "cult sympathizers" decide to file defamation lawsuits or report violations of the Political Parties Act, the DPK's internal 100-page report could be subpoenaed by prosecutors. This would take the narrative out of the party's control and put it into the hands of the judiciary.
  • The Identity Crisis: Watch for more friction between the "principled" wing of the party (like Lee Sung-yoon) and the "pragmatic" leadership. If the party continues to pivot toward tax cuts for luxury homeowners while silencing talk of its own political maneuvers, it may face a grassroots rebellion from its core supporters.

The ghost of Shincheonji isn't just about a religious group; it is a mirror reflecting the current state of South Korea's ruling party. Whether the DPK chooses to break the mirror or look honestly at the reflection will determine its path in the years to come.
