Shoes, Pebbles, and the Trap of Isolated Power in South Korea

September 24, 2026

Walking the streets of Seoul or following the nightly news, it is hard to escape the feeling that South Korean politics has become an exercise in endurance rather than governance. Every policy debate feels jagged, and every compromise leaves a dry, sandy irritation in the mouths of voters. Why does political life here constantly feel like walking in ill-fitting footwear?

This question was recently given a sharp, memorable frame by Yu Si-min, a prominent liberal commentator and former government minister. During a late-September appearance on a public broadcast program hosted by Tak Hyun-min, Yu offered a metaphor that has since dominated local political commentary: the dilemma of the "uncomfortable shoe and the pebble inside it." Far from being a casual literary flourish, the metaphor cuts straight to the root of the friction between state power and public expectation in South Korea today.

The Metaphor of the Uncomfortable Shoe

Yu described the presidency not as a prize to be enjoyed, but as a heavy, ill-fitting shoe forced upon a leader by the tides of history and the will of the electorate. Whether a leader actively wanted the role or not, once handed the mandate to govern, they have no choice but to walk the road ahead. Yet, when the shoe is too tight or poorly shaped to the wearer's foot, it breeds a strange, grinding friction.

To be sure, politics is not a theater for personal complaints. The public hands a leader their shoes with the expectation that they will walk steadily and guide the country forward. When a leader whines about the discomfort of the shoe without addressing *why* it hurts, the core compact of governance begins to fray. The frustration many citizens feel toward the current administration stems precisely from this gap: an administration that acknowledges its own discomfort—lamenting how hard things are—while remaining completely detached from the reasons why the public is suffering alongside them.

Accepting a mandate means accepting the weight of responsibility. The moment a leader begins to treat that weight merely as an unfair burden imposed by others, the administration loses its way.

Ignoring the Pebble in the Sole

Even the most determined walker on a rough path will eventually collect a pebble inside their shoe. According to Yu, this is where the administration's true isolation begins. The foot does not hurt because the shoe itself is inherently flawed, but because a small, sharp stone has worked its way inside. Yet, instead of stopping to take off the shoe and shake the stone out, the leader continues to limp forward, clutching a bruised foot in agony.

This image captures the current government’s defensive reflex toward criticism from the political opposition and the general public. Rather than treating dissent, skepticism, and social friction as problems to be solved, the administration treats them as hostile nuisances designed to torment the ruler. The basic, commonsense logic that removing the pebble will relieve the pain simply does not operate within the current corridors of power.

Pebbles do not always enter by accident. Sometimes they are invited in through poor communication strategies, aggressive rhetoric, and an overreliance on derogatory labels against political rivals. Using demeaning terms to score cheap political points does little to diminish the opponent's dignity, but it guarantees a steady supply of fresh gravel inside the administration's own shoes. The longer they walk without stopping to clear them out, the deeper the wounds become.

The Toxicity of Derogatory Language and Political Isolation

Yu has long warned against the corrosive effect of political name-calling. Language designed to belittle an opponent ultimately degrades the speaker first. Over the past year and more, South Korean politics has felt less like a contest of ideas and more like a barren wasteland of petty feuds. The administration's deepening isolation is not the fault of the opposition parties; it is a fortress of rejection built by the executive branch's own rhetoric.

Every time a public official resorts to cheap insults, public trust erodes a little more, and the stone inside the shoe grows sharper. While the president has occasionally expressed personal distress over the bitter political climate, these expressions rarely translate into a genuine effort to clear out the source of the pain.

Taking off a shoe to shake out a stone requires vulnerability. It requires admitting error and dropping the defensive armor of insults. Yet politics is supposed to be the art of bearing that exact discomfort for the greater good. When the political game devolves into a contest over who can inflict the deepest wound, the entire nation is dragged down into a swamp of institutional paralysis.

What Comes Next: The Courage to Stop and Start Over

Despite the grim diagnosis, Yu insists that hope is not entirely lost. The solution is remarkably simple, even if it requires political courage: stop, take off the shoe, empty the stones, and start walking again at a sustainable pace. Power is not a static monument; it is an organic relationship that shifts through interaction with the public.

Clearing out the pebbles is not an act of surrender. It is a mature pivot toward better governance. It means accepting criticism, widening the channels of communication, and placing the ordinary concerns of citizens back at the center of national administration. It is a recognition that dialogue is not about forcing an adversary to submit, but about removing the obstacles that prevent everyone from moving forward together.

For foreign observers trying to understand South Korea's turbulent political cycles, the "shoe and pebble" metaphor provides a valuable lens. The crisis facing the country's leadership is fundamentally self-inflicted—not because of insurmountable external shocks, but because of a stubborn refusal to diagnose internal failures honestly. Until the nation's leaders find the courage to sit down on the roadside, unlace their boots, and empty out what is hurting them, the entire country will remain stuck, limping along behind a government that refuses to look down at its own feet.
