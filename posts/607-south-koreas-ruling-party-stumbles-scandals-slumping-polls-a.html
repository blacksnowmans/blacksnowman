South Korea’s Ruling Party Stumbles: Scandals, Slumping Polls, and the Push for a Hardline Reset

October 02, 2026

For an outsider watching South Korea’s political theater, the headlines this week read like a psychological thriller crossed with a soap opera. On the surface, the country is grappling with familiar questions of governance, economic pressure, and institutional reform under President Lee Jae-myung. But inside the corridors of power, an acute sense of panic has set in. Poll numbers are sliding toward dangerous new lows, core supporters are openly questioning whether their party has lost its way, and the leadership appears paralyzed. What began as a series of petty operational mishaps—a stray name written on a notepad during a parliamentary session, a stray internet meme, a late-night dinner meeting—has metastasized into a full-blown crisis of confidence for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s main liberal party.

To understand why these seemingly minor blunders are sending shockwaves through the political establishment, one has to look past the daily news cycle and examine the deep structural anxieties plaguing the Korean left. The party that once prided itself on moral clarity and disciplined reform now finds itself bogged down by factional infighting, defensive posturing, and a growing disconnect with the very voters who brought them to power. As the DPK faces the terrifying prospect of a further 10% drop in public support, the question is no longer just about managing public relations—it is about whether the current leadership can salvage its reform agenda before the window of governance slams shut.

Notepads, Files, and the Paranoia of Insider Politics

The trouble began with a photograph. During a plenary session of the National Assembly, cameras zoomed in on a notepad belonging to Kim Min-seok, a senior DPK figure. Scribbled across the page were names like Han Dong-hoon—the former leader of the rival, conservative People Power Party—alongside terms like "Han Dong-hoon file" and "X-file." For a political culture already primed to suspect subterranean intelligence-gathering and backroom political warfare, the exposure of these notes was like dropping a match into a tinderbox.

Han Dong-hoon immediately seized on the opening, denouncing the memo as evidence of "dirty, behind-the-scenes engineering" and petty politics. From a purely administrative standpoint, politicians take notes all the time; a name on a pad does not automatically prove a conspiracy. Yet, in South Korea’s hyper-polarized political ecosystem, the *existence* of such a file—or even the careless display of it—is toxic. It validates the worst fears of the electorate: that political parties spend more time weaponizing dossiers against their rivals than addressing the cost of living or economic stagnation.

Kim Min-seok offered a limp defense, claiming some of the notes had been misread or misinterpreted. But in a party where trust has already evaporated, explanations sound like evasions. It highlights a recurring malaise within the DPK leadership: an apparent tone-deafness to how their actions play to the public. Voters do not care about the fine print of a politician's memo pad; they care about competence and transparency. When a party barely a month and a half into a new leadership term is bogged down by gossip over leaked notes, it is a glaring symptom of a leadership crisis.

The Trap of Factional Purges and Inflammatory Rhetoric

Compounding these operational missteps is a toxic hardening of internal factionalism. The DPK has long struggled to balance its moderate wing with its fiery, fiercely loyal core base. Lately, however, that internal tension has crossed the line from healthy debate into outright hostility.

Recently, hardline figures within the party have ratcheted up aggressive rhetoric against internal dissenters. Lawmaker Kang Su-young made headlines—and not in a good way—by openly suggesting that President Lee Jae-myung should "crush" or "suppress" party opponents by appointing loyalists like Kim Ji-yong. The use of the word *jinjap* (진압)—a term historically associated with military crackdowns and authoritarian suppression of civil unrest—stunned observers. Even within a ruling party desperate for unity, framing internal disagreement as a military-style insurrection to be crushed exposes a chilling disregard for democratic norms.

This heavy-handed mentality is bleeding down to local leadership as well. Ulsan Mayor Kim Sang-wook recently found himself at the center of a public backlash after verbally threatening a local media bureau chief during an official event, reportedly using crude language about "breaking heads." For a high-profile regional executive to resort to thuggish intimidation against the press does irreparable damage to the DPK's brand. Ironically, while Mayor Kim has been quick to lecture others on party discipline, his own municipal administration ranks near the bottom in public approval ratings, compounded by tone-deaf controversies like eating sandwiches during formal business briefings.

When figures who model themselves on ideological enforcers become the public face of the party, moderate and independent voters inevitably head for the exits. Politics should be an art of persuasion, not a contest of intimidation.

The Case for a Hardline Reset: Bringing Back Chung Chung-rae

With poll numbers cratering and the party drifting, a growing chorus of frustrated supporters is pointing to a potential escape hatch: bringing veteran firebrand Chung Chung-rae into a high-profile cabinet or administrative role, such as Minister of Justice or Chief Presidential Secretary.

This speculation was further fueled by a recent, unannounced four-hour dinner meeting between President Lee Jae-myung and Chung. To the DPK’s core base, Chung represents an antidote to perceived weakness. As key prosecutorial and legal reform posts—including the newly established Major Crime Investigation Agency—remain vacant, the party faces an uphill battle against deeply entrenched bureaucratic resistance. Supporters argue that achieving genuine, irreversible reform requires a political brawler who can withstand hostile parliamentary confirmation hearings without backing down.

Chung’s supporters view him as "bulletproof"—a figure who can absorb the inevitable counter-attacks from conservative opponents and push structural overhauls through to the end. His appointment would signal to the party's fading base that President Lee is done playing nice and is ready to wage total political war to protect his agenda.

Yet, this strategy carries immense risk. Appointing a polarizing partisan to a powerful post like Justice Minister practically guarantees a brutal confirmation showdown. If the nominee carries too much baggage, it could consume vital political oxygen and alienate the very moderate voters the party needs to win back. It is a high-stakes gamble: lean entirely into the base with a hardliner, or risk losing the entire reform momentum through cautious inaction.

Rebuilding the Base Before the Window Closes

The fundamental problem facing South Korea’s ruling camp is not a lack of policy ideas; it is a crisis of political self-awareness. When party representatives spend their time trading insults, playing with internet slang, or nursing internal vendettas, the public notices. Voters are smart enough to distinguish between genuine reform and the frantic self-preservation of a political class trapped in a bubble.

If the DPK is to halt its slide in the polls and recover its governing momentum, piecemeal apologies and defensive spin will no longer suffice. It requires a ruthless internal audit: cutting loose non-performing officials who mistake aggression for competence, prioritizing tangible policy delivery over symbolic factional wars, and listening to the legitimate frustrations of the electorate rather than dismissing critics as internal enemies.

The coming weeks will determine whether the administration can right the ship or if it will spend the remainder of its term fighting a defensive rear-guard action. South Korea’s political landscape is notoriously volatile, and fortunes can turn on a dime. But time is running out, and the public’s patience is wearing dangerously thin.

What to Watch Next

  • Cabinet Reshuffle Announcements: Keep a close eye on whether President Lee Jae-myung officially taps heavy-hitters like Chung Chung-rae for key posts like the Ministry of Justice, which would signal a definitive shift toward confrontational reform.
  • Confirmation Hearings: Watch the parliamentary vetting process for newly appointed anti-corruption and investigative agency chiefs. How the opposition handles these nominees will be a litmus test for the ruling party's legislative strength.
  • Poll Trajectory: Monitor weekly tracking polls to see if internal party discipline measures—or lack thereof—stem the bleeding of the DPK’s approval ratings among independent voters.
