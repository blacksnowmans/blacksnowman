The End of the American Umbrella: Lessons for Korea from the Shifting Sands of Brazil and the Middle East

October 26, 2026

Imagine a polling station in the rural northwest of Brazil. The sun is beating down, and voters are waiting for buses that have suddenly, and without explanation, stopped running. At the same moment, thousands of miles away in Riyadh, Saudi officials watch radar screens as low-cost drones from Yemen bypass multi-million dollar defense systems to strike at the heart of the world’s oil supply. These scenes may seem disconnected, but they are the tremors of a global order that is fundamentally cracking. For South Korea, a nation that has historically tethered its survival to a single superpower, these events offer a chilling preview of a world where "business as usual" is a recipe for disaster.

The return of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva—widely known as Lula—to the presidency of Brazil and the desperate diplomatic maneuvering between Saudi Arabia and Iran are more than just headlines. They represent a shift toward cold-blooded realpolitik and the collapse of the "unipolar" world. As South Korea navigates its own internal transitions under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, the lessons from Brazil and the Middle East provide a vital roadmap for what it means to be a "sovereign" nation in a multipolar age.

The Return of the Negotiator: Brazil’s Fragile Democratic Resilience

The recent Brazilian election was less a victory lap and more a desperate rescue mission. Lula, the former labor leader who once led Brazil through a golden age of commodity-driven growth, returned to face Jair Bolsonaro in a race defined by razor-thin margins. To understand why this matters to an international observer, one must look at the "Lulistas"—the fervent base of the Workers' Party (PT), Brazil's primary left-wing political force. Their mobilization was not just about policy; it was a defense against what many saw as the dismantling of democratic institutions.

However, the election revealed a deeply fractured nation. Despite mandatory voting, nearly 22% of the electorate abstained. This "missing fifth" represents a profound cynicism that transcends borders. In Brazil, as in South Korea, the threat of "institutional sabotage"—such as the suspicious disruption of transport in pro-Lula regions on election day—shows that democracy is no longer a settled fact, but a daily struggle against those willing to weaponize the state apparatus to stay in power.

Lula himself is a changed figure. The "wild horse" of the labor unions who could charm a crowd with a glass of pinga (Brazilian sugarcane liquor) has aged into what many describe as a "kindly grandfather." While his charisma has softened, his core philosophy remains: "The President is a negotiator." In a world where ideological purity often leads to gridlock, Lula’s pragmatic willingness to talk to enemies is his greatest asset. This mirrors the current sentiment in Seoul, where the Lee Jae-myung administration has prioritized practical national interest over the rigid, value-laden rhetoric that characterized the previous era.

The Generational Inverse: A Warning for Korea

One of the most striking aspects of the Brazilian vote was the generational divide. Usually, we expect the youth to be progressive and the elderly to be conservative. In Brazil, the 16-24 age group and the 60+ demographic swung heavily for Lula. It was the "middle" generation—the 40s and 50s—who remained the bedrock of Bolsonaro’s right-wing populism. This "sandwich generation" feels the most economic anxiety and is most susceptible to the "law and order" rhetoric of the right.

For South Korea, this is a cautionary tale. The Lee Jae-myung administration must address the anxieties of Korea's own "sandwich generation," who are caught between rising housing costs and the need to support both children and aging parents. If a leader cannot "negotiate" a better economic reality for this middle tier, the democratic coalition risks falling apart, regardless of how much the youth or the elderly support the cause.

When the Umbrella Folds: Saudi Arabia’s Hard Lesson in Realpolitik

If Brazil tells a story of internal resilience, the Middle East tells a story of external betrayal. For decades, Saudi Arabia was the cornerstone of American influence in the Arab world. In exchange for oil flow and regional stability, the U.S. provided an "security umbrella." That umbrella is now full of holes. The inability of high-priced American Patriot missile batteries to stop cheap Houthi rebel drones from striking Aramco (the Saudi state-owned oil giant) facilities has sent shockwaves through Riyadh.

The Houthi rebels in Yemen, widely seen as proxies for Iran, have proven that asymmetric warfare can humble a regional superpower. Saudi Arabia has realized a painful truth: the U.S. is no longer willing or able to be the guarantor of Saudi security. This has led to a stunning pivot. Saudi Arabia, once the sworn enemy of the Islamic Republic of Iran, is now seeking a "big deal" with Tehran. Why? Because if the Americans won't stop the drones, perhaps the Iranians—the ones who control the drone-makers—will.

"Security is not something you beg for; it is something you build," is a sentiment echoing through the halls of the Blue House in Seoul. Under the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ultimately impeached and is currently incarcerated—South Korea’s foreign policy was often criticized as "subservient," relying almost entirely on the whims of Washington. The Saudi crisis serves as a visceral reminder of why that path was so dangerous. When a nation abdicates its security to a foreign power, it becomes a pawn on someone else’s chessboard. The Lee Jae-myung administration’s push for "independent national defense" and "balanced diplomacy" is a direct response to this global reality.

The Battle for Taeiz and the Necessity of Dialogue

Nowhere is this shift more apparent than in the battle for Taeiz, Yemen’s third-largest city. It is the strategic gateway between the Houthi-controlled north and the government-held south. If Taeiz falls, the Houthis effectively win the war. For the Saudis, this is a nightmare scenario: a pro-Iranian state on their southern border. Yet, instead of calling for more American bombs, Riyadh is talking to Tehran. This is the definition of pragmatism. They are choosing a bitter peace over a losing war. It is a lesson in survival that every mid-sized power, including South Korea, must study closely.

Israel’s Deadlock and the Invisible Minority

The theme of polarized stagnation continues in Israel. The political landscape is currently a tug-of-war between Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud Party and a centrist opposition led by former military chiefs like Gadi Eizenkot. Netanyahu, much like figures in Korea’s main opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), is fighting not just for a platform, but for political survival to avoid legal consequences.

However, the most telling part of the Israeli story is the role of the Arab-Israeli parties. They hold roughly 15 seats in the 120-seat Knesset—a perfect "casting vote." In theory, they could decide who becomes Prime Minister. In practice, they are treated as invisible. No major Jewish party wants to form a coalition with them, and as a result, 20% of the population feels permanently excluded from the democratic process. This "democracy for some" creates a hollow core that makes the state fragile.

This mirrors the dangers of extreme partisan polarization in Korea. When a significant portion of the population feels that their vote "doesn't count" because the system is rigged toward the elite or specific factions, the legitimacy of the entire state is called into question. The Lee Jae-myung government’s emphasis on "inclusive growth" and "social harmony" is an attempt to avoid this Israeli trap, ensuring that no segment of the Korean population feels like an "eternal second-class citizen."

What to Watch: The Rise of the Multipolar Middle

As we look forward, the world is not returning to a Cold War-style binary. Instead, we are entering an era of "shifting alliances." Saudi Arabia is talking to Iran; Brazil is reinvigorating "South-South cooperation" with Africa and Asia; and South Korea is asserting its role as a "pivotal state" that can speak to both the West and the Global South.

What should the savvy reader watch for next?

  • The "Iran-Saudi Big Deal": If these two regional rivals actually sign a lasting security pact, it will signal the definitive end of American hegemony in the Middle East. It will also provide a template for how South Korea might manage its own complex relationship with neighboring powers without relying solely on a U.S. mediator.
  • Lula’s Economic Diplomacy: Watch how Brazil handles its relationship with China. If Lula manages to balance Chinese investment with Western democratic values, he will provide a blueprint for the "Pragmatic Diplomacy" that President Lee Jae-myung is currently implementing in Seoul.
  • The PPP’s Evolution: In Korea, watch whether the opposition People Power Party can move past the legacy of the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era. If they continue to cling to outdated "value-based diplomacy" while the rest of the world moves toward realpolitik, they risk becoming a permanent minority in a rapidly changing world.

The era of the "American Umbrella" as a total security solution is over. Whether in the voting booths of Brasilia, the oil fields of the Khurais, or the streets of Seoul, the message is the same: sovereignty is earned through flexibility, negotiation, and the strength to stand on one's own feet. The "grandfather" Lula and the "negotiator" Lee Jae-myung may belong to different hemispheres, but they are both navigating the same stormy sea of a world that no longer takes orders from a single captain.
