The Brutal Reality of Modern Marriage: Lessons from South Korea’s Divorce Courts

October 10, 2026

In South Korea, one of the most popular forms of "edutainment" involves listening to divorce lawyers recount the most grueling, messy, and bitter disputes from their consultation rooms. Among the most sought-after experts is Yang Na-rae, a divorce attorney whose insights have gained massive traction on YouTube. Joined by popular comedians Song Eun-i and Kim Sook, Yang strips away the romantic veneer of matrimony to reveal the "human condition" in its rawest form. These aren't just sensationalized stories; they are reflections of the volatile realities surrounding money, communication, and trust in modern Korean society. To those watching, it becomes clear: marriage is not a fairy tale—it is a high-stakes economic and social contract that, when neglected, crumbles under the weight of reality.

The Economic Reality of Marriage: Transparency as Survival

Attorney Yang Na-rae defines marriage primarily as an "economic entity." In her experience, the most dangerous trigger for divorce is the concealment of debt or financial instability before or during the union. While many couples try to present only their best selves to their partners, hiding financial burdens is a ticking time bomb. Yang suggests that in the current era, a practical, albeit unromantic, step for couples is to link their banking apps to provide total transparency regarding their assets and liabilities.

In South Korea, where the "economic status" of a partner is often a primary consideration for marriage, the failure to be transparent is treated by many as a breach of the fundamental agreement of a household. While the law recognizes assets formed during a marriage as communal property—subject to division upon divorce—the law cannot micromanage daily spending or personal allowances. When one partner monopolizes income or shuts the other out of financial decisions, it breeds a resentment that eventually kills the relationship. As Yang notes, prioritizing economic clarity is not about greed; it is a prerequisite for survival.

The Weight of the Economic Contract

South Korean courts have established precedents where one party unilaterally liquidating communal assets to the detriment of the other is treated as a major breach of duty. Beyond mere spending, the deliberate economic isolation or control of a spouse is increasingly viewed by the legal system as a threat to the other's autonomy, often carrying significant legal weight in divorce proceedings.

This "contractual" mindset has even begun to intersect with modern technology. There have been recent viral instances where prospective grooms have used AI tools like ChatGPT to calculate their partner’s salary, family background, and property value to decide whether the marriage is "worth it" in terms of potential future divorce settlements. When marriage is reduced to an actuarial calculation, the emotional bond becomes secondary to the bottom line, turning the institution into a fragile arrangement that ends the moment the numbers no longer add up.

When Silence Becomes the Divorce Trigger

Divorce inquiries in South Korea often spike during specific times of the year, most notably immediately following the Chuseok (autumn harvest) holidays. The sudden, prolonged proximity of family dynamics during these holidays acts as a pressure cooker, exposing cracks in relationships that are usually ignored during the routine of daily life. Conversely, periods like the Lunar New Year or May (designated as "Family Month") often see a dip in consultations, driven by a temporary, performative desire to keep the family unit intact for the sake of appearances.

However, many long-term marriages face a deeper, more silent crisis: the "roommate" phenomenon. After 15 or 20 years, many couples lose the ability to talk to one another, living as strangers under one roof. In the conservative legal climate of South Korea, which maintains a strict "fault-based" divorce system, "lack of communication" or "emotional coldness" is rarely enough to secure a divorce if the other party refuses to consent. This creates a trap of domestic silence where spouses remain legally bound to someone they can no longer stand.

Attorney Yang identifies this chronic lack of communication as the root cause of most failing marriages. The ability to express dissatisfaction without escalating to hostility is a skill that many couples fail to cultivate, leading them to bury their grievances until the distance between them becomes permanent.

The Boundaries of Betrayal: What Constitutes an Affair?

Perhaps the most contentious issue in Korean divorce law is defining the boundaries of an "affair." Clients often arrive at Yang’s office incensed by a spouse’s interactions with colleagues—frequent texting, 10-minute phone calls on the commute home, or following an ex-partner’s social media account. While these behaviors are deeply damaging to the emotional fabric of a marriage, the law requires more than mere suspicion or "inappropriate" contact to prove infidelity.

According to Yang, simple contact frequency or a quick chat on the way home is rarely enough to win a court case for infidelity. To be legally actionable, there must be evidence of physical intimacy, explicit romantic declarations, or consistent, secret nocturnal communication. This creates a frustrating gap between the victim’s sense of betrayal and the law's demand for concrete proof.

The situation grows even more complex when the "accused" partner attempts to deflect blame. It is not uncommon for a partner caught in an affair to dig up years-old, insignificant messages from the other spouse’s past to claim, "You did it too." However, under the current statute of limitations, these historical grievances are inadmissible. The law focuses on the present: a partner who has engaged in a six-month affair is the "at-fault" party, and attempts to use "whataboutism" to avoid responsibility are routinely dismissed in court.

What to Watch Next

As the conversation around marriage and divorce evolves in South Korea, keep an eye on three emerging trends:

  • Legislative shifts regarding "at-fault" divorce: There is growing societal pressure to move away from the strict fault-based system toward a more modern approach that acknowledges the reality of broken, unrepairable relationships, even without a clear "villain."
  • Pets in legal disputes: As more Koreans choose pets over children, courts are struggling with how to handle "custody" of animals, which are still legally classified as property. Look for more precedents that prioritize the animal's welfare over the owner's legal title.
  • The "Algorithm" of Marriage: As AI becomes more integrated into personal life-planning, expect more public debate about whether the data-driven approach to marriage is creating more stable unions or merely accelerating the "contractual" termination of relationships.

In the end, Attorney Yang’s message is clear: the most effective way to protect a marriage is not to prepare for a lawsuit, but to remove the environments—like unchecked social circles or digital secrecy—that allow temptation to take root. In an era where marriage is increasingly treated as a temporary arrangement, the only true safeguard remains the intentional, daily effort to communicate and trust.
