The Ghost of SillaJen: Why the Politics of Character Assassination Still Haunts South Korea

October 27, 2026

To scroll through the vibrant, often chaotic world of South Korean political YouTube or community boards like DC Inside is to enter a battlefield where the dead rarely stay buried. In the current era of the Lee Jae-myung administration, one might expect the discourse to be focused on the future—on the proposed Major Crimes Investigative Agency (a planned body to strip the prosecution of its direct investigative powers) or the ongoing reforms of the judicial system. Instead, a familiar specter has returned to the headlines: SillaJen. For those unfamiliar with the acronyms and the intricate web of Seoul’s "prosecution politics," the name refers to a years-old biotech scandal that has been repurposed into a potent weapon of character assassination against one of the liberal camp’s most prominent intellectuals, Rhyu Si-min.

The resurgence of this "zombie rumor" highlights a peculiar mechanism in Korean public life. It is a case where the facts have been litigated, investigated, and found wanting, yet the narrative persists because it serves a specific political utility. For the foreign observer, understanding the SillaJen conspiracy is not just about a failed drug trial or stock manipulation; it is a window into how the prosecution service, once led by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the now-impeached and imprisoned former official), allegedly weaponized financial data to target political enemies. Today, as liberal figures fight back with cold hard facts, they find themselves trapped in a "gaslighting" cycle: if they stay silent, the lie becomes the truth; if they speak up, they are accused of "unnecessarily stirring the pot."

The Biotech Boom and the Birth of a Conspiracy

To understand why a stock scandal is being discussed in the same breath as high politics, one must go back to the mid-2010s. SillaJen was once the darling of the KOSDAQ (Korea’s equivalent of the NASDAQ). The company was developing Pexa-Vec, an oncolytic virus therapy that many hoped would revolutionize cancer treatment. At its peak, SillaJen was a multi-billion dollar entity, fueled by the dreams of retail investors and the hype of the "K-Bio" boom. However, when the drug failed its clinical trials in 2019, the company’s stock plummeted, wiping out the savings of thousands of ordinary Koreans.

In the aftermath, allegations of insider trading and stock manipulation emerged. This is where the story shifts from a failed business venture to a political hit job. During the period when Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol served as the Prosecutor General, the "Special Investigation" units—often described as the elite, politically-charged arm of the prosecution—began looking for a "link" between the SillaJen executives and the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party). The primary target was Rhyu Si-min, the former Minister of Health and Welfare and then-chairman of the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation, a high-profile non-profit dedicated to the legacy of the late liberal president.

The "evidence" was flimsy at best. Rumors circulated that Rhyu had been involved in the company’s early funding or had used his political influence to boost the stock. The most cited "smoking gun" was a 2015 speech Rhyu gave at Pusan National University Hospital during a technical briefing for a SillaJen-related technology. To the prosecution and its allies in the conservative media, this was proof of a deep-seated connection. They alleged that Rhyu was the "political background" that allowed the scam to flourish. However, as subsequent investigations by the very same prosecution service eventually showed, Rhyu held no stock, received no kickbacks, and had no involvement in the company’s management.

The Cho Kuk Intervention: Deciphering the Data

The conspiracy theory recently regained momentum in right-wing online circles, prompting a forceful response from Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive minor party) and a former Justice Minister who himself was a target of the Yoon-era prosecution. Appearing on the popular political YouTube talk show Maebul Show, Cho presented a detailed breakdown of the SillaJen timeline to dismantle the rumors once and for all.

The financial reality, according to the data presented, is starkly different from the conspiracy. The major capital movements in SillaJen occurred in two main waves: a 45 billion won (about $33 million) stake purchase by a private equity fund in 2013, and the acquisition of 4.5 million shares by a company called Daeyu Gaia in 2015. These were massive institutional plays involving professional investors and corporate entities. Through years of exhaustive audits by the Board of Audit and Inspection (the state’s top watchdog) and invasive bank account tracking by the prosecution, the total amount of money linked to Rhyu Si-min or the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation was found to be exactly zero won.

Cho Kuk’s presentation highlighted that Rhyu’s 2015 speech was a standard congratulatory address, given at the request of an acquaintance, with no financial strings attached. Yet, the prosecution under Han Dong-hoon (a key lieutenant of Mr. Yoon at the time) reportedly spent months scouring the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation’s bank records without a warrant, hoping to find even a trace of SillaJen money. They found nothing. The "SillaJen link" was a bridge to nowhere, a political fabrication that failed to meet the threshold of a formal indictment against Rhyu, despite the immense pressure exerted by the investigative authorities.

The Mechanism of "Gaslighting" in Korean Discourse

The most fascinating—and troubling—aspect of this saga is how the conspiracy theorists have shifted their tactics now that the facts have been clarified. When Cho Kuk and other liberal voices used the Maebul Show to debunk the rumors, the response from certain online communities was not to engage with the data, but to attack the act of debunking itself. This is what many in the liberal camp describe as "narrative gaslighting."

The logic used by these detractors follows a cynical pattern:

  • If the target remains silent: "Look, they aren't denying it. They must be guilty. The SillaJen scam is a proven liberal crime."
  • If the target fights back with facts: "Why are you bringing this up again? You're just 'scratching a scab' and making it an issue. If you were truly innocent, you'd let it be forgotten. By defending yourself, you're actually hurting your own side by reminding everyone of the scandal."

This creates a "lose-lose" situation for the victim. The goal of the conspiracy theorists is not to prove a crime—that ship sailed when the prosecution failed to find evidence—but to maintain a "cloud of suspicion" that dampens the moral authority of liberal leaders. By framing the defense as "unnecessary agitation," they attempt to shame the victims into silence while the original lie continues to circulate as "common knowledge" in echo chambers. This tactic was famously used against Hwang Hee-du, a board member of the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation, when he attempted to legally challenge malicious memes. He was told that by taking action, he was the one "spreading" the negativity.

Why the SillaJen Ghost Matters Today

For the international reader, this might seem like an internal squabble over a dead issue. However, the SillaJen controversy is a lynchpin in the broader argument for judicial reform in South Korea. The Lee Jae-myung government and the ruling Democratic Party argue that the SillaJen investigation is a prime example of "prosecutorial overreach"—the practice of selecting a target first and then digging through their entire life (and the lives of their associates) to find a crime to fit the person.

The fact that this "rotten bait," as the source material calls it, is being revived now is no coincidence. As the government moves to establish the Major Crimes Investigative Agency and further reduce the power of the central prosecution, those who benefited from the old system are pushing back. By keeping the SillaJen rumors alive, they seek to portray the very people advocating for reform as "corrupt politicians trying to hide their past crimes." It is a classic diversionary tactic intended to erode public trust in the reform process.

Furthermore, this situation tests the maturity of the Korean media landscape. In an era where YouTube "influencers" and community boards can exert as much influence as traditional newspapers, the burden of fact-checking has shifted to the citizens. The success or failure of "fact-bombing"—the aggressive use of evidence to shut down rumors—will determine whether Korean politics can move toward a discourse based on policy rather than character assassination.

What to Watch Next

As the "SillaJen ghost" continues to haunt the digital halls of the Korean internet, there are several concrete developments to monitor:

  • The Expansion of Liberal Media: Look for how platforms like News Factory (hosted by Kim Eo-jun) and other progressive YouTube outlets coordinate to provide counter-narratives to online rumors. Their ability to mobilize "citizen fact-checkers" is a key pillar of the current government's support base.
  • Legislative Progress on Judicial Reform: The fate of the Major Crimes Investigative Agency will be the ultimate battleground. If the legislation passes, it will signal a definitive end to the era of "Special Investigations" that birthed the SillaJen conspiracy.
  • Legal Actions: Watch whether Rhyu Si-min or the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation pursues civil defamation suits against high-profile YouTubers or community posters. In Korea, the legal threshold for "defamation by factual or false statement" is a powerful, if controversial, tool for clearing one's name.
  • The Response of the People Power Party (PPP): As the main opposition, how the PPP chooses to engage with these rumors will be telling. Will they distance themselves from the "zombie bait" of the Yoon era, or will they continue to use it as a rhetorical cudgel in the National Assembly?

In the end, the SillaJen saga is a reminder that in the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, the truth is often less about what can be proven in court and more about who controls the narrative in the court of public opinion. For now, the liberal camp has chosen to stop hiding and start "scratching the scab," betting that a little temporary discomfort is a small price to pay for finally draining the poison of a decade-old lie.
