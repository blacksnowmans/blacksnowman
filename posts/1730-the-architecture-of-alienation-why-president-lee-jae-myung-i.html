The Architecture of Alienation: Why President Lee Jae-myung is Losing His Base

October 30, 2026

For the supporters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s current ruling party, the mood has shifted from the triumphalism of the election to a cold, creeping anxiety. The air in the "Blue House"—the presidential office and residence—is thick with defensive rhetoric, but the numbers tell a different story. President Lee Jae-myung, the man who rose from a provincial governorship to the highest office on a wave of populist reformism, is seeing his approval ratings stagnate in the low 30% range. To an outside observer, this might look like the standard "honeymoon's end" for any world leader. However, in the context of South Korean politics, these numbers represent something much more volatile: the "deconstruction" of the very political camp that built him.

This is not merely a story of shifting polls or economic headwinds. It is an internal collapse. The coalition of liberal activists, labor unions, and "reform-minded" citizens—collectively known as the democratic-progressive camp—is feeling a sense of profound betrayal. They did not vote for Lee Jae-myung as a cult of personality; they voted for him as a vessel for specific values: judicial reform, social safety nets, and a clean break from the conservative past. Today, many of those supporters feel that the vessel has been hijacked by a new inner circle more interested in consolidating personal power than fulfilling a mandate. As the president’s team pivots to blaming outside critics for the slump, the base is beginning to realize that the call is coming from inside the house.

Rhiu Si-min and the Painful Diagnosis of a Dying Camp

The current discourse reached a fever pitch following recent comments by Rhiu Si-min, a prominent liberal intellectual, author, and former cabinet minister. Rhiu has long been a "bellwether" for the Korean left, a man whose words can both soothe and ignite the party’s grassroots. His recent analysis of President Lee’s falling numbers acted like a bucket of cold water on a feverish party leadership. Rhiu argued that the current trajectory of the Lee administration is starting to look eerily like the final days of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently serving time in prison.

For DPK loyalists, the comparison to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is the ultimate insult, yet Rhiu’s logic is hard to ignore. He pointed out that when a leader stops listening to their core supporters and begins treating critical feedback as "incitement" or "betrayal," the administrative engine begins to seize. Rhiu’s intervention provided a sense of "psychological comfort" to many isolated supporters who felt something was wrong but couldn't quite articulate it. It was a confirmation that they weren't crazy—the government’s communication was indeed becoming rigid and arrogant.

However, the reaction from the President’s inner circle—the so-called "New Lee" faction—was telling. Rather than reflecting on Rhiu’s critique, they attacked the messenger. Some pundits close to the administration went as far as to claim that "Lee’s approval ratings are falling because Rhiu Si-min is talking too much." Rhiu’s response was a masterclass in political sarcasm: "If a few words from a pundit on a broadcast can cause the president’s approval rating to crash, then maybe Rhiu Si-min should be the president." This exchange highlights a dangerous trend in the current administration: a refusal to accept responsibility. When a government blames a single writer for its unpopularity, it reveals a profound lack of confidence in its own policies and leadership.

The Jung Chung-rae Void and the "Myeong-pick" Controversy

If Rhiu Si-min provided the intellectual diagnosis, the resignation of Jung Chung-rae provided the data point for the actual decline. Jung Chung-rae, a senior lawmaker known for his fiery rhetoric and uncompromising stance on reform, stepped down from his position as a party leader on June 24 to prepare for the upcoming party convention. To the "Gaetal"—the passionate, reform-oriented base of the DPK—Jung was more than just a politician; he was their "shield and speaker" within the party leadership.

The polling data shows a clear inflection point around mid-June. As Jung exited the daily leadership fray, the party’s defense line seemed to vanish. Jung represented the hope that, despite questionable decisions at the top, the "reformist DNA" of the party remained intact. He commanded a 46% support rate among party members, a figure that far exceeded personal popularity. It was a mandate for the values he stood for. With him gone, the vacuum was filled by what the Korean media calls "Myeong-pick"—the perception that President Lee is hand-picking loyalists for key positions regardless of their popularity or merit.

This "Myeong-pick" phenomenon came to a head during the nominations for the Seoul Mayor's race in the recent local elections. President Lee reportedly pushed his preferred candidate, Jung Won-oh, who ultimately lost. In a move that shocked the party faithful, the President did not take responsibility for the defeat. Instead, he publicly criticized Jung Chung-rae, suggesting the leadership’s strategy was "not a success." This act of "blaming others" (nam-tat) for one's own tactical failures has become a recurring theme. When the President later appeared to snub Jung during a formal send-off for a European diplomatic trip, the optics were clear: the administration was purging those who weren't 100% subservient. For the 46% of party members who backed Jung, this wasn't just a political reshuffle; it was an eviction notice from their own party.

The Ghost of 1990: Lessons from the "Three-Party Merger"

To understand why the Korean liberal base is so sensitive to these internal shifts, one must look back at the "Three Kims" era of the 1980s and 90s. This was the period dominated by Kim Dae-jung, Kim Young-sam, and Kim Jong-pil. The defining trauma of modern Korean liberalism occurred on January 22, 1990, when Kim Young-sam, a pillar of the pro-democracy movement, joined forces with the military-backed conservatives in what is known as the "Three-Party Merger."

Kim Young-sam framed it as a "decision to save the country," but to his supporters, it was the ultimate betrayal. He traded the values of the democracy movement for a shortcut to the presidency, effectively isolating the reformist stronghold of the Honam region (Gwangju and the Jeolla provinces) and creating a conservative "mega-party." Rhiu Si-min recently described this merger as "the worst atrocity in the history of Korean democracy." It taught liberal voters a hard lesson: a politician’s ambition can easily swallow the movement’s values.

The current anxiety surrounding President Lee Jae-myung is rooted in this historical memory. When the Lee administration talks about "expanding to the center" or "New Lee politics" while distancing itself from the "Pro-Moon" (Moon Jae-in) or "traditional reformist" factions, the base sees the shadows of 1990. They fear that Lee is attempting a "modern-day merger"—not with another party, but with a new, personalized power structure that discards the party’s historical identity. Korean voters have shown time and again that they do not follow a leader into a void; they follow a "line." They moved from Kim Dae-jung to Roh Moo-hyun not because of a personality cult, but because Roh was seen as the most effective tool to carry the torch of reform. If Lee Jae-myung extinguishes that torch to build his own lighthouse, the base will leave him in the dark.

The Arrogance of Personal Power vs. the Strength of the "Line"

The most dangerous delusion currently gripping the presidential office is the idea that the "core supporters" are fans of Lee Jae-myung the man, rather than Lee Jae-myung the reformer. In reality, the "core" is composed of people who want results on "unresolved tasks": curbing the excessive power of the prosecution, reforming the media landscape, and strengthening the social safety net. For these voters, the president is a tool—a means to an end.

By attempting to "privatize" the Democratic Party—making it "Lee Jae-myung’s DPK" rather than "The People’s DPK"—the administration is alienating the very people who protected Lee during his toughest political trials. Pundits like Yi Dong-hyeong, who have called for supporters of former President Moon Jae-in to "leave the party," are essentially advocating for political suicide. You cannot win a national election by cutting off half of your own camp.

The current "Dead Cross" in the polls—where negative sentiment significantly outweighs positive sentiment—is a warning that the administration is treading the same path as Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Mr. Yoon also retreated into a fortress of loyalists, ignored the "line" of his own party, and eventually lost the mandate to govern. If President Lee continues to allow his inner circle to "gatekeep" the presidency and purge symbolic figures like Jung Chung-rae, the 30% approval rating will not be a floor, but a ceiling.

What to watch next

  • The Party Convention: Watch whether the "Myeong-pick" candidates sweep the leadership or if the grassroots rebels by electing independent-minded reformists. This will be the ultimate test of the President's grip on the party machinery.
  • The "Honam" Sentiment: Keep a close eye on polling in the Jeolla provinces. If approval ratings in this traditional liberal stronghold dip below 40%, it signals a total breakdown of the democratic coalition.
  • Policy Pivots: Look for whether the administration doubles down on "personal politics" or returns to the "reform agenda" (prosecution/media reform) that originally energized the base. A return to these issues could signal a reconciliation with the camp's core.
