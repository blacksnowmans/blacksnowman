Why South Korea's Family Holidays Are Triggering a Surge in Divorces

September 30, 2026

For many outside Korea, the country's major traditional holidays like Chuseok (the autumn harvest festival) or Lunar New Year conjure images of ancestral rites, beautiful traditional clothing, and tables laden with food. But behind the postcard scenes lies a darker reality that legal professionals and family counselors know all too well: the moment the holiday ends, divorce inquiries skyrocket.

In South Korea, extended family gatherings act as an intense pressure cooker. Confined together for days, couples confront not just the exhaustion of travel and food preparation, but deep-seated structural tensions about class, parental control, and what marriage is actually supposed to be. A recent discussion featuring psychologist Park Sang-mi and divorce lawyer Lee In-chul laid bare how toxic traditional family dynamics have evolved into something modern, rigid, and increasingly destructive.

From Feuding Mothers-in-Law to Mother-in-Law vs. Son-in-Law

For decades, the undisputed villain of Korean holiday folklore was the gobu conflict — the friction between a domineering mother-in-law and an overworked, undervalued daughter-in-law. It was a dynamic immortalized in countless television dramas, where the husband sat helplessly in the middle.

According to experts, however, that dynamic is rapidly fading. Today, the sharpest tensions are shifting away from the traditional bride-and-mother-in-law dynamic toward a new flashpoint: the relationship between a mother-in-law and her son-in-law.

As younger generations push back against traditional gender roles, daughters are no longer willing to quietly absorb mistreatment at their husband's family home. But this shift has created a mirror-image problem at the wife's family home. Parents who raised their daughters with high expectations are watching their marriages closely, and when a son-in-law falls short of their standards, they are far less forgiving than previous generations.

Lawyer Lee In-chul notes that these modern disputes rarely end in an immediate divorce filing *during* the holiday itself. Instead, the holiday acts as a diagnostic X-ray, exposing fatal cracks in the marriage. Disparities in educational background, job prestige, and financial status — things couples might successfully ignore during the busy blur of everyday life — are laid bare under the microscopic examination of extended family.

The Resume-Driven Marriage Market Comes Home

In South Korea's intensely competitive society, getting a job at a prestigious conglomerate is treated like winning a medal. Many assume that marrying a high-achieving corporate professional provides an automatic shield against the kind of domestic stress that plagues less-affluent households.

Yet psychologists report a rising tide of counseling cases where even elite corporate husbands are subjected to brutal evaluations by their mothers-in-law over holiday dinner tables. Comments comparing a son-in-law's recent promotion speed to a neighbor's, or casually critiquing whether the couple's apartment is owned or rented, cut deep.

This is where Western-style individualism crashes violently into traditional Korean family authoritarianism. South Korea has largely adopted Western notions of nuclear family independence — young adults grow up expecting to live autonomously, manage their own careers, and run their own households. Yet the moment a holiday arrives, parents revert to deeply entrenched Confucian hierarchies, treating their adult children's households not as independent entities, but as subordinate branch offices of the extended family.

When parents use the guise of "helpful advice" to micromanage everything from household finances to private marital dynamics, it ceases to be family affection. It becomes psychological control.

When Parents Push for Divorce Even When the Couple Wants to Stay

Perhaps the most disturbing trend highlighted by experts is parental overreach that actively sabotages struggling marriages. It is not uncommon for a young couple to weather a rough patch, attempt to reconcile, and want to stay together, only to face aggressive pressure from the wife's mother to pull the plug.

Driven by wounded pride — the feeling that *their* daughter deserves better treatment — or bitter disappointment that the husband didn't match the family's predefined checklist, parents sometimes weaponize the holiday to push their children toward legal action. They act as prosecutors in a trial where the married couple are the reluctant defendants.

Marriage requires compromise, patience, and the resilience to weather mutual disappointments. But when meddling parents step in with scalpels, treating human relationships like failing stock investments, few marriages can survive the surgery.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's younger generations continue to prioritize personal independence over traditional filial obligations, the country's collective tolerance for holiday-induced misery is wearing thin.

Watch for whether employers and cultural institutions begin formally accommodating alternative holiday practices — such as skipping family visits altogether or traveling instead. More importantly, observe how legal definitions of family responsibility evolve in Korean courts as more couples legally assert their right to live free from parental interference. Until families learn to grant newly formed households genuine autonomy, Korea's festive holidays will remain a high-stakes stress test for the institution of marriage.
