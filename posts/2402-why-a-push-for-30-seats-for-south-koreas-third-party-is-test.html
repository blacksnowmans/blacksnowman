Why a Push for 30 Seats for South Korea’s Third Party Is Testing Both Sides of Politics

November 16, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape has entered an unusually restless phase under President Lee Jae-myung. Although the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the liberal ruling party—holds a legislative majority, voters who helped sweep the party into power are openly debating a provocative question: should they rally behind a smaller rival, the Rebuilding Korea Party, and push it toward 30 seats in the National Assembly just to keep the ruling camp in check?

The debate has been fueled in part by a widely circulated YouTube political commentary that argues loyal voters must stop handing out blank checks and instead practice "conditional" pressure politics. At the same time, institutional analysts, local governance observers, and empirical fiscal research push back against some of those demands, warning that overly aggressive political disruption or structural rule changes carry steep costs for legislative stability and public finances. Together, these competing perspectives illuminate a three-way struggle among the ruling DPK, the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), and the reformist Rebuilding Korea Party led by Cho Kuk.

For readers outside South Korea, the friction is easier to understand with a bit of context. Following the impeachment, removal from office, and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol, the DPK transitioned from opposition back into government under President Lee Jae-myung. Yet rather than settling into calm governance, both the ruling camp and the conservative opposition are grappling with fractured voter trust, fierce battles over parliamentary rules, and the growing power of partisan online media.

YouTube Factions, Local Gridlock, and the Cost of Constant Elections

Political YouTube channels have become a decisive force in shaping party coalitions in South Korea, though observers disagree on whether that influence is healthy. According to the viral YouTube commentary, a prominent tier of online commentators built massive followings by relentlessly attacking established liberal figures such as former President Moon Jae-in, Rebuilding Korea Party leader Cho Kuk, and prominent commentator Yoo Si-min. Rather than representing a normal generational transition after a change of government, the commentator argued, these efforts reflect a calculation to build closed, personalized political machines—"my own party" under a single faction’s control—leaving ordinary voters asking, "Why should I vote for your party?"

Media and political communication analysts offer a more nuanced reading. From their perspective, YouTube’s recommendation algorithms and lower barriers to entry also allow voices sidelined by South Korea’s top-down party nomination systems and veteran party elders to organize alternative constituencies. Even so, politics built around defining internal rivals as enemies carries an obvious risk: it rallies a core audience online while shrinking the broad coalition needed to win national elections.

Frustrated by politicians who ignore supporters between election cycles, the YouTube commentator went so far as to propose holding elections every single year instead of every two or four years. Even if annual ballots cost an extra 10 billion won (about $7 million), 100 billion won (about $70 million), or even 1 trillion won (about $700 million), the argument goes, constant accountability is worth it—otherwise, neglected voters eventually turn to "anger voting" that wipes out a party’s gains.

Macroeconomic and fiscal research, however, offers a sharp warning against shortening election cycles. An empirical study examining panel data from 226 basic local governments across South Korea between 2002 and 2017 documented a pronounced "political budget cycle": right before local elections, municipal total spending and capital expenditures spike by statistically significant margins as mayors and local politicians rush out visible construction and development projects to win votes. Critics of annual elections argue that putting voters at the polls every year would lock national and local governments into permanent short-term, populist spending, crowding out long-term urban planning and sustainable welfare budgets.

The tension between direct appeals to voters and institutional checks is already playing out in regional politics. In the southeastern industrial city of Ulsan, Mayor Kim Sang-wook has increasingly turned to YouTube broadcasts to air his frustrations directly to residents because conservative majorities in the Ulsan city and district councils repeatedly block his proposals and budgets. The YouTube commentary framed Ulsan as a cautionary tale for the DPK: even with a National Assembly majority today, losing public support could leave the ruling party paralyzed by hostile legislatures tomorrow. Defenders of local councils counter that scrutinizing budgets and checking executive power is precisely what legislatures are supposed to do—and that when mayors bypass compromise for online publicity battles, space for institutional negotiation shrinks.

The 20-Seat Threshold and the Gamble Over Conditional Support

At the center of the debate over the Rebuilding Korea Party’s future is a technical rule in South Korea’s National Assembly Act that carries enormous political weight. To form an official parliamentary "negotiating bloc," a party must hold at least 20 seats. Negotiating blocs control committee assignments, legislative scheduling, and key state subsidies; parties below that threshold have far less leverage on the floor.

With discussions underway between the DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party about easing rules for non-negotiating parties, the third party faces a strategic crossroads. As the YouTube commentary observed, if the ruling DPK lowers the institutional threshold to give the smaller party breathing room, the Rebuilding Korea Party may be tempted to accept that lifeline rather than fight the harder battle to win 20 or more seats on its own. Institutional reformers have long argued that lowering parliamentary thresholds aligns South Korea with mature multiparty democracies by amplifying minority voices. Skeptics counter that making it too easy to form blocs fragments the legislature, slows agenda-setting agreements, and leaves minor parties dependent on larger partners.

To prevent the Rebuilding Korea Party from settling comfortably under the DPK’s umbrella—and to stop the DPK itself from growing complacent—the commentary urged voters to embrace conditional backing:

"Supporters must attach conditions now. You have to say, 'We will help you if you do this.'"

Under that logic, voters would promise grassroots campaigning and a 30-seat push for the Rebuilding Korea Party only if it aggressively drives reform—turning the prospect of a 30-seat rival into leverage over the ruling DPK. The flashpoint is prosecutorial reform, a central pillar for South Korea’s liberal base. The commentary noted that while candidates unanimously campaigned on curbing prosecutorial power, lawmakers now disagree on how far reform should go once in office. It also criticized politicians who mobilized citizens into weekend street protests during the push for Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment and prosecutorial reform, only to dismiss those same supporters as "extreme" or "agitators" when they demand follow-through.

Moderates within the ruling camp respond that governing carries responsibilities opposition campaigning does not. From their standpoint, hastily enacting sweeping prosecutorial changes without safeguards risks creating investigative vacuums, operational disruption, or constitutional challenges. Still, critics note that when politicians campaign on sweeping promises and later scold voters without offering clear benchmarks, public trust erodes rapidly.

Opposition Survival Tactics and the Trap of Internal Control

Internal survival calculations are equally visible inside the main conservative opposition. The YouTube commentary scrutinized what it dubbed the "Washington tactic" of People Power Party chair Jang Dong-hyuk—accusing the leadership of assuming the party will lose upcoming electoral contests and using trips abroad to Washington to manage external optics while tightening control over domestic party machinery.

According to this critique, the real objective lies in party nomination powers. By retaining the authority to appoint local committee chairs across critical metropolitan battlegrounds such as Seoul, Gyeonggi Province, and Incheon, leadership can install loyalists and secure control of the party even after an electoral defeat. Sympathetic voices within or close to the conservative camp reject the label of defeatism, arguing instead that maintaining international diplomatic ties and reorganizing party ranks during an unfavorable political climate is a pragmatic, long-term move to prepare for future recovery. Yet the broader risk highlighted in the debate applies to both sides of the aisle: whenever party leaders prioritize factional preservation and closed nominations over competitive renewal, voters become more likely to abandon established parties altogether.

Inclusive Slogans Meet Administrative Reality

For President Lee Jae-myung’s government, the toughest test may not come from opposition maneuvers, but from the gap between polished branding and day-to-day competence. Since taking office, the DPK and government ministries have rolled out a string of inclusive campaigns and policy brands built around the phrase "for everyone"—including "Democratic Party for Everyone," "Startups for Everyone," and "K-Pass for Everyone."

The YouTube commentary suggested that these branding efforts likely originated with policy aides rather than President Lee personally, and acknowledged that projecting an inclusive image is sound strategy on paper. In practice, however, critics inside and outside the ruling camp argue that administrative execution has fallen well short of the slogans.

The sharpest example involves cybersecurity and personal data protection. In South Korea’s private sector, e-commerce giant Coupang was hit with fines in the 600 billion won range (roughly $430 million) over personal data leaks—underscoring how seriously regulators treat corporate negligence. Yet under the current administration, multiple government agencies suffered repeated personal data breaches over consecutive months, including programs targeting young entrepreneurs and startup initiatives run through the Ministry of SMEs and Startups. Young applicants who had submitted startup concepts to government contests reacted furiously online, asking whether their ideas had been exposed and mocking the administration’s flagship branding as "Data Leaks for Everyone."

What to Watch Next

Several concrete developments in the coming months will show whether South Korea’s major parties adapt to these pressures or deepen voter disillusionment:

  • National Assembly bloc rules: Watch whether the Democratic Party of Korea and the Rebuilding Korea Party formally move to revise threshold rules for non-negotiating groups, or whether the third party chooses to compete independently toward 20 or 30 seats.
  • Prosecutorial reform legislation: Track how the ruling party balances pressure from reformist voters for decisive bills against internal caution over judicial stability and investigative continuity.
  • People Power Party nominations: Observe how Jang Dong-hyuk’s appointment of regional committee leaders across Seoul, Gyeonggi Province, and Incheon shapes internal unity—or triggers backlash—within the conservative opposition.
  • Government data security fixes: Watch whether the Ministry of SMEs and Startups and the broader Lee Jae-myung administration overhaul digital security protocols to close the credibility gap behind their "for everyone" policy campaigns.
