The Smartphone Presidency: How Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Habit Is Isolating the Blue House

November 06, 2026

Late-night smartphone notifications rarely carry the weight of national policy, but in contemporary South Korean politics, a single tap on a touchscreen can reorder the political agenda. When President Lee Jae-myung recently logged onto his personal social media account and retweeted a short video clip featuring lawyer Kim Eun-il, it was not merely an act of casual digital sharing. The short-form video contained a sharp warning against political factions driven by empty slogans and backroom orders. Lee added his own commentary: "We must overcome both slogan politics, which rely on demagoguery, and order politics, which rely on patronage and connections. If you have been granted power, you must feel an equal weight of responsibility."

To an outside observer, this might sound like a routine executive call for political reform and ethical governance. Within South Korea's hyper-polarized political ecosystem, however, the retweet resonated as a direct strike against internal critics within Lee's own party. By using his massive online megaphone to amplify specific digital commentary, the president engaged in what local analysts call an intentional "shout-out"—a signal to his fierce online supporter base to target non-mainstream figures inside the ruling party. Rather than elevating executive discourse, the move has reignited a fierce national debate: is President Lee using social media to foster direct democratic engagement, or is he practicing a brand of partisan score-settling that degrades the dignity of the presidency and deepens South Korea’s political divides?

A Retweet as a Weapon: Decoding the War on "Slogan Politics"

Understanding why a YouTube retweet matters requires looking at how President Lee built his political career. As a former governor and municipal leader who rose outside traditional political dynasties, Lee has long relied on direct digital communication to bypass mainstream media channels and mobilize a passionate digital fandom. Now sitting in the Blue House as the head of the Democratic Party of Korea—South Korea's main center-left ruling party—Lee continues to treat his personal social media accounts as his primary command center.

Yet the nature of executive communication changes when one becomes the head of state. A president's word is not simply personal sentiment; it is interpreted by the public, the bureaucracy, and foreign governments as official state orientation. When Lee posted that political leaders must move past "slogan politics" while sharing a partisan video, political insiders read it as a thinly veiled attack on moderate and traditional factions within the ruling party who have expressed concern over executive overreach.

The contradiction in Lee's messaging has drawn sharp criticism. In a subsequent social media post, the president warned against the dangers of blind partisanship, writing, "If you sincerely believe that everything your side says is right and everything the opposing side says is wrong, you are in a serious state." He added that healthy debate requires acknowledging facts regardless of who presents them. While the sentiment aligns with core democratic principles, critics note that Lee rarely applies this standard to himself. When confronted with critical news coverage or pushback from judicial bodies, Lee has frequently lashed out, at times employing aggressive language such as "pigs only see pigs" to dismiss critics. By framing all opposition as the resistance of corrupt establishment interests while lecturing the public on objectivity, Lee risks reducing democratic discourse to a tool for marginalizing dissent.

The Core Dilemma: A president using social media to highlight specific commentators is rarely engaging in open dialogue. Instead, it serves as a mobilization signal, providing target coordinates to online loyalists and eroding the neutral dignity required of the head of state.

The Polling Trap: How Social Media Fatigue Feeds Executive Fragility

The immediate consequence of this combat-heavy social media strategy is visible in public opinion metrics. According to a poll conducted by Public Opinion Fair (commissioned by PennMike), President Lee’s national approval rating has hovered in a stagnant box-group of 32.6%, with negative evaluations soaring to 66.1%. More critically, the survey revealed that 62.5% of respondents viewed the president’s social media activity negatively, describing it as excessive and damaging to executive dignity. Only 31.9% viewed his online posts positively as a constructive effort to narrow the distance between the government and the public.

Disapproval of Lee’s online conduct is particularly severe among key demographics that often decide elections, including voters in their 20s (69.8% negative) and residents of the Seoul capital region (65.3% negative). Polling methodologies also highlight a growing divide: automated response system (ARS) surveys show approval locked in the mid-30s, reflecting deep fatigue among politically active voters who feel exhausted by continuous online warfare.

In South Korea’s presidential system, low approval ratings do not just hurt pride; they trigger a dangerous structural reaction across state institutions:

  • Investigative Pressure: Independent investigative bodies, such as the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO), tend to become far more assertive when a president's public support drops, sharpening their probes into administration allies.
  • Judicial Pushback: The judiciary, sensitive to shifts in public sentiment, shows less deference to executive priorities, accelerating legal scrutiny against ruling coalition figures.
  • Bureaucratic Paralysis: Civil servants in South Korea are notoriously sensitive to political power dynamics. When a president's popularity plunges into the 30% range, senior bureaucrats adopt a posture known locally as bokji-budong ("crouching low and doing nothing"), delaying key policy rollouts out of fear of post-administration legal retaliation.
  • Media Hostility: Rather than insulating the president, attempts to bypass traditional press outlets invariably alienate journalists, prompting broad-spectrum editorial hostility across both conservative and moderate news outlets.

During the previous administration of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ultimately impeached and removed from office—progressive commentators warned of the dangers of executive overreach and political polarization. For many of those same observers, watching the current administration succumb to low approval ratings caused by unforced digital errors evokes a troubling sense of history repeating itself.

Stranded Dolphins: The Dissolution of the Progressive Coalition

The deeper crisis facing the Lee administration is not merely external pressure from the opposition People Power Party, but the internal fragmentation of the progressive movement itself. Historically, South Korea's center-left coalition succeeded by maintaining a broad umbrella that included labor activists, liberal intellectuals, moderate civic groups, and pragmatists.

Today, that broad umbrella is being replaced by an aggressive ecosystem of partisan online commentators often dubbed the "New Lee Jae-myung" media. Dominating internet broadcasts and partisan YouTube channels such as "Democratic Party TV," these hardline commentators focus less on winning over swing voters and more on purging internal party rivals. Moderates who raise concerns about administration policy are routinely branded with hostile labels like "watermelons" (a derogatory term implying someone is green on the outside but red/conservative on the inside).

This dynamic creates what political scientists call a toxic echo chamber, driving the presidency into deep isolation. The phenomenon resembles a well-known marine tragedy: groups of dolphins unexpectedly swimming into shallow sandbars, where they become stranded and perish. By turning away from the broad ocean of the general public to wallow in the shallow waters of hardline internet fandom, the administration is effectively stranding itself on a political sandbar.

This insular strategy contrasts sharply with the legacy of past Democratic presidents. Former President Kim Dae-jung, despite surviving political persecution, prioritized national unity above all else, actively embracing political rivals to heal historical regional divides. Former President Roh Moo-hyun consistently risked his own political base to pursue policies he believed were in the national interest, such as negotiating the US-Korea Free Trade Agreement. By contrast, the current focus on online base mobilization risks trading long-term presidential legitimacy for short-term internet applause.

What to Watch Next

Despite mounting criticism, President Lee shows little intention of changing course. During an October 19 meeting with press corps members, Lee declared that social media was his "lifeline," asserting that direct digital engagement and live-streamed Cabinet meetings would continue uninterrupted. He even shared posts ranking world leaders by their social media followings to underline his commitment to digital outreach.

As the administration navigates this self-inflicted turbulence, international observers and market analysts should monitor three concrete indicators over the coming months:

  • Ruling Party Friction and Resignation Calls: Watch whether moderate Democratic Party of Korea lawmakers facing tough re-election prospects begin publicly demanding that President Lee step back from party affairs or surrender his party membership (taldang) to prevent executive drag on local contests.
  • Legislative and Bureaucratic Execution: Track whether major domestic legislative agendas stall in the National Assembly due to bureaucratic hesitation and a lack of cross-party dialogue, signaling that executive authority has effectively become paralyzed.
  • Diplomatic Leverage: Observe how foreign counterparts respond during upcoming international summits. South Korean presidents who suffer from persistent low domestic approval often find their bargaining power severely diminished when negotiating complex trade and security arrangements with key allies like the United States.

Direct communication is a powerful tool in modern politics, but it cannot replace the hard work of building broad coalitions and governing for the entire nation. Unless President Lee chooses to put down his smartphone and step off the partisan sandbar, his administration risks remaining trapped in a cycle of digital conflict while the urgent responsibilities of statecraft pass by.
