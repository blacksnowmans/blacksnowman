The Impeachment Trap: South Korea’s Judicial and Executive Collision

November 23, 2026

The streets of Daegu, the historical heartland of South Korean conservatism, are currently echoing with a rhetoric that would have seemed unthinkable just a few years ago. Under the cold November sky, thousands of supporters of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, have gathered not just to protest policy, but to demand the removal of the head of state. Their targets are twofold: President Lee Jae-myung and the legislative maneuvers of his ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK).

For a foreign observer, the current political climate in Seoul feels like a pressure cooker. Since the impeachment and imprisonment of the previous leader, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the country has remained in a state of perpetual political warfare. The current administration, led by President Lee Jae-myung, finds itself locked in a recursive cycle of "impeachment politics" with the opposition. While the ruling DPK uses its legislative majority to pressure the judiciary, the opposition PPP has taken to the streets, framing the government’s actions as an "insurrection" against the constitutional order.

What is at stake is no longer just a disagreement over taxes or diplomacy. It is a fundamental struggle over the separation of powers. When the ruling party moves to impeach the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, and the opposition responds by calling for the impeachment of the President, the institutional guardrails of the world’s most vibrant democracy begin to show signs of structural fatigue. This is not merely a local squabble; it is a crisis of legitimacy that is beginning to rattle the nation’s financial markets and its standing on the global stage.

The War on the Judiciary and the Chief Justice

At the center of the current storm is Jo Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Korea. In a move that has sent shockwaves through the legal community, the ruling Democratic Party has signaled its intent to pursue impeachment proceedings against him. The DPK’s justification rests on what they describe as "distorted prosecutions" and a failure of the judiciary to remain impartial in cases involving high-ranking officials. However, to the opposition, this is a transparent attempt to intimidate the courts.

Jang Dong-hyeok, a prominent representative of the People Power Party, recently addressed a massive crowd in Daegu, characterizing the DPK’s move as a "suicidal act" and an "act of rebellion." According to Jang, the attempt to remove Chief Justice Jo is a "bulletproof" tactic designed to delay or derail various legal proceedings surrounding President Lee Jae-myung. The rhetoric used—terms like "internal rebellion" or "tearing up the Constitution"—reflects a level of polarization where the opponent is no longer a partner in governance but an existential threat to the state.

Even the Reforming Korea Party (also known as the Reform Party), a third-party movement led by the young and tech-savvy Lee Jun-seok, has weighed in with uncharacteristic vitriol. Lee, who often positions himself as a rational alternative to the two-party duopoly, used the phrase "R.I.P. Democratic Party" to describe the ruling party’s judicial strategy. He warned that the DPK is inviting a massive public backlash that could "collapse the party in a single blow." This alignment between the main conservative opposition and the third-party reformists suggests that the ruling party may be overreaching, potentially alienating centrist voters who value judicial independence as a core tenet of Korean democracy.

The "Lee Jae-myung Bubble" and Market Anxiety

While the political theater plays out on the streets of Daegu and in the halls of the National Assembly, South Korea’s financial markets are beginning to signal deep unease. The term "Black Monday" has been circulating among traders, a reference to the fear of a sudden, catastrophic drop in the KOSPI (the Korea Composite Stock Price Index) as investors weigh the risks of prolonged political instability.

Jeong Jeom-sig, the Floor Leader of the People Power Party, has been vocal in warning the government about an impending economic correction. He has coined the term "Lee Jae-myung Bubble," suggesting that the current administration's economic policies are artificially inflated and destined for a painful burst. Jeong argues that the government’s obsession with political consolidation and its "impeachment war" is distracting it from urgent market stabilization measures. He has called for an emergency response plan before the next market opening, emphasizing that "while the government is busy tearing down the judiciary, the livelihoods of everyday investors are being sacrificed."

The economic stakes are high. South Korea is a trade-dependent nation with a massive retail investor base. Any perception that the rule of law is being compromised by executive or legislative overreach can lead to capital flight. For the foreign investor, the question is simple: Is the South Korean government more interested in settling political scores than in maintaining a stable environment for business? The opposition is betting that the public’s concern for their portfolios will eventually outweigh their loyalty to the President’s populist platform.

Strategic Realignment in the Semiconductor Belt

Amidst the high-level drama, a more subtle but equally important political reorganization is taking place in the "semiconductor belt" of southern Gyeonggi Province. This region, which includes cities like Yongin and Seongnam, is the engine of the Korean economy and a crucial swing district in any national election. Lee Gi-in, a key leader in the Reform Party, recently announced his departure from his decade-long political base in Bundang to run for a leadership position in the Yongin-Jeong district.

This move is strategic. Yongin is the site of massive planned investments by companies like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, worth hundreds of billions of dollars. By shifting his focus here, Lee Gi-in is attempting to position the Reform Party as the true representative of the "high-tech middle class"—voters who are tired of the ideological battles in Seoul and want a government focused on infrastructure, technology, and economic growth. This "Southern Gyeonggi Strategy" is an attempt to create a new political center of gravity that could potentially break the deadlock between the DPK and the PPP in the next election cycle.

Meanwhile, local governments are trying to distance themselves from the noise in the capital to secure practical gains. Leaders like the Mayor of Daejeon and the Speaker of the Daegu City Council have been visiting the National Assembly, not to join the protests, but to lobby for the "Local Council Act" and for the allocation of national budget funds for 2027. This highlights a growing disconnect: while the central political figures are engaged in a "war of all against all," local leaders are desperate to ensure that the actual machinery of the state continues to function and provide for their constituents.

A Mirror to Global Extremism

The source of much of the current tension in Korea is not entirely unique; it reflects a global trend toward political polarization and the rise of hardline rhetoric. Korean analysts have been drawing parallels between the domestic situation and recent events in Europe and the Middle East. The source material points to the rise of the Alternative for Germany (AfD), which has recently faced scandals regarding "remigration" plans for millions of residents, and the enduring influence of Marine Le Pen in France as she prepares for a 2027 presidential run.

The comparison is sobering. In Germany, the discovery of Nazi symbols within the military and the arrest of soldiers for far-right terror plots illustrate what happens when extremist political rhetoric seeps into state institutions. In South Korea, while the context is different, the underlying mechanism is similar: the use of exclusionary language and the delegitimization of state institutions (like the courts) to mobilize a fervent base. Even the violence of radical settlers in the West Bank is cited by Korean commentators as a warning of how far-right ideologies can lead to "state-sanctioned" or "state-ignored" violence against perceived enemies.

The danger for South Korea is that if the current "impeachment cycle" continues, it will normalize the idea that the losers of an election do not have to accept the outcome, and the winners do not have to respect the limits of their power. When a ruling party treats the judiciary as an obstacle to be cleared, and an opposition party treats the presidency as a target for immediate removal, the "democratic consensus" that has held Korea together since 1987 begins to dissolve.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will be critical for the trajectory of the Lee Jae-myung administration and the stability of the Korean state. Here are the specific developments to monitor:

  • The Impeachment Filing: Watch whether the DPK officially submits the impeachment motion for Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae. If they do, it will trigger a constitutional crisis that will likely land in the Constitutional Court, further paralyzing the government.
  • Market Performance: Keep an eye on the KOSPI and the Korean Won. If the "Black Monday" fears materialize and the market continues to slide, the PPP will gain significant leverage by blaming the "Lee Jae-myung Bubble."
  • The "Semiconductor Belt" Polling: As third-party figures like Lee Gi-in move into strategic districts like Yongin, look for local polling data. If the Reform Party gains traction, it could signal that the public is looking for an escape hatch from the two-party conflict.
  • Presidential Response: Thus far, President Lee has maintained a hardline stance. Any sign of a "grand compromise" or, conversely, an escalation in executive orders to bypass the legislature will dictate whether the temperature in Seoul rises or falls.

In South Korea, politics has always been a contact sport. However, the current collision between the executive and judicial branches suggests that the rules of the game are being rewritten in real-time. For the people of Korea, and those who invest in their future, the hope is that the country’s robust civic institutions can survive this latest test of the separation of powers.
