The Reform Paradox: Why South Korea's Ruling Party Is Losing Its Base

October 18, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, an old political rule of thumb has begun to circulate like a quiet death sentence: multiply the president's approval rating by three, and you get the ruling party’s seats in the next general election. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the arithmetic is chilling. In mid-October polls, disapproval of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration climbed to 64.0%, while his approval rating slumped to 31.7%. When asked about voting intentions for the upcoming general election, 51.7% favored the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), compared to just 28.4% for the DPK—a staggering 23.3-percentage-point chasm.

Under the Yeouido formula, that translates to a catastrophic 95 seats for the ruling party, well below a blocking minority in South Korea's 300-seat legislature. Panic has inevitably set in among party strategists. Yet the real crisis confronting the Lee Jae-myung administration is not found in the fickle swings of headline polling. It lies in a far more dangerous psychological rupture: the collapse of political efficacy among the very core supporters who brought the administration to power, triggered by a perceived retreat on prosecutorial reform and a deeply controversial appointment to lead a brand-new investigative agency.

The Math of Panic: Why Polling Does Not Guarantee a 95-Seat Disaster

Whenever tracking polls crater, party leaderships tend to succumb to defeatism. But historical election data demonstrates that translating national approval ratings directly into parliamentary seat counts fundamentally misreads the mechanics of South Korean elections. Korean voters do not cast ballots under a pure proportional representation system. Over 250 of the National Assembly's seats are decided in single-member, first-past-the-post districts, where a race can be won by ten thousand votes or by a single ballot.

A look at the past three general elections reveals consistent discrepancies between pre-election predictions and actual tallies:

  • The 20th General Election (2016): Under the conservative Park Geun-hye administration, presidential approval hovered around 39%, leading pollsters to predict 117 seats for the ruling Saenuri Party. When the ballots were counted, Saenuri won 122 seats, an error margin of five seats.
  • The 21st General Election (2020): Riding a wave of strong public approval (59%) during the progressive Moon Jae-in administration, models predicted 177 seats for the DPK; the party ultimately swept to a historic 180 seats.
  • The 22nd General Election (2024): During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was later impeached, removed from office for insurrection, and is currently imprisoned—his dismal 37.3% approval led analysts to project 111.9 seats for the ruling People Power Party. The PPP ended up securing 108 seats.

Beyond historical variances, recent local and by-elections have highlighted the systemic blind spots of conventional polling, particularly when it comes to capturing silent or reluctant voters. In the June 3 local elections, surveys leading up to the race showed the DPK candidate Jung Won-oh leading in the Seoul mayoral contest, yet the PPP incumbent Oh Se-hoon ultimately carried the capital by 1.02 percentage points to secure a fifth term. In South Gyeongsang Province, a pre-blackout poll by Hankook Research showed DPK candidate Kim Kyoung-soo leading at 45% against PPP candidate Park Wan-soo's 34%, yet Park won comfortably with 51.28% of the vote. Most dramatically, in the National Assembly by-election for Pyeongtaek-B, polls by Korea Research International and Kstat Research projected a tight race between DPK candidate Kim Yong-nam and Cho Kuk of the Rebuilding Korea Party, with PPP candidate Yu Eui-dong trailing in third place around the low 20% range. When the boxes opened, Yu took the seat with 34.83%.

Much of this disconnect stems from the phenomenon of "shy conservative" voters who declined to state their preferences to live telephone interviewers following the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, only to turn out reliably at the ballot box—a pattern captured far more accurately by automated response system (ARS) polls. The danger for the ruling party, therefore, is not an inevitable drop to 95 seats, but the paralysis that comes from treating flawed projections as destiny.

A Broken Promise: How a Prosecutor Came to Lead the Anti-Prosecution Agency

If the polling apocalypse is partly an illusion, the grassroots disillusionment driving it is entirely real. Progressive voters in South Korea have long viewed reforming the country's entrenched, politically aggressive state prosecution service as an existential priority. For decades, South Korean prosecutors held a near-monopoly on both criminal investigations and indictments, giving them enormous leverage over political life. The long-sought progressive solution was straightforward: separate investigation from indictment, reducing the prosecution service to a purely indicting body while transferring major investigative authorities to new, specialized agencies.

That foundational promise was upended when President Lee Jae-myung nominated Kim Ji-yong, a private attorney and veteran legal figure, as the inaugural head of the newly created Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA). Kim is a career prosecutor through and through: hailing from Buyeo, South Chungcheong Province, and a graduate of Sungkyunkwan University's law department, he spent 24 years inside the prosecution service, rising to head the Criminal Department at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office and leading the Chuncheon District Prosecutors' Office.

The presidential office justified Kim’s nomination by highlighting his deep courtroom experience, oversight expertise, and organizational management skills. But for civil society groups and reform-minded DPK partisans, the choice felt like an outright betrayal. Most damagingly, Kim had previously been a vocal, public proponent of allowing prosecutors to maintain direct supplementary investigative powers—the exact mechanism reform advocates had fought for years to strip away.

To many within the progressive coalition, appointing a 24-year prosecution veteran to lead an agency explicitly created to dismantle the prosecution’s investigative hegemony is not reform; it is bureaucratic sleight of hand. Critics have likened the move to a sprawling family-owned conglomerate (*chaebol*) creating two new corporate subsidiaries and appointing former division heads to run them. The sign on the door changes, but the alumni network, informal hierarchies, and institutional loyalties remain identical. Commentator Rhyu Si-min captured this cynicism bluntly, remarking that "every fish market rots" without fresh salt, and warning that President Lee appears to be wearing "shoes the public forced him into" rather than advancing an agenda he genuinely believes in.

The Danger of Dismissing Loyal Criticism

Compounding the frustration of the DPK’s base is the combative tone coming from the administration itself. Rather than addressing the ideological inconsistency of the appointment, the presidential office has largely dismissed internal dissent. Senior Presidential Secretary for Public Communication Sung Ki-hong defended the nominee by arguing that Kim's background had been thoroughly vetted and that labeling him sympathetic to conservative figures was an unfair smear. Nominee Kim Ji-yong himself offered little reassurance during public remarks, deflecting systemic questions with bureaucratic detachment by claiming that Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol had exerted the worst negative influence on the institution.

When reform-minded lawmakers, such as National Assembly member Park Eun-jung, raised principled objections during the vetting process, their critiques were cast aside by party insiders as excessive ideological purism. This posture risks alienating the very voters who braved rain and cold to rally behind the party during past crises.

There is also an institutional memory at play that the administration ignores at its peril. South Korea's modern political history shows that prosecutorial institutions rarely remain loyal to their political appointers once power begins to ebb. South Korean presidents who accommodated the prosecution apparatus in hopes of securing institutional peace often found those very investigative tools turned against them after leaving office. For a president who once candidly admitted after local election setbacks that worries over life after office kept him awake at night, compromising on prosecutorial independence is a precarious gamble.

Warning signs are already flashing in South Korea's traditional swing regions. In the June 3 local elections, voter turnout across the central Chungcheong region surged: Daejeon hit 59.7%, Sejong 62.5%, South Chungcheong 58.8%, and North Chungcheong 59.6%, driving the regional average to 60.15%—nearly ten points higher than the previous local contest (50.3%). High participation in Chungcheong, paired with early voting rates above 22% across the provinces, demonstrated an electorate actively evaluating national governance. If swing voters in Chungcheong and the competitive battlegrounds of the Seoul Capital Area conclude that the administration lacks the resolve to deliver on its core commitments, the DPK will find it impossible to hold the center.

What to Watch Next

The path forward for the Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party will be determined by how they handle the fallout from this nomination in the coming weeks:

  • The National Assembly Confirmation Hearing: Pay close attention to how nominee Kim Ji-yong answers questions regarding the separation of investigation and indictment. Any continued defense of prosecutorial supplementary investigative powers will deepen the revolt among progressive backbenchers and minor opposition allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party.
  • Executive Course Correction: Watch whether the presidential office digs in or signals a personnel pivot. Withdrawing the nomination or appointing staunch reform advocates to key deputy posts within the MCIA could stem the erosion of activist goodwill.
  • Polling Dynamics in the Seoul Capital Area and Chungcheong: The true test of whether this controversy has permanent electoral consequences will appear in district-level polling across Gyeonggi Province, Incheon, and the Chungcheong corridor, rather than broad national approval figures. If the gap between the DPK and the PPP widens further in these competitive seats, internal pressure on the Blue House to overhaul its domestic political strategy will become overwhelming.
