The Global Crisis of Fairness: What Student Protests in Paris and Political Gridlock in Seoul Reveal About the Future

October 09, 2026

When you look at the news lately, it is easy to feel as though the clock tracking social progress has somehow broken, leaving societies stuck between competing demands for institutional fairness and the protection of the classroom. Across the globe, from the tear-gas-hazy streets of Paris to the political corridors of Yeouido in Seoul, citizens are asking a fundamental question: Who is the modern state actually serving when it talks about fairness?

While the triggers differ—ranging from violent clashes over educational austerity in France to fierce legislative battles over prosecutorial reform under South Korea's liberal Democratic Party administration—these crises share a common root. They are symptoms of a growing disconnect between political elites and the younger generations who must live with the consequences of institutional decay. To understand why these parallel frustrations matter, we have to look past the surface headlines and examine how fiscal austerity, eroding classroom authority, and stalled political reforms are intersecting to threaten the social contract.

The Boiling Point in French Classrooms and the Danger of Austerity

France is currently gripped by a wave of high school and university student protests that go far beyond standard teenage rebellion. What began as localized student mobilization in the outskirts of Paris has metastasized into a nationwide movement. By early October, active demonstrations drew an estimated 266,000 protesters, resulting in nearly 7,000 arrests and upwards of 1,000 reported injuries. The situation reached a flashpoint when a 15-year-old student tragically lost a hand during clashes, fueling intense public outrage over police crowd-control tactics.

Students did not take to the streets for trivial reasons. France’s public education system is buckling under the weight of crumbling infrastructure, overcrowded classrooms, and brutal academic schedules. Most shocking is a severe nationwide teacher shortage: last year alone, a staggering 15.4 million hours of classroom instruction were canceled simply because schools had no educators to put in front of the rooms. When students show up to learn and find no one there, the promise of public education breaks down. Add to this widespread anger over Parcoursup, the centralized university admissions platform widely criticized for cementing educational inequality, and the students' motivation becomes starkly clear.

For international observers, the French crisis offers a chilling cautionary tale. The French government's aggressive push toward fiscal austerity—tightening budgets so severely that education spending fails to keep pace with inflation—comes with a heavy human cost. Sacrificing the educational rights of the next generation in the name of fiscal balance inevitably breeds social explosion. It proves that financial crises and educational decay are not isolated spreadsheet problems; they are political time bombs.

Erosion of Authority and the Crisis in South Korean Classrooms

While French students battle systemic neglect, South Korean classrooms are experiencing a parallel collapse of institutional authority. Despite ongoing legislative efforts to pass robust laws protecting teaching activities, teachers on the ground feel utterly abandoned. Basic, routine pedagogical actions—such as disciplining a misbehaving student or asking a student to stand facing the wall—frequently result in criminal complaints for child abuse. Armed with professional legal representation, frustrated parents can easily overwhelm individual educators, turning the teaching profession into a high-stakes hazard.

Vague legal definitions surrounding emotional abuse and negligence have effectively turned the law into a trap for teachers. When education professionals find themselves facing intense legal scrutiny, an entire career can unravel overnight. Yet administrative authorities have moved sluggishly. Although overall education budgets have expanded, funding is rarely directed toward expanding the teacher workforce or relieving systemic burnout. Instead, lawmakers frequently attempt to patch over structural cracks with superficial legal tweaks that leave the core problem untouched.

This dynamic has transformed schools from spaces of community and learning into tense environments of mutual surveillance. When classrooms become places where teachers and parents eye each other with legal suspicion, the foundational stability of the education system evaporates. Without structural reforms to shield educators from predatory litigation, South Korea’s educational future rests on a very fragile foundation.

Prosecutorial Reform and the Limits of Political Will

Underpinning many of South Korea’s domestic policy struggles is a deeper fight over state power, specifically the ongoing push for prosecutorial reform under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. The path toward reforming powerful investigative agencies has proven treacherous, as highlighted by contentious confirmation hearings for key figures like Kim Ji-hyung, nominated to lead the Serious Crime Investigation Agency.

The handling of these hearings has exposed deep anxieties within the ruling Democratic Party. Early decisions—such as omitting certain critical voices while including figures from older legal establishments—suggest that institutional caution and the preservation of entrenched influence are blunting the edge of reform. If the ruling party treats confirmation hearings as a mere formality or attempts to shield controversial nominees from rigorous scrutiny, it risks alienating younger voters who view the party as increasingly protective of its own institutional privileges.

Prosecutorial reform is not an abstract political talking point; it is a vital reckoning with past abuses, including historical scandals involving manipulated investigations and politically motivated indictments. When the political establishment treats structural reform like a bitter custody battle over institutional turf rather than a mandate for justice, the public grows deeply cynical. To maintain moral authority, the ruling bloc must embrace uncompromising verification rather than defensive circling of the wagons.

The Road Ahead: Rebuilding Social Trust

Whether examining the students marching through the boulevards of Paris or observing the stalled legislative reforms in Seoul, the underlying malaise is the same. Societies are struggling to deliver on promises of fairness, equitable education, and accountable governance. When fiscal restraint translates into broken schools, and when political reform bogs down in factional self-preservation, the social fabric frays.

Addressing these converging crises requires a clear-eyed return to foundational principles. For policymakers, it means recognizing that investing in the next generation—through robust educational support, protected teaching environments, and genuine accountability for powerful institutions—is not optional. For citizens, it requires maintaining the pressure of active civic engagement.

The Core Challenge: When systemic rules fail to protect the vulnerable, the entire social contract is called into question. Rebuilding trust requires political courage and an uncompromising commitment to institutional integrity.

The anger seen on the streets of Europe and the quiet frustration felt in East Asian classrooms stem from the exact same human desire: a world where fairness is a reality rather than a slogan. If societies fail to heed these warnings, they will continue to drift into deeper polarization. The task for political leaders and engaged citizens alike is to break through the inertia of privilege and build institutions worthy of the future they claim to serve.
