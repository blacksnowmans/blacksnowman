Why Smart Young Koreans Are Striking Out on Romance and Deep Thought

October 06, 2026

Open YouTube in South Korea today, and you are immediately met with videos asking a blunt question: "Why bother dating?" It is a sentiment that no longer raises eyebrows. With a smartphone that streams endless entertainment from across the globe and an urban infrastructure designed entirely for solo living, the friction of human relationships can feel entirely optional.

For a growing number of young people, the math simply does not work out. Why spend precious time and emotional labor on someone else when the return on investment is so low? Yet, as the population of smart, capable singles swells, we cannot simply write this off as a quirky shift in personal taste. When technology and capital reshape society this profoundly, they begin to warp human nature itself.

The Paradox of AI Convenience and the Death of Deep Thought

We are living at the absolute frontier of human science and technology. A flick of the finger commands artificial intelligence to draft reports, while algorithms understand our personal preferences better than we do ourselves. Yet, ironically, humanity has never felt more exhausted or imperiled.

Appearing on a recent talk show, Sang-wook Kim, a physics professor at Kyung Hee University—one of South Korea's most prestigious private universities—issued a sobering warning. He noted that if civilization ever regeded to a point where writing was no longer used, we would likely lose the capacity for deep thought itself. The ultimate cost of that loss, he argued, might well be democracy.

As generations raised on short-form videos and rapid-fire visual media expand, the ability to reason logically and think deeply is quietly withering away. Government data in South Korea consistently shows adult reading rates hitting record lows year after year. Grappling with text and reading between the lines has come to feel like grueling manual labor.

This matters because reading and writing are not merely tools for transferring information; they are the fundamental mechanics by which the human brain categorizes logic. Visual media allows the brain to passively fill in blanks without real engagement. The result is an illusion of intelligence masking the collapse of deep cognitive foundations. When we outsource our moral and factual reasoning to algorithms, the rational debate and compromise that underpins democracy evaporate with it.

The Cruelty of the Fast-Paced Digital Economy

Every wave of new technology brings an unwritten compulsion to adapt and consume at breakneck speed. Yet Professor Kim revealed a surprising personal habit: he uses new technology only after others have suffered through the bugs and the systems have fully stabilized. He still wears shoes that are over a decade old.

The cruelty of South Korea's hyper-efficient culture is laid bare when this "hurry, hurry" (*bali-bali*) ethos collides with daily life. Walk into a fast-food restaurant or café dominated by digital kiosks, and you will often see elderly patrons struggling to place an order. Instead of patience, the people lined up behind them sigh and shoot cold, judgmental glances. Corporate efficiency slashes labor costs, but leaves behind a vacuum where social safety nets and basic human empathy should be.

What Physics Teaches Us About Unchanging Human Nature

No matter how wildly the world changes, certain truths remain fixed. As Jeff Bezos once noted, the best way to prepare for the future is to focus on what *won't* change. In physics, when variables are unknown, scientists look for conservation laws—unchanging values that allow them to solve the problem.

Human nature has not changed fundamentally in 50,000 years. The hunter-gatherer instinct to gorge on food when we see it, or the deep-seated anxiety about what our tribe thinks of us, remains fully intact within our modern skulls. Our brains and bodies are still running on Paleolithic operating systems.

This is where education must adapt. Teaching short-term coding trends or transient software skills produces knowledge that rots almost instantly. Artificial intelligence can mimic human tasks, but it cannot replace the humanities and deep critical thinking. According to Professor Kim, the core subjects that must be preserved across generations are history, philosophy, art, mathematics, basic science, and the foundational acts of reading and writing.

Structural Cliffs and the Anguish of Frustrated Elites

South Korea boasts some of the highest university enrollment rates in the world, yet the reality awaiting graduates is grim. The country's youth suicide rate remains near the top of the OECD, pointing to a profound systemic pathology rather than isolated personal weakness. The social contract—study hard, get a degree, secure a stable career—has shattered.

The extreme wage gap between conglomerates and small-to-medium enterprises, combined with a dual-labor market, makes meaningful savings or asset-building nearly impossible for young workers. Coupled with astronomical housing costs in the Seoul metropolitan area, homeownership has become a mirage. Without parental financial backing, young people find themselves trapped in a rigid "spoon class" hierarchy where the myth of meritocracy has broken down.

This structural dead end breeds a paralyzing sense of futility. Historical dynamics research warns that the true danger of societal decay is not merely rising economic inequality, but the mass production of "frustrated elites." When university-educated youths with high expectations hit an economic brick wall and tumble downward, systemic stability fractures. Blaming young people for lacking grit or demanding too much is little more than a convenient excuse for an older generation that inherited the fruits of the country's past economic boom.

The Romance Strike as a Rational Survival Strategy

It is against this backdrop of structural despair that South Korea's birth rate fell to a historic low of 0.6-something, while the percentage of unmarried adults rejecting marriage as a necessity crossed a major threshold. Young people are staging what can only be called a romance strike.

Dating apps are everywhere, but young men and women are passing on relationships because the emotional, temporal, and financial costs represent a terrible risk. For young women, the horror of career interruption and the heavy burden of patriarchal family expectations make marriage look less like a partnership and more like penal servitude. These choices are not born of selfishness; they are rational, defensive strategies for survival.

Redefining Failure as Data

In a society obsessed with standardized success, failure feels like a death sentence. But through the lens of a scientist, failure is simply data. Running a laboratory experiment that yields no results is not wasted time; it is the vital process of eliminating wrong answers to narrow down the truth.

The painful heartbreaks, failed job applications, and missed opportunities of youth are not wasted years. They are necessary data points required to forge a resilient self. Stripping away the terror of falling behind allows individuals to step off the treadmill of constant social comparison.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this era of intense technological acceleration and economic polarization, several key indicators will determine whether the social fabric can adapt:

  • Labor Market Reforms: Watch whether the government and corporate sector make meaningful headway in bridging the wage and benefit gap between regular and non-regular workers, which directly drives youth despair.
  • Educational Curriculum Shifts: Monitor whether academic institutions begin moving away from rote memorization toward humanities-based critical thinking to counter the cognitive impacts of AI and short-form media.
  • Housing and Welfare Policy: Keep an eye on municipal and national initiatives aimed at stabilizing housing costs for single-person households and young families, the primary material barrier to demographic recovery.
