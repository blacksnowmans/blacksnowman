Seoul’s 94-Week Housing Rally Hits a Wall: Inside South Korea’s Decoupling Real Estate Market

November 27, 2026

In the quiet, cavernous sales offices of Helio City—a massive, 9,500-unit apartment complex in Seoul’s southern district of Songpa-gu—the mood has shifted noticeably. For nearly two years, property owners held all the leverage, routinely raising asking prices as buyers rushed in for fear of missing out. But by late November, asking prices on some units had dropped by as much as 400 million won (about $300,000 USD), with confirmed transaction prices tumbling over 200 million won ($150,000 USD) in just 17 days. Sellers who once dictated terms are suddenly finding themselves forced to negotiate or watch their listings collect dust.

This localized sharp turn is part of a broader shift across South Korea's capital. According to weekly data released on November 26 by the Korea Real Estate Board, a government agency that tracks housing market statistics, Seoul’s apartment sale prices managed a meager 0.01% gain from the previous week. While that technical bump extended Seoul’s winning streak to 94 consecutive weeks—the longest rally on record—the rate of expansion has slowed for six straight weeks to hit a 94-week low. With prices virtually flatlining, South Korea’s most watched property market is hovering on the brink of its first citywide downturn in years.

The slowdown is not a simple story of cooling demand across the board. Instead, a potent cocktail of rising interest rates, mounting holding taxes, and a widening divide between home buying and rental markets is creating an intricate web of pressures. For international observers trying to make sense of South Korea's macroeconomic health and policy landscape, understanding this moment requires looking beyond the single headline metric of 0.01% growth.

The Cracks in Premium Districts: From Gangnam to the Han River Belt

To understand why Seoul’s aggregate growth is grinding to a halt, one has to start at the top of the pyramid. Historically, Seoul’s property market is driven by the "Gangnam 3-gu"—the three affluent southern districts of Gangnam-gu, Seocho-gu, and Songpa-gu. These neighborhoods, along with central Yongsan-gu, represent the pinnacle of South Korean residential prestige. When prices rise here, they usually cascade outward to the rest of the city.

Right now, that transmission engine is running in reverse. Songpa-gu saw apartment prices plummet by 0.50% in a single week—its steepest weekly drop in roughly four years (205 weeks). Combined with ongoing weakness in Gangnam-gu and Seocho-gu, the southern luxury cluster has turned into the primary drag on Seoul’s overall housing index.

What makes the current downturn particularly significant is its geographic spread. The price decline is no longer confined to the southern enclave. In the latest survey, nine of Seoul’s 25 autonomous districts recorded negative growth. When including districts where prices remained completely unchanged, 10 out of 25 districts have now crossed into stagnation or outright contraction, up from eight the previous week.
