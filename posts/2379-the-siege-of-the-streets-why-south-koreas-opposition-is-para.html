The Siege of the Streets: Why South Korea’s Opposition is Paralyzing the National Budget

November 16, 2026

The square in front of Busan Station is usually a place of transit, a bustling hub for travelers catching the high-speed KTX train. But on November 15, the sound of rolling suitcases was drowned out by the roar of loudspeakers and the rhythmic chanting of thousands of protesters. Members and supporters of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, had abandoned the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul to take their grievances directly to the public. They held signs calling for a special counsel to investigate what they term "fabricated prosecutions" and demanding that the judiciary move faster on trials involving the current head of state.

This is not just a standard political rally. It is a calculated move into "street politics" that has brought the legislative process in Seoul to a grinding halt. At the center of the storm is the 821 trillion won (approximately $590 billion) government budget for next year. As the opposition moves its fight from the debating chamber to the pavement, the mechanisms of the South Korean state are beginning to seize up. For those watching from outside the peninsula, the spectacle offers a window into a democracy grappling with extreme polarization, where the courtroom and the street have become more important than the legislature.

The $590 Billion Standoff

Inside the National Assembly’s Special Committee on Budget and Accounts, the atmosphere is toxic. The government, led by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and President Lee Jae-myung, has submitted a massive spending plan for the coming year. At 821 trillion won, it is a blueprint for how the administration intends to navigate a cooling economy, an aging population, and a rapidly changing technological landscape.

However, the People Power Party has signaled a total boycott of the review process. The primary sticking point is the "Future Response Fund," a 162 trillion won (about $116 billion) initiative designed to seed long-term investments in technology and social safety nets. The PPP has laid down five non-negotiable conditions for the fund's approval, vowing that not a single won will move until the DPK capitulates to their demands. The DPK, in turn, has accused the opposition of holding the nation’s livelihood hostage to protect its own political interests.

This budget is more than just a ledger; it is the lifeblood of the country’s daily operations. It funds everything from local welfare programs to major industrial subsidies for the semiconductor sector. By walking away from the table, the PPP is betting that the public will blame the Lee Jae-myung administration for the ensuing paralysis. But the risk is high. If the legal deadline for the budget passes without a resolution, the government may be forced to operate on a "provisional budget"—a bare-bones spending plan that allows for little more than paying civil servant salaries, effectively freezing all new policy initiatives.

Legal Warfare and the Special Counsel Act

Why has the opposition chosen such an extreme path? The answer lies in the intensifying "judicialization of politics." The People Power Party is currently pushing for a "Special Counsel Act on Fabricated Prosecutions," a legislative maneuver aimed at investigating the investigators. They argue that the current administration has used the prosecution service as a political weapon, particularly in cases involving the President himself.

Senior PPP lawmakers, including Jung Jeom-sik, have escalated the rhetoric, insisting that President Lee Jae-myung must face immediate trial for alleged violations of the Public Official Election Act. They claim that judicial proceedings have been unnaturally stalled for over two years. "Even a president cannot stand above the law," Jung told supporters, warning that any cabinet members who refuse to endorse the Special Counsel Act could face future criminal prosecution themselves.

This focus on legal retribution has turned the National Assembly into a theater of litigation. Instead of debating policy, lawmakers are debating evidence and the impartiality of judges. The PPP's strategy is to create enough public pressure to force the President to exercise—or refrain from exercising—his veto power. By taking this fight to cities like Busan and regions like Gangwon, they are attempting to bypass the DPK's legislative majority and appeal to the "court of public opinion."

A House Divided: Internal Strife and Conspiracy Theories

While the PPP presents a united front on the streets, the party is secretly being hollowed out by internal division. The leadership, currently under Representative Jang Dong-hyeok, is facing a burgeoning rebellion from the "pro-Han" faction (supporters of former party leader Han Dong-hoon). Rumors are swirling that this faction is preparing an injunction to halt the leadership’s disciplinary actions against them, potentially sparking a "second civil war" within the party.

This internal instability makes the party more susceptible to the influence of the "alt-right" ecosystem. A bizarre recent controversy involving Lee Jun-seok, the former PPP chairman who now leads the minor Reform Korea Party, serves as a case in point. A popular Korean history lecturer with a massive YouTube following, Jeon Han-gil, recently amplified a long-debunked conspiracy theory suggesting that Lee Jun-seok entered Harvard University without taking an entrance exam, allegedly on the recommendation of a former president. Lee has since announced a defamation lawsuit against Jeon.

"When institutional politics merges with internet conspiracy theories, the public square is rapidly poisoned," noted one political analyst in Seoul. "Unfounded rumors become weapons of political assassination, and the line between fact and fiction vanishes."

The fact that such "birther-style" academic rumors are still gaining traction in 2024 highlights the erosion of constructive discourse. When the main opposition party is busy fighting internal legal battles and fending off YouTube-driven scandals, its ability to act as a credible check on the government is severely compromised. It forces the party to adopt even more radical stances to keep its core base energized, leading to the "street politics" we see today.

The Global Rise of the Far-Right Mirror

South Korea’s current situation is not an isolated phenomenon. The source material highlights a troubling parallel with the rise of far-right movements across Europe and the UK. Whether it is Reform UK dealing with donation scandals while its leader, Nigel Farage, pushes a hardline "security-first" agenda, or the Alternative for Germany (AfD) nominating a former soccer star like Thomas Berthold for the presidency to soften its image, the playbook is similar.

These movements thrive on social anxiety and the perception that the "establishment" is corrupt or unresponsive. In Korea, this manifests as extreme protests, such as the use of funeral-style banners featuring the President’s face—a move that local authorities have recently begun to crack down on. It also appears in the actions of groups like the Parents' Alliance for Children's Education Rights (PACER), which is currently demanding the resignation of the Seoul Education Superintendent over a "Hate and Discrimination Guidebook" intended for schools. They argue that human rights education is itself a form of discrimination against their values.

The common thread between these global movements and the current atmosphere in Korea is the rejection of the "middle ground." Politics is no longer seen as a process of compromise between competing interests, but as a zero-sum struggle between "patriots" and "traitors," or "the people" and "the elite." When the main opposition party adopts the language and tactics of these fringe groups, it validates the extremism and makes it nearly impossible to return to the negotiating table.

What to Watch Next

As the winter months approach, the political temperature in Seoul will likely continue to rise. There are three key indicators to watch that will determine if South Korea can avoid a total legislative meltdown:

  • The December 2 Budget Deadline: If the PPP continues its boycott past the legal deadline for the 821 trillion won budget, the economic fallout will be significant. Watch for whether the DPK attempts to pass a "stripped-down" version of the budget alone, or if a last-minute compromise on the Special Counsel Act is traded for budget cooperation.
  • The Outcome of internal PPP Injunctions: If the pro-Han faction successfully files an injunction against the current leadership, the PPP could effectively split. A fractured opposition would be less capable of blocking the government’s agenda but might also become even more desperate and radicalized in its rhetoric.
  • The Judiciary’s Response: The courts are now the ultimate arbiters of Korean politics. Any ruling on the President's ongoing legal cases or the constitutionality of the proposed Special Counsel Acts will serve as a trigger for massive rallies on both the left and the right.

South Korea has long been praised for its vibrant, if noisy, democracy. But the current shift toward street-level confrontation and judicial warfare suggests that the institutions designed to channel that noise into productive policy are under immense strain. For the people of Korea, the stakes are not just about who wins the next election, but whether the 821 trillion won meant for their future will ever actually be spent.
