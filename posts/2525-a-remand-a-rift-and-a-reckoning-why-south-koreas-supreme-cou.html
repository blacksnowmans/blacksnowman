A Remand, a Rift, and a Reckoning: Why South Korea’s Supreme Court Ruling Over Martial Law Sparked an Impeachment Crisis

November 20, 2026

As dusk fell over Seoul on November 19, crowds clutching candles began gathering outside the Supreme Court complex in the southern legal district of Seocho-dong. Across the city in Yeouido, inside the National Assembly building, lawmakers from South Korea’s ruling party filed into an emergency closed-door session, their expressions grim. According to a Korean political briefing and statements issued by the country’s major parties, both scenes were set off within hours by a single courtroom decision that shook the foundations of South Korea’s post-martial law reckoning.

Earlier that day, the Supreme Court en banc—the full bench of the nation’s highest court—overturned an appellate court’s 15-year prison sentence for former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo on insurrection charges. The justices sent the case back to the Seoul High Court with instructions pointing toward a partial acquittal. On the surface, the ruling appeared to be a narrow recalibration of legal liability for one former official. In reality, it fractured South Korea’s political landscape, prompting the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and its allies to launch an unprecedented bid to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae while the conservative opposition rallied to defend the courts against what it called political retaliation.

How the Supreme Court Separated Plotting from Official Duty

To understand why a single remand triggered a constitutional showdown, it helps to trace the case back to the December 3 martial law crisis—an episode centered on Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later removed from office through impeachment and is currently imprisoned. In the aftermath of that crisis, prosecutors brought insurrection-related charges against senior figures who held key government posts when martial law was declared.

In the second-instance trial, the Seoul High Court had handed Han Duck-soo a heavy 15-year prison term. Those appellate judges concluded that Han’s decision to convene and preside over a Cabinet meeting around the time of the martial law declaration was not a routine administrative act, but a core act of insurrection that gave a veneer of legality to what the lower court described as a self-coup.

The Supreme Court did not clear the martial law declaration itself. On the contrary, the full bench reaffirmed without hesitation that declaring martial law on December 3 was an unconstitutional and illegal act of insurrection designed to suspend the powers of the National Assembly. Where the Supreme Court diverged from the appellate court was on how to judge Han’s specific actions during the Cabinet meeting.

Under Korean criminal law, conviction on insurrection charges hinges on proving an active purpose of subverting the constitutional order. According to the Supreme Court’s reasoning, legal doubt remained over whether Han’s attendance, convocation of the meeting, and related signatures amounted to active participation aimed directly at overthrowing the constitution, or whether they constituted a formal execution of prime ministerial duties intended to prevent a vacuum in state administration. In other words, the justices drew a sharp distinction between plotting an insurrection beforehand alongside Yoon Suk-yeol and carrying out institutional procedures as prime minister once the crisis had already erupted.

Based on that distinction, the Supreme Court issued a partial not-guilty rationale on one of the three indicted acts of insurrection participation—specifically the Cabinet meeting convocation and resolution—and ordered a retrial at the Seoul High Court. Even so, Han was not released. The Supreme Court simultaneously extended his detention by two months, explaining that the remand does not amount to a full acquittal and that he must remain in custody while the appellate court retries the remaining charges.

The implications extend well beyond Han’s personal sentence. Legal observers cited in the Korean political report noted that this newly articulated standard will likely apply directly to upcoming trials for other senior officials who attended the same martial law Cabinet meeting, including former Interior and Safety Minister Lee Sang-min and former Justice Minister Park Sung-jae.

The Ruling Bloc’s Push to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

For South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea, which holds power in the government and dominates parliament alongside allied progressive parties, the ruling landed like a shockwave. Having viewed the original 15-year sentence as a benchmark for holding top officials strictly accountable for the December 3 crisis, Democratic Party lawmakers treated the remand as a betrayal by the judiciary.

During the party’s emergency general assembly on the afternoon of November 19, Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Min-seok delivered a blistering condemnation. According to party briefings, Kim denounced the decision as "an excuse for historical opportunists" and called it "a historical treason" that opened an escape route for insurrection criminals. Within the party, suspicion rapidly hardened that the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae was softening the legal consequences of Yoon Suk-yeol’s actions and eroding the rule of law.

Rather than stopping at rhetoric, Democratic Party leaders moved toward an extraordinary institutional response: impeaching the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. Following the emergency assembly, Kim announced that the party had decided to pursue Cho’s impeachment and had entrusted the specific procedures and legal framing to its leadership. After an emergency Supreme Council meeting, the party decided to formally adopt Cho’s impeachment as official party policy at a general meeting on November 20 and establish a dedicated impeachment task force. Party leaders made clear they intend to take the impeachment motion straight to a plenary vote in the National Assembly, skipping slower preliminary steps such as a parliamentary investigation.

Behind that urgency lies a deeper strategic fear inside the ruling party. Democratic Party officials worry that if the Supreme Court’s rationale for mitigating Cabinet-level responsibility stands unchallenged, it could ultimately weaken accountability in Yoon Suk-yeol’s own ongoing legal proceedings.

Smaller parties aligned with the ruling bloc moved even faster. On the evening of the ruling, the Rebuilding Korea Party—a progressive-leaning reformist party led by Cho Kuk and Shin Jang-sik—organized the candlelight vigil outside the Supreme Court in Seocho-dong. Party leaders accused the court of abandoning its duty to protect the public and turning itself into an "insurrection laundering house," while labeling Chief Justice Cho an accomplice in a "judicial coup." The Rebuilding Korea Party also pledged to publicly release the list of lawmakers who sign the impeachment motion, with Cho Kuk arguing that the ruling was the predictable fallout from the Democratic Party’s earlier reluctance to impeach the Chief Justice. The Progressive Party, a minor left-wing party, joined civic groups outside the courthouse as well, branding Cho’s court an "enemy of democracy" and demanding his immediate resignation and impeachment.

The Conservative Pushback and the Separation of Powers

South Korea’s conservative opposition interpreted the Supreme Court’s decision—and the ruling bloc’s reaction—in starkly opposing terms, framing the day’s events as a test of whether courts can survive political pressure.

The People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, welcomed the remand. In a statement released shortly after the verdict, the party argued that the Supreme Court had dealt a blow to what it called the ruling bloc’s political "insurrection framing" and demonstrated that a baseline of judicial fairness remained intact by rejecting blanket criminal liability for officials present during the martial law process.

Once the Democratic Party and its allies pivoted toward impeaching Chief Justice Cho, the People Power Party’s tone turned sharply confrontational. At an emergency press conference, party leaders denounced the impeachment drive as "thug-like political retaliation," describing it as a "parliamentary coup" and a "suicide bombing act that destroys the separation of powers." Warning that impeaching the head of the judiciary simply because a ruling displeases the majority party would spell the end of South Korea’s rule of law, the People Power Party pledged an all-out political fight to block the move.

The Reform Party, a smaller opposition party, voiced similar institutional alarm. In an official commentary, the Reform Party stressed that the Supreme Court’s ruling must be respected, noting that Supreme Court justices and the Chief Justice are not subordinates of any president or political party. Attempting to punish judges politically for their legal interpretations, the party argued, threatens judicial independence and strikes at the core of the constitutional separation of powers.

Taken together, the standoff lays bare a profound constitutional dilemma highlighted in Korean political analysis. On one side, granting a partial acquittal rationale to a prime minister who presided over a martial law Cabinet meeting strikes many citizens as deeply out of step with public demands for accountability after December 3, even if grounded in narrow legal doctrine. On the other side, a legislature reaching immediately for the impeachment of a Chief Justice over an adverse ruling puts South Korea’s democratic checks and balances under severe strain.

What to Watch Next in Seoul’s Courts and Parliament

With both camps dug in and little room left for compromise, several concrete milestones will show whether South Korea’s confrontation escalates into a full-blown constitutional crisis:

  • The November 20 Democratic Party vote: Watch whether the ruling party’s general assembly formally adopts Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s impeachment as official party policy, launches its impeachment task force, and sets a date to bring the motion directly to the National Assembly floor.
  • Coalition pressure and street mobilization: The Rebuilding Korea Party’s pledge to publish the names of impeachment signatories—alongside ongoing protests in Seocho-dong with the Progressive Party and civic groups—will test how fiercely the broader ruling bloc presses hesitant lawmakers to back the motion.
  • The Seoul High Court retrial and ripple effects: Han Duck-soo remains behind bars under his two-month detention extension as his case returns to the appellate court. Watch how Seoul High Court judges weigh the remaining charges under the Supreme Court’s guidance, and whether other former Cabinet officials awaiting trial, including Lee Sang-min and Park Sung-jae, successfully use the new legal standard in their own defense.

How South Korea navigates both the retrial of a former prime minister and an unprecedented attempt to oust its top judge will define the country’s politics through the end of the year—and test the durability of its constitutional order.
