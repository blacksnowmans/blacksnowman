The Great Schism of South Korean Media: Why Legacy Newsrooms are Losing Themselves on YouTube

October 27, 2026

Imagine picking up a copy of a prestigious, left-leaning South Korean newspaper. You read a sternly worded editorial criticizing the current administration’s communication style, calling for more "dignity" and "restraint" from the Blue House. But then, you open YouTube, click on that same newspaper’s official channel, and find a neon-colored thumbnail featuring a caricature of an opposition leader with the caption: "THEY ARE JUST DOGS BARKING."

This is the surreal reality of South Korea’s current media landscape. Under the presidency of Lee Jae-myung, of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea’s current ruling liberal party), the country is navigating a complex era of post-impeachment rebuilding. Yet, the very institutions meant to provide clarity—the legacy newspapers—are suffering from a deep-seated identity crisis. In a desperate scramble for views and "algorithmic favor," these outlets have begun to operate with a split personality: "serious" in print, but "clickbait-obsessed" on YouTube. This shift is not just a marketing tactic; it is a fundamental breakdown of journalistic integrity that is poisoning the public discourse.

The Double Life of Kyunghyang Shinmun

A striking example of this phenomenon recently emerged involving Kyunghyang Shinmun, one of South Korea’s major liberal-leaning legacy newspapers. The paper has built its reputation over decades as a voice for social justice and rigorous political analysis. However, its YouTube arm, Kyunghyang TV, appears to be playing by an entirely different set of rules.

The friction came to a head following a segment on the channel’s show, "Gu Gyo-hyeong’s Political Emergency Exit." The video offered a full-throated defense of President Lee Jae-myung’s use of social media, arguing against critics who claim the President’s direct communication style is unstatesmanlike. The video was so favorable that President Lee himself shared a clip of it on his X (formerly Twitter) account to communicate with his supporters.

The problem? Kyunghyang Shinmun’s own editorial board has spent months publishing pieces expressing "grave concern" over the President’s SNS habits, urging him to adopt a more traditional, formal communication style. Within the same building, the writers were calling for restraint while the YouTubers were cheering for the fight. This isn’t just a difference of opinion; it’s a structural contradiction. To chase the YouTube algorithm, legacy outlets are adopting the aggressive, mocking tone of "political YouTubers"—the very people they often look down upon in their columns. When a legacy outlet uses thumbnails that bundle opposition leaders and conservative commentators together under mocking labels, they abandon their role as a neutral arbiter and become just another loud voice in the digital shouting match.

The Shadow of Kim Ou-joon and the Laziness of "Person-Based" Framing

To understand why Korean media is so fixated on YouTube, one must understand the shadow cast by Kim Ou-joon. Kim is perhaps the most influential and polarizing media figure in modern South Korea. From his early days founding Ddanzi Ilbo (a satirical webzine) to the massive success of his podcast Naggomsu and his chart-topping radio show News Factory, Kim has consistently outpaced legacy media in building a dedicated, paying audience.

Following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the media landscape has shifted, but the legacy press’s obsession with Kim remains. For conservative outlets and even some moderate legacy papers, "Kim Ou-joon" has become a lazy shorthand for anything they don’t like about the current political climate. Instead of analyzing why millions of citizens are choosing to get their news from independent YouTube channels like Humility is Hard News Factory, legacy media simply labels it as "the Kim Ou-joon effect" or suggests that the public is being "manipulated" by a few powerful voices.

This framing is a sign of intellectual laziness. By attributing the diverse, spontaneous political participation of the Korean public to the "orders" of figures like Kim Ou-joon or the author and former minister Rhyu Si-min, legacy media ignores the actual grievances and aspirations of the voters. It is easier to demonize a single "influencer" than it is to engage with the complex reasons why the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition) is struggling to connect with the younger generation. This "person-based" framing turns every policy debate into a grudge match between personalities, stripping away the substance that a healthy democracy needs to function.

The "Opposition Manual" and the Erasure of Real Issues

The degradation of media quality is also evident in how the opposition—the People Power Party (PPP)—and its allied YouTubers interact with the press. There is a perceptible "manual" of talking points that has become ubiquitous in South Korean political talk shows. These talking points are designed to deflect and distract rather than engage.

For instance, whenever a major policy failure or an internal scandal within the opposition is brought up, the response is almost always a scripted counter-attack: "Why aren't you talking about Rhyu Si-min?" or "Why won't the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation speak up about Han Dong-hoon (the current leader of the PPP)?" This tactic, known locally as "water-muddying," is intended to shut down legitimate criticism by demanding an impossible level of "both-sidesism" that focuses on historical figures or media personalities rather than the politicians currently holding (or seeking) power.

The danger here is that real, life-altering issues are being buried. South Korea is currently facing significant economic headwinds, from rising inflation to a volatile housing market. Under the Lee Jae-myung administration, the government is attempting to navigate a "Great Transformation" in the economy and welfare state. However, if you were to judge the state of the nation solely by the YouTube thumbnails of major newspapers, you would think the only thing happening in the country is a series of petty insults between Han Dong-hoon and various pro-government commentators. When legacy media prioritizes "tasty" mockery over dull but essential policy analysis, the public loses its ability to hold power—both ruling and opposition—accountable.

The High Cost of Chasing the Algorithm

Why does this matter to the international reader? Because South Korea is often a "canary in the coal mine" for digital media trends. The country has some of the highest high-speed internet penetration and smartphone usage rates in the world. What we are seeing in Seoul—the total subversion of legacy journalistic standards to the whims of the YouTube algorithm—is a warning for the rest of the world.

When institutions like OhmyNews (a pioneer in citizen journalism) or Hankook Ilbo (a major centrist daily) feel the need to "youtubify" their content to survive, the very concept of "truth" becomes tribal. If the editorial board and the YouTube team can’t even agree on a basic tone or stance, the reader—and the viewer—eventually stops trusting both. The loss of credibility is permanent. A reader might click on a sensational thumbnail once, but they won't turn to that outlet when they need to understand the nuances of a new tax law or a diplomatic crisis.

Furthermore, this media environment makes it incredibly difficult for the current government to build consensus. In the wake of the national trauma surrounding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment and the subsequent legal proceedings, the country needs a shared set of facts to move forward. If the media is busy manufacturing "rage-bait" for clicks, that shared reality becomes impossible to achieve.

What to Watch Next

The "YouTube-fication" of Korean news is reaching a breaking point, and several key developments will determine whether legacy media can recover its soul.

  • Internal Revolts: Watch for growing tension within newsrooms like Kyunghyang Shinmun and OhmyNews. Younger reporters and traditional editorial boards are increasingly at odds with "Digital Strategy" departments that prioritize views over ethics.
  • Regulatory Pressure: There is an ongoing debate in the National Assembly regarding the "publicness" of YouTube news. While direct regulation of YouTube content is a minefield for free speech, expect new discussions on how traditional media companies are held accountable for the content they produce on third-party platforms.
  • The "Han Dong-hoon" Factor: As the leader of the main opposition People Power Party, Han Dong-hoon’s media strategy is almost entirely built around viral moments and sharp retorts. Watch how legacy media handles his upcoming campaigns; will they analyze his policies, or will they simply serve as a megaphone for his most "clickable" insults?
  • Subscription Models: As the "ad-sense" revenue from YouTube forces media into a race to the bottom, keep an eye on whether any Korean outlets can successfully pivot to a subscriber-funded model that rewards depth over speed.

In the end, legacy media in South Korea is learning a painful lesson: when you try to beat YouTubers at their own game, you don't just lose the game—you lose yourself. For a democracy still finding its footing after the upheaval of recent years, the restoration of a dignified, fact-based press is not a luxury; it is a necessity for survival.
