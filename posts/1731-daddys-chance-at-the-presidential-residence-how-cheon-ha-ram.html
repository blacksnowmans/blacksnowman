'Daddy's Chance' at the Presidential Residence: How Cheon Ha-ram Exposed the Lee Administration's Vulnerabilities

October 30, 2026

For millions of young South Koreans struggling to pay rent on cramped rooftop rooms or windowless studio flats known as gosiwon, securing basic housing in Seoul feels like a lifelong uphill battle. So when news broke during the annual parliamentary audit that President Lee Jae-myung's adult, gainfully employed second son was living rent-free alongside the presidential couple inside the country’s most exclusive state-funded compound, the reaction was swift and viscerally negative.

The controversy was brought to light by Lawmaker Cheon Ha-ram, a prominent voice from the minor opposition Reform Party. By confronting government officials over the living arrangements at the presidential residence in Hannam-dong, Cheon ignited a broader debate about political privilege, double standards, and administrative opacity. Coupled with a second revelation—that the administration quietly discarded tens of thousands of direct public petitions without a response—the audit has dealt a significant blow to the ruling Democratic Party’s populist, citizen-first image.

The High Price of Free Housing: 'Daddy's Chance' in Hannam-dong

The presidential residence in Hannam-dong, situated on prime real estate overlooking the Han River in central Seoul, is South Korea's premier state housing facility. Maintained at immense taxpayer expense, the heavily fortified compound is designed to host official diplomatic events and accommodate the head of state for national security and executive duties. It was never intended as a private, rent-free residence for adult extended family members.

During the parliamentary audit, Cheon Ha-ram pointedly asked whether it was acceptable for an adult son—capable of independent economic activity—to reside in state-owned luxury housing free of charge. Cheon invoked the bitter local phrase "daddy's chance" (appa chance), a term popularized in South Korean political discourse to describe elite children enjoying unearned perks purely off their parents' power and position.

"This reality stands in stark, painful contrast to the daily housing struggles of ordinary young adults," Cheon argued during the legislative panel. "The presidential residence is a public asset operated by tax dollars to facilitate public duties, not a site for private family welfare."

Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party attempted to defend the administration. Representative Lim Kwang-hyun pushed back during the audit, claiming that labeling a family living together as "daddy's chance" was overly harsh political grandstanding. However, the defense resonated poorly with a public sensitive to intergenerational inequality.

The political risk for President Lee Jae-myung is heightened by political history. During the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was subsequently impeached and imprisoned—the Democratic Party built its political capital by relentlessly criticizing allegations of nepotism and backroom privileges among conservative elites. Now holding executive power, the Democratic Party faces the exact weapon it once wielded: accusations that privilege remains intact regardless of who occupies the presidential office.

Deleting 38,000 Voices: The 'People's Mailbox' Scandal

The housing controversy is not the only headache facing the administration from Cheon's audit questioning. The Reform Party lawmaker also exposed deep structural flaws in the executive office’s public communication strategy, revealing that the administration had discarded 38,000 petitions submitted to the "People's Mailbox" without issuing a single response.

The People's Mailbox was launched amid much fanfare as a direct civic engagement portal, promising that everyday citizens could bypass bureaucratic red tape and voice their grievances directly to the president's staff. For citizens suffering from economic hardships or regulatory deadlocks, the channel represented a direct line to executive power.

According to Cheon, instead of processing these appeals, the administration quietly purged 38,000 entries. When government spokespersons claimed that the petitions had been safely transferred to e-People (Gukmin Sinmungo), the national online civil petition portal operated by the Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission, Cheon countered that official records showed no evidence of such a transfer ever taking place.

"A government that boasts about open communication while quietly shredding 38,000 desperate pleas from its citizens is engaging in showcase administration," Cheon stated during the audit proceedings.

Assuming an average of just ten minutes spent drafting each entry, South Korean citizens spent more than 6,300 collective hours appealing to their government, only for their messages to vanish into an administrative black hole. The revelation undermines one of President Lee's central political promises: that his administration would remain an accessible, transparent government responsive to grassroots demands.

Cheon Ha-ram's Electoral Gamble in the Democratic Stronghold

Beyond his aggressive performance in the parliamentary audit, Cheon Ha-ram is navigating a delicate political maneuver within his own party. The internal tension over his future trajectory reached a turning point on October 29, when the leadership of the minor opposition Reform Party officially yielded to Cheon's insistence on contesting a constituency in Suncheon, South Jeolla Province.

The Reform Party—a young, reformist political faction seeking to challenge both the ruling Democratic Party and the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP)—had initially pressured Cheon to run in the suburban "semiconductor belt" of southern Gyeonggi Province. The party leadership viewed Gyeonggi's young, tech-worker demographic as fertile ground for a third-party breakthrough in the upcoming 2028 general elections.

Cheon, however, refused to abandon Suncheon, a city in the traditional Honam region that has long served as an impenetrable stronghold for the Democratic Party. Cheon's strategy is to prove that the Reform Party can break regional monopolies by offering a pragmatic, policy-focused alternative directly in the ruling party's backyard.

  • The Leadership Compromise: Chief Supreme Council Member Lee Ki-in announced that the party leadership would respect Cheon's choice, lifting the previous freeze on his local organizational appointment.
  • The Upcoming Battle: Cheon is now set to build his local groundwork in the Suncheon-A district, paving the way for a high-profile showdown against expected Democratic candidate Kim Moon-soo.
  • Third-Party Viability: By securing his home turf, Cheon aims to demonstrate that political reform in South Korea requires competing in tough regional battlegrounds rather than relying solely on safe, suburban swing districts.

For the Reform Party, resolving this internal dispute allows leadership to present a unified front. Rather than fighting over internal candidate placement, the party can focus its ammunition on scrutinizing executive power and positioning itself as the most effective watchdogs in the National Assembly.

What to Watch Next

As the parliamentary audit wraps up, the fallout from Cheon Ha-ram's revelations will test the Lee administration's ability to manage controversy around transparency and ethical standards. Key developments to monitor in the coming months include:

1. Presidential Compound Guidelines: Watch whether the presidential office issues formal regulations governing who may reside at state facilities. Without clear legal and ethical guidelines regarding adult family members living at state-funded residences, opposition parties—both the main conservative People Power Party and minor third parties—will continue to leverage the issue as proof of elite entitlement.

2. Audit Remediation for Civil Petitions: The presidential staff faces pressure to explain the fate of the discarded 38,000 petitions. Watch whether the government initiates a retroactive review or establishes an automated, verifiable pipeline between specialized presidential suggestion boxes and the official e-People system.

3. Regional Dynamics in Suncheon: With Cheon Ha-ram's district candidacy officially confirmed by his party, his grassroots campaign in South Jeolla Province will serve as a bellwether for whether third-party politicians can dismantle the Democratic Party's historic hegemony in the Honam region ahead of the 2028 legislative elections.
