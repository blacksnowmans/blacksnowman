How a Seven-Point Slump is Threatening South Korea's Ruling Party

October 13, 2026

When a new administration takes office, political analysts usually expect a traditional honeymoon period—a few months of goodwill where the public gives leaders the benefit of the doubt. But in South Korean politics right now, that script has been completely torn up. Just weeks after the launch of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, the ruling liberal bloc is facing a severe reality check. The turbulence is centered on Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), whose early missteps have sent shockwaves through the country's political landscape.

For observers outside South Korea, watching the local political arena can feel like trying to drink from a firehose. Between shifting alliances, intense media scrutiny, and rapid-fire polling data, it is easy to miss the structural shifts happening beneath the surface. Yet what is happening to the Democratic Party today is not just a run-of-the-mill disagreement; it is a textbook case of how a governing party can begin to lose its grip even when it holds the presidency. To understand why this matters, we have to look past the daily headlines and examine how the ruling party's internal fractures and communication failures are colliding with a skeptical public.

The Shocking Collapse in Public Approval

The immediate catalyst for the current panic is a set of brutal polling numbers. According to a survey conducted by Asia Economy and Realmeter among 1,006 registered voters nationwide, the Democratic Party’s approval rating crashed to 35.5%—a staggering 7.0 percentage point drop in just a single week. Meanwhile, the conservative People Power Party climbed to 38.4%, effectively erasing the liberal lead and putting the conservative opposition neck-and-neck or slightly ahead within the margin of error.

In South Korean politics, a seven-point collapse in seven days is not a statistical noise; it is a five-alarm fire. When Kim Min-seok took over the party leadership, he grandly promised to boost approval ratings by 10 percentage points within his first 100 days. Instead, he is watching his core support erode, particularly among progressives and voters in their 40s—the bedrock demographic that forms the foundation of liberal electoral victories. When these voters start walking away, the ruling party loses its margin for error.

Veteran political commentators have pointed out an uncomfortable truth about how Korean political movements behave under stress. When a government is popular, hardcore factions and vocal surrogate commentators blend into the background. But the moment a crisis hits, these same self-described loyalists are often the very first to turn on the leadership and start throwing stones. This phenomenon is currently playing out in real-time, as internal finger-pointing replaces unified strategy.

Internal Dissent and the Danger of Friendly Fire

The friction inside the Democratic Party is no longer being hidden behind closed doors. During recent high-level supreme council meetings, senior party figures like Choi Min-hee and Park Joo-min delivered thinly veiled warnings that sounded more like indictments. Critics within the party argue that the leadership is suffering from severe operational overload, trying to juggle too many initiatives at once while producing zero tangible results for the public. Instead of the disciplined, laser-focused legislative pushes of past eras, the current leadership is being criticized for running a disorganized operation that looks less like a governing party and more like a rudderless opposition.

Worse still, the ruling party has developed an unfortunate habit of turning its weapons inward. During recent parliamentary audits of Gyeonggi Province, some Democratic Party lawmakers joined conservative opponents in launching sharp attacks on senior figures like Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae. Rather than closing ranks against external pressure, parts of the liberal camp have allowed themselves to get sucked into framing traps set by the conservative press, blaming internal figures for a national polling slump that stems from much deeper governance issues.

Add to this simmering brew the lingering questions over alleged religious infiltration during past party primaries—specifically concerning the secretive Shincheonji religious movement—and the leadership is sitting on a powder keg. If the party fails to transparently clear up how outside groups may have influenced its internal mechanics, it risks handing its opponents a permanent narrative of corruption and subversion.

The President's "My-Way" Communication Style

Compounding the party's woes is the communication style of President Lee Jae-myung. Following a high-stakes press conference where the president acknowledged public frustrations and promised a fresh approach, many hoped for a cooling-off period. Yet, President Lee quickly doubled down on his preference for direct social media communication, signaling that he intends to keep posting on his own terms regardless of political blowback.

While direct digital engagement was a hallmark of his populist appeal, critics argue that unchecked presidential messaging often steps on the party's toes, giving conservative media and opposition lawmakers endless ammunition. When the executive branch and the party leadership fail to sing from the same hymn sheet, it creates a vacuum of leadership that external critics are only too happy to fill. Every time the presidential office reacts defensively, the space for the party to present itself as competent and grounded in everyday livelihoods shrinks.

What to Watch Next

As the clock ticks down toward the end of Kim Min-seok's self-imposed 100-day deadline, the pressure on the ruling bloc will only intensify. Here are the concrete developments to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • The 100-Day Milestone: Watch for whether the leadership quietly drops its ambitious polling targets or attempts a drastic structural shakeup before the deadline hits.
  • Internal Disciplinary Actions: Pay attention to how the party handles the public infighting between lawmakers and regional executives like Governor Choo Mi-ae. A failure to enforce party discipline will signal a weak leader.
  • Handling of Institutional Inquiries: Keep an eye on how transparently the party addresses lingering controversies, including the internal investigation into outside religious group interference.
  • Voter Sentiment in Core Demographics: Future weekly tracking polls will show whether the bleeding among voters in their 40s and progressive base has stopped or if the alienation is permanent.

Elections in South Korea are ultimately decided by everyday concerns—jobs, inflation, housing, and basic competence. If the ruling Democratic Party continues to spend its energy on internal squabbling and defensive public relations rather than tangible governance, no amount of political spin will save it from a severe reckoning at the ballot box.
