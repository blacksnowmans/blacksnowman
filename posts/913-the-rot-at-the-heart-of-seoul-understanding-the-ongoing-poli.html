The Rot at the Heart of Seoul: Understanding the Ongoing Political Turmoil

October 10, 2026

If you have been following the news from Seoul lately, you might find yourself struggling to distinguish between genuine policy debate and a chaotic, low-stakes brawl. The current political landscape in South Korea has reached a point where even seasoned observers are throwing up their hands in exhaustion. It is a world of constant leaks, shifting allegiances, and a pervasive sense that the real needs of the public are being sacrificed for the sake of petty, personalized power struggles.

To understand what is happening, one must look past the surface-level shouting matches. We are witnessing a systemic failure where the country's legislative body—the National Assembly—has become a theater of dysfunction, largely driven by the controversial political trajectory of Han Dong-hoon, a polarizing figure who has effectively shattered the traditional rules of Korean political decorum.

The Erosion of Legislative Standards

The sentiment in Yeouido, the district in Seoul that serves as the heart of South Korean politics, is one of deep cynicism. There is a growing, uncomfortable consensus that the National Assembly has devolved into a place where compromise is dead and vitriol is the only currency. This dysfunction is perhaps most visible in the ongoing saga surrounding Han Dong-hoon, whose recent moves have exposed a vacuum of leadership and control within the conservative camp.

Han, who has frequently acted as a disruptor—sometimes operating as an independent force, other times maneuvering to reshape his party—has effectively abandoned the conventional political playbook. When he stepped down from his post, the resulting vacuum did not lead to peace; rather, it triggered a deeper, more volatile cycle of infighting. The media is currently obsessed with "what Han will do next," but this focus misses the forest for the trees: none of this maneuvering is actually improving the lives of ordinary citizens. It is a zero-sum game of self-preservation that has paralyzed the legislature.

The Ghost of the Past: Irony in the Power Struggle

Perhaps the most surreal aspect of the current climate is the shadow cast by the former president, now-imprisoned, Yoon Suk-yeol. The discourse in political circles is often dominated by whispers regarding the contents of Kim Keon-hee’s (the former First Lady’s) mobile phone, with many speculating that the device contains explosive data regarding Han Dong-hoon’s past actions.

It is a dark irony that Yoon Suk-yeol, now serving time in prison following his impeachment, is effectively watching his legacy—and the people he once trusted—tear each other apart. The sight of former power brokers, who once held the reins of the state with an iron grip, now trading secrets and threats against one another is, in the eyes of many, a black comedy. While the public struggles with genuine economic challenges, the focus in the halls of power remains locked on scandalous leaks and internecine warfare. This is not merely a gossip-filled distraction; it is a clear indicator of how deeply institutional rot has penetrated the South Korean power structure.

The Stalled Promise of Prosecution Reform

Prosecution reform has been the "third rail" of Korean politics for years, and the recent "Kim Seung-won incident"—a sudden and opaque exit of a key figure tasked with driving these reforms—has sparked a wave of public despair. Many supporters of the current ruling party, the Democratic Party of Korea (the liberal-leaning party now led by President Lee Jae-myung), see this not as a simple personnel change, but as a victory for the entrenched "prosecution cartel."

When a key reformist figure is forced out just days before a critical juncture, it suggests that the reform effort is being systematically dismantled from within. Critics argue that the prosecution, likely working through figures like Han Dong-hoon, is successfully manipulating the political environment to ensure that the status quo remains intact. This has led to growing calls for "radical" appointments—a departure from the polite, consensus-driven approach that has repeatedly failed against a determined, entrenched opponent. The lesson here is brutal: in a system where the opposition is willing to weaponize every lever of power, a "moderate" response is often indistinguishable from surrender.

What to Watch Next: Can the Cycle Be Broken?

As the Democratic Party of Korea navigates the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, it faces a dual challenge. On one hand, it must govern effectively while the main opposition, the People Power Party (the conservative party), remains mired in self-destructive infighting. On the other, it must avoid the trap of complacency. The real danger for the ruling party is the perception that it, too, is becoming part of the "Yeouido" culture of stagnation.

Observers should watch for three key developments in the coming weeks:

  • The "Radical" Shift: Look for whether the administration opts for high-impact, unconventional appointments to the Ministry of Justice, signaling a move away from safe, establishment-friendly candidates.
  • Media Accountability: Pay attention to the independent media and investigative commentators (such as those featured on platforms like News Factory). Their ability to bypass traditional, "both-sides" journalism is increasingly shaping public perception and forcing politicians to address uncomfortable truths.
  • Public Sentiment vs. Political Theater: As the fatigue of the general public hits a breaking point, watch for whether the Democratic Party can pivot from reacting to conservative scandals to centering its agenda on tangible welfare and structural reform.

Ultimately, this is a moment of reckoning for South Korean democracy. The power games between individuals like Han Dong-hoon and the remnants of the previous administration are a symptom of a larger, systemic malaise. Unless the legislative and judicial systems can be fundamentally purged of the "cartel" influence that has prioritized self-interest over the public good, these cycles of corruption and scandal are destined to repeat. The era of polite, incremental reform is over; the future of Korean politics now rests on whether the current leadership has the resolve to dismantle the machinery of the old guard entirely.
