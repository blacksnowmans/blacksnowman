Decoding Bad Omens: The Psychology Behind Everyday Superstitions and Anxiety

September 08, 2026

Stepping out of the front door in the morning only to find a car inexplicably blocking the crosswalk, or spilling a few drops of coffee onto a favorite shirt — these are minor, everyday frustrations that almost everyone has experienced. Under normal circumstances, these random mishaps are easily brushed aside. However, on days leading up to a major decision or during moments of quiet inner anxiety, these coincidences can suddenly ignite a sense of impending doom. Thoughts race in an instant: "Why is my luck so terrible today?" or "Is this a sign that something important is about to fall apart?"

Human nature compels us to label these moments as "omens," treating them like mysterious warning lights flashing to signal a future catastrophe. Yet, a closer look reveals that this grand illusion of the omen is actually a sophisticated cognitive trap constructed by the human brain. It is essentially a game of stitching together random fragments of the world to amplify our own anxiety. Understanding why we become so obsessed with these ominous signs requires examining the hidden psychological mechanisms at play.

The Scientific Roots of Weather Lore and Modern Misinterpretations

Not all traditional folk omens are baseless superstitions. Many are the result of sharp observation accumulated by earlier generations over long periods, often concealing surprising scientific principles. The classic saying, "When swallows fly low, rain is on the way," is a prime example. Just before it rains, atmospheric humidity spikes. This heavier air makes insects' wings damp, preventing them from flying high and forcing them close to the ground. Swallows, which rely on hunting these insects, naturally fly lower in pursuit of food—a straightforward ecological chain reaction.

Similarly, the elderly observation that "my joints ache before it rains" is more than mere complaining. As a low-pressure system approaches, atmospheric pressure drops, relatively increasing the internal pressure within human joints. This microscopic pressure differential subtly irritates the tissue surrounding the joints, stimulating nerves and registering as a sharp ache. These phenomena are not supernatural predictions of the future, but rather ancient records of survival—the body physically sensing the physical changes of nature.

The problem arises when these scientific facts are distorted into entirely unrelated directions in modern times. People frequently mistake physical weather-sensing responses for signs of impending misfortune, or misinterpret minor daily coincidences as signatures of personal failure. Wisdom once used to read the rhythms of nature has somehow become a shackle we use to bind ourselves.

A Blocked Crosswalk and the Spiral of Anticipatory Dread

To see this phenomenon vividly, consider a morning heading toward Daejeon Station for a filming trip to Seoul. On that particular day, taxis were lined up along a crosswalk without a traffic light, and a passenger car was parked completely sideways right in the middle, blocking the path entirely. Naturally, frustration flared up. In the name of acting civilized and calm, however, the author simply stood close to the vehicle, physically expressing a silent protest: "You are blocking my path. Move faster!"

Then came a sudden moment of objective self-awareness: "What am I even doing? I can just walk around it..." Stepping quickly behind the car, the crosswalk was crossed without further incident. But the real trouble began immediately afterward. That fleeting moment of minor inconvenience began to balloon inside the mind into a massive narrative: "A car blocked the crosswalk? This is an ominous sign that today's filming in Seoul is going to be an absolute disaster." If that chain of thoughts had not been broken right then and there, the consequences would have been predictable.

The train ride from Daejeon to Seoul would have felt like sitting on pins and needles. The mind would have filled with anxious delusions: "What if the train gets delayed?" "What if the camera equipment suddenly breaks down when I arrive at the set?" "What if my tongue gets tied in front of the camera and I keep making mistakes?" Because the monster of anxiety grows larger the more it is fed, this state of mind would have led to standing in front of the camera far more rigid and tense than usual, draining the voice of ease and ultimately guaranteeing the very mistakes one feared.

Apophenia and Confirmation Bias: The Brain's Survival Traps

Finding Patterns in Randomness: Apophenia

Psychology and cognitive science offer precise explanations for this behavior. American scientific skeptic Michael Shermer emphasizes concepts like "apophenia" or "patternicity." This refers to the powerful human cognitive tendency to forcibly find meaningful patterns within completely unrelated, random data. It is precisely identical to looking at the countless scattered stars in the night sky and drawing lines to force them into the shapes of the Big Dipper or Orion, or finding the profile of a puppy or a human face in passing clouds.

Why did the human brain evolve such seemingly useless traits? The surprising answer is survival. When ancient ancestors heard a rustling in the bushes, treating it lightly as a gentle breeze rather than a dangerous sign of an approaching predator and running away immediately vastly increased their survival probability. Even if that sound genuinely was just the wind, it cost them nothing; the alternative mistake would have meant being removed from the gene pool permanently. In short, the human brain was engineered to weave coincidences into inevitabilities in order to detect danger in advance.

Yet in modern society, this evolutionary legacy produces all sorts of side effects. A completely random and incidental event—such as a single old, parked car—is forcibly combined with a personal schedule and over-packaged as a survival warning light reading, "Beware today." The brain is merely trying its best to protect itself, but that excessive loyalty prevents us from seeing objective reality, walking us straight into a labyrinth of self-made anxiety.

The Magic of Confirmation Bias

Once apophenia takes root, a massive amplifier known as "confirmation bias" begins to operate. This psychological bias involves cherry-picking information that fits a pre-existing ominous hunch while consistently ignoring peaceful evidence to the contrary. Modern individuals easily fall into this cognitive trap of seeing only what they want to see and believing what they want to believe.

The moment someone firmly believes they experienced a bad omen in the morning, every single event that catches their eye throughout the rest of the day begins to function solely as "proof of misfortune." Spilling a drop of coffee on the desk, the elevator arriving one minute later than usual, a blunt greeting from a coworker—all of it gets collected. It serves as perfect reinforcement to self-indoctrinate: "That ominous morning sign was right after all!" Conversely, the brain cleanly erases the numerous positive events, such as hitting only green traffic lights or having a pleasant conversation with an accidental acquaintance, simply because they do not align with the narrative of impending doom.

This confirmation bias extends far beyond everyday individuals; mountains of research show that even professional groups are far from immune. Medical professionals fixate stubbornly on symptoms matching an initial diagnosis, legal practitioners lock onto evidence supporting a suspect's guilt, and academics value only results proving their own hypotheses. This is also why politicians supporting specific policies list only the benefits while ignoring fatal side effects. Confirmation bias effectively excises the objective view of the world, locking individuals inside prisons of their own creation.

The Danger of Cognitive Traps

People often pride themselves on making rational and logical judgments, but reality frequently follows scripts written by the brain for its own convenience. Particularly at moments requiring critical decisions, internal prejudices act as veils obscuring the truth. A mindset that sees only what it wants to see ultimately sows social conflict and catastrophe, leaving individuals lost in hollow certainties.

Self-Fulfilling Prophecies and Sporting Superstitions

From a Single Sock to Collapsing Performance

The critical reason ominous signs do not remain mere illusions but step out into actual reality is the "self-fulfilling prophecy." Proposed by sociologist Robert Merton, this concept describes a vicious cycle where the moment a prediction or expectation takes hold in the mind, individuals unconsciously begin behaving in ways that actually bring about that prediction, ultimately manifesting the result in reality. Just like believing the morning traffic obstructed a filming session, which induced extreme tension and ultimately led to actual mistakes.

The professional sports world serves as a stark stage for this mechanism. Famous baseball and soccer players often insist on quirky superstitions, such as always putting on the right sock first on game day or eating a specific brand of cutlet the night before a match. If a player accidentally puts on their left sock first, plays a match, and loses, they rarely attribute the defeat to rigorous strategic analysis or poor physical condition. Instead, they fall into the trap of apophenia, firmly concluding, "Ah, we lost because I put the left sock on first this morning."

When that same player puts on the left sock first on a future day, the self-fulfilling prophecy bares its teeth. Deep-seated anxiety and lingering dread—"What if we lose again today?"—tense the player's entire body, causing mistakes in plays that would normally be executed effortlessly, thereby recording another loss. It is not the superstition that controls the player, but rather the player's anxiety about the superstition that destroys their athletic performance. Everyday superstitions, such as avoiding seaweed soup on exam day or fearing broken mirrors, are simply alternative names for this self-fulfilling prophecy.

The 1910 Halley's Comet Panic

This bizarre self-deception within individual minds occasionally spills over into massive collective madness, creating historical disasters. A prime example is the panic surrounding the approach of Halley's Comet in 1910. In past eras lacking modern science, astronomical events like comets, solar eclipses, or lunar eclipses were routinely viewed as ominous signs of impending national catastrophe. Rumors of the end of the world circulated widely, driven by a primal terror of the unknown.

In 1910, groundless rumors spread globally that the tail of Halley's Comet contained toxic gas that would strip Earth of all its oxygen. Countless people who firmly believed this simple astronomical event was an ominous sign of imminent apocalypse plunged into intense panic. People stockpiled gas masks, gathered in churches to pray, squandered entire fortunes, and wandered the streets in fear as if the world were ending. A simple cosmic light show, combined with internal human anxiety, directly generated real social chaos.

This historical event stands as poignant evidence of how fragile the foundation of an omen is, and how destructive human anxiety can be. The comet itself caused no harm; what broke society down was the fear and ominous anticipation projected onto it. As history proves, what turns the world into a hellscape is not random events, but our twisted interpretations of them.

Reframing Omens as Mirrors of the Inner Mind

Omens Are Mirrors, Not Prophecies

Faced with the persistent ghost of ominous signs, how should we respond? The primary truth to realize is that an omen is not a cold prophecy of the future, but merely a mirror reflecting the current state of our deepest inner mind. The real reason for panicking over the car blocking the Daejeon Station crosswalk was not the car itself; it was the overwhelming burden and underlying anxiety—"What if I mess up today's filming in Seoul?"—already filling the mind.

With an internal landscape saturated in anxiety and tension, every minor, accidental event in the external world morphs into powerful proof of that anxiety. On a joyful, unburdened travel day, encountering the exact same rude car blocking the path would likely have elicited a wry laugh: "Well, a quirky little episode right at the start of the trip. That's part of the journey." The physical event was identical down to the second, yet the interpretation was entirely different.

Therefore, when ominous feelings begin to creep in at the thresholds of life, the solution is not to tremble at external coincidences. It requires pausing to look quietly inward: "Ah, I am feeling quite tense about what is coming. My mind is very anxious." Acknowledging and soothing the troubled mind turns the sign into a gentle check-in rather than a warning directed at the world.

Reclaiming the Power of Interpretation

Most physical events occurring in the world are entirely value-neutral "happenings" devoid of inherent good or evil. Rain falling, a cherished coffee mug dropping and breaking, a passing bird grazing a window, or a car stalling are merely physical phenomena within the universe. Painting them with colors of "ominousness," "bad luck," or "good fortune" rests entirely in our own hands. Psychology terms this process "cognitive restructuring."

This involves consciously breaking the habit of viewing the world through a negative frame and practicing the swap to a positive or at least objective lens. Breaking a favorite cup on the way to work does not warrant beating the floor in despair over a ruined day; it can be instantly reframed: "An old cup broke, giving me an excuse to buy a fresh, pretty one to refresh my mood. It's like a quiet notification telling me to be extra careful today." Faced with the exact same broken cup, one person reads the prologue of misery, while another finds a minor turning point.

Everything changes by 180 degrees depending on the meaning assigned to it. Ultimately, omens are not laws of nature brimming with scientific inevitability like the instincts of swallows predicting rain. They are grand interpretation games born of fragile human cognition. The world does not drive us into misfortune; we attach the label of misfortune to minor coincidences and walk into it ourselves.

On life's grand stage, we are not helpless victims of a pre-written future, but active interpreters deciding how to read the present moment. The countless random fragments thrown into daily life hold no power on their own. Whether to weave those fragments into a monster of ominous dread or to discover sparks of good fortune and ease rests entirely in our own hands.
