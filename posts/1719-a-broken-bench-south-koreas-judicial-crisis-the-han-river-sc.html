A Broken Bench: South Korea’s Judicial Crisis, the Han River Scandal, and the Push to Impeach the Chief Justice

October 30, 2026

Across South Korea’s political landscape, a confrontation of historic proportions is taking shape inside the marble halls of the Supreme Court and the National Assembly. While the country continues its political realignment under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, deep-seated tensions within the nation’s judiciary have erupted into the open. At the center of the storm sits Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-de, facing escalating demands for his impeachment, alongside Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, the conservative heavyweight currently mired in a multi-million-dollar riverboat procurement scandal. For observers outside Korea, the dispute may look like partisan friction, but it touches upon a far more fundamental question: has the country’s judicial apparatus become an instrument of political preservation?

Public confidence in the bench has deteriorated rapidly following a string of controversial rulings. Critics argue that the courts, shaped significantly during the tumultuous tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who now sits behind bars following his impeachment and removal from office—continue to operate under an institutional double standard. The progressive camp contends that while progressive politicians have faced sweeping, unsparing scrutiny, conservative figures enjoy judicial rationales that defy common sense.

The Logic of Impunity: Oh Se-hoon, Myung Tae-kyun, and the Courts

The immediate spark behind the push to impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-de was an appellate court ruling that acquitted Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon in a case involving the controversial political broker Myung Tae-kyun. The prosecution had centered on illicit polling and campaign finance violations, with observers widely expecting a conviction given the paper trail established in earlier proceedings.

In the court of first instance, judges took a meticulous approach to the evidence. Rather than relying solely on the word of Myung, the trial court cross-referenced testimony from several figures, including Kim Man-bae and Kang Hye-kyung, corroborating specific financial transfers with contemporaneous banking records. That lower court handed down a conviction specifically regarding 21 million won (about $15,000) tied to five targeted opinion polls. The ruling carefully unpicked the narrative: whenever testimonies aligned with objective banking records—such as a 10 million won transfer to an associate’s bank account—the court treated it as established fact.

The appellate court, however, dismantled this structure, issuing an outright acquittal for Mayor Oh. What shocked legal scholars and the broader public was the rationale offered by the bench. Rather than demonstrating an evidentiary flaw, the appellate judges leaned on a sentiment that legal analysts have termed painfully flimsy: effectively arguing that a figure of Oh Se-hoon’s stature "would have had no reason to break the law" in such a manner. In doing so, the court excused the ultimate political beneficiary of the operation while the intermediary who facilitated the funds and the deputy mayor were convicted.

This stark exercise in selective credibility has infuriated the governing Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). In cases involving Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the judiciary repeatedly leaned on Myung’s testimony as dependable and consistent whenever it supported an acquittal; yet, when the same witness’s accounts implicated Mayor Oh, the bench discarded his testimony as utterly unreliable. For many South Koreans, this double standard confirms suspicions that the judiciary under Chief Justice Jo is shielding conservative elites behind subjective character endorsements rather than applying statutory law.

Riverboats and Kickbacks: The Hangang Bus Scandal

While Mayor Oh navigated his courtroom challenges, a second, equally combustible crisis emerged over his signature municipal initiative: the "Hangang Bus," an ambitious commuter water-ferry network across the Han River. Investigations conducted by the Seoul Metropolitan Council revealed a procurement process riddled with inexplicable irregularities.

Most glaringly, the Seoul Metropolitan Government awarded a multimillion-dollar contract to a small firm named Gadeok Heavy Industries—a company that had never built a single passenger vessel. During a focused one-week inquiry, city council members uncovered evidence pointing to an estimated 320 million won (roughly $235,000) in illicit kickbacks surrounding the procurement process. A whistleblower from within the administration came forward, disclosing that documentation specifically prepared for criminal referral had been systematically shredded and destroyed.

When the matter was referred to the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), South Korea’s state watchdog, the agency dismissed the request on the very day it was received. This unusually swift brush-off prompted accusations that state inspection bodies were actively running interference for the conservative mayor. Mayor Oh subsequently went before the city council and public forums, claiming that the BAI had thoroughly examined and formally dismissed the audit request due to a lack of wrongdoing.

That claim quickly unraveled. Leaked agency correspondence confirmed that the BAI had not dismissed the complaint on its merits; it had merely paused action because another administrative inquiry was already underway. The Democratic Party promptly filed criminal charges against Oh for distributing false information under the Public Official Election Act. Under South Korean law, potential political candidates are held strictly liable for deceptive factual assertions designed to sway voters. With the statutory six-month limitation period set to expire in early December, prosecutors face immense pressure to decide whether to indict the mayor before the legal window closes forever.

A Two-Track Legislative Push: Impeachment and Investigation

Faced with judicial reticence and investigative foot-dragging, the progressive majority in the National Assembly is moving aggressively. Led by the DPK and supported by Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party, lawmakers have laid out a dual strategy: a comprehensive parliamentary investigation (gukjeong josa) coupled with formal impeachment proceedings against Chief Justice Jo Hee-de.

The Rebuilding Korea Party has taken an especially uncompromising line, insisting that progressive lawmakers must not let procedural compromise delay their timeline. Following a formal investigation request submitted on November 5, the coalition aims to adopt an investigative plan by November 19, regardless of whether the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), attempts a legislative filibuster.

"The National Assembly cannot allow parliamentary inquiries to become an excuse to delay impeachment," the leadership argued, pointing out that waiting for bipartisan consensus with the PPP would only grant targets time to destroy evidence.

For the progressive bloc, suspending Chief Justice Jo from his duties is not merely punitive; it is seen as a necessary protective measure. A chief justice under formal impeachment is barred from exercising administrative power over the court system, removing the leadership’s capacity to assign panel judges or influence upcoming high-stakes rulings on election law. Progressive strategists argue that only by removing Jo from the head of the Supreme Court can lower-court judges rule without internal pressure from senior conservative judicial cartels.

Internal Stakes and What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly gears up for this institutional clash, the ruling Democratic Party is also managing its own internal architecture. A key contest is underway for the leadership of the party’s Women's Committee. While party elections often fly under the media radar, this specific position carries an automatic seat on the Candidate Recommendation Committee (gongcheon gwanri wiwonhoe)—the powerful body that vets, selects, and greenlights candidates for future local and general elections.

The race between two-term lawmaker Moon Jeong-bok and first-term representative Kim Nam-hee has drawn intense scrutiny. While conservative media outlets have sought to portray the race as a bitter factional feud, party insiders have worked to frame it as an exercise in managed consensus. The ultimate objective is clear: to maintain party unity so that internal friction does not undermine the broader legislative push against judicial bias and municipal corruption.

The coming weeks will mark a defining juncture for South Korea’s legal and political stability. Key developments to monitor include:

  • The December Deadline on Mayor Oh: Watch whether state prosecutors indict Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon under the Public Official Election Act before the statutory limitation expires in early December, or whether the case is quietly dropped.
  • The November 19 Legislative Milestone: Observe whether the DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party succeed in passing their parliamentary inquiry plan over the People Power Party’s objections, establishing a special committee to probe the Hangang Bus kickbacks.
  • The Impeachment Motion Against Chief Justice Jo: Monitor the drafting and introduction of the formal impeachment motion against Jo Hee-de, which would immediately suspend him from his duties and trigger an unprecedented trial before the Constitutional Court.

South Korea’s democracy has demonstrated an extraordinary capacity to withstand executive abuse, as shown by the historic impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Now, however, the battlefield has shifted to the courts. If the legislature moves to suspend the head of the judiciary while dragging the Seoul mayor into criminal court, South Korea will enter an uncharted era of constitutional stress testing—one where the limits of judicial immunity are laid bare for all to see.
