Beyond Public Protests: Why South Korea’s Judicial Overreach Demands Legislative Reform

September 08, 2026

Walk through the bustling streets of Seoul near the National Assembly, and you can practically feel the high-voltage tension hanging in the air. For months, South Korea’s political discourse has been trapped in a relentless, exhausting loop of legal battles and courtroom drama. When elections conclude, standard political logic dictates that parties should pivot to governing, managing public welfare, and solving pressing economic issues. Yet, Yeouido—Seoul’s political epicenter—remains consumed by allegations, judicial risks, and endless debates over high-profile trials.

At the center of this storm are legal cases involving major political figures, most notably President Lee Jae-myung. Within the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), various strategies have been floated to bring these trials to a sudden close. Among them, the concept of a prosecutorial withdrawal of indictment has become a fiery, highly controversial talking point. But while television commentators and partisan talk shows treat withdrawal as a political silver bullet, prominent legal scholars argue that this obsession rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of criminal procedure.

Enter Cho Kuk, a former law professor at Seoul National University and a prominent criminal law scholar whose past academic writings on penal law remain among the most heavily cited in the country. Stepping into the debate with the precision of a seasoned academic, Cho offered an assessment entirely out of step with partisan expectations. Rather than indulging in comforting political fantasies, he systematically dismantled the legal feasibility of an unconditional withdrawal of indictment, pointing instead toward a different, more structural solution. Cutting through emotional slogans, his analysis exposes a deeper, more alarming reality: South Korean politics has become dangerously, perhaps unsustainably, judicialized.

The Legal Dead End of Withdrawing Indictments

Why First-Instance Trials Represent a Hard Legal Boundary

The debate gained intense momentum when Rep. Kim Seung-won—co-leader of a newly formed intra-party group dedicated to pushing for the withdrawal of indictments—was nominated for a major cabinet post. Lawmakers within the liberal bloc began eyeing indictment withdrawal as a swift escape hatch for President Lee’s legal troubles. From a purely legal standpoint, however, this strategy colludes directly with a hard statutory wall.

Under South Korea's Criminal Procedure Act, prosecutors are legally permitted to withdraw an indictment *only* while a case is pending in the first-instance trial court. Once a case leaves the initial trial and ascends to an appellate court—the second instance—the legal mechanism for a simple prosecutorial withdrawal vanishes. If the legislature were to force the issue by passing a special law specifically designed to cancel an ongoing appellate proceeding, it would almost certainly be struck down as unconstitutional. A court reviewing such a statute would issue an immediate referral to the Constitutional Court, creating a catastrophic constitutional crisis born from a botched attempt at political engineering.

President Lee’s public election law violation case sits squarely inside this very trap. Recall the dramatic proceedings when Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae mobilized a full bench to overturn an appellate acquittal in a blistering nine-day turnaround. That case has now bounced back to the second-instance appellate court. Even if legal proceedings resume or pause following shifts in political power, the binding nature of the Supreme Court's ruling makes it virtually impossible for an appellate panel to reverse the framework of guilt. Treating this as a candidate for indictment withdrawal ignores the rigid boundaries of the courtroom.

The Boomerang Effect of Partisan Posturing

When politicians ignore statutory limits and aggressively push for impossible legal remedies, the political fallout is swift. The People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) has seized upon these legal overreaches to tighten the screws, demanding records of past indictment withdrawals from the Ministry of Justice and the Supreme Court to press their advantage. Conservatives point to rare historical anomalies—such as a 1980s-era special pardon or technical procedural violations—to argue that President Lee's case is fundamentally different.

In this high-stakes environment, hardline politicians and allied online commentators insisting that "withdrawal is the only way out" are offering little more than empty echoes. Worse, they provide their political opponents with ample ammunition. Anyone who points out these objective legal realities risks being labeled a traitor or a liability by extreme factions. Yet screaming emotional slogans cannot magically erase a Supreme Court ruling. Denying the hard boundaries of the legal system only drives political actors deeper into a corner, handing the opposition a legitimate, rules-based club to swing.

The Toxic Legacy of the Public Official Election Act

How a 2000 Legislative Change Created a Monster

To understand the root cause of this paralysis, one must look beyond temporary trial tactics and examine the statute at the heart of the controversy: the Public Official Election Act, specifically its clause penalizing the publication of false information. This particular provision has carried a fatal flaw since its inception.

If a candidate blatantly fabricates or conceals objective, verifiable facts—such as their birthplace, family relations, personal identity, property holdings, or academic credentials—the boundary between truth and falsehood is clear-cut. However, a major revision in the year 2000 introduced a broad and slippery term into the statute: "acts" (행위). The moment this vague concept was layered into the law, the landscape changed overnight. Suddenly, ordinary political discourse—who a politician met, what casual remarks were exchanged, and how subjective human memory recalled fragments of past conversations—became fair game for prosecutorial scrutiny.

This single word effectively transformed the election law into an all-purpose toxic tool. Prosecutors gained sweeping, arbitrary power to drag politicians into court over differing interpretations of past conduct and speech. In the current case involving President Lee, an appellate court initially issued an acquittal, only for the Supreme Court to reverse it with a guilty interpretation. If legal experts themselves remain bitterly divided over the exact meaning of a disputed political statement, how can ordinary politicians possibly navigate such a treacherous legal minefield?

The Over-Criminalization of Politics

This phenomenon is what legal analysts term the "over-criminalization of politics." Instead of leaving sharp political clashes and policy debates to be settled by voters at the ballot box, prosecutors intervene at every turn, dragging routine political disagreements into criminal courtrooms. The prosecutor's office effectively holds the political survival of elected officials in its hands—all because of a single ambiguous word.

The absurdity of this framework is highlighted by its indiscriminate boomerang effect. Recently, former President Yoon Suk-yeol faced a heavy prison sentence in a lower court ruling stemming from statements related to a controversial spiritual figure. While conservative opponents initially cheered the verdict, a closer look revealed a chilling irony: that ruling simply copied and pasted the exact legal reasoning deployed by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s Supreme Court in the earlier push against President Lee.

In other words, the legal weapon forged to bludgeon a political rival ultimately loops back to strike one's own camp. Under this system, the ruling party faces potential financial penalties running into tens of billions of won (roughly $30 million) if the precedent holds. Progress and conservatism alike are battered by an exhausting cycle where political expression is chilled, and ultimate arbiter status is handed over to unelected judges and prosecutors.

The Real Legislative Remedy: Seeking a Dismissal of Prosecution

A Long-Dormant Bill Awaiting Action in the National Assembly

If partisan maneuvering and indictment withdrawals are dead ends, how can South Korea break this exhausting cycle? The answer lies not in breaking the law, but in fixing it. A legislative amendment to the Public Official Election Act—sponsored by liberal lawmakers and cleared by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee over a year ago—sits quietly in the parliamentary pipeline.

The core mechanism of this bill is remarkably straightforward: it excises the vague and expansive concept of "acts" from the false-information provision. By narrowing the statute down to clear, objective factual criteria, pending cases prosecuted under the ambiguous old standard immediately lose their legal footing. Once the National Assembly amends the law, courts would be legally obligated to issue a formal "dismissal of prosecution" (면소 판결) on the grounds that the necessary legal prerequisites for a continued trial no longer exist. This is the cleanest, most airtight resolution available.

Bringing this long-dormant bill to a plenary floor vote should not be treated as a partisan football. As legal scholars argue, stripping away the over-criminalization of politics is a basic necessity for a healthy democracy, regardless of political camp. Leaving this reform gathering dust in a drawer while politicians bicker publicly serves no one.

Once enacted, the effect of this amendment would extend across political lines. It would provide a structured exit ramp for pending cases involving figures from across the political spectrum—creating a clean, systemic path toward dismissals that applies equally to past and present leaders.

A Two-Track Strategy: Accountability Coupled with Reform

Legislative reform alone, however, cannot completely wash away the scars of past prosecutorial overreach. There remain specific instances—such as the Seongnam FC investigations—where credible allegations suggest state power was abused and evidence was systematically manipulated. True accountability requires a rigorous, procedural "two-track strategy."

First, formal state institutions must step up. Whether through internal Ministry of Justice inspections, investigations by the Corruption Investigation Office for High-Ranking Officials, or a dedicated special counsel, allegations of investigative misconduct and evidence fabrication must be officially investigated and verified by state bodies. Only when a recognized oversight body formally confirms that procedural rules were violated can a trial prosecutor find a legitimate, lawful basis to withdraw an indictment.

The fierce pushback from conservative lawmakers against targeted inspections of certain prosecutors illustrates just how sensitive the political establishment is to this accountability track. Their knee-jerk defense of suspect investigators proves that systemic oversight strikes directly at the heart of their vulnerabilities. Combining systemic legislative reform with institutional oversight offers the only viable exit from the current constitutional bog.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this turbulent legal and political landscape, several concrete milestones will determine whether the nation slides deeper into judicialized chaos or steps back toward legislative sanity:

  • Plenary Action on the Election Act Amendment: Monitor whether the National Assembly brings the long-stalled Public Official Election Act amendment—specifically targeting the ambiguous "acts" clause—to the floor for a formal vote.
  • Appellate Court Responses: Observe how second-instance panels handle cases remanded by the Supreme Court, particularly regarding whether they signal any procedural limits to ongoing political prosecutions.
  • Progress on Oversight and Inspection: Track official moves by the Ministry of Justice, independent inspection bodies, or potential special counsel initiatives examining allegations of investigative misconduct during past high-profile probes.
  • Inter-Party Negotiations: Watch for shifts in how the ruling and opposition parties handle legal reform proposals, moving away from emotional rhetoric toward structural changes in the criminal code.

South Korea stands at a critical crossroads. Continuing to delegate political survival to the pens of judges and the charging discretion of prosecutors threatens to hollow out democratic norms. By trading partisan slogans for structural legislative reform, the National Assembly has a narrow window to restore the separation of powers and return the fate of governance to the voters where it belongs.
