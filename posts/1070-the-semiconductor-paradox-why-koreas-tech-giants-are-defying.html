The Semiconductor Paradox: Why Korea’s Tech Giants Are Defying Market Logic

October 13, 2026

If you have been tracking the South Korean stock market recently, you might have heard the ominous whispers of the "6,000 collapse"—a fear that the benchmark KOSPI index is spiraling toward disaster. At the center of this anxiety are Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the twin pillars of the Korean economy. As their stock prices flounder, a wave of panic has swept through the market. But is this slump a signal that the foundational industry of the digital age is crumbling, or is it merely a temporary technical glitch caused by a mismatch in supply and demand?

Markets have a habit of forgetting the fundamentals. When times are good, investors pile in without question; when prices wobble, they immediately assume the sky is falling. To cut through the noise, we look to the analysis of Roh Keun-chang, head of the research lab Nokkeun Chaeum, a 28-year veteran of the industry. His conclusion is striking: we are currently in a peculiar phase where market prices are distorting our view of the actual industrial outlook, rather than reflecting it.

The New Normal: A Trillion-Dollar Memory Market

The character of the semiconductor market has fundamentally shifted. While many investors remain fixated on sluggish PC and smartphone demand, the real engine of the industry has migrated to data centers. The DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory) market alone is estimated at about $680 billion this year, and projections suggest it will cross the $1 trillion threshold next year. This is not just a price fluctuation; it is a structural "New Normal" where the entire scale of the industry is leveling up.

NAND flash memory is following suit, with growth expected to surge from $340 billion this year to $420 billion in 2025. Combined, the global memory industry is becoming a $1.4 trillion behemoth. Where the memory sector was once a "cyclical industry" prone to wild swings based on the broader economy, it is now a structural growth industry riding the massive wave of AI infrastructure investment.

Crucially, this growth isn't driven by blind optimism; it is fueled by a desperate, real-world scramble for supply. Client inventories have stabilized, and talk of "production cuts"—a common rumor among skeptics—is detached from reality. You cannot discuss production cuts when inventories are tightening. The current price increases are the result of basic, healthy supply-and-demand logic.

Bottlenecks as Truth-Tellers

To understand the depth of this demand, look at the "bottom" of the value chain: the wafer manufacturers. When Japanese firms like Shin-Etsu and SUMCO report strong earnings, it is a definitive sign that the entire industry is thriving. During a field visit in June, even experts were surprised to find that while equipment and substrates were in short supply, materials were relatively abundant. Now, even that final line of defense is facing shortages.

If the market were in a true crisis, this wouldn't be happening. Around the world, ten major "greenfield" fabrication plants—entirely new facilities built from the ground up—are currently under construction. Industry giants like Taiwan's TSMC, America's Micron, and South Korea’s Samsung and SK Hynix are pouring massive capital into these projects. They are doing so because demand is unequivocally outpacing supply. Equipment manufacturers like Applied Materials and Lam Research are not reporting record-breaking forecasts because of a fantasy; they are seeing the start of an investment cycle that will carry the industry through 2026 and beyond.

Key Takeaways:
  • The DRAM market is on track to exceed $1 trillion for the first time next year, driven by HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) demand.
  • With inventories stabilizing across the supply chain, there are no meaningful production cuts; instead, we are in a natural price-appreciation phase.
  • The construction of ten new global fabrication plants proves that AI infrastructure investment is not a temporary fad.

Data Centers: The New Order of Semiconductors

During the previous market cycle of 2017–2018, data centers accounted for only about 34% of server shipments. The remaining 66% was tied to consumer products like PCs and smartphones, meaning that a dip in consumer spending caused a chain-reaction collapse in the server market. Today, that ratio has flipped. Consumer electronics now represent a much smaller piece of the pie, and over half of server DRAM goes directly to data centers.

This has significantly reduced volatility. Memory manufacturers no longer need to be held hostage by the whims of the consumer market. With hyper-scalers and companies like NVIDIA clamoring for supply, the manufacturers are in a position of power. Customers are now willing to pay a premium just to secure volume.

NVIDIA’s shift toward "system supply"—where they design entire rack layouts (NVL systems) rather than just selling individual chips—is a boon for memory firms. By bundling hardware, NVIDIA is effectively acting as a shock absorber that stabilizes pricing. Furthermore, the expansion into automotive and humanoid robotics, such as Tesla’s Optimus, guarantees that the need for high-capacity memory will continue to grow exponentially.

The Paradox of Samsung’s Undervaluation

Samsung Electronics presents a fascinating paradox. The company is projected to see significant operating profits next year, potentially exceeding 500 trillion won (roughly $370 billion). Yet, its stock price remains stuck near its book value. For a company with an ROE (Return on Equity) exceeding 50%, a PBR (Price-to-Book Ratio) near 1.2x is historically cheap.

This "undervaluation paradox" is largely due to the South Korean stock market’s extreme sensitivity to macro-level fluctuations. When risk-aversion hits global markets, foreign investors mechanically sell off Korean tech giants like Samsung and SK Hynix, not because of an issue with the industry, but because of a need to adjust their Korean portfolio weightings. With foreign ownership of Samsung falling to around 48%—a ten-year low—the selling pressure is likely reaching its exhaustion point.

Furthermore, under the current administration led by President Lee Jae-myung, there is a strong push toward strengthening shareholder return policies. With Samsung signaling dividends around 10,000 won per share, the stock is becoming an attractive alternative to traditional bonds. Investing in a company with a 50% ROE trading at such low multiples is a rare opportunity, provided you have the patience to wait for the market to correct its error.

Geopolitics and the Path Forward

The current landscape is not without risks. Uncertainty surrounding the incoming U.S. administration and its stance on semiconductor subsidies—evidenced by recent comments regarding the potential renegotiation of existing contracts—poses a challenge. The days of relying solely on foreign subsidies are ending. South Korean firms must adopt a "self-reliant" strategy, balancing global operations with domestic technological dominance.

Ultimately, technology is the only thing that cannot be betrayed. Regardless of U.S. policy or Chinese ambitions, South Korea remains the essential engine of the global tech infrastructure. Whether it is Elon Musk’s satellite networks or the global push toward full autonomous driving, the core components will almost certainly bear the mark of Korean innovation.

While the stock market tests our patience, the underlying data remains unchanged. We are witnessing a transition where the digital economy is being rebuilt on a foundation of massive AI infrastructure. For those who can see past the current short-term volatility, the fear-driven sell-offs of today may well look like the final chance to board the train before the next cycle of growth takes off. The engines of the modern world are not stopping; they are only just beginning to accelerate.
