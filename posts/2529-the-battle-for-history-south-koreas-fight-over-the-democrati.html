The Battle for History: South Korea’s Fight Over the Democratic Meritorious Persons Act

November 20, 2026

Inside the main chamber of the National Assembly in Seoul, the air is thick with a familiar, wearying tension. It is November 19, and the usual business of government—debating budgets or fine-tuning infrastructure—has ground to a halt. Instead, the floor is occupied by a succession of lawmakers speaking for hours on end. This is a filibuster, the ultimate parliamentary tool of delay, and the subject of the fight is not the future, but the past. At the heart of the storm is the "Democratic Meritorious Persons Act," a piece of legislation that has become a lightning rod for the deep ideological divisions that define modern South Korean politics.

For the ruling Democratic Party, led by the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the bill is a long-overdue debt of honor. It seeks to provide official recognition and support to those who were injured or killed during various pro-democracy struggles that occurred outside the two most famous uprisings in Korean history. For the main opposition, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), the bill is something far more cynical: a "privilege act" designed to reward the political allies of the ruling party and elevate former student activists into a new, hereditary social class at the taxpayer's expense.

To understand why a bill about events that happened decades ago can paralyze a modern G20 legislature, one must understand the unique way South Korea treats its history. In Korea, history is not just a record of the past; it is the primary source of political legitimacy. By deciding who counts as a "meritorious person," the state isn't just handing out healthcare subsidies—it is deciding whose version of Korea's difficult journey to democracy is the "official" one.

Expanding the Circle of Honor

South Korea already has robust laws to honor those who fought for its freedom. Currently, the state provides significant benefits to those involved in the April 19 Revolution of 1960 (which toppled the country’s first president) and the May 18 Gwangju Democratization Movement of 1980 (a bloody uprising against military rule). These individuals are classified as "meritorious persons," a status that brings with it not only immense social prestige but also government support for medical care and housing.

However, the Democratic Party argues that this creates an unfair hierarchy. Between 1960 and the late 1980s, thousands of other Koreans were imprisoned, tortured, or killed in smaller-scale protests, labor movements, and student demonstrations that eventually forced the country to adopt a direct presidential election system in 1987. Under current law, many of these individuals are technically recognized as "democracy movement participants," but they do not receive the same level of state support as those from the 1960 or 1980 events.

The proposed Democratic Meritorious Persons Act aims to close this gap. During the plenary session, Representative Heo Young of the Democratic Party stood to explain the proposal. He argued that the state has a moral obligation to care for those who sacrificed their physical health or their lives to build the democratic foundation upon which the current government stands. The bill focuses on providing "minimal honor," specifically targeting medical support and nursing care for those who suffered physical injury or the families of those who died. For the Democratic Party, this is about the state finally taking responsibility for its own history of authoritarian violence.

"Activist Laundering" or National Duty?

The People Power Party sees the situation through a completely different lens. Shortly after the bill was introduced, the PPP launched a filibuster to block its passage. Their primary argument is that the bill is too vague and prone to abuse. They have dubbed it the "Activist Privilege Act" and, more sharply, "Activist Laundering."

The core of the opposition’s concern lies in the criteria for who qualifies. Because the pro-democracy movement in the 1970s and 80s was broad and often underground, the PPP argues that it is difficult to distinguish between those who truly fought for democracy and those who were involved in radical anti-state activities. Representative Jeong Jeom-sig of the PPP delivered a scathing critique during the filibuster, calling the bill a "pension plan for the activist class." He argued that the bill could potentially grant "meritorious" status to individuals who were convicted of violating the National Security Act or those whose actions were not clearly aligned with democratic values.

The PPP also raised concerns about the fairness of the national veterans' benefit system. They argue that by expanding the "meritorious person" status so broadly, the government is diluting the honor of those who fought in the Korean War or those who died in the line of duty as police officers or firefighters. Representative Jang Dong-hyeok accused the Democratic Party of prioritizing "hereditary privileges" for their political base while ignoring the needs of the general public. The opposition claims that the bill includes hidden perks for the children of activists, such as preferential treatment in university admissions or job placements—a claim the Democratic Party has repeatedly and vehemently denied.

The Mechanics of a Parliamentary Standoff

The current standoff is not just an ideological debate; it is a tactical battle of parliamentary procedure. The Democratic Meritorious Persons Act has a long and troubled history. It was previously introduced during the 21st National Assembly but was allowed to expire when the term ended. Now, in the 22nd National Assembly, the Democratic Party is using its majority to force the issue to a conclusion.

When the bill was brought to the floor on November 19, the PPP immediately moved to a filibuster. Under South Korean law, a filibuster—officially called a "round-the-clock debate"—can continue indefinitely unless a motion to terminate it is passed. However, there is a catch: a motion to end the debate can only be voted on 24 hours after it is filed, and it requires a three-fifths majority (180 out of 300 members) to pass.

The Democratic Party filed that motion at 2:29 PM on November 19. This set a 24-hour clock in motion. During those 24 hours, members of the PPP took turns at the podium, speaking at length about the perceived injustices of the bill. Meanwhile, Democratic Party members like Representative Min Byeong-deok took their own turns to counter the opposition's claims, arguing that the "privilege" narrative was a fabrication designed to stir up public resentment. This procedural dance ensures that the bill will likely pass, given the Democratic Party’s numbers, but it also guarantees maximum political friction and public exposure for the opposition’s grievances.

The High Cost of Political Gridlock

While the televised speeches in the main chamber grab the headlines, the real-world impact of this fight is being felt in the committee rooms. The Political Affairs Committee, which oversees the Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs as well as the Financial Services Commission, has effectively stopped functioning. The PPP has boycotted the committee meetings in protest of the Democratic Party's "unilateral" push for the history bill.

This paralysis has collateral damage. Several "People’s Livelihood Bills"—legislation aimed at addressing urgent economic issues for ordinary citizens—are now stuck in limbo. Most notable among these are the "Three Housing Supply Acts," a package of bills designed to stabilize the real estate market and accelerate the construction of new homes to combat rising housing costs.

The opposition argues that the Democratic Party is holding the national economy hostage to pass a "sectarian" bill. The ruling party counters that it is the opposition’s boycott and filibuster that are preventing progress. For the average voter, the result is the same: critical legislation regarding housing and finance is being sacrificed on the altar of a decades-old historical dispute. The gridlock highlights the zero-sum nature of current Korean politics, where neither side is willing to grant the other a "win," even at the cost of stalled public policy.

What to Watch Next

The immediate future of the Democratic Meritorious Persons Act is fairly predictable, but its long-term consequences are not. Here is what to look for in the coming days and weeks:

  • The Final Vote: Once the 24-hour period for the filibuster expires on the afternoon of November 20, the Democratic Party will move to forcibly end the debate and hold a vote. Given their majority, the bill is almost certain to pass the National Assembly.
  • The Executive Response: While the bill will pass the legislature, it will then land on the desk of President Lee Jae-myung. Given that his party is the one driving the legislation, he is expected to sign it into law, cementing it as a core achievement of his administration’s efforts to "rectify history."
  • The Implementation Battle: Once the bill becomes law, the focus will shift to the Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs. The specific process for screening and selecting the new "meritorious persons" will be scrutinized intensely. Any individual with a controversial past who receives the status will likely become a new flashpoint for political conflict.
  • Public Sentiment: The PPP is banking on the idea that the public will view this as an act of political cronyism. Watch for opinion polls in the aftermath of the vote to see if the "privilege" narrative takes hold or if the public views the act as a fair resolution of historical grievances.

The battle over the Democratic Meritorious Persons Act is a reminder that in South Korea, the past is never truly dead. It is a living, breathing part of the current political landscape, capable of stopping the wheels of government even as the country faces modern challenges in housing, technology, and global markets. As the filibuster winds down and the vote approaches, the country remains divided not just by what it wants for its future, but by how it chooses to remember its past.
