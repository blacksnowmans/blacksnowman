Legal Battles, Budgetary Clashes, and Fading Giants: Inside South Korea's High-Stakes Political Warfare

October 25, 2026

To understand the rhythm of power in South Korea, one must look at two distinct districts separated by the Han River: Yeouido, the island where the National Assembly stands, and Seocho-dong, the neighborhood that houses the country's supreme legal authorities. When the political process stalls in Yeouido, the battles inevitably migrate across the river to the courts of Seocho-dong. Conversely, every ruling handed down by a judge immediately detonates inside the legislative chamber.

That combustible feedback loop was on full display in late October. An appellate court handed a high-profile acquittal to Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, completely scrambling the political landscape ahead of the next presidential cycle. Simultaneously, the National Assembly was descending into rancor during the final stretch of its annual parliamentary audit (known locally as gukjeong gamsa), clashing over a massive 162 trillion won (roughly $120 billion) strategic fund and bitter fights over labor deregulation. Beneath the day-to-day partisan trench warfare, the death of a revered pro-democracy veteran offered a poignant, stark reminder of how far contemporary Korean politics has drifted from the art of compromise.

The Courtroom as Campaign Ground: Oh Se-hoon's Acquittal Shakes the Field

Few events alter the trajectory of South Korean politics faster than a courtroom verdict. Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a senior figure in the conservative People Power Party (the country's primary opposition party), secured a clean acquittal in his second-stage appellate trial. For months, legal entanglements had hung over Oh like a sword of Damocles, dampening his political capital and complicating his future ambitions.

The moment the verdict was announced, the People Power Party celebrated the outcome as "the triumph of natural justice" (sapil-gwijeong). Conservative lawmakers immediately went on the offensive, demanding public apologies from the special prosecution team and alleging that the case had been a politically driven smear campaign. With Oh's legal shackles removed, the conservative bloc suddenly found its presumptive lineup for the next presidential race revitalized—even as his return introduces fresh factional jockeying within his own party for leadership dominance.

Across the aisle, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea was visibly rattled. Party spokespersons pushed back hard, insisting that an acquittal on technical legal grounds does not equate to moral or political innocence. Democratic lawmakers urged an immediate appeal to the Supreme Court to uncover the "substantive truth." More combative voices within the party aimed their fire directly at the bench led by Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, floating the possibility of parliamentary inquiries or even judicial impeachment motions.

"Politics must never be decided by the judiciary."

This terse remark, posted by conservative Daegu Mayor Hong Joon-pyo shortly after the ruling, cut straight to the core problem. South Korean politics has fallen into an unsustainable pattern where rival parties criminalize their policy disputes. Whenever politicians suffer a legislative defeat, they file criminal complaints; whenever a judge issues a verdict, the losing faction accuses the judiciary of partisan bias or orchestrates retaliatory pressure. This "judicialization of politics" relieves politicians of their duty to negotiate, leaving the stability of the entire republic tied to individual court rulings.

Clashing Over Trillions: The Future Fund, Labor Caps, and Prosecutorial Audits

While the courts dominated the headlines, the legislative committees inside the National Assembly turned into ideological battlegrounds over the national purse and regulatory power.

At the center of the dispute was the government's proposed "Future Response Fund," an enormous initiative totaling 162 trillion won (about $120 billion) designed to secure next-generation growth engines and national competitiveness amid a shifting global economy. The Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party defend the fund as essential, proactive spending needed to navigate industrial transitions.

The opposition People Power Party, however, launched an all-out offensive against the fund, branding it as a government "slush fund" ripe for fiscal mismanagement. Conservative lawmakers introduced counter-legislation aimed at reinforcing regional finances and mandating sovereign debt repayments to rein in state spending. Tensions reached a boiling point over the bureaucratic architecture itself: efforts by the administration to grant the central planning office autonomy from the traditional oversight of the Ministry of Economy and Finance triggered sharp accusations that conventional economic checks were being deliberately bypassed.

Simultaneously, the parliamentary audit of the newly established Prosecution Service (an investigative and prosecutorial body restructured under legal reforms) descended into chaos over past indictments against President Lee Jae-myung. Conservative opposition lawmakers fiercely attacked recent moves to dismiss and drop charges against the president, decrying them as an abuse of power designed to shield the sitting head of state. Democratic lawmakers countered that dismissing these charges was a necessary correction of past targeted, politically motivated prosecutions launched during the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was previously impeached and removed from office before his imprisonment.

Beyond fiscal allocations and prosecutorial power, the audit exposed deep fractures over industrial policy through the proposed Mega-Regional Special Zone Act. The legislation aims to stem regional population decline by granting tax incentives and regulatory relief to struggling provinces outside greater Seoul. However, a major point of contention emerged over a provision exempting designated zones from the country's strict 52-hour statutory workweek cap:

  • The Business Community: Industry lobbies argued that regional revival is impossible without flexible working arrangements, demanding swift passage to attract corporate investment outside Seoul.
  • Labor Organizations: Labor unions fiercely protested, warning that the exemption creates a legal loophole for dangerous, prolonged working hours and erodes fundamental worker rights under the guise of regional parity.
  • The Democratic Party Dilemma: The ruling party found itself caught in a painful internal rift, torn between its foundational identity as a pro-labor party and the pragmatic economic necessity of reviving hollowed-out provincial economies.

The Presidential Rebound and a High-Stakes European Summit

Against this backdrop of legislative friction, President Lee Jae-myung saw his public approval ratings climb back into the 40 percent range. The modest rebound provided the administration with much-needed political breathing room following recent policy friction and cabinet controversies.

A significant driver of this momentum—and a subject of fierce debate—has been President Lee's reliance on direct communication. Declaring early on that social media is his administration's "lifeline," the president and his cabinet ministers have taken to digital platforms at all hours to explain initiatives directly to the public. Supporters praise this approach for dismantling rigid bureaucratic walls and creating transparent, real-time engagement. Critics and opposition figures argue that a nation cannot be governed via short social media posts, warning that bypassing formal press conferences and parliamentary consultations risks turning governance into a polarized digital echo chamber.

On the international stage, the diplomatic schedule is accelerating. The presidential office confirmed that German Chancellor Friedrich Merz will visit South Korea on October 29 for a bilateral summit with President Lee. With the war in Ukraine dragging on and global supply chains destabilized by US-China competition, cooperation with Europe's industrial powerhouse is of paramount importance. The talks are expected to focus heavily on advanced industrial supply chains, clean energy cooperation, and shared security concerns.

Simultaneously, Unification Minister Chung Dong-young held talks with the European Union's Indo-Pacific envoy, aiming to expand diplomatic room for multilateral engagement on the Korean Peninsula. Veteran foreign policy hands in Seoul continue to stress that South Korea must steer clear of rigid ideological diplomacy, balancing national security partnerships with pragmatic, profit-oriented realism.

The Passing of a Democracy Pioneer and the Shadows of History

Amid the noise of budget brawls and courtroom verdicts, the passing of former Home Affairs Minister Choi Hyung-woo at age 91 marked the closing of a monumental chapter in Korean modern history. Choi was widely revered as the "right hand" of the late President Kim Young-sam. Alongside his political contemporary Kim Dong-young, Choi formed the legendary vanguard of the "Sangdo-dong faction," enduring state surveillance, brutal interrogation, and political bans during South Korea's military dictatorships to bring about civilian democracy.

The composition of Choi's funeral committee spoke volumes: it was co-chaired by Kwon No-gap—the legendary right hand of the late progressive President Kim Dae-jung—alongside veteran conservative Kim Deog-ryong. In the 1970s and 1980s, the Sangdo-dong (Kim Young-sam) and Donggyo-dong (Kim Dae-jung) factions fought relentlessly for political primacy. Yet, when confronting military authoritarianism, they set aside rivalries to sit across the table, hammer out joint democratic manifestos, and compromise.

The dignity of that history contrasted sharply with events unfolding inside the National Assembly on the very same day. Lawmakers from the conservative People Power Party hosted a forum titled "Park Chung-hee the Revolutionary," re-evaluating the former military general who seized power in a 1961 coup and ruled until his assassination in 1979. The Democratic Party erupted in condemnation, accusing the opposition of sanitizing authoritarian rule and attempting to erase the brutal human rights violations of the Yushin era under the guise of celebrating economic modernization.

What to Watch Next

South Korea's political landscape now enters an exceptionally volatile phase. As the dust settles on this round of legal and legislative battles, several concrete developments will determine the political trajectory in the months ahead:

  • The Supreme Court Appeal: Whether state prosecutors pursue an expedited final appeal against Mayor Oh Se-hoon will determine how quickly the People Power Party can solidify its leadership race and present an organized alternative to the Lee Jae-myung administration.
  • Budget Appropriations Deadlines: The battle over the 162 trillion won Future Response Fund must transition from rhetorical attacks to actual budget reconciliation. Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party makes concessions on debt repayment rules to avert a legislative stalemate before the statutory budget deadline.
  • The Mega-Regional Compromise: Keep a close eye on the Democratic Party's internal debate regarding the 52-hour workweek cap in regional special economic zones. If a compromise is struck with corporate lobbies, it will signal a notable pro-growth policy pivot for the Lee administration.
  • The Merz-Lee Summit (October 29): Concrete announcements regarding defense procurement, critical minerals, and strategic industrial supply chain compacts between Seoul and Berlin will reveal how effectively the administration is translating its domestic political rebound into geopolitical leverage.

South Korean democracy was forged through immense sacrifice by leaders who treated political opponents as rivals to be persuaded, not enemies to be destroyed by prosecutors. Until Yeouido's lawmakers reclaim the responsibility of legislating and negotiating rather than outsourcing their decisions to courtrooms, the systemic gridlock gripping the country will remain unresolved.
