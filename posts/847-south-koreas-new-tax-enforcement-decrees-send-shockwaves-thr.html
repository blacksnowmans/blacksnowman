South Korea's New Tax Enforcement Decrees Send Shockwaves Through Property Markets

October 09, 2026

For South Korea's real estate owners, watching tax policies change is like walking on a tightrope. Every time a new regulation drops, multi-home owners lose sleep wondering what has shifted beneath their feet. Recently, the government introduced a package of four enforcement decree amendments, including changes to the Income Tax Act. According to tax accountant Lee Jang-won, these updates go far beyond tweaking a few figures—they carry enough explosive force to fundamentally alter the trajectory of South Korea's housing market.

Critics are already accusing policymakers of flip-flopping, arguing that policy consistency has become a luxury. But what exactly has changed to put the market on edge? The core issues involve shortening the grace period for temporary two-home owners to qualify for capital gains tax exemptions, and setting strict deadlines for waiving heavy capital gains taxes on registered rental apartments in regulated zones. This analysis breaks down the anatomy of these new tax rules to help property owners map out their next moves.

Shrinking Timelines for Temporary Two-Home Owners

The decision to slash the grace period for temporary two-home owners in regulated zones from three years down to two has dealt a major blow to the market. Initially, the three-year rule was maintained under the rationale that because overall housing supply was restricted, owners needed time to sell existing properties. With this amendment, tax authorities are sending a blunt message: sell quickly if you want your tax exemption. Lee Jang-won warns that many owners are overlooking the true impact of this shortened timeline and risk facing severe financial penalties later.

The devil is in the details regarding eligibility. The two-year restriction applies strictly when a new home is acquired on or after August 4, 2026, and the previous home is sold on or after October 1, 2026. The choice of August 4 stems from the tax revision announcement made the day prior, on August 3. By defying market expectations and pressing for expedited sales, the government is deliberately narrowing the escape routes for multi-home owners.

The ripple effects extend far beyond capital gains tax. Under property holding taxes like the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax, the privileges that allowed a two-home owner to be treated as a single-home owner—granting a basic tax deduction of 1.2 billion won (about $870,000) alongside elderly and long-term holding deductions—will also be compressed into a two-year window. Because these holding taxes are locked in annually on June 1, tax burdens are projected to surge starting from the June 1, 2027 assessment date. The state is effectively chasing dual goals: capturing tax revenue while forcing inventory onto the market.

The Supreme Court Warning on Three-Home Status

Parallel to these amendments, property owners must heed a critical Supreme Court precedent (Case No. 2024Du55426). Many individuals rely on the temporary two-home exemption while overlooking the rule that if they already held two homes *at the time* they purchased a new property, the exemption does not apply. Upholding the principle of strict legality in tax law, the court ruled that exemptions only apply when a buyer owns strictly one home when acquiring a replacement property.

In the past, many buyers blithely expanded portfolios to three or four homes under the assumption that simply selling an older property would suffice. No longer. The number of homes owned at the exact moment of acquiring a new property dictates tax fate. Expecting an exemption simply because selling one home trimmed a portfolio down to two from three is a dangerous gamble. The courts and the administration are sending an aligned message: multi-home owners must clear out their holdings.

Setting Hard Deadlines for Rental Apartments

The centerpiece of the new decrees is the introduction of expiration dates for heavy capital gains tax exemptions on registered rental apartments in regulated zones. Back when Kim Hyun-mi served as Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, the government dangled carrots to encourage multi-home owners to register as official landlords, promising exemptions from heavy capital gains taxes in return. Today, those carrots have effectively turned into timebombs. Lee Jang-won notes that while an extension through 2027 provides a brief reprieve, it may be the last one property owners see.

Many housing providers locked their assets away for eight years while capping rent increases at 5%, fulfilling what they were told was an important public service. Market conditions have shifted, however, and the government is signaling that extensions are finished. This restriction specifically targets apartments, inflicting pain distinct from owners of villas or non-apartment housing. For landlords who watched apartment values soar while their rental income remained legally capped, the bitter frustration is palpable.

A widespread hazard lies in misinterpreting the sunset clauses. Many operators mistakenly assume that if their mandatory eight-year rental period ends on December 15, 2027, they automatically get an extra year on top of that to sell. In reality, authorities mandate that while the property must remain a registered rental until the deadline, it must be sold *within one year after* the deadline expires to secure the special tax exemption. Missing this window causes years of accumulated tax benefits to vanish like a mirage.

Double Jeopardies of Holding Taxes and Land Transaction Permits

Completing the mandatory eight-year rental term does not end an owner's troubles. Once the comprehensive real estate holding tax exemption expires, annual tax bills can skyrocket—turning a manageable 10 million won (about $7,300) into 100 million won (about $73,000) almost overnight. The moment June 1 passes without a sale, unsold apartments return as crushing liabilities for multi-home owners.

Furthermore, designated land transaction permit zones complicate matters further. If a tenant occupies the property, buyers are legally required to move in and reside there within four months, rendering many transactions practically impossible. If a tenant refuses to vacate, owners cannot sell and are left to absorb the full brunt of the tax bill. Because rental housing regulations are exceptionally intricate, property owners should consult a certified tax professional at least a month in advance.

What to Watch Next

Property owners must shift from trusting official policy announcements to calculating the cold, hard numbers beneath them: every tax break now has a firm expiration date, and every regulatory framework carries hidden tripwires.

As South Korea's real estate framework continues to tighten around multi-home portfolios, property holders must monitor several concrete developments:

  • The June 1, 2027 Tax Assessment Date: This is the critical juncture when the compressed two-year holding tax exemptions and revised capital gains rules begin hitting taxpayers' wallets directly. Monitor your property count and sales timelines against this date.
  • Local Government Land Transaction Permits: Watch for changes in designated permit zones, as tenant residency requirements will dictate whether you can physically complete a sale before tax deadlines arrive.
  • Automatic Rental Registration Expirations: If you hold terminated rental units, verify your cancellation certificates immediately. You must sell your primary residence within five years of a registration's revocation date to claim exemptions.
  • Professional Consultations: Given the rapid shifts in regulatory interpretation, verify your exact property acquisition dates and sales windows with a qualified tax accountant rather than relying on generalized market assumptions.
