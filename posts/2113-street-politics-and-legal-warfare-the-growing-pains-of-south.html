Street Politics and Legal Warfare: The Growing Pains of South Korea’s Post-Impeachment Democracy

November 08, 2026

On November 7, the area surrounding Gyeongbokgung Station in central Seoul—a stone's throw from the halls of power—transformed into a sea of red. Supporters of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, filled the streets with banners and rhythmic chants. The air was thick with the kind of polarized energy that has come to define Korean politics in the post-impeachment era. This wasn't a standard political rally; it was a "large-scale outdoor struggle," a desperate pivot by an opposition party that has found itself increasingly marginalized within the walls of the National Assembly.

The catalyst for this sudden eruption into the streets was the passage of the "Special Counsel Act on Fabricated Indictments." To the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the act is a necessary scalpel to excise the rot of political maneuvering that characterized the previous administration. To the PPP, it is a "Prison Break Law" designed to insulate President Lee Jae-myung from his own legal history. As the legislative gates closed on their ability to block the bill, the conservatives took to the asphalt, signaling a breakdown in the deliberative process of the republic. For the outside observer, this moment captures the precarious state of Korean democracy: a landscape where the rule of law is a battlefield, and the "plaza"—the street—has become the court of last resort.

The Battle of the "Prison Break Law" vs. Judicial Reform

At the heart of the current deadlock is a fundamental disagreement over the history of the Korean prosecution. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence, the prosecution was often accused by critics of becoming a political weapon. The Democratic Party argues that many of the indictments brought against their members during that era—including those against President Lee Jae-myung—were "fabricated" or "targeted" to silence political rivals.

The "Special Counsel Act on Fabricated Indictments" aims to investigate these claims and potentially vacate past charges. DPK leadership maintains that this is about restoring the integrity of the judiciary. "This is a legitimate legislative effort to uncover the truth behind the targeted investigations and fabricated indictments of the political prosecution under the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol," a DPK spokesperson stated. They argue that without a thorough cleaning of the "prosecutorial dictatorship" of the past, the country cannot move forward.

The People Power Party, however, views this as an existential threat to the constitutional order. Jang Dong-hyeok, the leader of the PPP, stood before the crowds at Gyeongbokgung and declared that President Lee Jae-myung was "no longer the president of the Republic of Korea" in their eyes. He framed the Special Counsel Act as a "coup" against the parliament. By calling it a "Prison Break Law," the PPP suggests the ruling party is using its majority to rewrite history and erase the legal records of its leaders. This rhetoric—calling for the impeachment of a sitting president who was elected after the previous one was removed—illustrates how the cycle of political retribution in Seoul has reached a fever pitch.

Asphalt Politics and the Loss of Parliamentary Dialogue

In South Korea, "asphalt politics" refers to the practice of taking political grievances directly to the streets, often involving highly charged, emotional, and sometimes extremist rhetoric. While street protests have a storied history in Korea’s democratization, their current usage by the main opposition party suggests a worrying inability to find compromise within the National Assembly, the country's 300-seat unicameral legislature.

The scene on November 7 was a textbook example. PPP Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik characterized the passage of the bill as an "act of internal rebellion" and an "assembly coup." By using such militaristic language, the opposition is moving away from policy-based criticism and toward a narrative of total war. When the largest opposition party abandons the floor of the Assembly to scream for impeachment on the streets, it signals to the public that the institutional guardrails of democracy are no longer functioning.

This shift to the streets is often a symptom of "dopamine politics"—the need to feed the most fervent, angry supporters with constant escalation to maintain their engagement. For the PPP, this strategy carries significant risk. While it solidifies their base, it alienates the moderate voters who are more concerned with the slowing economy and the rising cost of living than with relitigating the prosecutorial scandals of the previous decade. By focusing on "impeachment slogans" rather than "livelihood legislation," the opposition risks becoming a party of the plaza rather than a party of the people.

Internal Fractures and the Shadow of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol

Despite the outward show of unity on the protest stages, the People Power Party is a house divided. The shadow of the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol continues to loom large over the conservative movement. Within the party, a "reformist" wing is attempting to distance the PPP from the legacy of the Yoon era, which was marked by the constitutional crisis that led to his impeachment. During a recent general meeting of party members, a resolution was adopted opposing any calls for a "return" of the Yoon-era political style.

Political analysts, such as Seo Jeong-uk, have noted that the current alliance between PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok and influential figures like Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon is a "time bomb." This alliance is largely strategic, born out of a shared need to oppose President Lee, but it lacks a unified vision for the party's future. The internal struggle is essentially a battle for the soul of Korean conservatism: should the party remain tied to the hardline "prosecutorial" identity of the past, or should it transform into a modern, policy-oriented conservative force?

This identity crisis is further complicated by the "fake news" ecosystem that surrounds Korean politics. A prominent example is the legal battle involving Lee Jun-seok, the head of the New Reform Party and a former PPP leader. Lee has recently moved to press charges against a popular history instructor, Jeon Han-gil, who repeatedly accused Lee of forging his academic credentials. These types of conspiracy theories—often birthed on extreme right-wing YouTube channels—frequently migrate into mainstream political discourse, forcing leaders to spend more time in courtrooms than in committee meetings. This "polluted public square" makes it nearly impossible to have a rational debate about the Special Counsel Act or any other major policy.

A Global Echo: The Retreat of Far-Right Populism

The drama in Seoul does not exist in a vacuum. On the same day the PPP was rallying in the streets, global news cycles were reporting on the shifting fortunes of far-right movements in the West. The recent midterm setbacks for Donald Trump in the United States have sent ripples through the international populist network. In the UK, Nigel Farage of Reform UK is facing a more complicated political calculus, and in France, Marine Le Pen’s National Rally is carefully recalibrating its messaging to focus more on youth anger and economic anxiety rather than pure ideological extremism.

In Germany, the far-right AfD (Alternative for Germany) has been rocked by a scandal involving leaked emails that suggest links to neo-Nazi sympathizers and illegal funding. This has led some German officials to consider pursuing a constitutional ban on the party—a move that mirrors the intense "constitutional defense" arguments being made in Korea.

The lesson for Korea is that while populism and street-level agitation can provide a temporary boost in polls, they often lead to a dead end. When these movements fail to deliver actual governance or when they are caught undermining the democratic system itself, the backlash from the "silent majority" can be severe. The PPP’s current strategy of "asphalt politics" mirrors the tactics of global populist movements that have recently seen their momentum stall. If the Korean opposition continues to rely on the "dopamine" of the protest rather than the hard work of legislative compromise, they may find themselves following the same downward trajectory as their counterparts in the West.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward the end of the year, the tension between the ruling party’s legislative agenda and the opposition’s street-level resistance will likely heighten. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Veto Power: Watch how President Lee Jae-myung handles any potential pushback from the judiciary or the remnants of the conservative establishment. While the Special Counsel Act has passed, its implementation will be a logistical and political nightmare, likely leading to more court challenges.
  • The PPP’s Leadership Stability: The "time bomb" alliance between the PPP leadership and Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon will be tested as the party prepares for upcoming local and regional contests. If the street protests fail to move the needle on public opinion, expect the internal "reformists" to make a move to oust the hardliners.
  • The Prosecution’s Reaction: The Special Counsel Act is a direct attack on the institutional power of the state prosecution. How the rank-and-file prosecutors react—whether through quiet resistance or public protest—will determine if the "prosecution reform" actually takes root or simply creates a new layer of bureaucracy.
  • The Battle Against "Fake News": The outcome of Lee Jun-seok’s legal battle against academic forgery claims will serve as a bellwether for whether the Korean legal system can effectively deter the conspiracy-driven "asphalt" influencers who currently hold significant sway over the political base.

Ultimately, the events of November 7 remind us that democracy is not a spectator sport. When the halls of parliament go silent and the streets grow loud, the responsibility falls on the citizens to look past the slogans and demand a return to the governance of facts and fairness. The "Special Counsel Act" might be about the past, but the way Korea handles the fallout will define its future.
