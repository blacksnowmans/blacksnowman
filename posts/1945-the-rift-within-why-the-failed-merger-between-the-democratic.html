The Rift Within: Why the Failed Merger Between the Democratic Party and Rebuilding Korea Party Matters

November 04, 2026

Imagine a high-stakes strategy meeting at the Blue House, where the atmosphere is thick with the scent of traditional tea and the heavy weight of political legacy. President Lee Jae-myung, the current leader of South Korea and a figure who has long advocated for a unified liberal front, sits across from the elders of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation’s ruling liberal party. The goal is simple: consolidation. By merging with the Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP)—the smaller, fiercely loyalist liberal party founded by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk—the President aims to create a legislative juggernaut capable of pushing through his reform agenda without the friction of minor-party negotiations.

However, outside that room, the plan is falling apart. The man responsible for executing this vision, DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok, is being accused of something rare in the high-stakes world of Seoul politics: "political illiteracy." Critics within the party and the broader liberal base are lamenting that President Lee has been "left looking like a fool" by his own party leader. What began as a strategic move to solidify the ruling coalition has devolved into a bitter internal feud involving allegations of data manipulation, factional sabotage, and even the bizarre invocation of religious cults to stir fear.

To the outside observer, this might look like standard partisan bickering. But in the context of the Lee Jae-myung administration, which rose to power following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the stakes are existential. If the ruling party cannot maintain internal discipline, its ability to govern a polarized nation and counter the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, will be severely compromised.

The Mechanics of a Political Betrayal

The controversy began in earnest on January 22, when Representative Jung Chung-rae officially proposed a merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party. At the time, this was seen as a direct extension of President Lee’s long-standing philosophy of "Great Liberal Unity." The logic was sound: the RKP shares much of the DPK’s DNA, and a formal union would streamline the voting bloc in the National Assembly.

Yet, the momentum was almost immediately strangled by the DPK leadership. Chairman Kim Min-seok and a tight circle of associates—including lawmakers Kang Deuk-gu, Hwang Myeong-seon, and Lee Yeon-ju—allegedly moved to block any democratic consensus. Rather than putting the merger to a vote of the entire party membership or conducting transparent public opinion surveys, this leadership group is accused of steering the party into a "minority rule" structure. By controlling the agenda of the party convention and stifling the voices of rank-and-file members, they effectively vetoed the President’s policy from within.

The core of the criticism against Chairman Kim is that he failed to distinguish between a "proposal" and a "notification." In high-level politics, a merger is a delicate dance of negotiation. By treating it as a unilateral decree or, conversely, as an optional suggestion that could be ignored, Kim displayed a lack of the "political sense" required to lead a ruling party. While President Lee was hosting dinners with five-term veteran lawmakers to reaffirm the necessity of the merger, his party leader was reportedly looking the other way. This isn't just a difference of opinion; it is being framed as a direct defiance of the President’s governing philosophy.

A War of Numbers: How Polls Were Weaponized

To justify their opposition to the merger, Kim’s faction relied on a specific narrative: that the public simply didn't want it. Lee Yeon-ju, a prominent figure in Kim’s circle, argued before other lawmakers that the merger was a "losing card" for upcoming local elections, citing polls that showed high opposition.

However, a closer look at the data reveals a more cynical strategy. According to surveys commissioned by Hankook Ilbo and the polling firm STI, support for the merger was actually overwhelmingly high among the core supporters of both the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party. In one poll, approval among RKP supporters reached 64%.

The "opposition" that Kim’s faction cited was reportedly manufactured by mixing in the opinions of People Power Party supporters. Naturally, voters for the conservative opposition would oppose a move that makes the liberal ruling party stronger. By using the disapproval of their political rivals to justify blocking the will of their own party members, Kim’s leadership engaged in what many are calling a "political fraud." This manipulation of statistics didn't just mislead the public; it disenfranchised the party’s own base, who saw the merger as the best path forward for the Lee administration.

The fallout from this has been reflected in Kim’s own approval ratings. Currently, Kim Min-seok’s job performance is being rated lower than even some figures in the opposition PPP. More tellingly, there is not a single region in the country—not even the liberal stronghold of the Jeolla provinces (Honam)—where positive evaluations of his leadership outweigh the negative. When a Democratic Party leader loses the trust of Honam, they are effectively a "dead man walking" in political terms.

The Spectre of Shincheonji and Internal McCarthyism

Perhaps the most bizarre turn in this saga is the emergence of what critics call "internal McCarthyism." In an apparent attempt to further delegitimize the pro-merger faction and the party members calling for reform, Chairman Kim and his allies raised the specter of Shincheonji.

Shincheonji is a controversial religious organization in Korea, often described as a cult, which gained international notoriety during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. In Korean politics, accusing a group of being "infiltrated by Shincheonji" is a common, if desperate, tactic to trigger public revulsion. Kim’s leadership claimed there was a "secret tripartite alliance" involving the religious group, though the numbers they cited shifted wildly—from 30,000 members to 50,000, then down to 10,000.

This tactic draws a direct parallel to the era of Joseph McCarthy in the 1950s United States. Just as McCarthy waved a "list of communists" that he never actually produced, Kim’s faction has been pressured by fellow Supreme Council members to provide evidence of this religious infiltration. To date, none has been forthcoming.

Beyond the lack of evidence, there is a legal minefield here. If the party leadership actually did cross-reference the private membership lists of the DPK with the membership lists of a religious organization without consent, they would be in flagrant violation of the Personal Information Protection Act. In their zeal to maintain factional control, the leadership may have discarded both political ethics and legal boundaries.

The Widening Chasm within the Ruling Party

Why does this internal squabble matter to someone outside of Korea? Because the Democratic Party is not just a political organization; it is the engine of the current government. President Lee Jae-myung’s mandate is built on the promise of decisive action—reform of the prosecution, economic democratization, and a firm stance on national sovereignty.

When the party leader is at odds with the President, the entire legislative process grinds to a halt. The Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Cho Kuk, has seen its support rise as voters grow frustrated with the DPK’s internal paralysis. This creates a "split-screen" effect: a President trying to lead a nation, and a party leader trying to protect a faction.

The media is no longer shielding Kim. While some conservative outlets are watching with glee, the liberal press is beginning to treat his political career as being on "hospice care." The silence from many veteran reporters is not out of respect, but rather a calculation of when the eventual collapse will occur. The consensus is that Kim has missed his window to apologize or course-correct. By framing any criticism as "anti-party activity," he has alienated the very reformers the party needs to survive the next election cycle.

"Politics is the art of capturing both justification and practical interest. In this merger controversy, Chairman Kim Min-seok has managed to lose both."

The Democratic Party now faces a choice. It can continue to allow a "betrayal DNA" to dictate its internal movements—where local constituency interests and factional loyalty trump the national agenda—or it can return to its "innovation DNA." The latter would require a transparent investigation into the polling distortions and a genuine opening of the party’s decision-making processes to its members.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will be a litmus test for the stability of the Lee Jae-myung government. Here are the concrete developments to keep an eye on:

  • The Party Convention Fallout: Watch for a formal motion from the rank-and-file members or reformist lawmakers to demand Chairman Kim’s resignation. The pressure will likely peak if the DPK’s approval ratings continue to trail behind the PPP in major metropolitan areas.
  • The RKP’s Independence: If the merger remains dead, observe how the Rebuilding Korea Party positions itself. They may transition from a "friendly ally" to a "competitive alternative," potentially siphoning off more of the DPK’s core supporters before the next major elections.
  • Legal Challenges: The allegations regarding the mishandling of party member data to "investigate" religious ties could lead to formal complaints. If the prosecution (which remains a contentious body in Korean politics) opens an inquiry, it could provide a pretext for a leadership change.
  • Presidential Intervention: While President Lee typically avoids direct interference in party administration, the "manpower crisis" within the DPK may force his hand. Look for shifts in the Blue House staff or the appointment of "pro-Lee" veterans to key party positions to bypass Kim’s circle.

Ultimately, the strength of a democracy is measured by the health of its political parties. For the Democratic Party of Korea, the "Kim Min-seok problem" is a symptom of a larger struggle to define what it means to be a ruling party in a post-impeachment era. Whether they can reconcile the President’s vision with the party’s internal machinery will determine the success of the Lee Jae-myung presidency.
