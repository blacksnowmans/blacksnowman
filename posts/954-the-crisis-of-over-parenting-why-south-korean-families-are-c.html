The Crisis of Over-Parenting: Why South Korean Families Are Choosing "Interest Diets"

October 10, 2026

Walk into a playground or a modern apartment complex in Seoul, and you are likely to witness a scene of intense, high-stakes management. Parents are not merely watching their children; they are hyper-focused, hovering to prevent every minor stumble, mediating every social friction, and catering to every whim. Many of these parents describe themselves as physically and emotionally exhausted, often feeling that despite pouring every ounce of their energy into their children, the results are far from satisfying. It is a phenomenon that has become so pervasive that prominent pediatricians are now publicly urging parents to stop treating child-rearing as an all-consuming, life-erasing project.

Dr. Ha Jung-hoon, a well-known pediatrician in South Korea who has gained a massive following for his pragmatic, no-nonsense approach to child development, has become a vocal critic of this trend. His message is blunt: modern parents are suffering because they have fundamentally misunderstood the goal of parenting. By attempting to curate a frictionless life for their children, they are inadvertently creating what he calls "mental obesity"—a condition where a child, pampered with excessive love and constant attention, loses the ability to cope with the reality of a world that does not revolve around them.

The Illusion of Perpetual Devotion

In traditional Korean society, raising a child was a collective effort, but it was rarely the performative, high-pressure task it has become today. The current obsession with "excessive parenting" stems from a misplaced belief that every emotional need must be met instantly and that any sign of distress—a tantrum, a frustration, or a social failure—is a failure of the parent.

Dr. Ha argues that this constant interference is actually a form of unintentional harm. When a child is never allowed to feel the discomfort of waiting, the frustration of a task they cannot complete, or the sting of a "no," they never develop the internal muscles required for self-regulation. Instead, they come to view the world as a place that should cater to their desires. When these children eventually reach school age, the collision between their pampered expectations and the cold realities of the classroom can be devastating.

This dynamic has even been linked to the broader collapse of classroom authority in South Korea. The tragic and widely mourned case of a teacher at Seoul Seo-i Elementary School, who took her own life amid reports of harassment by overbearing parents, brought this issue to the national forefront. It highlighted a system where parents, having protected their children from all forms of consequence at home, view any attempt by an educator to discipline or guide their child as an affront. By shielding children from the concept of duty and responsibility, parents have inadvertently destroyed the very social structures necessary for a functioning school environment.

The "5% Rule" for Healthy Independence

To combat this, Dr. Ha advocates for what he calls the "5% rule": parents should strive to be present but deliberately leave a 5% gap in their child’s needs. By not fulfilling every single desire, parents create space for the child to experience minor frustrations, which are the essential building blocks of resilience and independence.

This approach requires parents to reclaim their own lives. In many Korean households, the child has become the absolute center of the universe—a concept that puts immense, unsustainable pressure on the parents and creates an unhealthy dynamic where the child is the "master" of the house. Dr. Ha emphasizes that children need to see their parents leading their own lives—pursuing hobbies, maintaining a social life, and holding their own ground. When a parent is a confident individual rather than a 24-hour servant, the child learns to respect that boundary and views the family as a unit that they must contribute to, rather than an entity that serves them.

This "interest diet" is not about neglect; it is about healthy detachment. It is the recognition that the role of a parent is not to guarantee a smooth life, but to prepare the child to navigate an uneven world.

Discipline as a Shield, Not a Weapon

A frequent pushback against this philosophy is that strict discipline or firm boundaries might leave emotional scars. However, Dr. Ha differentiates between healthy, consistent structure and harmful authoritarianism. He argues that children actually feel more secure when they understand the rules of their environment.

When a child learns that eating at the table is a rule, or that there are times when they must wait for their parents, they are learning "self-regulation." This is the foundational skill for adulthood. Without it, the child who becomes an adult will struggle with the basic responsibilities of life, whether it is managing personal finances, navigating workplace hierarchies, or dealing with the inevitable disappointments of professional life. The "hard work" of discipline in the early years is, in reality, a shield that protects the child from being blindsided by the demands of the adult world.

A Path Toward Sustainable Parenting

What should parents watch for next? The challenge lies in a cultural shift. South Korean parenting has been heavily influenced by a "keeping up with the neighbors" mentality, where education and protection are seen as competitive sports. Moving away from this requires a collective recognition that the current model is broken.

  • Observe the boundaries: Start by identifying one area where you are currently over-managing your child—such as homework or conflict resolution with friends—and step back.
  • Prioritize the couple: Re-center the marriage or the parent’s personal growth. A happy, fulfilled adult is a better role model than a martyr who has sacrificed their identity for their child.
  • Normalize "No": Understand that denying a request is not a failure of love, but a vital lesson in reality testing.

The transition from a "child-centered" home to an "adult-led" home is not an easy one, especially in a society that often equates parental martyrdom with virtue. However, for parents looking to reduce their stress and raise a child who is actually capable of functioning in the real world, the solution is clear: stop trying to be the perfect servant, and start being a parent who knows how to say no. When the parent is happy and independent, the child, by extension, gains the freedom to grow into their own person.
