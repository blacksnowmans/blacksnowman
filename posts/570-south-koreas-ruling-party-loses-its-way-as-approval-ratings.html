South Korea’s Ruling Party Loses Its Way as Approval Ratings Slide and Reform Stalls

September 10, 2026

For anyone watching South Korean politics closely, the current mood is one of profound frustration. The bold blueprints for reform and change that filled the air after the last presidential election now feel distant. Week after week, public opinion polls deliver the same bruising news: a widening gap between what President Lee Jae-myung says in public and what his administration actually delivers on the ground. When even the rules governing investigative authorities—the crown jewel of promised prosecutorial reforms—become bogged down in bureaucratic self-preservation, voters are left exhausted.

How did the administration’s ambitious agenda veer so far off course? Drawing on political commentary from recent media broadcasts, it is worth dissecting the painful realities currently facing South Korea’s political establishment and why this erosion of trust matters far beyond domestic headlines.

The Growing Chasm Between Presidential Rhetoric and Policy Reality

Back in January, President Lee Jae-myung signaled an unprecedented appetite for real estate reform. He targeted not just general housing market stabilization, but non-business corporate land holdings and agricultural plots, rolling out detailed and aggressive measures. For millions of South Koreans locked out of a notoriously brutal housing market, the rhetoric sparked a fragile hope that this time, things might actually be different.

By May, however, that fierce tone had visibly softened. Whether cowed by market resistance or sapped of internal momentum, the administration began to lose its footing. When a major tax package dropped in August, it contained some targeted heavy lifting for ultra-luxury homes in affluent Seoul districts like Gangnam and Seocho, alongside higher tax burdens for non-resident property owners. Yet analysts pointed out a glaring imbalance: the loopholes and tax-cut beneficiaries vastly outnumbered those targeted for heavy taxation. What was heralded as a war on real estate speculation turned out to be a half-measures compromise that disappointed the public while teaching wealthy investors exactly where to look for an escape hatch.

The final blow came in late August. The president loudly reaffirmed his commitment to tax justice, only for the policy to pull back just two days later. This cycle—soaring rhetoric that shakes the political landscape, followed by timid policy execution that retreats at the first sign of pushback—has become a debilitating habit. When a leader's words and actions chronically contradict one another, public trust dissolves.

Prosecutorial Reform and the Return of Investigative Power

Nowhere is the gap between reformist promises and institutional reality sharper than in the ongoing battle over investigative guidelines. A central pillar of the progressive platform has always been curbing the immense, historically unchecked power of South Korea’s prosecutors. Yet under the latest revisions to investigative guidelines—hammered out through inter-agency talks between the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, the police, and the coast guard—critics argue that prosecutors are quietly regaining their old supervisory leverage over law enforcement.

The structural irony runs deeper. The Ministry of Justice, an agency historically synonymous with the very prosecutorial establishment it is meant to oversee, continues to write these rules. Even under an administration pledged to overhaul the justice system, key posts remain occupied by figures steeped in the prosecutorial tradition. Despite the veneer of inter-agency cooperation, the legal technocrats have engineered a framework that preserves judicial control in the hands of the traditional elite, effectively blunting the very reforms voters demanded.

A Freefall in the Polls: 37.4% Approval and Counting

Numbers rarely lie, and the latest data from the RealMitor poll paints a grim picture. President Lee’s positive approval rating dropped for the eighth consecutive week, hitting 37.4%, while negative sentiment climbed to 59.5%. The net deficit now stands at a formidable 22 percentage points, marking a lowest-ever watermark for the administration.

Gallup Korea surveys reflect similar pain points. Public dissatisfaction is increasingly driven by personnel choices and persistent anxieties over the economy, livelihood issues, and interest rates, alongside ongoing disappointment with real estate policies. Even traditional supporters who delivered a sweeping legislative majority are retreating into a weary, watchful neutrality. Meanwhile, the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has seen its numbers tick upward—not necessarily because voters have fallen in love with the opposition, but because the ruling camp's stumbles have handed them a powerful rebound effect.

What to Watch Next

As the administration navigates the back half of its term, several critical indicators will determine whether it can arrest its decline or slide further into lame-duck territory:

  • Substantive Policy Shifts vs. Apologetics: Watch whether the presidential office abandons external scapegoating and begins addressing the structural contradictions in its tax and real estate rollouts.
  • The Implementation of Investigative Rules: Pay close attention to how the newly revised prosecutorial and police guidelines are actually enforced in high-profile cases, which will test whether institutional checks on prosecutors are real or illusory.
  • Party Dynamics and Legislative Momentum: With the gap between the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition narrowing into single digits, look for signs of whether the ruling bloc can maintain the cohesion necessary to pass core legislative agenda items.
  • Polling Trajectories Beyond the Margin of Error: Monitor whether the ongoing drop in presidential approval stabilizes or triggers deeper defections among core progressive demographics.

Politics remains an art defined by results. If an administration built on the promise of fundamental fairness fails to tangibly improve ordinary lives, eloquent speeches and defensive messaging will not save it. The coming months will reveal whether this administration has the stomach to stop blaming its critics and return to the difficult, painful work of genuine reform.
