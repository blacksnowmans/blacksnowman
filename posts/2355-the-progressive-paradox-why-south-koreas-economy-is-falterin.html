The Progressive Paradox: Why South Korea’s Economy is Faltering Under President Lee Jae-myung

November 15, 2026

The screens in Seoul’s financial district are flashing red, and the mood on the street is turning increasingly sour. For an administration that rose to power on the promise of being the "economically capable" choice, the current reality is a jarring wake-up call. South Korea’s benchmark stock index, the KOSPI, has faced brutal volatility, and the "livelihood economy"—the day-to-day financial reality for ordinary citizens—is in a state of visible decay. What was marketed as a new era of "Great Unity" and the "restraint of the strong to help the weak" has morphed into a crisis of confidence that threatens the very foundation of the current government.

This is not merely a natural downturn in the global business cycle. Critics and market analysts alike are beginning to describe the current economic malaise as a "man-made disaster," born from a toxic mix of policy incompetence and anachronistic decision-making. President Lee Jae-myung, representing the Democratic Party (DP)—South Korea’s current ruling party—now finds himself at a crossroads. By pivoting toward "growth-first" rhetoric—a logic traditionally associated with his conservative rivals—he risks alienating his core support base while failing to produce the results that would win over the center.

As the country looks toward the future, the stakes could not be higher. The structural failures of today are setting the stage for a potentially catastrophic 2027, where a global AI bubble burst could collide with a domestic asset deflation crisis. Understanding why this matters requires looking past the government’s glossy "Mega Project" announcements and into the widening gap between macroeconomic data and the lived experience of the Korean people.

The Ghost of Failed Reforms and the Identity Crisis of the Left

To understand the current political climate, one must recognize the weight of history on the Democratic Party. For many voters, the DP has shifted from being an "icon of reform" to a source of frustration. Recent polling suggests a hardening of hearts, with nearly half of the electorate expressing a firm refusal to support the party. This isn't a temporary fluctuation; it is a judgment on a perceived lack of competence and a perceived betrayal of progressive values.

The situation draws haunting parallels to the era of the late Roh Moo-hyun (President from 2003-2008). Roh, a progressive icon, saw his approval ratings collapse from 70% to below 30% following a credit card debt crisis and a perceived pivot toward neoliberal policies that abandoned his base. Today, President Lee Jae-myung appears to be walking a similar path. By embracing the "growth first" logic of the establishment to seek broader appeal, the administration has left the "structural majority"—the working class and small business owners—behind.

The political cost of this pivot is immense. The DP, once characterized by its internal diversity and grassroots energy, has become increasingly centralized under President Lee's singular leadership. In contrast to the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, which currently boasts multiple potential presidential contenders, the DP’s fate is inextricably tied to one man. If the economy fails, the party fails with him. This centralization has stifled internal criticism and led to a government that seems more focused on power struggles than on reading the shifting winds of the global macroeconomy. Without a course correction, analysts warn of another "lost decade" for the Korean left, echoing the period from 2008 to 2016 when conservatives held a firm grip on power following the impeachment-era chaos.

The Mirage of Growth: Falling Wages and Rising Inequality

While the government points to export figures to claim economic health, the internal data tells a story of "livelihood bankruptcy." For the average wage earner, the numbers are grim. During the final months of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration—the former conservative president who was impeached and is currently imprisoned—the average real monthly wage for laborers was approximately 4.068 million won (about $2,920). Under President Lee, that figure has actually ticked downward to 4.066 million won. When accounting for inflation, Korean workers are effectively taking a pay cut while the cost of living soars.

The gap between the haves and have-nots is widening at a record pace.

  • Irregular workers: The wage ratio for irregular workers (contract or part-time) compared to regular employees has dropped to 52.1%, the worst level on record.
  • SME Gap: The wage gap between employees at large conglomerates and those at Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) has widened by more than 2 percentage points, now sitting at 60.8%.
  • Youth Employment: For those aged 15 to 29, jobs are vanishing. While the Moon Jae-in administration saw modest growth in youth jobs, the current administration is overseeing a disappearance of roughly 179,000 youth positions per month.

This is the "Growth First" fallacy in action. The government argues that the "pie" must be enlarged before it can be shared, but the current data suggests the pie is only growing for those at the very top. Household consumption has remained stagnant for nearly a decade, with real spending in the third quarter of this year stuck at 2017 levels. When the bottom 50% of the population cannot afford to spend, the domestic economy withers, leading to a wave of small business closures and a surge in non-performing loans. The "trickle-down" effect, long touted by conservatives and now seemingly adopted by the Lee administration, has once again failed to materialize.

The Semiconductor Mirage and the "Jobless Growth" Trap

A centerpiece of President Lee’s economic strategy is the so-called "Mega Project," a massive 113 trillion won (approximately $81 billion) investment deal with Samsung Electronics. The President has framed this as a "Second Miracle on the Han River"—a reference to the rapid industrialization of the 1970s under Park Chung-hee. However, this comparison misses a fundamental shift in how modern economies function.

The original "Miracle" was built on heavy and chemical industries that required massive amounts of human labor, creating a "virtuous cycle" where industrial growth led directly to mass employment and rising wages. In contrast, the semiconductor industry is the poster child for "jobless growth." It is a capital-intensive industry, not a labor-intensive one. Modern semiconductor fabrication plants are marvels of automation; they require billions of dollars in machinery but relatively few workers. For every $700,000 invested today, the number of jobs created is a tiny fraction of what it was forty years ago.

Furthermore, the current boom in semiconductors is a "reflector" effect—a result of the global AI frenzy led by U.S. tech giants—rather than a direct result of domestic policy. When Samsung or SK Hynix (another major Korean chipmaker) see record profits, that wealth stays within a very small circle of high-income engineers and shareholders. It does not flow into the local neighborhood restaurants or the traditional markets. By betting the nation’s future almost entirely on this one sector, the government is neglecting the broader ecosystem of SMEs that actually employ the majority of Koreans.

Policy Blunders and the Crisis of Market Trust

The recent collapse of the KOSPI has highlighted a devastating lack of financial expertise within the administration. A comparison with Taiwan is instructive. When geopolitical tensions flared earlier this year, the Taiwanese stock market—also heavily dependent on semiconductors—dropped about 4.5%. The KOSPI, however, plummeted 17%. The difference? The Taiwanese government utilized its "National Financial Stabilization Fund" to actively support market demand and prevent panic. The Lee administration, by contrast, largely stood by, citing "market principles."

Perhaps the most criticized move was the introduction of "Single Stock Leverage ETFs" in May. In a market like South Korea’s, which is smaller and more prone to manipulation than New York’s, these high-risk instruments acted like gasoline on a fire. They amplified volatility, leading to massive sell-offs that wiped out retail investors. When questioned about this during a press conference, President Lee’s response—suggesting that "someone else" must have made the decision—sent shockwaves through the market.

"A government cannot stand without the trust of its people (Mu-sin-bul-lip). To have the President appear unaware of a major policy shift that triggered a market meltdown is the height of administrative incompetence."

This "knowledge elitism" is a recurring theme. Many government officials are accused of practicing "intellectual flunkyism"—blindly importing Wall Street theories and applying them to the unique Korean market without considering the structural differences. While U.S. markets are deep enough to absorb short-selling and high-frequency trading, the Korean market is easily swayed by foreign institutional moves, leaving individual "ants" (retail investors) to bear the brunt of the losses.

The Looming Shadow of 2027

The most harrowing prospect is what analysts call the "2027 AI Bubble Burst" scenario. Currently, global Big Tech firms are spending trillions on AI infrastructure, but the actual revenue generated from AI services remains significantly lower. If—or when—this bubble bursts, South Korea, as a primary hardware supplier, will be the first to feel the impact.

This potential global shock would hit a Korea that is already weakened by domestic asset deflation. As stock values drop, a "reverse wealth effect" takes hold: people who feel poorer spend less. This is already visible in the 3.65 million won ($2,600) average monthly household expenditure, which has seen a sharp decline. When the stock market fails, the real estate market—the primary vehicle for Korean household wealth—usually follows. With Seoul apartment transaction volumes already dipping, the stage is set for a synchronized collapse of asset prices and domestic consumption.

Meanwhile, the external environment is becoming hostile. China is facing its own "middle-income trap" and aging population crisis, exporting its economic risks outward. The "Third China Shock" is expected to target Korea’s high-tech manufacturing directly. Without a robust domestic safety net or a diversified economic base, South Korea is drifting toward a state of "Galapagos-ization"—isolated and vulnerable.

What to Watch Next

  • The Financial Stabilization Fund: Watch whether the government finally abandons its "hands-off" approach and activates state funds to prop up the KOSPI. Continued inaction will be a signal that retail investors are on their own.
  • SME Support Measures: Look for any shift in rhetoric away from "Mega Projects" toward direct support for small businesses and the self-employed, who are currently drowning in debt.
  • The 2028 General Election Strategy: As the DP prepares for future elections, the internal struggle between "Growth First" centrists and "Universal Basic Income" progressives will determine the party's—and the country's—economic direction.

For the Lee Jae-myung administration, the time for "economic presidential" slogans has passed. The public is no longer looking for grand visions; they are looking for a reason to believe that their real wages will stop falling and that the market they invested their life savings in is not a rigged game. If the government cannot provide that, the "lost decade" they fear may become an inevitable reality.
