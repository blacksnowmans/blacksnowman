How Generative AI is Breaking South Korea's Elite Classrooms—and Why the Struggle is the Point

October 09, 2026

Step onto a South Korean university campus today, and you can feel the quiet panic. In lecture halls from Seoul National University to Yonsei, professors stare out at rooms of students whose relationship with learning has been fundamentally fractured by generative artificial intelligence. Essay mills run around the clock, cheating scandals have led to bitter standoffs over zeroed-out midterm grades, and educators wonder out loud whether the country's relentless test-prep machine has finally produced a generation that cannot think for itself.

This anxiety is not just about academic dishonesty. It touches a deeper nerve in a society that built its post-war miracle on rote memorization, elite university credentials, and grueling cram schools. Now, an algorithm can write a better term paper than an exhausted undergraduate in seconds. So what, exactly, is a school for anymore?

In a widely discussed appearance on a YouTube interview series hosted by renowned evolutionary biologist Choi Jae-cheon, Cambridge philosopher of science Ha-Soo Chang offered a sharp diagnosis of this crisis. Moving past both tech utopianism and hollow panic, Chang argued that the crisis of AI in education is not a failure of technology, but a crisis of human experience.

The Death of the Lecture and the Robbing of Experience

The traditional image of a professor standing at a blackboard pouring knowledge into passive students is effectively dead. Chang didn't mince words during his interview, pointing out that even the most brilliant lecture, if it merely transmits information and ideas, is redundant—all of that is already in books, and now, it is instantly retrievable via AI.

While students often blame AI for the temptation to cheat, the deeper issue is that the utility of the knowledge-injection model of education has flatlined. When students outsource their critical thinking and problem-solving to large language models, they aren't just saving time; they are outsourcing the friction of learning.

Chang argues that this constitutes a true educational catastrophe. The greatest danger of over-reliance on AI is that it robs humans of necessary "experience." Just as early mathematics education required students to struggle through arithmetic before calculators could be useful, the AI era requires learners to grapple with foundational principles and messy processes. Staying up all night wrestling with a difficult problem is not an inefficient way to acquire information—that agonizing process *is* the moment genuine learning occurs.

Recent studies on undergraduate AI usage bear this out. When students rely on tools to summarize readings and smooth over logical gaps, the volume of mental training required to build a coherent argument plummets. Education is quietly shifting from a place where minds are built to a space where unique human experiences are quietly hollowed out.

When Cheating Becomes Systemic: The Evaluation Dilemma

This dynamic has thrown South Korea's hyper-competitive evaluation systems into chaos. At top-tier institutions, mass cheating scandals involving generative AI have forced professors to hand down mass failing grades, triggering fierce back-and-forth between students and faculty. At Cambridge, the question keeps administrators awake at night: How do you evaluate a student when everyone has access to superhuman intelligence?

Students feel the vertigo just as keenly. Without clear institutional norms on how much AI is permissible—and where the line is crossed—everyone is guessing. Some European universities have rolled back the clock, returning to handwritten blue-book exams and chalkboard sessions. Others, like the National University of Singapore, have embraced a different path, permitting AI use outright while requiring students to transparently disclose how they used it and what value they added on top of it.

Chang notes that because AI-generated text easily surpasses the quality of an average undergraduate paper, professors can no longer gauge a student's actual competence through final texts alone. Instead, evaluation must shift to what the student *added* to the machine's output. Yet South Korea's broader educational machinery remains stuck in the past, grading students with yardsticks built for a pre-AI world.

The History of Science as a Cure for Certainty

To understand how to fix this, Chang suggests looking backward—specifically at the history of science, a field where he has spent decades exposing how shaky our "common sense" truly is. He points out, for instance, that long before J.J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897, the battery had already been invented nearly a century earlier by Alessandro Volta in 1800. For decades, scientists argued bitterly over how batteries actually worked, operating without any concept of electrons and relying on intuitive, physical metaphors like fluid and heat flow. Volta himself thought of voltage in terms of water or air pressure—a linguistic ghost that survives in the word "voltage" today.

Science is not a neat sequence of settled truths handed down from a textbook; it is a messy history of false starts, weird analogies, and conceptual confusion. Chang’s specialty is taking what looks like self-evident common sense and making it strange, forcing students to realize that they actually understand very little.

This stands in stark contrast to modern science education, where school laboratories are engineered so that every experiment has a pre-determined, correct outcome. When students get a different result, they usually blame the equipment and fudge their lab reports. But real science—and real learning—begins when you do not know the answer, stumbling through a maze with no map.

Consider Michael Faraday, the 19th-century British experimental physicist who discovered electromagnetic induction by playing around with magnets and coils like a child with toys. He did not start with a clean theoretical framework; he blundered into rules by wrestling with messy, unpolished physical realities. Today, research on AI-assisted learning consistently shows that language models improve critical thinking only when students use them to fuel debates and generate counter-arguments, rather than to swallow neat conclusions whole.

LLMs as Statistical Mimics, Not Minds

We marvel at what large language models can produce, but we must be clear-eyed about what they actually are. AI generates answers that look like absolute truth because it is fundamentally a statistical mimic—a machine trained to calculate the "average" of all human text accumulated up to this point.

Chang illustrates this with a personal anecdote about his failed attempts to learn tennis. Despite taking lessons in the United States and trying three separate times, he never mastered the sport well enough to consistently hit a decent serve. Yet precisely because he sweated and flailed on the court himself, he can watch Roger Federer or Novak Djokovic play and comprehend, on a visceral level, just how far beyond normal human capability their mastery truly is.

Someone who has never swung a racket will never truly understand the magnitude of an athlete's skill. Similarly, no matter how well an AI plays Go or generates gorgeous images, it cannot replace the human act of placing a stone, feeling the weight of the match, and experiencing genuine accomplishment. Skipping the process to steal the result erodes the foundation of what makes human endeavor meaningful.

How, then, should we treat AI? Chang suggests viewing it as a capable research assistant. No professor would ever take a brilliant young assistant's draft and publish it without checking the work, simply because the assistant is smart. The final judgment and the ultimate responsibility always rest with the human who commissioned the work.

University freshmen today are digital natives who often understand AI tools far better than the faculty teaching them. Simply banning the technology is a losing game. Instead, educators must redesign courses around the friction of close reading and hard thinking—forcing students to sit with difficult texts the way philosophers once wrestled with Kant, using AI as a tool to interrogate ideas rather than an oracle that delivers final answers.

Beyond the Exam Hell: Reclaiming the Meaning of Life

South Korea’s public education system remains locked in a high-stakes race toward a single finish line: university entrance exams. It is a system so grueling that dropouts are common and schools frequently fail to teach basic social and emotional resilience. In a phase of life where teenagers should be asking fundamental questions about how to live—as ancient sages urged—they are instead treated as processors for multiple-choice optimization.

South Korea’s government pours massive sums into training AI talent, viewing technology as an economic engine for global competitiveness. But if the humans wielding that technology lack a foundational philosophy and an internal compass, the society they build will be hollow. When an AI can instantly supply every correct answer, the question "Why must we study?" inevitably collapses into the deeper, human question: "Why must we live?"

You do not need to out-calculate an algorithm for your life to have meaning. The clumsy, imperfect effort of struggling through a task yourself—even if a machine could do it faster—is where human value resides. The shockwave of generative AI is an uncomfortable wake-up call, but it is also a golden opportunity to tear down and rebuild our schools.

As elite universities abroad experiment with dropping traditional majors in favor of intensive reading of classics and hands-on recreation of historical scientific experiments, they are rediscovering a profound truth: touching the raw, messy history of discovery creates a reverence that rote memorization can never match.

We must not let ourselves become passive riders on an AI-driven conveyor belt. The friction of growth—failing to serve a tennis ball, constructing a flawed scientific hypothesis, sitting in a classroom and wrestling with a difficult sentence—is the only armor that keeps us human. Algorithms may hold every answer, but they can never steal the mess of the journey.

  • Shift from transmission to experience: Rote memorization and standard lectures are dead; education must focus on the friction of genuine problem-solving.
  • Recognize LLMs as statistical echoes: AI models calculate averages of past human labor; they cannot replicate true human agency or bear moral responsibility.
  • Embrace the "assistant" model: Treat AI as a capable research aide, not an oracle, keeping humans firmly on the hook for final judgments and critical interpretation.
