The Myth of Pragmatism: Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Liberal Base Is Unraveling

November 01, 2026

When Lee Jae-myung stepped into the presidential office in Yongsan, the political stars appeared to aligned almost impossibly in his favor. His predecessor, Yoon Suk-yeol, had been impeached and removed from office following an extraordinary crisis over an attempted insurrection. The conservative opposition, the People Power Party, was left shattered, branded by the public as an accomplice to a disgraced regime. For South Korea’s center-left Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), it seemed to be a moment of total political dominance—a chance to reshape the nation’s economy, curb entrenched interests, and lock in a lasting progressive majority.

Yet today, President Lee finds his approval ratings stuck in a persistent rut, while the broader reform coalition that brought him to power faces a growing crisis of confidence. Instead of seizing a commanding lead, the administration is grappling with deep internal friction and the erosion of its core constituency. To understand how South Korea’s ruling party arrived at this impasse, one must look at two critical misjudgments made early in Lee’s term: a misreading of his election victory and a dangerous illusion sparked by a global stock market boom.

The Illusion of 49.42 Percent and the Warning from the Working Class

The roots of the current political dilemma trace back to the ballot box. In the March 2022 presidential election, Lee Jae-myung narrowly lost to Yoon Suk-yeol by just 0.74 percentage points, capturing 47.83 percent of the vote. Following Yoon’s dramatic removal from office on April 3, Lee got his second chance under conditions that could not have been more favorable. The conservative establishment was completely discredited, and the liberal camp seemed poised for a landslide.

Yet when the votes were counted, Lee secured 49.42 percent. While higher than his 2022 tally by 1.59 percentage points, it fell short of a clear majority. Strikingly, Lee’s vote share was slightly lower than the combined total of the main conservative candidates, Kim Moon-soo and Lee Jun-seok, who together garnered 49.49 percent. Even with the political field tilted entirely in his favor, more than half of the electorate declined to endorse him.

Inside the presidential office and the DPK leadership, this outcome triggered a fundamental miscalculation. Advisers concluded that the liberal coalition alone could never secure long-term political stability or win future elections. They believed the administration’s survival depended on pivoting to the right to court moderate and center-right voters. However, this interpretation misread the structural dynamics of South Korea’s electorate.

A look at modern Korean political history reveals a consistent pattern: liberal presidents, including Moon Jae-in (who won with 41 percent in a crowded five-way race in 2017), have rarely commanded absolute majority support at the ballot box. This is not merely an electoral anomaly; it reflects a deep-seated skepticism among working-class voters, low-income households, and daily wage earners. Over two decades of alternating power between conservatives and liberals, the economic reality for average workers has remained stubbornly difficult. Wealth inequality has widened, real estate prices have soared out of reach, and corporate conglomerates retain a firm grip on the market.

By interpreting the 49.42 percent finish as a mandate to appease moderate conservatives rather than a warning to deliver tangible structural reforms for working families, the Lee administration made its first strategic misstep.

The 49.42 percent tally was not an invitation to abandon the party’s base—it was a cold warning that working-class voters were tired of compromise without results.

The Stock Market Mirage and the Perils of "Pragmatism"

Early in Lee’s presidency, an external windfall masked these political vulnerabilities. A global surge in demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure triggered a massive semiconductor boom, lifting South Korean tech heavyweights. Foreign capital poured into the local exchange, propelling the benchmark KOSPI index up by 60 to 70 percent.

International commentators noted that the Lee government had stepped into office at an exceptionally lucky moment. Yet within the administration, this market rally was interpreted not as a lucky global macro cycle, but as proof that Lee’s self-styled "pragmatic governance" was working. Aides convinced themselves that market gains were attracting moderate voters into the DPK’s fold.

This economic illusion had direct policy consequences. To keep the stock rally going and avoid unsettling corporate leaders, the administration began quietly deferring core progressive promises. Key pledges—such as reining in the powerful state prosecution service, tackling unfair market practices by family-controlled business groups (chabol), and strengthening labor protections—were sidelined under the rationale that aggressive reform might spark market instability.

The leadership operated under the assumption that its core progressive base had nowhere else to go. But as promised structural reforms slowed to a crawl, core supporters felt sidelined. The strategy of broadening the tent by taking the base for granted left the administration's political foundations increasingly fragile.

Identity Crisis: Abandoning Progressive Roots for Divisive Populism

Lee Jae-myung’s rise from a regional mayor to a presidential contender was built on a distinct political identity summarized by two core catchphrases: Eokgang Buyak ("restraining the powerful and assisting the weak") and Daedong Sesang ("a world of grand harmony and shared prosperity"). His appeal lay in his image as a resolute champion willing to challenge entrenched privileges on behalf of ordinary citizens.

That posture stands in sharp contrast to the administration's recent direction. Around his 500th day in office, President Lee sparked controversy within his own camp by publicly stating, "We cannot win reelection just by rallying among ourselves." To many long-time supporters, this sounded like an explicit pivot away from the reform agenda that brought him to office.

As economic tailwinds began to cool and polling numbers stalled, the administration’s rhetoric took a sharper turn. To bolster falling numbers, figures within the ruling bloc have increasingly embraced divisive, populist framing—at times leaning into anti-immigrant sentiment and criticizing multicultural initiatives.

For South Korea’s progressive movement, which has historically centered its platform on human rights, labor solidarity, and social equity—legacies associated with figures like former President Roh Moo-hyun and the late progressive lawmaker Roh Hoe-chan—this shift represents a troubling turn. Attempting to shore up poll numbers through divisive rhetoric risks alienating the core reform coalition entirely.

Chasing Youth Votes on the Trading Floor

A central pillar of the administration's current strategy has been an explicit attempt to win over voters in their 20s and 30s by focusing heavily on stock market performance. With traditional paths to wealth accumulation like homeownership becoming increasingly difficult, retail investing has emerged as a major political priority for younger South Koreans. The government has prioritized stock market stimulus in hopes of converting index gains into political loyalty.

However, this index-centric focus comes at a cost. Middle-aged workers in their 40s and 50s—who form the backbone of South Korea's industrial workforce—face mounting job insecurity, rising debt burdens, and volatile asset prices, yet find their structural challenges largely unaddressed by market-focused measures. Fundamental policy priorities, such as employment stability, working-hour regulations, and social safety nets, have taken a back seat to short-term market indicators.

Relying on equity market performance as a primary source of political legitimacy is inherently risky. Stock rallies driven by global tech cycles can reverse quickly when international demand shifts. If the market cools while underlying economic pressures remain unaddressed, the administration risks losing both retail investors and its traditional working-class base.

What to Watch Next

As President Lee Jae-myung approaches the midpoint of his term, the Democratic Party of Korea stands at a critical juncture. Whether the administration can stabilize its approval ratings and unify its coalition depends on several key developments in the coming months:

  • Legislative Pivot on Structural Reform: Watch whether the DPK uses its parliamentary majority to push forward long-delayed structural reform bills—such as prosecution overhauls and labor legislation—or continues to prioritize market-friendly, right-leaning compromise.
  • The Durability of the Tech Cycle: Monitor South Korea's semiconductor export figures and the KOSPI index. A downturn in global tech demand would test whether the administration has a viable economic platform beyond equity market performance.
  • Internal Friction Within the Liberal Camp: Pay attention to growing pushback from progressive civic groups, labor unions, and party traditionalists who are questioning the administration's shift away from its core agenda.
  • Populist Rhetoric and Electoral Strategy: Track whether the ruling party doubles down on nationalist and divisive messaging to maintain poll numbers ahead of upcoming local elections, or attempts to rebuild a unified coalition grounded in economic reform.

For international observers tracking East Asian politics, South Korea offers a compelling case study in the limits of technocratic pragmatism. Without a firm policy anchor grounded in its core principles, a governing coalition risks losing its identity—and its majority—far faster than it built them.
