The Maternity Leave Backlash: How a South Korean News Anchor’s Resignation Sparked a National Firestorm

November 18, 2026

On the morning of November 17, South Korean media outlets launched a synchronized barrage of headlines targeting a single working mother. Lim Hyun-ju, a prominent television personality and longtime news anchor at MBC (Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation, one of South Korea’s leading public television networks), had just announced her departure from the station. Within hours, tabloid portals and online forums were ablaze with a single, toxic accusation: that she had exploited the company’s parental leave system to the maximum, drawing corporate benefits for nearly two years across two pregnancies, only to quit the very second her leave expired.

Commenters accused her of "bleeding her colleagues dry," a biting Korean colloquialism reserved for workers whose extended absences force their peers to absorb punishing extra workloads without additional compensation. By mid-afternoon, more than two dozen major outlets had amplified the claims. Yet by evening, the entire narrative had collapsed under the weight of easily verifiable employment records. The controversy, and Lim’s defiant public rebuttal, opened a window into the raw anxieties, online vigilante culture, and deep workplace friction that define the realities of motherhood in modern East Asia.

The 21-Month Myth: Anatomy of a Workplace Moral Panic

The rumor that swept through South Korean media on November 17 was remarkably specific, which gave it an immediate veneer of credibility. Online posts claimed that Lim had taken parental leave twice, racking up a combined 1 year and 9 months (21 months) away from her desk, before handing in her resignation the moment she was due to return. In a media ecosystem where unverified forum chatter frequently graduates into breaking news, digital outlets such as TenAsia, News1, and TV Report quickly published stories scrutinizing her departure.

To an outside observer, an employee using statutory parental leave and then choosing a career change might seem like a private decision. In South Korea, however, it touches an exposed nerve. Because many Korean corporate environments and media organizations operate with lean staffing models and rarely hire permanent replacements for staff on leave, an employee’s prolonged absence often translates directly into mandatory overtime and burnt-out peers. When a worker finishes their leave and exits the company permanently, coworkers frequently feel abandoned, left with the accumulated exhaustion while the departing colleague leaves with full statutory benefits.

Riding this preexisting social resentment, anonymous detractors weaponized Lim’s personal life. Having given birth twice, she was presumed to have taken the absolute legal maximum leave for each child. The "21-month" figure became an unquestioned premise, transforming a standard career transition into an alleged moral failing and an abuse of corporate goodwill.

Dismantling the Numbers: 10 Months, Full-Term Broadcasts, and Forfeited Leave

Faced with a rapidly spreading smear campaign, Lim chose not to issue a vague, corporate-vetted non-denial. Instead, she took to her personal channels to release precise dates and employment figures that completely demolished the internet’s math.

Far from taking two extended leaves totaling 21 months, Lim revealed that she had taken parental leave exactly once in her entire career. That single leave occurred this year and lasted just 10 months—an 11-month gap from the fictitious claims circulating online. More importantly, she had not walked away the instant her leave expired. In fact, she had legally protected parental leave days still remaining on the books, but chose to forfeit the remainder so that she could formally conclude her tenure and allow MBC to plan its roster without lingering ambiguity.

Furthermore, Lim recounted her rigorous schedule leading up to both deliveries. During both of her pregnancies, she anchored live television broadcasts until she was full-term. Rather than checking out early, she remained on air in front of millions of viewers until days before giving birth. After each delivery, she bypassed immediate long-term parental leave entirely, returning to her high-pressure anchor desk just three months postpartum. The narrative that she was an opportunistic freeloader who left her newsroom colleagues in the lurch was completely contradicted by her documented broadcast record.

"I Endured Going Braless, but Not Falsehoods"

Lim’s public rebuttal was striking for its blunt refusal to play the contrite public figure. In her statement, she addressed the vicious online rhetoric head-on, citing the accusation that she had "bled her colleagues dry" before firing back with a pointed cultural reference: "I could tolerate [controversies over] going braless, but I cannot tolerate falsehoods."

The remark referenced Lim’s well-known history of challenging rigid social expectations placed on female broadcast journalists in South Korea. In past years, she made national waves by anchoring prime-time news wearing eyeglasses—a standard practice for male anchors, but historically taboo for women on Korean television—and later participated in a televised documentary experiment where she went braless during a regular broadcast. While she had stoically weathered the ensuing cultural outrage and online trolling regarding her personal lifestyle and wardrobe choices, she drew a hard line at distortions of her professional ethics.

"I could tolerate going braless, but I cannot tolerate falsehoods."

By bringing hard data—her single 10-month leave, her forfeiture of remaining benefits, and her three-month postpartum returns—she forced an immediate retreat across the entertainment press. Outlets like Sports World published formal corrections, and headlines shifted from questioning her departure to documenting her swift debunking of the rumors. By the time evening commute news roundups were published by outlets like JoyNews24 and Sports Kyunghyang, Lim’s defense was featured prominently alongside major national developments, including Grammy nominations for Blackpink’s Jennie and legal reports on prison sentences handed down to malicious online commenters.

The Double Standard for Working Mothers Across East Asia

The viral explosion surrounding Lim’s departure was not an isolated event. Coverage by sports and culture outlet MHN Sports noted a simultaneous debate emerging in Japan, where another veteran female broadcaster left her network after 15 years amid similar social scrutiny over maternity leave usage.

Across both countries, governments are sounding emergency alarms over catastrophic demographic collapses and historic low birth rates, rolling out cash incentives and publicly urging young professionals to start families. Yet inside actual workplaces, the cultural penalties for exercising statutory parental rights remain severe. A working mother who takes extended leave is frequently viewed as a burden to her team; if she returns quickly, she faces judgment over neglecting family obligations; and if she eventually decides to reevaluate her career path after childbirth, she is treated with suspicion, accused of having "exploited" the system on her way out the door.

The fact that an accomplished anchor who worked live television while heavily pregnant had to publicly tally her months away from work just to protect her reputation demonstrates the precarious position working mothers occupy. Even within elite, well-resourced institutions like major television networks, the baseline assumption from the public was guilt until proven innocent.

What to Watch Next

As the immediate media frenzy subsides and Lim embarks on her post-MBC career, the controversy leaves several important dynamics to monitor in South Korea's evolving workplace culture:

  • Accountability for Unverified Online Aggregation: South Korean media watchdogs and legal authorities have increasingly cracked down on anonymous "cyber-wreckers" and online slander, highlighted on the same day by prison terms for malicious online commenters. Watch whether public figures like Lim pursue civil or criminal defamation claims against outlets and individuals that fabricated specific employment records.
  • Workplace Structural Reform in Lean Industries: The visceral public reaction against staff taking leave stems directly from companies failing to provide backfill staffing during parental absences. Observers should track whether mid-sized and large corporations, under pressure to address demographic decline, introduce formal temporary-hire policies to ease the burden on remaining colleagues.
  • Media Coverage Patterns on Female Professionals: The rapid 24-hour arc of this story—from uncritical amplification of an internet rumor to a complete retraction after documented proof was published—underscores the volatility of coverage surrounding women who leave long-held corporate roles. Whether major domestic outlets adopt stricter fact-checking standards before publishing speculative reports on employee resignations remains a key test for digital journalism ethics in Seoul.
