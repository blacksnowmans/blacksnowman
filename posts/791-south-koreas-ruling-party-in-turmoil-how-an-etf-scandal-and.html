South Korea’s Ruling Party in Turmoil: How an ETF Scandal and Slipping Polls Reveal a Crisis of Leadership

September 17, 2026

Step inside the quiet panic rooms of Seoul's ruling camp, and you will find an administration frantically pointing fingers. As presidential approval ratings slide into the low 30s, the official narrative is one of external headwinds and stubborn personalities. But peel back the surface-level shouting matches among party heavyweights, and a darker reality emerges. The current political crisis is not merely a bad news cycle or a clash of egos; it is the predictable byproduct of a system that punishes dissent, ignores expert warnings, and leaves ordinary retail investors to foot the bill for reckless policymaking.

To understand why South Korea's dominant political machinery is currently stalling, one has to look past the daily theater of parliamentary infighting and examine two converging flashpoints: an explosive leaked document regarding high-risk financial products, and a growing rebellion among the party's own core constituencies.

Blame Shifting at the Top and the Silencing of Dissent

When poll numbers tank, the traditional playbook in Korean politics is simple: find someone to blame. Within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's main liberal party, commentators and party insiders have split into predictable factions. Some pundits attribute the erosion of support to the president's unyielding personal style, arguing that a refusal to bend breaks collective leadership. Others point fingers at progressive firebrands and internal rivals, treating any policy critique as an act of treason.

This blame-shifting reached a fever pitch as party insiders launched coordinated attacks against veteran progressive figures like Choo Mi-ae, a former Justice Minister known for her uncompromising stance. Remarks from senior figures warning that politicians who fail to deliver locally will be barred from future nominations exposed a raw truth about contemporary party management: it increasingly resembles a top-down, authoritarian structure where loyalty trumps debate. When lawmakers like Park Bum-kye chime in to urge restraint—while simultaneously bungling coalition negotiations and threatening legal action against critics—the image projected to the public is one of chaotic self-absorption.

Beneath this soap opera of internal feuds lies a deeper structural rot. Lawmakers who privately despise the heavy-handed leadership style refuse to speak out. Why? Because they fear the opaque candidate nomination system, which leaves enough wiggle room for party elites to sideline internal critics. Terrified of losing their political futures, ruling party legislators have chosen quiet compliance over honest governance, transforming the party into an echo chamber.

The 15-Seat Rule and the Erosion of Parliamentary Norms

This top-down authoritarianism is not confined to internal party elections; it spills directly into the National Assembly. Recently, the DPK leadership unilaterally notified smaller opposition parties—including the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by figures like Cho Kuk—of a sudden push to lower the threshold for forming an official parliamentary negotiating bloc from 20 seats down to 15.

The move was executed without genuine prior negotiation. Smaller parties, some of whom were pushing for a 10-seat threshold, were presented with a take-it-or-leave-it ultimatum. For a party that styles itself as the custodian of South Korean democracy, treating legislative rules as royal decrees rather than subjects for consensus has alienated potential allies and hardened the opposition.

Political scientists often warn that when a ruling party relies entirely on the raw willpower of its leadership rather than institutionalized compromise, the system eventually fractures. The current friction within the liberal camp is not a temporary glitch; it is the structural cost of institutionalizing an environment where leadership equals dictation, and consultation is treated as an optional luxury.

The Leverage ETF Scandal: Ignoring the Experts

While parliamentary maneuvers and internal purges dominate the headlines, the most explosive revelation involves a closed-door policy meeting held back in January. According to an investigative report by local broadcaster YTN, the Blue House and financial regulators aggressively pushed forward a controversial plan to approve new single-stock 2X leverage Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) and capture offshore crypto-related investment demand at home.

The ambition was clear: generate financial excitement and capture retail liquidity. The problem was that almost everyone with actual market expertise screamed warnings. According to the leaked documents, every single domestic asset management firm and securities company in South Korea—with the sole exception of Samsung Securities—objected to the plan, marking the leverage ETF proposals with explicit warnings of extreme market risk.

Despite a unanimous chorus of warnings from the financial sector, the policy was jammed through. Crucially, the closed-door meeting where this was orchestrated was presided over by then-Prime Minister Kim Min-seok. When financial policy is divorced from market reality and driven by political desire, disaster usually follows.

When 20-Something Investors Pay the Price

The human cost of this policy arrogance is now manifesting in the worst possible place for the ruling party: the collapse of youth support.

Young South Koreans in their 20s—historically a vital engine of progressive support—have watched their portfolios get systematically dismantled. Driven by economic anxiety and a desire for upward mobility, young retail investors rushed into the newly enabled high-risk financial products. Market analyses show that in certain single-stock 2X leverage products, losses among young investors have reached a staggering 60% to 70%, with some specific assets leaving nearly 75% to 80% of young market participants facing catastrophic drawdowns.

To dismiss these losses as mere market volatility is politically suicidal. This was not an act of bad weather; it was a man-made storm engineered by regulators who ignored institutional warnings. When a generation of young citizens realizes that the political establishment they trusted not only failed to protect their financial future, but actively built the financial traps that drained their savings, the betrayal cuts deep.

The administration's approval ratings among young voters plunging into the low 30s are the direct mathematical output of this policy failure. While lawmakers spent their days playing musical chairs with committee seats and fighting factional proxy wars, a generation of first-time investors saw their financial foundations evaporate.

What to Watch Next

As the political fallout from the leverage ETF memo and the sliding approval ratings deepens, several critical developments will determine whether the ruling camp can stabilize or spiral further into crisis:

  • Calls for Accountability: Watch whether opposition parties successfully push for formal parliamentary investigations or special prosecutor probes into who ultimately signed off on the ignored industry warnings within the Prime Minister's office.
  • The Nomination Backlash: Keep an eye on internal dissent within the DPK. If rank-and-file lawmakers continue to bite their tongues out of fear of rigged candidate selection, expect more underground leaks and quiet defections.
  • Youth Sentiment Shifts: Monitor upcoming economic sentiment data for 20- and 30-year-old demographics. Their alienation from the ruling party could permanently redraw South Korea's electoral map heading into upcoming local and general political cycles.

Policies driven by raw political ambition rather than economic reality always come due. For South Korea's ruling establishment, the bill has finally arrived—and the payment is being extracted directly from their core supporters.
