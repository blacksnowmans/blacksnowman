How to Walk 800 Kilometers with Only 3.5 Kilograms: Lessons from the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Standing at the starting line of the Camino de Santiago, facing the 800-kilometer (nearly 500 miles) stretch ahead, every pilgrim confronts a primal wave of panic: How am I going to carry all of this? Packing for the famed pilgrimage across northern Spain is rarely just about logistics; it is an exercise in brutal honesty. The number 3.5 kilograms (about 7.7 pounds) is not merely a weight on a scale. It is the defining metric of your trip's survival and a direct test of how much emotional and physical baggage you are willing to shed.

When travelers decide on a whim to book a ticket to Paris and head for the trail, they often make the mistake of packing for every hypothetical disaster, letting their bags balloon past 10 kilograms (22 pounds). But the Camino is an unforgivingly honest trail. It presses down on your legs with the exact weight of the worries you packed into your fabric. Weeks into the trek, as you watch exhausted fellow travelers agonizing over which items to throw away at local hostels, a singular truth becomes glaringly obvious: preparing for the Camino is never about figuring out which high-end brands to buy, but rather deciding what you can bear to leave behind.

The Science of Ultralight Packing

Ask seasoned hikers about the realities of long-distance trekking, and they will point to a strict threshold: 7 kilograms (about 15 pounds). Cross that magical line, and the physics of the trail turn hostile. The moment your pack exceeds 7 kilograms, the probability of developing acute lower back and ankle pain skyrockets to 90%. Worse still, every additional kilogram compounds exponentially, acting like a slow-acting poison that chips away at the remaining cartilage in your knees.

Keeping a pack down to an astonishing 3.5 kilograms requires meticulous intentionality. The secret lies not just in choosing a lightweight bag, but in harness mechanics. A proper pack distributes weight off the shoulders entirely, transferring it directly to the pelvis via hip straps. Prioritize gear that hugs your torso and features breathable mesh backing over flashy brand names. Furthermore, external straps are invaluable. Relying solely on internal storage means dumping out your entire life onto a dirty hostel floor just to find a rain jacket. A 30-to-40-liter capacity is the sweet spot for a reason: human nature dictates that if you have extra space, you will find a way to fill it.

Footwear and toiletries consume the lion's share of your pack's volume. While it is tempting to bring separate sneakers and sandals, packing duplicate footwear is a fast track to physical misery. Many veterans survive the entire distance in a single pair of sturdy sandals, though closed-toe variants are recommended to protect the heels. When it comes to clothing, adopt a strict wash-and-wear philosophy. One lightweight top, a windbreaker, and a compact down jacket are plenty. For bottoms, quick-dry athletic trousers are standard, while female pilgrims frequently swear by a single, versatile dress that eliminates the headache of coordinating separates for post-hike evenings.

Smart On-the-Trail Adjustments

Deciding to travel without a power bank might feel like a reckless gamble, but it is ultimately an exercise in energy conservation. Modern smartphones easily last a full day if switched to power-saving mode, and you rarely find yourself with a dead battery by the time you reach an afternoon village. Similarly, while picking up a tourist SIM card at Incheon International Airport before leaving South Korea is convenient, buying a local prepaid card in Spain can reduce costs significantly.

Blisters, meanwhile, are practically a rite of passage along the 800-kilometer route. The golden rule is keeping your feet dry through a "double-sock" strategy: thin toe socks underneath thicker trekking socks to minimize friction. If blisters do form, seasoned walkers advise passing a sterile needle and thread through the fluid pocket to let it drain continuously without ripping the skin off entirely. Listening to your body's signals always supersedes rigid adherence to a schedule. Rushing to keep pace with faster strangers is why eight out of ten pilgrims suffer injuries within the first three days.

Navigating the Credencial and the Compostela

The journey to Santiago de Compostela is as much about collecting documentation as it is about putting one foot in front of the other. The Credencial, or pilgrim passport issued at regional starting points like Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, serves as your official identity on the trail. It grants access to pilgrim hostels and acts as the ultimate proof required to claim your Compostela—the official completion certificate.

To qualify, modern regulations require walking at least 100 continuous kilometers (about 62 miles) of an officially recognized route, finishing directly at the Cathedral of Santiago. You must collect at least two stamps, or sellos, per day from local churches, cafes, or restaurants. These administrative checkpoints ensure integrity, rendering skipped sections via motorized transport invalid.

Yet, seasoned travelers often note that the paper certificate is merely a ceremonial byproduct. The true value of the journey lies in the ephemeral tokens exchanged along the way: rosaries, polished stones, or small pouches brought from home. Discovering these items dangling from a stranger's pack bridges linguistic divides, forging an instant sense of solidarity.

What to Watch Next

As you plan your own journey—whether physically packing your bags or simply looking to declutter your daily routine—keep a close eye on the evolving regulations for long-distance routes in Europe. Official bodies periodically update the rules for the final 100-kilometer stretch, altering stamp requirements and accommodation protocols.

More importantly, watch your own tendencies toward accumulation. The next time you feel overwhelmed by the pace of modern life, remember the minimalist philosophy of the trail: true endurance comes from what you choose to let go of, not what you manage to drag along.
