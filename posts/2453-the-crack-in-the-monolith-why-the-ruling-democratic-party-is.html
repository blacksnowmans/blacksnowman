The Crack in the Monolith: Why the Ruling Democratic Party is Tearing Itself Apart Over Choi Min-hee

November 18, 2026

Late into the night on November 17, as the neon lights of Seoul’s political district dimmed, the offices of the Democratic Party of Korea—the nation’s ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung—remained a hive of frantic activity and whispered dissent. What began as a standard internal disciplinary matter has spiraled into a full-blown civil war, exposing deep fractures between the party’s idealistic newcomers and its pragmatic veterans. At the center of this storm is Choi Min-hee, a member of the party’s Supreme Council, whose recent conduct has ignited a firestorm that threatens the internal stability of the government's legislative base.

For an outside observer, party infighting is common. However, in the high-stakes world of Korean politics, where party discipline is usually ironclad and factional loyalty is the primary currency, the public revolt of 55 first-term lawmakers against a sitting member of the leadership is a seismic event. This is not merely a squabble over words; it is a battle for the soul of the ruling party and a test of whether the administration can maintain a unified front as it navigates a contentious legislative agenda. The fallout, characterized by some as a "psychological split" within the party, suggests that the "monolith" of the Democratic Party may be more fragile than it appears.

The Revolt of the Freshmen: 55 Lawmakers Demand Resignation

The escalation reached a tipping point on the afternoon of November 17, when a group known as Deomincho—the official association of first-term Democratic Party lawmakers—issued a stinging public statement. In the hierarchy of Korean politics, "freshman" lawmakers are expected to learn the ropes and follow the party line. Instead, 55 of them broke ranks to demand the "severe punishment" and immediate resignation of Choi Min-hee from her leadership position.

The sheer number of signatories is significant. With over 50 newly elected representatives putting their names on a document that essentially calls for a "purge" of a Supreme Council member, the message to the party leadership was clear: the status quo is no longer acceptable. According to reports from major outlets like the Kyunghyang Shinmun and Hankyoreh, these lawmakers argued that Choi’s recent controversies—which involve leaked recordings and questionable public statements—have become a liability that the party can no longer afford to carry.

However, the unity of this "freshman revolt" was not absolute. Observers noted that members of the pro-administration faction (often referred to as Chin-chung, or those closely aligned with the President's core circle) pointedly abstained from the statement. This absenteeism confirms a secondary fissure: even among the newest members of the National Assembly, there is a divide between those who prioritize party reform and those who prioritize defending the administration’s leadership at all costs. This internal friction within the freshman group suggests that the conflict is not just between generations, but between competing visions of how the ruling party should exercise its power.

The Veteran Guard’s Plea for Pragmatism

As the younger lawmakers were preparing their offensive, the party’s "old guard" was busy behind the scenes trying to prevent a total meltdown. Over the same weekend, a group of senior Democratic Party heavyweights, including the seasoned political strategist Park Ji-won, held an emergency meeting to discuss a way out of the crisis. Their conclusion was the polar opposite of the freshman demand: they advocated for "light punishment" (gyeong-jing-gye).

Park Ji-won, a man who has survived decades of South Korean political turbulence, was vocal in his reasoning. By 11:00 PM that night, his stance was being broadcast across major networks like Yonhap News TV. His argument was simple: "We must not provide the spark for internal division." From the perspective of the veterans, a heavy-handed punishment or a forced resignation of a Supreme Council member would be like performing surgery with a chainsaw. It might remove the "problem," but it would leave the party bleeding and vulnerable to the opposition.

"The senior members of the party have gathered their opinions against a heavy punishment for Choi Min-hee," Park reportedly stated. "At a time when the party needs to be focused on governance, we cannot allow ourselves to be consumed by an internal civil war."

This highlights a fundamental tension in Korean political management. The seniors view the party as a delicate ecosystem that requires careful balance and compromise to survive. To them, the first-term lawmakers are playing with fire, risking a "psychological split" that could render the party ineffective in the National Assembly. This clash of philosophies—principled accountability versus pragmatic stability—is now the primary fault line in the ruling party.

Transcripts, Lies, and the "Han Duck-soo" Defense

To understand why these two groups are at each other's throats, one must look at the catalyst: a series of leaked recordings and the subsequent attempts to explain them. The controversy involves Kim Min-seok, another high-ranking Democratic Party official, who revealed that portions of a "Choi Min-hee transcript" had been in the party's possession since October. The fact that the leadership knew about these potentially damaging recordings for a month before they became public has fueled accusations of a cover-up and poor crisis management.

The content of the controversy reached a bizarre peak when Choi Min-hee attempted to explain away some of her more problematic statements. She claimed that her remarks were a result of "confusion with the impeachment of Han Duck-soo." (Han Duck-soo is a prominent political figure whose potential impeachment has been a recurring theme in legislative battles). This explanation, however, backfired spectacularly. The original whistleblower who provided the recordings told the Weekly Kyunghyang that they "burst out laughing" upon hearing Choi’s excuse, calling it an absurd attempt to rewrite history.

The situation was further complicated by Choi’s appearance on News Factory, a highly influential pro-Democratic Party YouTube and radio program. During the broadcast, discussions turned toward whether Kim Min-seok should face legal consequences for his role in the transcript leak. When a Supreme Council member is debating legal action against a fellow party member on a public platform, it is a sign that the "Rubicon has been crossed." Local broadcasters like KBC Gwangju described Choi’s recent public apology not as a gesture of peace, but as a "deep bow before a final battle"—a signal that she has no intention of going quietly.

The Ethics Tribunal and the Shadow of the Opposition

With the party unable to resolve the issue through internal dialogue, the matter has been officially referred to the Ethics Tribunal (Yulli-sim-pan-won). This is the party’s internal judiciary, and its decision will be the final word on Choi’s political future. However, the referral has expanded the scope of the scandal. On the same day that Choi’s case was sent to the tribunal, allegations surfaced regarding "manipulated disciplinary actions" within the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, involving their figures like Han Dong-hoon and Bae Hyun-jin.

The simultaneous eruption of ethics scandals in both the ruling and opposition parties has created a "universal crisis of trust" in the National Assembly. For the Democratic Party, the stakes are higher because they hold the presidency. If the Ethics Tribunal sides with the senior members and issues a light punishment, they risk a full-scale mutiny from the 55 first-term lawmakers and their supporters. If they issue a severe punishment, they risk the "war" that political commentator Chin Jung-kwon warned about in the Sisa Journal, where he noted that the party is already in a state of "psychological secession."

The reference to "psychological secession" or a "psychological split" (sim-ri-jeok bun-dang) is particularly chilling for the Lee Jae-myung administration. It implies that while the lawmakers may still wear the same party badge, they no longer share a common purpose. In the history of Korean politics, such psychological splits are almost always the precursor to a formal party fracture, similar to the events that led to the downfall of previous administrations.

What to Watch Next

As the Ethics Tribunal begins its deliberations, the next few weeks will determine whether the Democratic Party can remain a cohesive ruling force or if it will devolve into a collection of warring factions. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Tribunal’s Verdict: If the punishment is seen as a "slap on the wrist," watch for a secondary, more aggressive statement from the 55 freshman lawmakers. They have already staked their reputations on this demand for accountability.
  • The "Chin-Chung" Move: Keep an eye on the pro-administration lawmakers who stayed silent during the initial revolt. If they eventually move to support Choi, it will be a sign that President Lee Jae-myung’s inner circle prioritizes protecting its own over party-wide reform.
  • The Whistleblower’s Next Move: The individual who leaked the transcripts has already publicly mocked Choi’s excuses. If more recordings are released, it could make the senior members' "light punishment" stance untenable.
  • Impact on President Lee’s Agenda: Every hour spent on the "Choi Min-hee civil war" is an hour not spent on the government's budget or policy initiatives. If this drags on, the opposition People Power Party will likely capitalize on the ruling party’s paralysis.

The Democratic Party currently finds itself in a classic political trap: the cure for the internal division—a harsh punishment—could be just as damaging as the disease itself. In the coming days, the party must decide if it values the career of one Supreme Council member more than the unity of its legislative majority.
