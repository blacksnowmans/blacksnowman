The Veneer of Legality: How South Korea’s Supreme Court Split Over the Night of Martial Law

November 20, 2026

Picture the scene inside the presidential office complex in Seoul’s Yongsan District on the night of December 3, 2024. Shortly after 8:00 PM, then-Prime Minister Han Duck-soo and a handful of senior ministers were summoned to a private room. There, they were shown drafts of an emergency martial law decree—a sweeping order designed to shut down the National Assembly, suspend basic civil liberties, and mobilize military force. Rather than raising an immediate alarm to protect South Korea’s constitutional order, Han set about solving an operational problem for the leader in the room: he needed to gather enough cabinet members to meet a constitutional voting quorum.

What unfolded over the next two hours remains one of the most perilous moments in modern South Korean political history. The attempt by Yoon Suk-yeol to institute a "self-coup"—using state power to dismantle the very democratic institutions meant to check his presidency—collapsed within hours, crushed by the bravery of citizens who rushed to defend parliament and lawmakers who voted to invalidate the order. Yoon Suk-yeol was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and incarcerated. But the legal battle over who aided that conspiracy, and how, continues to fracture South Korea’s highest court.

At the center of this legal reckoning is a fierce debate within the Supreme Court En Banc over the conduct of former Prime Minister Han. While the court's majority acquitted Han of key insurrection-related charges regarding his actions prior to the formal declaration, a scathing dissenting opinion authored by Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyoung-mi exposes the profound danger of allowing bureaucrats to provide a procedural shield for unconstitutional power grabs.

The Cabinet Meeting as a Legal Shield for a Self-Coup

In constitutional scholarship and political science, a "self-coup" occurs not when an outside military junta overthrows a government, but when an already lawfully elected leader abuses emergency powers to break through constitutional constraints and permanently neutralize the legislative and judicial branches. When Yoon Suk-yeol attempted to paralyze parliament on December 3, he executed a classic self-coup. Because such acts originate within the formal structure of the state, they are often far more insidious than armed rebellions. They arrive wrapped in official paperwork and administrative formality.

South Korea’s Constitution (Article 89) and its Martial Law Act (Article 2) explicitly mandate that any declaration of emergency martial law must undergo prior deliberation by the State Council—South Korea's cabinet. This requirement was engineered as a vital constitutional circuit breaker, preventing a single head of state from unilaterally invoking military rule.

Yet on that December night, the Cabinet meeting served not as a brake on tyranny, but as its alibi. According to court records, the gathered ministers were given no opportunity for substantive debate. Yoon Suk-yeol entered the room at 10:16 PM, delivered a two-minute unilateral notification, and departed to broadcast the declaration to the nation at 10:27 PM.

As Justice Oh Kyoung-mi observed in her dissenting opinion, the mandatory constitutional procedure of Cabinet deliberation was corrupted into a tool designed to grant an illusion of legality to an unconstitutional rebellion. By orchestrating the assembly of ministers simply to tick a procedural box, senior officials enabled the regime to claim that statutory steps had been followed, covering a lawless decree in the outer garments of legitimate administration.

"The constitutional procedure of State Council deliberation and resolution deteriorated into a tool providing the outer appearance of legality to an unconstitutional and illegal act of insurrection," Justice Oh wrote in her opinion.

A One-Hour Continuum: Intent and the Legal Arbitrage of Time

The crux of the division within the Supreme Court En Banc lies in how judges interpret the timeline of December 3. The court’s majority drew an artificial line at 10:27 PM—the exact minute martial law was formally declared to the public. The majority held that while assistance rendered after the declaration might constitute aiding an insurrection, Han’s actions beforehand—such as convening the Cabinet and urging ministers to attend—did not establish a deliberate "purpose of disrupting the Constitution" (국헌문란 목적). The majority framed these pre-declaration actions as mere administrative routine or attempts to advise against the decision.

Justice Oh’s dissent forcefully rejected this temporal splicing. The events between 8:00 PM and 10:27 PM took place in the exact same location, involving the exact same actors, driven by a continuous sequence of decisions spanning barely two hours. To isolate the pre-declaration minutes and label them as standard administrative duties distorts both the physical and psychological reality of the evening.

Under South Korean criminal law, the offense of insurrection requires proving a specific intent to disrupt the constitutional order. Justice Oh argued that this legal standard does not require a perpetrator to explicitly desire the downfall of democracy; it is satisfied when an official fully recognizes that their actions will facilitate an unconstitutional outcome and chooses to proceed anyway—a legal concept known as willful tolerance or intent through acceptance.

When Han organized the Cabinet meeting despite seeing the unconstitutional martial law decree with his own eyes, he knew that a two-minute meeting without debate would serve only to validate a self-coup. By choosing to push the process forward to achieve a quorum, he willingly accepted the destruction of the constitutional order as a consequence.

Inside the Yongsan Reception Room: Quorum Chasing and Silence

The evidentiary record highlighted in Justice Oh’s opinion paints a detailed picture of bureaucratic compliance over a frantic two-hour period:

  • 8:00 PM: Five core cabinet members, including the Prime Minister, were informed of the martial law plan inside the presidential office and physically reviewed draft decree documents detailing the suspension of constitutional organs.
  • 9:29 PM: Han returned to the presidential office and explicitly stated that they needed to gather enough members to meet the Cabinet's voting quorum.
  • 9:37 PM: Han personally phoned Minister of Agriculture Song Mi-ryung, demanding her immediate presence at Yongsan without revealing that martial law was about to be declared.
  • 10:17 PM: Once 11 ministers—the exact threshold for a quorum—were assembled in the grand reception room, the meeting was called to order, lasting just two minutes as Yoon Suk-yeol spoke uninterrupted.
  • 10:43 PM: After martial law was declared, Han remained alone with then-Interior Minister Lee Sang-min for 16 minutes in the reception room to discuss follow-up document measures, including plans to cut off electricity and water supplies to targeted institutions.

If the Prime Minister had truly intended to uphold his constitutional duty, he would have shared the decree documents with his fellow ministers upon their arrival, organized a joint refusal, or used his constitutional authority to block the declaration. Instead, his focus remained strictly mathematical: securing 11 bodies in a room.

The post-declaration conduct further dismantled the defense of mere passive compliance. In the days following the collapsed coup, officials colluded to backdate Cabinet endorsement documents and subsequently destroyed them to cover their tracks—actions that point to an acute awareness of guilt rather than innocent administrative routine.

Flawed Precedents and the Reckoning for Bureaucrats

To justify acquitting the former Prime Minister on pre-declaration charges, the Supreme Court majority referenced a March 24, 2025 decision by the Constitutional Court (24-Heonna-9), which had dismissed an impeachment motion against Han. That earlier ruling concluded that Han’s attempt to convene the Cabinet could be construed as an effort to gather ministerial consensus.

However, Justice Oh pointed out a critical flaw in relying on that precedent: timing. In March 2025, investigators had not yet secured key physical evidence. Full-scale forced investigations and searches of the Yongsan presidential office took place only after Yoon Suk-yeol was formally removed from office by the Constitutional Court on April 4, 2025. It was during those later raids that prosecutors recovered crucial closed-circuit television (CCTV) footage from the reception room, exposing the stark reality of the two-minute silence and the subsequent 16-minute secret meeting on cutting utility power.

Using an early impeachment dismissal—rendered during an evidentiary void—to grant criminal immunity in light of newly uncovered video evidence turns judicial logic on its head.

South Korea’s modern democratic history has seen previous dictators and coup leaders eventually brought to justice, yet the senior bureaucrats who quietly drafted their orders and provided procedural cover often escaped accountability. Justice Oh’s dissenting opinion serves as a vital marker for the future. It asserts that when high-ranking officials lend public authority and procedural legitimacy to an illegal power grab, their paperwork is as dangerous as a tank in the street.

What to Watch Next

  • Prosecutorial Strategy in Parallel Trials: Watch how special prosecutors apply Justice Oh's doctrine of "willful tolerance" in ongoing trials of secondary cabinet officials and senior aides accused of assisting the December 3 declaration.
  • Legislative Reforms to Emergency Powers: Look for legislative proposals in the National Assembly, led by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, to reform the Martial Law Act to mandate real-time audio/video recording of emergency Cabinet meetings and impose strict criminal penalties for attempting to bypass substantive deliberation.
  • Judicial Precedent: Observe whether future Supreme Court panels gradually adopt the dissenting view that pre-declaration administrative preparations for an illegal coup cannot be neatly severed from the coup itself.
