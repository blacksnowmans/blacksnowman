The 90 Percent Illusion: Why South Korea’s 'Nationalized' Firefighters Are Bankrupting Local Governments

October 26, 2026

When a firefighter rushes into a burning high-rise in Gyeonggi Province, the patch on their uniform reads like an emblem of uniform, nationwide protection: they are official national civil servants of the Republic of Korea. In April 2020, following years of public petitioning and high-profile disaster mobilization during catastrophic east coast wildfires, the central government under then-President Moon Jae-in formally nationalized the country’s regional fire services. The reform was celebrated as a milestone for public safety, promising that rescue workers would no longer have to purchase their own protective gloves or rely on broken equipment funded by impoverished rural districts.

Six years later, that national status has revealed itself to be a legal fiction with a crushing price tag. Behind the national badge, more than 90 percent of the money required to pay firefighters' salaries and keep fire trucks running is still squeezed out of local government budgets. In South Korea’s most populous province, Gyeonggi, that contradiction recently detonated into an explosive political feud, pitting regional leaders against the presidential administration of President Lee Jae-myung and laying bare an unsustainable system where vital public safety depends on cigarette sales and cyclical housing speculation.

The Illusion of 'Nationalized' Firefighters: 90% Borne by Local Coffers

The core dysfunction of South Korea's fire service lies in the yawning gap between legal status and fiscal reality. According to data from the National Fire Agency and the National Assembly Research Service, the central government in Seoul contributes only a sliver of the actual funds needed to keep fire stations operational.

Last year, out of a nationwide fire service budget of 8.15 trillion won (about $6 billion), state treasury subsidies totaled just 869.7 billion won (about $644 million)—a meager 10.7 percent. Local governments were forced to absorb the remaining 89.3 percent, or 7.28 trillion won (about $5.4 billion). When narrowed down to personnel costs, the disparity becomes even more acute. Out of 6.51 trillion won (about $4.8 billion) in total firefighter labor expenses this year, central government allocations via the dedicated Fire Safety Grant Tax accounted for only 549.6 billion won (about $407 million), or 8.4 percent. The remaining 91.6 percent—nearly 5.96 trillion won (about $4.4 billion)—must be footed entirely by regional administrations, many of which are sinking under mounting debt.

Because local authorities pay the bills, a citizen's basic safety net is dictated not by national standards, but by the postal code where an emergency happens. Disparities highlighted in recent legislative audits illustrate a fractured republic of safety:

  • Staffing density: In 2024, a single firefighter in wealthy Seoul was responsible for 1,255 residents; in rural South Jeolla Province (Jeonnam), the ratio was one firefighter per 396 residents.
  • Gear allowances: In Busan and Jeju Island, annual clothing and uniform allowances hovered around 250,000 won (about $185) per officer. In the industrial hub of Ulsan, that figure reached 700,000 won (about $520)—almost triple.
  • Professional training: While North Chungcheong Province spent 428,000 won (about $315) per officer on outsourced specialized training, the port city of Busan allocated a mere 41,000 won (about $30), representing a tenfold gulf.

Far from creating a unified national safety umbrella, the reform created what front-line officers cynically refer to as "national status in name only"—nationalized credit for the central state, with the financial burden offloaded onto regional councils.

'Political Burning at the Stake': Choo Mi-ae's Provocation

The simmering tensions finally boiled over during a recent National Assembly audit of Gyeonggi Province, the economic engine wrapping around Seoul. Confronted with a spiraling budget deficit, Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae caused an uproar by referring to the structural burden of fire service personnel costs as a "fiscal straw" that was draining the province's resources.

The backlash was immediate. Opposition politicians and media commentators accused Choo of denigrating the sacrifices of front-line first responders who risk their lives daily. Yet inside provincial headquarters, the comment reflected a desperate zero-sum reality. The province's internal Fiscal Innovation Task Force had identified mandatory fire service expenditure as the single largest structural factor suffocating the provincial treasury, forcing officials into an unpalatable choice between funding vital social welfare for vulnerable residents and maintaining emergency response infrastructure.

Choo apologized for the insensitivity of the metaphor, but refused to walk back the structural warning. Taking an aggressive stance, she declared:

"Even if I am subjected to a political burning at the stake, I want reform on this issue to be realized. The political establishment must approach this responsibly. Hiding behind my controversial choice of words to launch attacks will not solve the fire service's financial crisis."

Choo’s defiance underscored a systemic taboo in Korean politics. While politicians across the ideological spectrum routinely champion better conditions for firefighters to win public favor, few are willing to engage with the politically toxic question of who should write the check.

'A Local Matter'? The Constitutional Clash Inside the Ruling Camp

The controversy is particularly acute because of who currently occupies the presidential office. During his own tenure as governor of Gyeonggi Province, President Lee Jae-myung was one of the loudest critics of this fiscal arrangement, repeatedly demanding that the central government fund the nationalized service it had created.

Now in the presidential office, however, the administration's posture has shifted noticeably toward legal conservatism. President Lee has reiterated the constitutional and statutory doctrine that firefighting remains fundamentally a "local administrative task" (자치사무). While the administration has signaled support for upgrading gear and expanding specific equipment subsidies, it has resisted taking over the multi-billion-dollar personnel payroll from the national treasury, arguing that national civil servant status does not automatically convert regional operations into a central mandate.

This stance runs headlong into empirical research. A landmark study by the Korea Research Institute for Local Administration (KRILA), a government-affiliated research body, revealed that when broken down by operational reality, between 79 and 80 percent of modern firefighting duties function de facto as national administrative responsibilities. In an era marked by cross-regional wildland conflagrations, high-tech industrial chemical spills, complex high-rise fires, and massive crowd-crush disasters like the Itaewon tragedy, disaster response can no longer be segmented by municipal borders.

Compounding the problem is the bizarre tax mechanism used to fund central support. The national Fire Safety Grant Tax distributed by the Ministry of the Interior and Safety is funded primarily by the individual consumption tax levied on tobacco products. In effect, South Korea’s first-response apparatus is tied directly to domestic cigarette sales: as smoking rates fluctuate or decline, the revenue stream for critical safety infrastructure contracts with it. This precarious linkage has prompted lawmakers like the Democratic Party of Korea's Jung Chung-rae to introduce legislation requiring the state to cover any deficits in firefighter labor costs, while other bills spearheaded by lawmakers like Lee Yeon-joo demand that the central government assume at least 80 percent of all fire expenditures.

Real Estate Volatility and the Hangover of National Deficits

Why did Gyeonggi Province's balance sheet collapse so suddenly into this emergency? The answer lies in the deeply distorted tax structure of South Korea’s local autonomy model.

Although Gyeonggi Province hosts some of the world's largest semiconductor and advanced manufacturing facilities, generating enormous economic output, virtually all lucrative corporate and personal income taxes flow straight into the national treasury. In contrast, regional governments rely heavily on volatile property transaction levies, particularly the local acquisition tax (취득세).

Under former conservative Governor Nam Kyung-pil, a booming real estate market kept provincial coffers full and debt low. During Lee Jae-myung’s gubernatorial tenure, high property tax receipts initially allowed Gyeonggi to aggressively pay down provincial bonds, though borrowing increased to support emergency basic income during the COVID-19 pandemic. Under the subsequent tenure of Governor Kim Dong-yeon, however, real estate transactions froze. As acquisition tax receipts plunged, the provincial administration turned to issuing local bonds and raiding dedicated reserve funds to plug holes, turning routine operations into an ongoing debt roll-over.

This regional crisis was dramatically worsened by the macroeconomic policy of the previous administration. During the term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment and removal from office—the central government enacted sweeping corporate and comprehensive real estate tax cuts. The resulting multi-trillion-won shortfall in national tax collections triggered automatic, statutory reductions in local allocation taxes and regional education grants. When the central government dried up its own revenue pool, the financial shockwave hit local councils immediately, leaving them to fund nationalized emergency personnel while their own revenues evaporated.

What to Watch Next

As the conflict intensifies, several milestones will determine whether South Korea can reform its public safety funding before the next major disaster exposes its cracks:

  • Legislative amendments to the Fire Service Finance Support Act: Watch whether bills drafted by ruling-party lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae—which would mandate an 80 percent central state funding floor for fire personnel—make it past the National Assembly’s fiscal committees against pushback from budget bureaucrats.
  • The decoupling from tobacco taxes: Pay attention to discussions backed by the National Assembly Research Service regarding alternative permanent funding mechanisms, such as mandatory fire insurance surcharges or an independent National Fire Safety Fund, to replace the unstable cigarette excise tax.
  • Internal tensions in the Democratic Party of Korea: With local government heads like Governor Choo Mi-ae openly revolting against central budget transfers, observe how President Lee Jae-myung balances his past identity as a champion of municipal fiscal rights against the macroeconomic constraints of running the state treasury.

Until Seoul resolves the gap between administrative titles and actual cash, the personnel defending South Korea's front lines will remain caught in a dangerous limbo: wearing a national badge on their chest, but reliant on an empty municipal wallet.
