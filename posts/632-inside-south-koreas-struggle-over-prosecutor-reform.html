Inside South Korea's Struggle Over Prosecutor Reform

October 03, 2026

For decades, political talk shows and YouTube political channels in South Korea have returned to a single, exhausting refrain: prosecutor reform. Every time a new administration takes office, the country's powerful investigative agency is hauled onto the operating table, yet the institutional culture stubbornly refuses to change. Watching recent discussions between political figures like Choi Kang-wook and Han Chang-min on a political commentary program, one is left with a deep sense of disillusionment about how an issue that once commanded such unified hope completely lost its way.

South Korea is currently navigating a massive institutional restructuring, splitting investigative functions away from traditional prosecution offices through the creation of new bodies such as the Serious Crimes Investigation Office and the Public Prosecution Office. Yet personnel disputes and the ghost of an entrenched elite cartel continue to haunt the system. Simply changing job titles and hanging new signs on government buildings cannot dismantle a decades-old ecosystem of "prosecutorial fundamentalists" overnight.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The double standards in personnel appointments expose the hollowness of partisan loyalty labels within the prosecution.
  • Successful establishment of the new investigative and prosecution agencies requires thorough personnel purges and systemic completion, not just structural re-labeling.
  • The public must remain vigilant against prosecutorial fundamentalists who weaponize technocratic expertise to preserve their privileges.

The Double Standards of Prosecutorial Appointments

Why the Rules of the Game Shift Depending on Who Holds Power

When Choo Mi-ae served as Justice Minister, her personnel decisions sparked fierce backlash from within the prosecution and the conservative opposition. Their argument at the time relied on a clever linguistic twist: they claimed that anyone Choo promoted could not genuinely be considered "pro-Choo," since the minister chose them. Underneath lay a pervasive double standard: anyone favored by one side was labelled political corruption, while promotions among their own inner circle were treated as purely meritocratic.

Fast forward to the subsequent conservative administration under President Yoon Suk-yeol, and the hypocrisy became staggering. Even as figures closely tied to the president occupied top posts, loyalists brushed off accusations of political bias with a straight face. Critics during the broadcast targeted this exact contradiction, noting the absurdity of claiming that promotions under a past minister proved independence, while identical patterns under a conservative president were dismissed. Standards stretch and shrink entirely to suit political convenience.

Weaponizing Procedural Legitimacy

When prosecutors discipline or sideline specific figures, they routinely invoke "procedural legitimacy." Yet this vocabulary has rarely served to protect human rights; instead, it has functioned as a sharp weapon to suppress opposing factions. Cases from recent years—such as the indictment of prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong for alleged physical abuse, or the controversial travel ban imposed on former Vice Justice Minister Kim Hak-ui—reveal how indictments approved by senior officials often functioned as political retaliation dressed up in the language of the rule of law.

When prosecutors manipulate indictment decisions based on organizational self-interest and wrap those choices in procedural cellophane, it amounts to collective insubordination. Treating deeply flawed administrative processes as sacred texts to block reform is no longer acceptable. Ultimately, the procedures prosecutors champion serve primarily to shield their own misconduct and maintain institutional power.

Cowardly Prosecutors Hiding Behind Majority Rule

Decision-making inside the prosecution is notoriously cowardly. During the Kim Hak-ui departure ban case, when the investigative team pushed hard for indictments during a meeting chaired by the Deputy Prosecutor General, leadership forced the decision through under the guise of a majority vote. When the fallout inevitably arrived, those same officials engaged in convenient memory loss. As commentators noted, officials often make token statements of concern during closed-door meetings simply so they can later claim they "voted against it," regardless of where they actually signed.

Senior prosecutors hold ultimate authority and bear ultimate responsibility. Deflecting accountability behind the shield of majority rule is a fatal institutional disease. Leaders issue orders when things go smoothly, but vanish like cameos the moment a scandal breaks, claiming they dissented all along. Without shattering this cowardly safety net, any talk of reform remains an empty slogan.

The Launch of New Investigation and Prosecution Agencies

A Scrambled Start with Bare-Minimum Infrastructure

While the blueprints for reform were grandiose, the physical reality of launching the Serious Crimes Investigation Office and the Public Prosecution Office was remarkably threadbare. Both agencies opened their doors in a rush without permanent leadership, leaving offices without basic IT infrastructure, internet cables, or functioning climate control. Reporters noted that newly appointed regional heads walked into empty rooms with bare concrete and makeshift construction zones.

This slapdash administration highlights a deeper crisis of competence. When undertaking a massive structural overhaul of the criminal justice system after seven years of debate, failing to plan basic logistics invites failure. As critics pointed out, the core issue is not merely partisan affiliation, but whether the personnel operating the system possess a genuine reformist mindset. A poorly prepared launch invites administrative chaos, handing ammunition to opponents of reform.

The Desperate Tactics of Former Prosecutor-Attorneys

As direct investigative powers shift away from the traditional prosecution service, former prosecutors who built lucrative careers on exclusive legal connections—known locally as *jeongwan yewu*, or preferential treatment for former officials—face a sudden drying up of their slush funds. Unsurprisingly, they are not sitting quietly. Rumors from the legal community suggest senior veterans are constantly calling junior prosecutors currently working inside the system, whispering that the political tide will eventually turn and urging them to hold their ground.

Their playbook is designed to grind the new system to a halt. The strategy involves instructing remaining personnel inside the new prosecution offices to pick apart and stall every incoming case forwarded by investigative teams, ensuring that cases only move forward when specific high-priced alumni are hired as defense counsel. To block these obstructive legal tactics, the appointment of agency leadership is critical. Observers have argued that leadership should be drawn from judicial or defense backgrounds rather than the prosecutorial ranks, ensuring that leaders can spot and neutralize bureaucratic sabotage.

The Nature of Prosecutorial Fundamentalism

Hannah Arendt’s 'Banality of Evil' Inside the Bureaucracy

One of the most striking analytical parallels raised during recent political debates is the application of Hannah Arendt’s concept of the "banality of evil" to the South Korean prosecution service. The everyday bureaucratic mindset that allowed historic atrocities to occur without personal remorse is precisely mirrored within the Korean prosecutorial apparatus. Officials carry out systemic overreaches while convincing themselves they bear no personal responsibility, simply following standard operating procedures and internal hierarchies.

For decades, a culture has been institutionalized where prosecutors who exercise independent conscience are marginalized, while those who close their eyes to political directives capture high office. Entrusting the future of a democratic state to legal fundamentalists shaped by this internal culture is akin to leaving children in the care of starving tigers. Unless this organizational logic is dismantled, the nation remains effectively colonized by its own legal elite.

The Cowardly Paper Trail of "Objection Statements"

Nothing illustrates the hypocrisy of the prosecution better than the formal mechanism known as the "objection statement." Although the formal principle of a unified prosecutorial voice was ostensibly abolished, senior orders remain absolute law on the ground. When working-level prosecutors are pressured by superiors to pursue questionable investigations and fear taking the fall later, they file formal objection statements—essentially creating a paper shield to say, "I put my dissent in writing so you can't blame me later."

When legal officers charged with upholding justice spend more energy managing bureaucratic liability than protecting the public, the administration of justice collapses into farce. A system that relies on defensive paperwork to survive is fundamentally broken.

The Imperative of Personnel Accountability

Why Institutional Reform Fails Without Structural Purges

A recurring critique from civic participants in recent public forums highlights the missing element in every reform package: genuine personnel accountability. The architects and primary actors behind systemic political crises and institutional abuses have frequently remained untouched while agency titles and building signs were changed. Much like historical failures to address collaborators following periods of authoritarian rule, leaving key perpetrators in positions of power guarantees that old habits will survive.

During past administrations, reform efforts faltered precisely because they stopped at structural rearrangement without addressing the underlying personnel. Relying on technocratic arguments—such as the claim that "only insiders with decades of experience can manage the agency effectively"—invites the very architects of past abuses back through the front door under the banner of professional expertise.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea’s newly restructured investigative and prosecutorial bodies begin their operations, several concrete milestones will determine whether this reform succeeds or dissolves into another half-measured compromise:

  • Leadership Integrity: Watch whether newly appointed regional heads and agency chiefs actively disrupt traditional insider cartels or merely manage the status quo.
  • Case Handling and Warrants: Monitor how the newly separated investigative and public prosecution offices handle high-profile indictments, specifically whether procedural hurdles are used to stall cases or protect political allies.
  • Personnel Accountability: Pay close attention to whether working-level prosecutors who engaged in past political overreaches face transparent administrative reviews or quietly secure promotions.
  • Public Sentiment vs. Institutional Output: Track whether tangible reductions in prosecutorial overreach begin to shift public opinion away from partisan cynicism and toward trust in the new legal framework.
