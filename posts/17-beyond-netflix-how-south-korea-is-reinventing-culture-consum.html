Beyond Netflix: How South Korea Is Reinventing Culture Consumption for the Chuseok Holidays

September 24, 2026

As the annual Chuseok autumn harvest holiday approaches in South Korea, millions face a familiar dilemma. We tell ourselves that this extended break will be different—that we will finally read serious literature, visit art galleries, and nourish our minds rather than binge-watching streaming services until our eyes ache. Yet, when the moment arrives, paralysis sets in. We scroll endlessly, unsure of where to begin in a sea of endless entertainment options.

Recently, a cultural panel discussion broadcast on YouTube attempted to cure this exact paralysis. Hosted by a rotating cast of critics in place of the show's regular host, the session featured three distinct voices from South Korea's contemporary arts landscape: essayist Yang Da-sol, art museum director Lee An, and music critic Lee Dae-hwa. Rather than offering superficial gossip or generic gift guides, these experts dissected the underlying anxieties and desires of modern Korean society. Their unfiltered recommendations reveal much about what contemporary audiences are lacking, and what they desperately seek to heal.

Trading Screen Time for the Heavy Weight of Literature

The conversation began with a direct challenge to digital fatigue: turning off the television and picking up a book. Essayist Yang Da-sol focused first on Agota Kristof’s modern classic The Notebook, The Proof, and The Third Lie (published in Korea by Gachi). As Yang readily admitted with self-deprecating humor, the book is famous enough to sit proudly unread on thousands of bookshelves across the country. "It’s so intimidating," Yang confessed. "It sat on my shelf until I finally cracked it open."

Written in the deceptively simple style of children's diary entries, the trilogy quickly suffocates the reader with the brutal reality of war and displacement. In a society where the threat of geopolitical conflict is never entirely theoretical, Kristof's work forces an uncomfortable confrontation with human nature. For readers looking to strip away lightweight entertainment during a holiday break and probe the darker corners of human psychology, dusting off this European masterwork remains an essential exercise.

Moving from European trauma to domestic history, Yang also championed Jung Ji-a’s novel Sholdol (published by Dongnyok), a spiritual successor to Jung’s critically acclaimed hit My Father’s Liberation Diary—a book previously endorsed heavily by prominent progressive politician and author Rhyu Si-min. Yang praised Sholdol for demonstrating how ordinary, working-class lives—in this case, the harsh existence of a coal miner—connect to the grand, sweeping currents of national history. Even as heavy industry fades from the immediate urban landscape of modern Seoul, the book argues that every individual life leaves a profound, unerasable historical footprint.

The Globalized Art Scene and the Triumph of Mannerism

Shifting from the written page to the visual arts, museum director Lee An provided a window into South Korea’s rapidly evolving gallery scene. The globalization of Korea’s art market has accelerated dramatically in recent years, largely catalyzed by the arrival of Frieze Seoul, which has brought international mega-galleries like White Cube, Thaddaeus Ropac, and Perrotin into direct contact with local collectors. Alongside this commercial boom, world-renowned Korean installation artists like Do Ho Suh—whose immersive fabric architectures such as the "Nest/s" exhibition have captivated crowds at venues like the Seoul Museum of Art—have achieved rare parity between critical acclaim and mainstream popularity.

Yet Lee also urged audiences to look backward to understand the radical nature of artistic independence, highlighting the "500 Years of Spanish Art: From El Greco to Sorolla" exhibition at the Seoul Arts Center. The centerpiece of this collection, El Greco’s The Holy Family, tells a familiar story of institutional rejection. When the Greek-born painter sought patronage at the Spanish royal court during the late Renaissance, the king rejected his work, finding his elongated figures and swirling, unstable forms deeply unsettling.

Instead, El Greco found a home among conservative regional church authorities in Toledo who were willing to embrace his idiosyncratic mannerism. The historical irony—that a monarch's rejections became timeless masterpieces—serves as a sharp reminder in hyper-competitive South Korea that chasing consensus often destroys originality, while sticking to one's unique vision is the only path to endurance.

From Underground Clubs to Morning Raves

Perhaps the most striking evolution discussed by the panel concerned how South Koreans experience music and nightlife. Music critic Lee Dae-hwa pointed out the generational shift in how people listen to recorded music together. Gone are the days of cramped, dimly lit indie music listening circles tucked away in the back alleys of Seoul's Hongdae or Ewha Womans University districts.

Today, these gatherings have been rebranded as "listening parties" or "listening sessions," hosted in sleek, highly curated spaces in trendy neighborhoods like Seongsu-dong, equipped with multi-channel surround-sound systems. Rather than consuming music in isolation through high-end earphones at home, young urbanites gather in high-fidelity public spaces to display their sonic tastes and bond over shared auditory experiences. Music has transformed from a solitary sensory input into a core pillar of social currency and personal identity.

Taking this trend a step further, director Lee An introduced one of the most unexpected cultural crossovers of recent years: the "morning rave." Traditionally, club culture in South Korea has been defined by late-night excess—heavy drinking, crowded dance floors past midnight, and the notorious struggle to catch a taxi at dawn, all of which exact a heavy toll on the following day's professional life.

In response to the burnout of nocturnal nightlife, morning raves begin at 7:00 AM. Attendees show up in running shoes and athletic gear, drink non-alcoholic beverages in spaces pulsing with DJ equipment, dance furiously to work up a sweat, and then head straight to the office feeling refreshed. This strange yet functional marriage of corporate wellness culture and club hedonism illustrates the lengths to which stressed modern urbanites will go to carve out spaces for psychological release without sacrificing their daily routines.

"We ask ourselves whether we are missing what truly matters amidst the daily flood of automated content."

What to Watch Next

  • The Chuseok Literary Test: Monitor whether traditional bookstores see a noticeable bump in physical sales for heavy European classics and localized historical fiction over streaming platforms during the holiday week.
  • The Seongsu-dong Audio Scene: Watch for further commercial expansion of high-end acoustic listening bars as major brands look to sponsor communal music curation spaces targeting Gen Z and Millennial consumers.
  • The Institutionalization of Wellness Parties: Keep an eye on the corporate sponsorship and urban proliferation of sober morning events, which are rapidly transitioning from underground novelties to mainstream lifestyle markers in Seoul's commercial districts.
