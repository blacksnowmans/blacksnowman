A Smear Campaign, a Secret Apologies, and a Party in Turmoil: Inside the Legal Crisis Facing DPK Leader Kim Min-seok

November 06, 2026

Three months after capturing the leadership of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), Kim Min-seok sat in a closed-door meeting of party lawmakers and quietly conceded what his critics had suspected all along. The sensational conspiracy theory that helped propel him to victory—that his primary rivals had formed a secret alliance with Shincheonji, a controversial religious movement widely viewed with deep public hostility in Korea—was completely devoid of evidence.

During the heated national convention, Kim had broadcast these explosive claims from every podium, talk show, and political YouTube channel available. He framed the election as a battle against a "three-way secret alliance" composed of anti-establishment figures, factionalists, and cult operatives. But inside that locked room, far from the cameras and the voting public, his grand conspiracy collapsed into a single, terse line of acknowledgment: an investigation had turned up nothing.

What Kim hoped would be a quiet political closure has instead ignited an unprecedented crisis inside the ruling party. For international observers tracking South Korea’s political landscape under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, this scandal is more than internal party infighting. It represents a potential legal unraveling for the party leadership, raising severe questions under Korean election law, privacy statutes, and the basic norms of party democracy.

The Fabrication of the Shincheonji Conspiracy in the DPK Leadership Primary

To understand why this admission has caused such a earthquake within the DPK, one must understand the political weight of the accusations Kim Min-seok leveled during the party’s national convention. In South Korea, labeling a political opponent as being connected to Shincheonji—a fringe, apocalyptic religious group that has frequently drawn intense public scrutiny and political opprobrium—is the ultimate smear. It is designed to render an opponent politically toxic to mainstream voters.

Throughout the leadership campaign, Kim repeatedly insisted that he possessed concrete evidence of a coordinated effort by Shincheonji to infiltrate the primary and swing the vote toward rival candidates, most notably Representative Jung Chung-rae. Appearing on high-profile political commentary shows, including Kim Ou-joon’s influential YouTube program, Kim Min-seok projected supreme confidence. He reminded viewers of his past political foresight, boasting that he was not the kind of figure to launch baseless accusations without rock-solid backing. In public media appearances throughout October 2024, he promised that a comprehensive report proving organized religious intervention would soon be made public.

The strategy worked. The framing of the election as a defense against a covert cult takeover panicked rank-and-file party members. It sowed deep distrust, polarized the electorate, and significantly altered voting behavior during the primary. Kim secured the leadership post, while his primary rivals were left defending their legitimacy against accusations of colluding with a fringe religious sect.

Yet, ninety days later, the promised evidence never materialized. The admission that no such proof existed has left party members feeling manipulated. For thousands of voters who cast their ballots believing they were protecting the party from external subversion, the realization that the crisis was manufactured from whole cloth has transformed political trust into outrage.

The Legal Trap: Article 52 and the Hypocrisy of Double Standards

The fallout facing Kim Min-seok extends far beyond wounded political pride. It has entered the dangerous territory of criminal liability under Korean law. The primary legal battleground is Article 52 of South Korea's Political Parties Act, which explicitly governs false statements made during internal party elections.

Korean law treats lies told within party primaries with extreme severity to preserve the integrity of democratic institutions:

  • General False Statements: Disseminating false information during an internal election to get elected or to help another get elected carries a penalty of up to three years in prison or a fine of up to 6 million won (about $4,400).
  • Intent to Defeat a Rival: If false claims are distributed with the specific intent of causing a rival candidate to lose—as appears to be the case here—the punishment escalates significantly to up to five years in prison or a fine of up to 10 million won (about $7,300).

Under South Korean law, a conviction resulting in a prison sentence or a substantial fine can strip an individual of their right to run for public office, effectively ending their political career. Legal experts point out that Kim’s closed-door admission is effectively a "smoking gun." By officially acknowledging that his claims had no factual basis, Kim has handed prosecutors and internal critics definitive proof that the statements disseminated during the campaign were false.

Defenders of the party leader have attempted to argue that Kim never explicitly mentioned candidate names on stage, and that his remarks were merely "political expressions" common in high-stakes campaigning. However, South Korean legal precedent does not require an explicit name to establish criminal defamation or false statement violations. Under established jurisprudence, if the context, timing, and audience make it obvious who the target is, legal identification is satisfied. In a two-horse leadership race, attacking the opposing camp as a cult-backed faction unambiguously targeted Jung Chung-rae and his supporters.

This defense creates a glaring moral dilemma for the Democratic Party of Korea. During the presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—the DPK fiercely demanded strict judicial punishment against Mr. Yoon for making false statements under election laws. When Mr. Yoon’s defense team argued that his contested remarks were off-the-cuff political debate, DPK lawmakers rejected the defense, insisting that falsehoods corrupting election integrity must be prosecuted without exception.

For the DPK to now adopt the exact same defense it once condemned creates an embarrassing double standard. Applying a lenient standard to its own leadership while demanding maximum penalties for political enemies undermines the party’s moral authority as the governing party under President Lee Jae-myung.

Backfiring Defenses and Escalating Criminal Risks

As party loyalists scrambled to protect Kim Min-seok, their defense strategies unexpectedly opened up new legal vulnerabilities, exposing the leadership to additional criminal investigations.

In an effort to soften the blow of Kim's admission, some allied lawmakers claimed that the party had indeed observed signs of mass enrollments by Shincheonji followers, even if a formal "three-way alliance" could not be proven. However, this line of defense immediately triggered alarms under South Korea’s strict privacy regime. Legal analyst Kim Kwang-min publicly raised a critical objection: under the Personal Information Protection Act, an individual's religious affiliation is classified as highly sensitive personal data.

"To claim that the party confirmed Shincheonji members were joining means someone cross-referenced party membership registries against church member lists. Religion is sensitive personal data protected by law. Obtaining and matching such lists through legal means is virtually impossible. The party must explain exactly how it obtained those church rosters."

If party officials or campaign staff illegally acquired, possessed, or cross-analyzed religious membership rosters to vet party voters, they face separate, severe criminal prosecution under privacy legislation. Furthermore, weaponizing religious membership in a political contest touches upon sensitive constitutional terrain. Article 20 of the South Korean Constitution guarantees the separation of church and state. By making religious identity a tool for political purging and internal surveillance, the leadership risks embroiling the ruling party in constitutional controversies.

Recognizing the legal exposure, senior figures within the DPK are breaking ranks. Supreme Council member Lee Sung-yoon, a former high-ranking prosecutor, publicly demanded during a leadership meeting that the party launch a formal internal investigation under Article 52 of the Political Parties Act. When a former prosecutor sitting on the party's highest executive body invokes criminal statutes against his own party leader, it signals that the legal risks are impossible to sweep under the rug.

"Smear on the Podium, Apologize Behind Closed Doors"

Beyond the legal statutes, the political management of the apology has incensed the DPK’s rank-and-file base. The contrast between how the accusation was delivered and how the apology was rendered has exposed deep divisions within the ruling party.

The smear campaign was launched in full view of the national press, streamed on major online networks, and delivered from bright campaign stages. The retraction, however, was delivered in a closed-door meeting of lawmakers, away from the public eye, summarized in a brief sentence. General party members and the targets of the smear were left out of the room entirely.

Jung Chung-rae, the former Supreme Council member targeted by the allegations, captured the mood of the aggrieved faction in a succinct Facebook post: "An apology is the duty of the perpetrator; forgiveness is the right of the victim."

Rather than facilitating reconciliation, the leadership's handling of the aftermath has been marked by attempts to silence internal dissent. Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee disclosed that when she tried to raise questions during an executive meeting regarding the origins of the Shincheonji claims, she was shut down and told to drop the topic entirely. This heavy-handed attempt to suppress inquiry has convinced many grassroots members that the secret apology was not an act of genuine repentance, but a calculated political maneuver to avoid accountability while keeping the party leadership intact.

Grassroots anger is now mobilizing. Nine prominent DPK member organizations have formed a coalition to demand Kim Min-seok’s immediate resignation. They are invoking Article 22 of the DPK Party Constitution, which enshrines party member sovereignty, arguing that a leader who used fabricated intelligence to usurp the votes of party members has lost the legitimacy to govern.

What to Watch Next

The crisis surrounding Kim Min-seok is rapidly shifting from political debate to judicial execution. Over the coming weeks, several key developments will determine whether the DPK leader can survive the fallout:

  • Police and Prosecutorial Action: Multiple civic groups and party members have filed criminal complaints alleging violations of Article 52 of the Political Parties Act and the Personal Information Protection Act. Watch for whether law enforcement formally opens an investigation or issues subpoenas for campaign communication records.
  • Ethics Tribunal Petitions: DPK members are submitting formal petitions to the party's Ethics Tribunal, demanding the maximum internal penalties: suspension of party rights, expulsion, or removal from office. How the tribunal handles these petitions will test whether the ruling party retains internal self-regulatory mechanisms.
  • Court Injunctions: Disgruntled party members are preparing to file an injunction with the civil courts to suspend Kim Min-seok’s duties as party leader. If a court accepts the argument that the election outcome was tainted by illegal false statements, the DPK could be thrown into leadership paralysis.
  • Impact on President Lee Jae-myung’s Agenda: As the ruling party, the DPK needs absolute cohesion to drive President Lee Jae-myung's legislative priorities through the National Assembly. Prolonged internal warfare over election fraud allegations risks paralyzing the ruling bloc and handing significant leverage to the main opposition People Power Party.

In democratic politics, errors in policy can be debated and corrected. But when an election victory is secured through manufactured smears, the damage strikes at the core of democratic legitimacy. For Kim Min-seok and the Democratic Party of Korea, the closed-door admission was not the end of a political nuisance—it was the opening chapter of a legal reckoning.
