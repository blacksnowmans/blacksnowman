Insurrection Disputes and Shield Accusations: How South Korea’s Presidential Audit Ground to a Halt

October 30, 2026

Inside the committee rooms of South Korea’s National Assembly in Yeouido, parliamentary audits are rarely quiet affairs. Yet on the morning of October 29, the House Steering Committee—the legislative body charged with the direct oversight of the Presidential Office—descended into extraordinary chaos almost before the opening gavel had finished echoing. Shouting matches erupted across the aisle, lawmakers exchanged furious rebukes, and within two hours of convening, the session was forced into an abrupt recess. Rather than a rigorous line-by-line review of executive policy under President Lee Jae-myung, the audit quickly collapsed under the weight of unresolved constitutional trauma and bitter partisan score-settling.

To foreign observers, parliamentary disruptions might look like familiar political theater. But in South Korea, the annual audit (known locally as gukjeong gamseon) is a central pillar of legislative power, where lawmakers have sweeping authority to summon high-ranking officials and demand state records. When the House Steering Committee freezes, the entire apparatus for vetting the nation’s highest office stalls. What derailed the October 29 hearing was a raw clash over two intractable issues: the refusal of an official to apologize for comments downplaying military insurrection, and an aggressive push by the conservative opposition to turn the hearing into an inquiry against President Lee’s inner circle.

The Ghost of Insurrection: The Showdown over Kim Tae-gyu

The primary trigger for the immediate paralysis was the presence of Kim Tae-gyu. Kim had drawn fierce national backlash for past remarks that appeared to defend or deny the insurrectionary nature of historical military strongman Chun Doo-hwan’s coup, as well as the unconstitutional actions of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. In South Korea's current political climate, this is not an academic debate. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was stripped of his presidency through constitutional impeachment and is currently serving time in prison following his catastrophic attempt to overturn the constitutional order. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, treating Mr. Yoon’s actions as anything less than an illegal rebellion strikes at the very heart of the democratic republic.

From the moment the session began, Democratic Party lawmakers argued that someone who actively denies historical and contemporary insurrections has no constitutional standing to oversee state affairs or audit the Presidential Office. Lawmakers cited Kim's past disregard for investigations into tragedies such as the Itaewon crowd crush, demanding his immediate resignation from his Steering Committee duties and an unequivocal apology on the record.

Instead of seeking common ground, Kim’s response in the committee room intensified the standoff. Faced with formal demands for an apology regarding his duty to uphold the Constitution, Kim refused to offer one. At one point, he went so far as to cover his ears as lawmakers voiced their formal objections—a visual slight that Democratic Party members condemned as an open insult to the authority of the National Assembly. As shouting intensified between ruling lawmakers demanding his removal and opposition figures attempting to shield him, proceedings became impossible to conduct, forcing the committee chair to call a recess after barely two hours.

A Bitter Proxy War over Key Witnesses and 'Bulletproof' Allegations

While the ruling Democratic Party focused on constitutional integrity, the conservative opposition People Power Party launched a parallel offensive aimed squarely at the executive branch. The People Power Party accused the ruling party of constructing a "bulletproof shield" to protect close aides of President Lee Jae-myung by blocking their appearance as audit witnesses.

The core of the opposition's attack centered on the refusal to summon figures such as Kim Hyun-ji and Jeong Jin-sang. Depicting these figures as unaccountable power brokers operating behind the scenes, conservative lawmakers escalated their rhetoric with historical caste analogies, accusing the administration of creating an untouchable hierarchy within state institutions. They claimed the administration was treating specific figures as though they stood above the Supreme Court or the legislature itself.

Democratic Party lawmakers dismissed the opposition's witness list demands as a calculated sideshow. Democratic floor leader Han Byung-do countered that the People Power Party’s true motive was not responsible governance or administrative scrutiny, but a deliberate effort to inflict political damage on President Lee Jae-myung's administration. Rather than evaluating the government’s policies on economic livelihood, diplomatic initiatives, and institutional recovery, Han argued, the opposition was attempting to dredge up recycled controversies, old rumors surrounding presidential quarters, and debunked personal allegations.

Democratic Party Floor Leader Han Byung-do warned that baseless attacks in the audit chamber would carry tangible consequences, specifically cautioning People Power Party lawmaker Joo Jin-woo that peddling defamatory claims would invite strict legal liability.

Gridlock, Adjournment, and an Uneasy Backroom Compromise

With both sides entrenched, the committee remained deadlocked for most of the day. A second attempt to resume the hearing met the exact same roadblock: Democratic Party members insisted that an unrepentant official who denies the illegality of past insurrections could not participate, while the People Power Party accused the majority of silencing dissent and staging a legislative blackout.

The impasse highlighted the deep structural tension in Yeouido following the restoration of civilian constitutional norms under the Lee Jae-myung administration. For the ruling Democratic Party, drawing a firm line against figures who minimize the crimes of Chun Doo-hwan and Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is seen as a baseline civic obligation—a necessary defense against normalizing authoritarian overreach. For the opposition People Power Party, which has struggled to redefine its identity in the post-impeachment landscape, the audit provided an opportunity to mount an offensive against the executive branch by alleging cronyism.

Late in the day, after repeated suspensions, committee leaders reached an uneasy procedural arrangement: the audit would move forward only by effectively excluding Kim Tae-gyu from the proceedings. By sidelining the official whose presence ignited the day’s fury, lawmakers tacitly acknowledged that the hearing could not function while he remained at the table. Yet the compromise came only after a full day of legislative business had been lost to procedural combat.

What to Watch Next

The breakdown of the October 29 Steering Committee audit leaves behind sharp animosities that will shape the remainder of the legislative calendar. Several key developments will determine whether parliamentary proceedings can return to regular order:

  • Legal Actions Against Slander: Watch whether Floor Leader Han Byung-do and the Democratic Party follow through on formal legal complaints against Representative Joo Jin-woo and other opposition members over defamation and ungrounded accusations made during the hearing.
  • The Status of Kim Tae-gyu: Sidelining Kim from a single hearing does not resolve his broader role. Look for continued pressure from ruling party lawmakers to secure his permanent resignation or formal censure from key committee positions.
  • Substantive Policy Scrutiny: With the audit clock ticking, the committee must still address critical domestic priorities—including state budgeting, economic relief measures, and institutional reforms under President Lee Jae-myung. Observers should track whether the opposition shifts toward genuine policy critique or doubles down on targeting presidential aides as the session draws to a close.
