Inside the Lame-Duck Presidency: Infighting, Leaks, and the Cracking Foundation of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 05, 2026

Less than two years into its term, South Korea's ruling administration is exhibiting classic, unmistakable symptoms of a lame-duck presidency. On the surface, official briefings maintain a veneer of calm, but beneath the waterline, a wave of leaked documents, bitter factional infighting, and the abrupt departure of top aides reveal an executive branch under severe internal strain. For outside observers watching Seoul's fast-paced political arena, these developments offer a revealing look at the fragility of power when public support begins to erode.

The recent chaos—spilling out through political gossip sheets, independent YouTube broadcasts, and explosive whistleblowing by journalists—goes far beyond typical political maneuvering. It exposes the raw, unvarnished realities of an administration struggling to keep its house in order. To understand why these cracks matter for South Korea's political trajectory, it is necessary to look past the official press releases and examine the structural fractures widening inside the highest offices of power.

The Resignation of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik and the Fall of the 'Seongnam Line'

The first major tremor came in late October, when Kang Hoon-sik, the presidential chief of staff, abruptly tendered his resignation after roughly 15 months in office. While a 15-month tenure is not exceptionally short by historical standards—since South Korea's democratization, presidential chiefs of staff have served an average of about 13 months—the manner of Kang's departure was anything but routine.

Days prior, political gossip sheets—widely known in Korea as jjirashi—circulated explosive claims regarding internal turmoil at the Blue House. According to these reports, a controversial personnel mishap had initially focused public scrutiny on other officials, while shielding Kang. This reportedly infuriated President Lee Jae-myung, who allegedly confronted Kang with the accusation, "Did you orchestrate this leak?" While such gossip must be taken with a grain of salt, Kang's sudden exit immediately afterward lent considerable weight to the rumors.

More importantly, Kang's departure triggered a fierce tug-of-war over his successor. Reports emerged that the "Seongnam Line"—the tight-knit faction of loyalists who rose alongside Lee during his time as mayor of Seongnam—attempted to push their own candidate for chief of staff. However, pushback from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the primary center-left party currently in power) reportedly blocked the appointment. If true, this represents a symbolic turning point: the once-untouchable Seongnam faction, which rode the president's coattails to absolute influence, is rapidly losing its grip on the inner workings of government. Power is a zero-sum game, and as approval ratings slip, internal loyalty is the first casualty.

Whistleblowing from the Press Corps and the Anatomy of a Cover-Up

As internal discipline frays, the traditional barrier separating the executive office from the public eye has begun to crumble. A striking example emerged when journalist Park Hyun-gwang appeared on an independent commentary program to air a startling grievance: he claimed to have experienced institutional ostracization and bullying within the presidential press corps.

In South Korea, relations between the government and the media carry deep historical baggage. The press room system dates back to authoritarian eras, evolving over decades into an entrenched, exclusive club where access was tightly managed. While successive democratic governments have attempted to reform these briefing rooms, the tension between state management of information and journalistic independence remains a reliable barometer of a regime's health.

For a credentialed reporter to publicly accuse the administration of blackballing him on a political broadcast indicates just how thoroughly the executive's moral authority has cratered. Park clearly calculated that the regime was no longer strong enough to retaliate effectively. Predictably, the digital trail of the interview was quickly scrubbed under the pretext of protecting the reporter, but in the era of short-form video and viral online communities, trying to suppress such information is like trying to block the sun with a hand. No one within the administration's press office faced disciplinary action for the incident—largely because they were only following orders from above.

Factional Fault Lines and the Spectre of a Split in the Democratic Party

President Lee Jae-myung’s rise to power was not without friction. Unlike traditional stalwarts of the liberal camp who trace their lineage back to former presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in, Lee built his base on the party's periphery. Many veteran figures within the Democratic Party knew Lee well before his national ascent, and they harbor deep reservations about his leadership style.

This history explains why, even as presidential approval ratings plummet, lawmakers rushing out to mount blind, vociferous defenses of the administration are remarkably few in number. A symbiotic political alliance forged out of short-term utility rather than shared philosophy is structurally fragile. If the president's ratings continue to drag down the broader party's standing ahead of future local and national elections, a fracture within the Democratic Party becomes a mathematical certainty.

On one side sit lawmakers whose entire political survival is tethered directly to Lee's personal faction; on the other are traditionalist party members striving to preserve the institutional heritage of past progressive governments. As ideological coherence takes a backseat to raw pragmatism, the absence of a shared political vision leaves the administration increasingly isolated.

Echoes of Past Collapses and the Trap of Isolation

In presidential systems, public approval is the ultimate engine of governance. When that engine stalls, it triggers a predictable sequence: a freefall in authority, mutual recriminations among inner-circle elites, scandals involving presidential aides, and ambitious potential successors rushing to distance themselves.

Critics of the administration note an unsettling parallel to the final, embattled days of the previous conservative administration led by Yoon Suk Yeol. Like his predecessor, President Lee has shown an increasing tendency to grow defensive, brush aside constructive criticism, and isolate himself from dissenting voices within his own camp. Commentators have even drawn comparisons to psychological thrillers, suggesting that powerful leaders become so consumed by paranoia that they systematically push away anyone who remembers their past vulnerabilities.

Perhaps the most telling commentary on this political paralysis comes from a wry piece of dark humor circulating among political pundits: "If we simply pretend Lee Jae-myung is a conservative president, he's actually doing a remarkably good job for a conservative." It is a cynical coping mechanism born of disappointment—a sign that a segment of the progressive base no longer views the current administration as a true torchbearer of their movement.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea heads toward a politically charged winter, several concrete milestones will determine whether the administration can arrest its decline or slide further toward total paralysis:

    The Successor to the Chief of Staff: Watch who ultimately fills the vacant chief of staff position. If the post goes to an independent figure with broad party backing, it signals a concession to the party mainstream. If it goes to another hardline loyalist, expect the internal civil war to intensify.
    Local Election Maneuvering: Keep an eye on figures like former chief of staff Kang Hoon-sik and whether he transitions directly into a high-profile electoral race, such as a run for Seoul mayor. Candidate positioning by ruling party heavyweights will show just how aggressively prospective successors are trying to insulate themselves from a sinking presidency.
    Polling and Party Defections: Monitor whether ruling party lawmakers begin openly breaking ranks on key legislative votes or policy initiatives. A definitive split between Lee's loyalists and traditional party factions will be marked by public policy rebellions in parliament.

The core question facing the administration is simple: Can the Blue House step on the brakes before the vehicle goes off the cliff, or will it continue accelerating into the abyss?
