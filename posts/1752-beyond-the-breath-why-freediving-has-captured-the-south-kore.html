Beyond the Breath: Why Freediving has Captured the South Korean Imagination

October 31, 2026

Imagine, for a moment, the deafening silence of the deep ocean. There is no engine roar, no chatter of a crowded Seoul subway, and no ping of a smartphone notification. There is only the rhythmic beat of your own heart and the crushing, yet strangely comforting, embrace of the water. For a growing number of South Koreans, this sub-aquatic silence is becoming the ultimate luxury. In a society often criticized for its "Pali-pali" (hurry-up) culture and relentless competitive pressure, the sport of freediving—diving on a single breath without the aid of oxygen tanks—has evolved from a niche extreme hobby into a mainstream lifestyle movement centered on mental healing and self-confrontation.

The final week of October brought this trend into sharp focus. From high-profile celebrity "failures" to record-breaking human endurance and brewing international controversies, the world of freediving is reflecting the anxieties and aspirations of modern life. In South Korea, where the "healing" industry is a multi-billion dollar sector, the act of holding one's breath is being rebranded as the ultimate form of meditation. It is no longer just about how deep you can go, but about how well you can know yourself in the dark.

The Humanization of the "Mermaid" Challenge

In the world of Korean entertainment, image is everything. Celebrities are usually expected to showcase perfection, but actress Yu In-young recently broke this mold by sharing a deeply personal account of her struggle with the sport. Yu, a well-known figure in Korean television and film, documented her journey to obtain an AIDA2 certification. AIDA, or the International Association for the Development of Apnea, is the global governing body for the sport, and the AIDA2 level is the first step toward becoming a truly autonomous freediver, requiring students to master both "static" (holding breath while stationary) and "dynamic" (swimming distance) disciplines, as well as reaching depths of up to 20 meters.

Yu’s declaration that she would "become a mermaid" was met with initial excitement, but it was her subsequent confession of failure that truly resonated with the public. She admitted to feeling "impatient and upset" after failing the certification test, citing difficulties with equalization—the process of balancing the pressure in the ears as one descends. For many Koreans, seeing a glamorous star admit to a physical and emotional setback was refreshing. It highlighted a core truth of the sport: the water does not care about your status or your fame. If your mind is not calm, your body will refuse to descend.

This "failure" narrative is actually a major selling point for freediving in Korea. In a culture where the Lee Jae-myung administration continues to navigate a high-pressure economic landscape, the idea that it is "okay to fail" provided a rare moment of catharsis. Yu’s experience demonstrated that freediving is a "meditative silence" rather than a display of raw strength. It has become a drama of self-growth where the obstacle is not the depth of the water, but the weight of one's own expectations.

The Philosophy of the Abyss: Returning to the Surface

The cultural shift toward freediving is further evidenced by recent deep-dives—pun intended—by major media outlets. GQ Korea recently interviewed prominent athletes sponsored by Garmin, the Swiss-American tech giant that has become the dominant provider of diving computers in the Korean market. Athletes Kim Woong-jin and Oh Hye-geum spoke not of records, but of the philosophy of survival. Kim’s mantra, "You must know how to return to go deeper," serves as a poignant metaphor for life in a hyper-competitive society. It suggests that true ambition requires the wisdom of self-restraint.

Oh Hye-geum’s perspective was equally grounded, telling the magazine, "It's okay if you can't do it today; you learn more on those days." This mindset is a direct challenge to the traditional Korean educational and corporate ethos, where a "bad day" is often seen as a lack of effort. In freediving, a bad day—where your breath is short or your ears won't pop—is simply data. It is an objective observation of your body’s current state. This psychological benefit, often referred to as "underwater meditation," is why many tech workers in Pangyo (Korea's Silicon Valley) and office workers in Gangnam are flocking to indoor diving pools like DeepStation.

"In the water, you cannot lie to yourself. If you are stressed, your heart rate will be high, and your dive will be short. The water is the ultimate lie detector."

Global Feats and Ethical Shadows

While Korea focuses on the lifestyle and healing aspects, the international freediving community has been rocked by events that push the boundaries of human biology and professional ethics. In Croatia, a diver recently set a staggering record by holding his breath for 29 minutes and 3 seconds. While this was achieved through "oxygen-assisted" apnea—where the diver breathes 100% pure oxygen before the attempt—the feat remains a testament to human endurance. Enduring the buildup of carbon dioxide and the involuntary contractions of the diaphragm for nearly half an hour is a physiological marvel that has fascinated Korean sports scientists and hobbyists alike.

However, the sport's growing popularity has also brought its darker side to light. Ocean Ramsey, a world-famous American influencer and shark conservationist known for her "glamour" shots with Great White sharks, has recently come under fire. Reports from media outlets like Dexerto suggest a toxic work environment behind the scenes of her operations. Allegations include unpaid training for staff and, more alarmingly, pressuring employees to dive in dangerous conditions without adequate safety protocols.

For the Korean community, which prides itself on the "Buddy System" and strict safety adherence, this news was a sobering reminder. Freediving is a sport built entirely on trust. If the leader of a community or a company devalues the safety of their team for the sake of a viral video, the entire foundation of the sport is compromised. It serves as a cautionary tale: as freediving becomes more commercialized and "Instagrammable," the core value of human life must remain the priority.

Technical Mastery and the "Leaky Glottis"

As the sport matures in Korea, the conversation is moving from "how do I start?" to more nuanced technical discussions. On platforms like Reddit's r/freediving, which is closely watched by English-speaking expats in Korea and tech-savvy locals, the "Leaky Glottis" has become a hot topic. This phenomenon occurs during the final stages of ascent when the air in the lungs expands as pressure decreases. If a diver cannot keep their glottis (the part of the larynx consisting of the vocal cords) tightly closed, air can leak out, leading to a loss of buoyancy or even a blackout.

These technical hurdles are being met with a burgeoning market for high-end equipment. Korean divers are moving away from generic gear and toward specialized tools. There is a growing emphasis on "low-volume" masks—masks that have very little air space inside, making them easier to equalize at depth. Brands like Diveinn have become popular for direct imports, though many local beginners are warned to test masks in person to ensure a perfect seal. After all, a leaking mask at 20 meters (about 65 feet) is more than an annoyance; it’s a distraction that can lead to panic.

The equipment trend is also shifting toward aesthetics and functionality. "Skin" material suits, which reduce friction and look sleek in underwater photography, are becoming the standard for those training in Seoul’s Olympic-sized pools. Meanwhile, long fins made of carbon fiber are the "status symbol" of the diving world, though experts warn that beginners should stick to more flexible plastic or fiberglass fins to avoid muscle strain and focus on proper kicking technique.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the winter months, the focus of the Korean freediving community will shift from the open seas of Jeju Island to the high-tech indoor pools that dot the peninsula. Here is what to keep an eye on in the coming months:

  • The "Safety First" Regulatory Shift: Following the controversies surrounding international influencers, expect South Korean diving associations to double down on safety certifications and "Buddy" requirements in commercial pools.
  • Celebrity Resurgence: Yu In-young has already signaled her intent to retake her AIDA2 exam. Her success (or second failure) will likely spark another wave of public interest in the "honesty" of the sport.
  • Technological Integration: Watch for Garmin and other wearable tech companies to release more "lifestyle" oriented diving watches that bridge the gap between a daily smartwatch and a professional dive computer, catering to the "weekend warrior" diver.
  • Winter Diving Tourism: While the domestic waters cool down, expect a surge in "diving tours" to the Philippines and the Maldives, where Korean communities have established dedicated resorts catering to the specific needs of Seoul’s freedivers.

Ultimately, the rise of freediving in South Korea is a symptom of a society looking for a way to breathe again. In the deep, there is no one to impress and no one to compete with. There is only the next breath, and the quiet realization that, sometimes, the bravest thing you can do is simply stay calm in the dark.
