The Trial That Could Unseat Seoul’s Mayor: Oh Se-hoon and South Korea's Political Brokers

October 04, 2026

Walking through Seoul's political landscape right now feels less like observing a stable democracy and more like watching a high-stakes legal thriller where the ending remains completely unwritten. At the very center of this drama is Oh Se-hoon, the high-profile mayor of Seoul and a heavy hitter in South Korea's conservative camp. As Oh fights for his political life in an appellate court, the case has pulled back the curtain on a murky underworld of political brokers, unrecorded campaign funds, and the uncomfortable closeness between elected officials and the fixers who help them win.

For readers outside South Korea, local political scandals often look like standard bureaucratic squabbles. But the legal battle surrounding Mayor Oh hits at the foundational plumbing of how elections are financed and won in one of Asia's most vibrant democracies. With a crucial appellate ruling looming, the stakes are enormous not just for Oh's career, but for the stability of the Seoul metropolitan government itself.

Inside the Appellate Court: The Battle Over a Disputed Recording

The core of Oh's defense in his ongoing appellate trial rests on a single piece of evidence: a telephone recording dated March 6, 2021. The audio features a conversation between Myung Tae-kyun, a controversial political broker, and Kim Han-jung, a businessman and political backer, recorded shortly after Oh was confirmed as the candidate for the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, in the Seoul mayoral race.

In the recording, Myung can be heard saying, "Anyway, Oh Se-hoon doesn't know. He doesn't know, so don't worry. I'll handle it. Did I ever take a single penny from Oh Se-hoon?" Kim chimes in to suggest that no one demanded it and that he acted independently. Oh's legal team has seized upon these snippets to argue that the illegal payment for opinion polls—which landed Oh a guilty verdict in the first trial—was initiated by rogue associates acting on their own accord, without the mayor's knowledge or consent.

Special prosecutors, however, are pushing back hard against this narrative. They point out that out of some 35 recorded files in possession, the defense hand-picked just three that favored the mayor, raising serious questions about the integrity and completeness of the evidence. Legal experts remain skeptical that these selective audio clips can dismantle the solid web of circumstantial evidence that convinced a lower court to hand down a guilty verdict.

The Weight of the First-Insance Verdict and Fading Alibis

To understand why this appellate trial is so tense, one must look back at the first-trial ruling. A lower court previously found Oh guilty of violating the Public Official Election Act and the Political Fund Act, sentencing him to a fine of 10 million won (about $7,000) and an order to forfeit 21 million won (about $15,000). Under South Korean law, any elected local government official who receives a fine of 1 million won or more that is upheld by the Supreme Court is automatically stripped of their office.

The first-trial judges carefully examined ten opinion polls provided by Myung, concluding that Oh was fully aware of and directed half of them. Even though Oh was acquitted on the other five counts, the court made it clear that the core of the illegal funding scheme remained intact. Special prosecutors have maintained their original sentencing demands of a year and a half in prison and heavier forfeitures, signaling that the prosecution views the lower court's logic as fundamentally sound.

Compounding Oh's troubles is the collapse of a key logical defense. The mayor's camp had hoped to lean heavily on the testimony of Kim Chong-in, a veteran political heavyweight and former emergency committee chairman for the conservative party, to frame the events as decisions made entirely by party elders outside Oh's purview. Yet when Kim took the stand, he drew a firm line, stating he never had such deep conversations with Myung and offering no alibi to shield the mayor. Stripped of this protective narrative, Oh's defense has narrowed significantly.

Political Brokers and the Reality of South Korean Election Culture

The scandal highlights a persistent and toxic gray area in Korean politics: the reliance on shadowy political brokers who operate just beyond the reach of formal campaign laws. Figures like Myung pull strings behind the scenes, conducting polls, orchestrating narratives, and trading on influence, only for mainstream politicians to distance themselves when the legal hammer finally falls.

When questioned by reporters outside the courthouse, a visibly tense Mayor Oh snapped at journalists, repeating his refrain that "the truth will always come out" before brushing past them. Yet legal observers note that a confident politician usually displays a calmer demeanor. The defensive reactions suggest a deep underlying anxiety that the appellate court will see through the fragmented recordings and uphold the lower court's career-ending judgment.

If the appellate court upholds the original sentence—or if it is finalized by the Supreme Court—Seoul will face a major constitutional and administrative shock. A mayoral by-election would plunge the capital city back into political turmoil, wasting public resources and disrupting city administration just as major urban projects are underway.

What to Watch Next

Key Milestones to Follow:

  • The Appellate Ruling: Watch for the exact timeline of the appellate court's verdict, which will determine whether Mayor Oh retains his office or faces immediate disqualification pending a final Supreme Court appeal.
  • Election Invalidity Petitions: Monitor separate legal challenges regarding administrative hiccups during recent local elections, which could independently trigger a massive by-election in Seoul if courts or election commissioners intervene.
  • The Broker's Next Moves: Pay attention to how political broker Myung Tae-kyun and his legal representatives respond as additional audio files and testimonies are weighed, which could further damage or reshape the defense strategies of mainstream politicians involved.

Ultimately, the legal battles surrounding Seoul's leadership are a test of whether South Korea's judicial system can hold high-ranking executives accountable to the same rules as ordinary citizens. As the court prepares to deliver its final verdict on these tangled webs of campaign funding and broker influence, the rest of the country—and international observers watching the health of Korean democracy—will be holding their breath.
