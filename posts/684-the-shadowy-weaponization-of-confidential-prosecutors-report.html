Black Snowman

The Shadowy Weaponization of Confidential Prosecutors' Reports in South Korea

September 11, 2026

When you listen to the rumors currently swirling in South Korean political circles, it is hard not to feel a cynical sense of disbelief. It forces one to ask: Is this truly the state of the rule of law in the Republic of Korea? The recent controversy surrounding a so-called "indictment plan report" disclosed by lawmaker Han Dong-hoon is more than just another partisan spat; it is a clinical display of the festering rot within the nation’s prosecution service. Watching how investigative secrets are leaked, weaponized, and deployed as a tool for political assassination is nothing short of chilling.

We are no longer discussing mere "suspicions." We have entered an era where confidential internal prosecutorial documents are being plastered all over social media. Where did this document come from? Whose hands did it pass through before reaching Han, a prominent member of the People Power Party (the main conservative party in South Korea)? Why now? As we peel back the layers of this incident, we find a reality where a powerful state agency appears to be privatizing its authority to serve political ends.

The Anatomy of a Leak: Why This Isn't Just an "Accident"

During a recent parliamentary interpellation, Vice Minister of Justice Lee Jin-soo stated the obvious: "Internal reports created during the decision-making process of the prosecution must not be leaked externally under any circumstances." Yet, the reality is far removed from this principle. As legal experts have noted, these "indictment plan reports" are classified internal documents that are not even included in official criminal trial records. They are essentially private strategic memos for prosecutors. The fact that a document inaccessible to defense attorneys has found its way into the public sphere is definitive proof that this leak could only have originated from within the organization itself.

The destructive potential of such a document is immense. It outlines precisely how prosecutors intend to manipulate a case, what specific sentences they hope to secure, and who they intend to drag into the net. This is a malicious act designed for "branding"—marking a suspect as a criminal in the court of public opinion before they have even seen their day in court. When a law enforcement agency treats sensitive, non-public data as a political pamphlet to be distributed to allies, it confirms that institutional discipline has completely collapsed.

Equally frustrating is the response of Han Dong-hoon. While he claims he "did not receive any materials from the prosecution," few believe this at face value. Someone leaked the data; someone used it for political gain. How long are we expected to watch this cartel operate under the guise of "whistleblowing" while the rule of law is dismantled? This leak must be investigated transparently. Otherwise, all internal investigative files will effectively become advertising flyers for political hit jobs.

"Poison" and "Big Brother" Frames: The Art of Political Theater

The prosecution has a peculiar, recurring habit: when they cannot find enough evidence to build a solid case, they resort to the "suspension of indictment" (a decision not to prosecute despite suspecting guilt). In a healthy system, this is reserved for cases where mercy is warranted. In the hands of politically motivated prosecutors, however, it is a "dirty trick" used to avoid the embarrassment of a "not guilty" verdict. By refusing to drop the charges entirely, they leave a lingering, unverified stain on the target’s reputation, which can then be dragged out whenever the political climate demands.

Consider the recent case involving Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Seung-won. The prosecution attempted to frame his legitimate legislative inquiry regarding a citizen's petition as an illicit "solicitation." In reality, no money changed hands, and no personal gain was realized. Yet, the prosecution treated it like a major criminal scandal to sway public opinion. This is the classic "dusting" technique: search a target’s life until you find a speck of dirt, label it a crime, and perform a character assassination.

Lawmaker Han Dong-hoon has even taken to calling this the "big brother and poison case" in public statements, suggesting a soap-opera-like plot where a woman tries to kill her brother with poison. Such rhetoric from a former legal professional is incredibly base. By reducing a complex administrative matter—in this case, coordinating the swift processing of COVID-19 treatment approvals—to a sensationalized narrative, he diverts the public’s attention away from the actual, boring reality of public service and toward a fabricated, scandalous "evil."

The Road Ahead: Who Watches the Watchmen?

The Democratic Party has finally formed a task force to address these issues, but the task ahead is gargantuan. The core of this problem is the privatization of judicial information. When a law enforcement agency becomes a political actor, the entire concept of a fair trial vanishes. The use of these leaks to destroy political opponents is a direct threat to the democratic process.

What should we watch next?

  • The Investigation's Reach: Will the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) or the police have the spine to conduct a truly independent investigation into the source of the leak? Given their track record, skepticism is high, but this is the ultimate test of their independence.
  • Legal Accountability: We need to see if the legal concept of "official secrets disclosure" is applied equally to high-profile politicians, regardless of their status or party affiliation.
  • The "Han Dong-hoon" Factor: We must monitor whether this, and similar disclosures, are shielded under the "parliamentary immunity" of a lawmaker, or if they constitute an abuse of process that falls outside the scope of legitimate legislative work.

The prosecution is meant to hold the power of indictment, not act as a political vanguard. Those who treat confidential files as political weapons are not serving the law; they are subverting it. We live in an era where investigative secrets are being sold for cheap political points. It is time for the public to demand that the prosecution be stripped of its ability to act as a law unto itself. If these patterns are not broken, the South Korean judicial system risks becoming nothing more than a trash bin of broken trust.
