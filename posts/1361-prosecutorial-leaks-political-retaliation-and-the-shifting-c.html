Prosecutorial Leaks, Political Retaliation, and the Shifting Culture of Korean Street Protests

October 20, 2026

To understand the friction at the heart of South Korea’s legal and political system, one must observe two distinct spaces: the sealed conference rooms of the state prosecution service and the open plazas of downtown Seoul. In South Korea, high-stakes political battles rarely stay confined to parliamentary debate. Instead, they oscillate rapidly between courtroom maneuverings—where indictments and confidential investigative notes become political ammunition—and public street demonstrations that can mobilize hundreds of thousands of citizens. Today, both arenas are facing a severe stress test as the country confronts the lingering fallout of prosecutorial overreach alongside alarming new fractures in its historic tradition of peaceful protest.

Recent revelations regarding selective information leaks at the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office, coupled with escalating hostility among fringe rally participants near Seoul’s Olympic Park, have ignited a fierce debate over civil liberties and institutional accountability. As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, who heads the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, works to restore democratic procedures and address past institutional abuses, the legal and social boundaries governing public power and civic assembly have once again taken center stage.

The Selective Enforcement Trap: Indictments, Leaks, and Han Dong-hoon

At the center of the current legal controversy is the question of what constitutes an illegal leak of official secrets versus an act in the public interest. On October 19, 2026, South Korean public broadcaster MBC aired an investigative report scrutinizing how internal prosecutorial files and indictment drafts from the Seoul High Prosecutors' Office had been disseminated. Running under the headline “‘Leaking secrets can constitute a crime’… Suggesting illegality towards Han Dong-hoon,” the report highlighted that high-ranking figures, including former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon, could face serious criminal liability under laws penalizing the disclosure of confidential official information.

The controversy goes far deeper than an isolated procedural lapse. For years, South Korea's state prosecution service has faced intense criticism for maintaining an elastic standard regarding confidentiality. During the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—prosecutors routinely leaked damaging investigation notes to the press under the guise of public interest when targeting political rivals. Yet, when investigative misconduct, internal files, or flawed indictments by the prosecution itself were exposed to the public, the agency quickly threatened critics with heavy criminal penalties for leaking state secrets.

This structural double standard has drawn unsettling comparisons to South Korea's authoritarian past. Observers have pointed out that treating selective information disclosures as treasonous acts of secret-leaking echoes the military dictatorship of Park Chung-hee in the 1970s. Under Park's authoritarian regime, a draconian penal clause known as "defamation of the head of state" was deployed to criminalize any critical reporting or dissent as an existential threat to the nation. When law enforcement agencies begin treating legal records as proprietary shields to punish critics while weaponizing those same materials against opponents, the rule of law ceases to be an impartial framework and becomes an instrument of political coercion.

From Molotov Cocktails to Candlelight: How Korea Tamed the Street

While prosecutors wrestled with legal integrity behind closed doors, South Korean society spent decades constructing an entirely different bulwark against authoritarianism: a uniquely disciplined culture of street assembly. Across international media, large-scale political demonstrations in Western democracies often feature chaotic clashes. In France, for instance, widespread protests frequently spiral into burning barricades, property destruction, and the organized looting of high-end storefronts. Such property damage often distracts from the core social grievance and burdens ordinary small business owners.

South Korea's modern protest culture, by contrast, followed a very different historical trajectory. During the turbulent pro-democracy struggles of the 1980s, students and activists regularly fought riot police with Molotov cocktails and paving stones. The turning point arrived with the progressive administration of President Kim Dae-jung in the late 1990s. The government instituted a formal policy halting the use of tear gas canisters against protesters. In return, civil society groups abandoned petrol bombs, breaking the cycle of escalatory street violence.

This mutual de-escalation bore fruit in 2002 during the massive memorial vigils for Shim Hyo-sun and Shin Mi-seon, two middle school girls struck and killed by a U.S. military armored vehicle. Those gatherings replaced clenched-fist combat with peaceful, family-oriented candlelight vigils, featuring music, public speeches, and open participation by parents pushing baby strollers. Over the next two decades, this "candlelight assembly" ethos became the signature of Korean civic action. Millions took to the streets during the historic protests that led to the impeachment of former President Park Geun-hye, as well as the broad mobilizations against the unconstitutional actions of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. Throughout these massive gatherings, ordinary commerce continued uninterrupted; not a single store was looted, nor did panic-buying break out.

That progress was not without painful exceptions. The 2009 Yongsan tragedy under the conservative administration of President Lee Myung-bak—where an aggressive police raid on evicted commercial tenants occupying a building rooftop resulted in a fatal fire that killed six people—served as a sobering reminder of the lethal consequences of excessive state force. Yet, the broader historical consensus held: the state was expected to exercise restraint, and the public maintained rigorous discipline.

Vigilantism at Olympic Park: New Cracks in Civic Restraint

That long-standing consensus of civic restraint, however, is now confronting dangerous friction from radicalized political fringes. Recent rallies staged by far-right extremist groups around Olympic Park in southeastern Seoul have raised alarm bells over what many observers describe as the emergence of vigilante intimidation, or "white terror."

Eyewitness reports and social footage from these demonstrations revealed scenes that shocked South Korean civil society. Rally participants surrounded unsuspecting passersby who held differing political viewpoints, threatening them with collective physical violence. In several instances, demonstrators unlawfully demanded that ordinary pedestrians open their bags and submit to unauthorized identity checks and physical searches—behaving as if the mob possessed official policing powers. Such behavior marks an aggressive shift away from constitutional assembly toward unlawful intimidation and near-riot conditions.

"Exercising the freedom of assembly does not grant any political movement the authority to usurp the state’s monopoly on policing or violate the fundamental human rights and privacy of fellow citizens."

For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, these developments present an urgent governance challenge. Under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, social polarization deepened dramatically as legal institutions were seen as openly partisan. With the Democratic Party now in power and the People Power Party serving as the main conservative opposition, judicial authorities are tasked with demonstrating that the law applies equally to all factions. Authorities must vigorously defend the constitutional right to peaceful demonstration while drawing a strict, uncompromising line against mob intimidation and vigilante violence in public parks.
