Power Plays and Policy Sprints: The Democratic Party’s Tightening Grip on the National Assembly

November 14, 2026

Inside the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly, the atmosphere is one of frantic, calculated motion. While the halls of the main opposition party, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), sit largely empty as their members take to the streets in protest, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is busy. With a commanding 170-seat majority, the DPK is not just occupying the vacuum left by the opposition; it is actively reshaping the legislative and judicial landscape to support the core initiatives of the President Lee Jae-myung administration. From multi-billion dollar infrastructure blueprints to high-stakes warnings issued toward the nation's top judges, the DPK is signaling that it intends to govern with a "speed-war" mentality, brooking little interference from either the streets or the bench.

For those outside Korea, the current political scene might look like standard partisan bickering, but the stakes are significantly higher. South Korea is currently navigating a delicate transition. Following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the country is under the leadership of President Lee Jae-myung. The Democratic Party, now the ruling power, is attempting to dismantle the remnants of the previous administration’s influence while simultaneously pushing a massive industrial and social agenda. This dual-track approach—cleaning the house while building the future—is what defines the current legislative session.

The Empty Desks and the Lunar Metaphor

The first point of friction is the physical absence of the opposition. Han Byung-do, the Floor Leader of the Democratic Party of Korea—essentially the party’s lead strategist within the legislature—has grown visibly impatient with the People Power Party's decision to conduct "out-of-chamber" protests. In South Korean politics, when a minority party feels it has no path to victory through voting, it often takes to the streets to rally public sentiment, a tactic known as "outside struggle."

Han’s critique was sharp and aimed at the heart of the PPP’s relevance. "Come back to the National Assembly, not the moon," he remarked, a biting metaphor suggesting that the opposition is living in an alternate reality while critical national business remains unfinished. His frustration stems from the timing: the National Assembly is currently in the middle of reviewing next year’s national budget. For the ruling DPK, every day the PPP spends holding rallies in regional strongholds like the Busan-Gyeongnam area is a day wasted on what they term "neglect of duty."

The DPK leadership has officially branded the PPP’s street tactics as "breach of trust against the people's livelihood." Their logic is simple: the people elected representatives to debate numbers, laws, and policies within the assembly walls, not to shout slogans into megaphones. By framing the opposition's protests as a dereliction of duty, the DPK is positioning itself as the only adult in the room—the "working ruling party" that stays late to hammer out the details of the budget while the other side is "on vacation" in the streets. This rhetorical positioning is crucial for the DPK as it seeks to maintain its mandate and prove that a liberal administration can be fiscally and administratively more competent than its predecessors.

A Warning to the Judiciary: The Red Line of Justice

Perhaps the most aggressive move by the DPK involves its stance toward the judiciary, specifically Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae of the Supreme Court of Korea. There is a growing sense of alarm within the ruling party that the judiciary might be acting as a lingering pocket of resistance. Specifically, the DPK is closely monitoring the potential release of Han Duck-soo, a figure who served in high-ranking positions during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and is seen by the DPK as a central figure in the previous administration’s failures.

Kim Min-seok, a prominent DPK lawmaker, issued what can only be described as a final warning. "Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae's judiciary must not cross the 'forbidden line' of releasing Han Duck-soo," he stated. The sentiment within the party is that the judiciary must reflect the "will of the people" and the "legal justice" established after the fall of the previous administration. For the DPK, Han Duck-soo's release would be more than just a legal decision; it would be a political provocation, a sign that the "judicial anchors" left behind by the Yoon era are still actively trying to subvert the current government’s efforts to clean up past corruption.

This tension has escalated to the point where the DPK is openly discussing impeachment for the Chief Justice. Representative Seo Young-kyo noted that the grounds for impeaching Cho are "already too many." In the South Korean context, the threat of impeachment against a Chief Justice is a nuclear option. It signals that the ruling party is willing to test the boundaries of the separation of powers if it believes the courts are obstructing the "cleansing" of the state. The DPK’s argument is that judicial independence should not be a shield for political bias. As they see it, the judiciary is at a crossroads: it can either align with the new era of accountability or face a direct confrontation with a legislature that holds the power of the purse and the power of removal.

"The attempt to release Han Duck-soo is an act that shakes the very foundation of the rule of law that the Lee Jae-myung government is trying to establish. If the judiciary cannot break free from the shadows of the past, it is a deception of the sovereign citizens."

The Real Estate Tug-of-War: Jongbuse and the Middle Class

While the DPK is aggressive on the judicial front, it is being much more cautious regarding tax policy, specifically the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (known in Korea as Jongbuse). For years, this tax has been a political lightning rod. Originally designed to curb property speculation by taxing owners of expensive homes, it eventually began to hit middle-class families in Seoul as property values soared, leading to a backlash that many believe contributed to the DPK’s previous electoral struggles.

Recently, a internal debate broke out within the DPK about relaxing the tax. One proposal suggested raising the basic deduction for non-resident homeowners to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million). However, the party’s "mainstream" faction, including those aligned with high-ranking member Jung Chung-rae, quickly pushed back, citing concerns over tax justice and equity. This internal friction led the opposition PPP to mock the DPK, calling the debate a "four-day rebellion" that ended in a whimper.

But for the DPK, this isn't just "falling in line." It is a delicate balancing act. The party needs to win over the middle-class voters in the Seoul metropolitan area who feel burdened by high property taxes, but it cannot abandon its core principle that unearned income from real estate should be heavily taxed to ensure social fairness. The DPK is currently searching for a "third way"—a policy that provides relief to genuine homeowners without opening the floodgates for speculators. This internal "node-struggle," as it's called in Korea, is a sign that the party is no longer just an opposition force throwing stones; it is a ruling party grappling with the messy reality of governance.

A $60 Billion Blueprint for Mega-Growth

Perhaps the most ambitious project on the DPK’s current agenda is the "Mega Growth Committee." Led by lawmakers like Lee Un-ju and An Ho-young, this committee is tasked with ensuring that South Korea doesn't lose its edge in the global semiconductor war. The committee has identified a critical bottleneck: infrastructure. Without a massive influx of power, water, and roads, projects like the Yongin Semiconductor Cluster—a massive industrial hub south of Seoul—will fail before they even start.

The scale of the DPK’s ambition is reflected in the numbers. The committee has called for 84 trillion won (approximately $60 billion) in infrastructure investment over the next decade. Of this, the DPK is pushing for 50 trillion won (about $36 billion) to be funded directly by the national treasury. This is a significant shift. Traditionally, infrastructure for industrial parks was often left to the private sector or local governments. The DPK is now arguing that in the current global economic climate, infrastructure is a matter of national security.

In particular, Representative Lee Un-ju has emphasized that "power infrastructure is a life-or-death issue for the semiconductor industry." She has even proposed using a "Future Response Fund" to bypass traditional budget hurdles and secure funding early. This reflects a broader shift in the DPK’s identity. While they remain a party of social welfare, they are increasingly positioning themselves as the party of "strategic industrialism"—arguing that the state must be the primary investor in the technologies that will fund the welfare state of the future. By focusing on regional clusters like North Jeolla Province (Jeonbuk), they are also trying to marry this industrial strategy with their long-standing goal of balanced regional development.

Watching the Horizon: Deadlines and Decisions

As the November legislative session continues, there are several key indicators to watch that will determine the success of the DPK’s "speed-war" strategy:

  • The Budget Deadline: The constitutional deadline for the national budget is December 2. If the DPK uses its majority to pass a budget without the PPP’s participation, it will further consolidate their image as a "unilateral ruling power," for better or worse.
  • Judicial Rulings: Any decision by the courts regarding Han Duck-soo or other figures from the Yoon Suk-yeol era will be a flashpoint. If the courts release these figures, expect the DPK to move forward with concrete steps toward judicial reform or even impeachment proceedings against top judges.
  • Digital Asset Legislation: Keep an eye on Representative Min Byung-dug’s timeline for the Digital Asset Act. With a goal to finalize it by early January, the DPK is looking to provide a regulatory framework for South Korea’s massive crypto-trading population, a move that could have significant market implications.
  • Local Government Scrutiny: The investigation into the "Hangang Bus" project—a pet project of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon (a key PPP figure)—will serve as a proxy battle. The DPK-controlled city council is using this to highlight what they call "administrative opacity" in opposition-led local governments.

The Democratic Party of Korea is currently operating with a sense of historic mission. They view themselves not just as a government, but as a corrective force intended to steer the country away from the "chaos" of the Yoon Suk-yeol years. By moving fast on the budget, standing firm against the judiciary, and laying out multi-billion dollar industrial plans, they are attempting to build a "fortress of governance" that can withstand the protests of the opposition and the skepticism of the courts. Whether this "speed-war" leads to a more efficient state or a deeper constitutional crisis remains the central question of Korean politics today.
