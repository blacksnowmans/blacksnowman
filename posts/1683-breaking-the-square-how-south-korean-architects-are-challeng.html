Breaking the Square: How South Korean Architects Are Challenging the Standard Apartment Box

October 29, 2026

Step onto any street in Seoul, Busan, or Daegu, and you will be greeted by an endless expanse of gray. The South Korean urban landscape is dominated by the standardized square: boxy apartment complexes, uniform multi-family villas, and grid-like commercial buildings. For decades, this vertical conformity has dictated how millions of Koreans live, reducing homes to predictable floor plans and standardized rooms where even the wallpaper colors often blur together. But a quiet architectural rebellion is underway, challenging the assumption that modern urban living must be lived inside a right angle.

To understand what happens when architects reject the square box, we can look at the work of renowned South Korean architects Lim Hyung-nam and Roh Eun-joo. Their recent residential and commercial projects offer a fascinating case study in how spatial design and philosophy can fundamentally alter a resident’s daily life. Far from mere aesthetic exercises, these buildings are a direct critique of the cookie-cutter environments that define contemporary Korean cities.

The Geometry of Liberation: Circles and Sunlight Wells

At first glance, one of Lim and Roh’s newly designed urban buildings looks deceptively conventional. The lower floors are optimized as commercial space—currently functioning as a bakery—with efficient layouts designed to maximize utility. Yet the true value of the structure lies in how it breaks away from rigid geometry. To prevent the interior from becoming monotonous, the architects carved a circular motif into the core of the building, creating an organic, concentric structure that extends upward through four floors.

At the heart of this design is what the architects call a "sunlight well." Instead of boxing bedrooms inside rigid four-cornered walls, the interior layout follows the soft, curving contours of a circular wall. Psychologically, the difference is profound. Moving from a square room to a fluid, curved space changes how a resident experiences movement and privacy. The curved corridors and rounded doors break up the visual boredom of straight lines, offering depth and spatial variety in a tight urban footprint.

Of course, experimental design involves a degree of trial and error. The architects freely admit that certain hyper-personalized features—such as an indoor net hammock suspended over the living space—might look striking in a portfolio, but may not see heavy everyday use. Yet even these quirks highlight the collaborative nature of good architecture: the willingness to test how human beings actually inhabit a space rather than forcing them into a rigid mold.

Verticality and the Psychology of Sightlines

In densely populated South Korean cities, space is at a premium, forcing architects to look upward rather than outward. Lim and Roh mastered this verticality by playing with asymmetric sightlines. In their mixed-use building, the structure is deliberately designed so that occupants on the upper residential levels can look down into the commercial spaces below, while those on the ground floor cannot peer back up. This clever manipulation of sightlines maximizes the sense of connection and openness without compromising privacy.

On the top floor, which serves as a living area and playroom, four ceiling skylights flood the interior with natural light. Looking down past the suspended hammock reveals a deeply vertical spatial experience—a rare luxury amidst the claustrophobic walls of the typical city neighborhood. By prioritizing vertical flow and natural illumination, the architects transformed a constrained urban lot into an emotionally rich sanctuary.

Architecture as a Catalyst for Urban Succession

Perhaps the most compelling aspect of these bespoke buildings is their ambition to transform the surrounding neighborhood. The architects view their work through the lens of biological "succession"—the ecological process where abandoned farmland slowly sprouts weeds, bushes, and eventually towering trees. In a neighborhood filled with aging, uninspired buildings, a single architecturally bold structure acts as a seed of change.

Rather than relying on top-down, government-led urban redevelopment projects, Lim and Roh hope their design will trigger a domino effect. When neighbors see a building that embraces curves, natural light, and organic forms, it challenges them to rethink their own properties. It suggests that improving the quality of urban residential culture does not require massive bureaucratic overhauls, but rather individual acts of architectural courage.

This philosophy stands in sharp contrast to the societal pressure that pushes generations of Koreans to accept standardized apartment living as the default. We are conditioned from birth to believe that a good life means a larger square footage in a high-rise complex. But when architects introduce elements like sunlight wells and non-rectilinear rooms, they expose the hidden claustrophobia of the standard apartment box.

Working With, Not Against, the Land

The architects' philosophy extends beyond residential housing into commercial spaces. When commissioned to design an office building on a steeply sloped plot of land—a scenario where most developers would simply flatten the earth with bulldozers—Lim and Roh chose to honor the topography. They utilized the natural elevation changes to organically connect the subterranean levels, saving construction costs while achieving a rare harmony between nature and structure.

In another clever subversion of convention, they placed an eight-car parking lot on the ground floor rather than burying it deep underground. This strategy preserved the commercial value of the street-level space while maximizing convenience, proving that geographical disadvantages can be turned into unique design advantages through clever spatial planning.

Ultimately, these projects demonstrate that a building should never be treated as a finished, static product. Much like the bakery on the lower floor, which is intentionally designed to expand upward into the upper levels if the business grows, a good building must age, breathe, and evolve alongside its occupants.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea grapples with an aging housing stock and a cultural fatigue toward monotonous apartment complexes, keep an eye on how independent architects approach small-lot urban infill projects. Watch for whether municipal zoning laws begin to offer more flexibility for non-rectilinear designs, and whether neighborhood-level transformations—the domino effect of single aesthetic interventions—begin to take root in older districts of Seoul and regional cities. Most importantly, observe how younger generations of homeowners begin to prioritize customized, fluid living spaces over traditional square footage, signaling a generational shift in what it means to call a place "home."
