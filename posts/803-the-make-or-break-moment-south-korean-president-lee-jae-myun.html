The Make-or-Break Moment: South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Seventh Press Conference

September 18, 2026

Tomorrow at 10:00 AM, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung will step into the Yeongbingwan—the state guest house at the presidential office—for his seventh press conference. The atmosphere inside and around the administration is markedly different this time; the President reportedly delayed his schedule by a day just to hold intensive preparatory meetings. With his approval ratings at an all-time low, persistent personnel controversies, and a lack of clarity in policy direction, the stakes could not be higher. Throughout history, these televised addresses have served as either a restorative tonic or a poisonous blow to a leader’s political survival. As we await the event, it is crucial to understand the precarious position the administration currently occupies.

The Gravity of Approval Ratings and the Lessons of History

The success or failure of an administration is rarely determined by polished rhetoric. It comes down to whether a leader can provide answers that resonate with the public's expectations and whether they can approach the people with humility. According to Gallup Korea, the job approval ratings for South Korean presidents during the second quarter of their second year in office were: Roh Tae-woo (28%), Kim Young-sam (55%), Kim Dae-jung (52%), Roh Moo-hyun (34%), Lee Myung-bak (27%), Park Geun-hye (50%), Moon Jae-in (60%), and Yoon Suk-yeol (34%).

President Lee Jae-myung currently finds himself in a similar range. A recent survey by Realmeter showed his approval rating at 33.8%, marking a decline for nine consecutive weeks, while his disapproval rating has surpassed 60% for the first time since taking office. Numbers, however, are only as honest as those who read them. While presidential aides often attempt to deflect blame onto external factors—such as ongoing security investigations or opposition party maneuvers—the public is clearly drifting away, with the loss of support among the youth demographic being particularly sharp.

History provides a roadmap for how leaders handle such troughs. Roh Moo-hyun engaged in direct debates with the public; while his numbers did not immediately rebound, he is remembered for his sincerity. Lee Myung-bak famously apologized after public outcry over policy, which helped restore his governing momentum. Moon Jae-in, meanwhile, utilized unscripted press conferences, though he faced backlash over specific remarks regarding his former Justice Minister. The lesson is clear: for a leader in crisis, evasion is a path to irrelevance, while direct confrontation of the issues is the only viable route to recovery.

What Must Be Addressed: Beyond Rhetoric

Presidential spokesperson Sung Ki-hong has stated that the upcoming 90-minute conference will be divided into two sessions: politics/diplomacy and policy/economy. The administration has expressed an intent to shorten the distance between the President and reporters and even incorporate real-time comments from the public. Yet, the form matters less than the substance. The public is not looking for standard, canned responses; they are looking for a frank admission of why the country has reached this point and how the trajectory will change.

There is fear among some in the President’s circle that issues like potential constitutional amendments or legal pardons will turn into a "black hole" that consumes all other policy discussions. They urge the President to avoid these topics to stay on message. However, attempting to dodge these issues will only allow the embers of controversy to grow. True leadership is not about kneeling to the public, but about breaking through the stalemate by addressing the hardest questions head-on.

The Disconnect Between the Government and the Youth

Politics does not exist only in high-level discourse; it is felt in the everyday lives of citizens. A recent incident involving the university admissions server—which crashed, leading to a botched "extension" of the application deadline that unfairly exposed competitive ratios—has left young students feeling that the "rules of the game" were changed mid-play. This has sparked planned protests near the National Assembly.

Furthermore, the issue of deploying troops to the Strait of Hormuz has become a flashpoint for anxiety. While the government maintains that no decision has been made, public opposition is overwhelming. Gallup Korea data shows that opposition to deployment is not limited to the youth; it is a cross-generational consensus. Even among those in their 60s and 70s, more than half oppose the move. Young people are not asking for vague diplomatic slogans; they are demanding a clear, unequivocal "no" to being drawn into foreign conflicts.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward tomorrow, the challenge for President Lee is twofold. First, he must address the recent personnel failures—specifically those involving ministerial candidates—with genuine accountability rather than defensive justification. The public needs to see a President who understands that acknowledging a mistake is a sign of strength, not weakness. Second, he must provide a concrete, revamped vision for the economy and real estate policies that directly acknowledge the struggles of the average household.

The danger is that if the President hides behind prepared defenses, this press conference will be remembered as another "lost opportunity." If he chooses to be honest, however, he may finally begin to rebuild the damaged trust between the administration and the people. The public, while frustrated, has a keen eye for sincerity. Whether this conference becomes a turning point or a footnote in history depends entirely on whether the President chooses to treat the people as citizens to be respected, rather than critics to be managed.
