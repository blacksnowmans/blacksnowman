The Battle for the Soul of the Democratic Party: Purging the "Cuckoos" from Within

October 09, 2026

For many grassroots supporters of South Korea's ruling Democratic Party, the current political landscape feels increasingly alien. Imagine watching a house you spent decades helping to build slowly filled with people who ignore the original blueprints, mock the founding spirit, and seem more interested in self-promotion than the party's core mission. This is the mounting crisis of identity currently paralyzing the Democratic Party—the party that has successfully propelled Lee Jae-myung to the presidency.

There is a growing, palpable sense of urgency that "cuckoos"—political opportunists who have infiltrated the Democratic Party—can no longer be tolerated. These individuals, who occupy positions of influence under the party banner, are accused of acting against the very values the party was built upon: those of the late Presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in.

The Internal War: Disciplining Dissent

The recent controversy sparked by Kang Seong-pil, a former deputy spokesperson for the Democratic Party, serves as a stark illustration of this divide. Kang publicly called for disciplinary action against former Minister of Justice Choo Mi-ae, suggesting that party members should circulate a petition to punish her for expressing her political views.

To many, this demand for "internal discipline" is a chilling echo of the authoritarian tactics once employed by conservative parties in South Korea—specifically the People Power Party, the main opposition party. Using the guise of "party unity" to silence dissenting voices is viewed as a betrayal of the democratic, debate-driven culture that the Democratic Party claims to uphold. Critics argue that the party is strengthened by diversity and rigorous discussion, not by suppressing those who challenge the mainstream narrative. The real issue, according to party purists, isn't Choo Mi-ae’s expression of opinion, but the presence of figures like Kang who seek to impose a top-down, "totalitarian" control over internal discourse.

Privatized Power and the "New Lee Jae-myung" Faction

The controversy extends to high-ranking officials such as Representative Kim Min-seok. Allegations have surfaced suggesting that key party positions are being filled by a tight circle of allies, turning party administration into a "private enterprise" of sorts. Leaked internal communications, which reportedly featured discussions about "seizing control," have alarmed many rank-and-file members.

This raises a fundamental question about the role of the party in the Lee Jae-myung era. If the Democratic Party acts merely as a rubber stamp for the executive branch, it fails its constitutional duty to serve as a check on power. Even under an administration led by one's own party, healthy internal debate and critical oversight are essential for government success. When political power is privatized and used as a tool for factional gain, the party loses its moral standing with the public. Those who pay their modest monthly party dues—the literal foundation of the party’s finances—are beginning to feel that their voices are being drowned out by a clique of opportunists.

Lessons from Abroad: The Price of Compromise

Looking at the broader international context, some are drawing parallels to the struggles of progressive movements in countries like Brazil. When left-leaning governments take power and begin to compromise with the establishment to appease markets or media conglomerates, they often alienate the very people who put them in office. There is a strong sentiment that the Democratic Party must remain true to its identity: it must advocate for the working class and prioritize the economic well-being of ordinary citizens over the interests of the wealthy elite or the appeasement of "PC" ideologies that distract from core structural issues.

The recent shift toward post-neoliberal policies in the UK under Prime Minister Barnum is being cited as a potential model. Proposals like large-scale public housing and state-led care services—placing community assets under public control—are exactly the kind of "true progressive" actions that supporters want to see. Instead of being swayed by voices within the party that lean toward capitalist logic, many argue that the Democratic Party must deliver tangible, public-facing policies. If the party fails to carry out the mandates for prosecutorial, judicial, and media reform, it risks suffering the same fate of disillusionment that has plagued progressive movements elsewhere.

The Human Cost of "Cheap" Convenience

The disconnect between the party's elite and its base is perhaps most visible in the gig economy. Recent reports from Gyeongsan, North Gyeongsang Province, noted that delivery platform workers were receiving as little as 940 KRW (about $0.70) per delivery. While platforms often blame "system errors," the reality for riders is that their wages are plummeting while the cost of living rises.

This is a human rights issue. By classifying delivery riders and couriers as "independent contractors," these platforms avoid the responsibilities of a standard employer, effectively stripping workers of minimum wage protections and social insurance. Critics argue that the Democratic Party, which claims to represent the working class, must stop defending these platforms. If the party continues to favor the "convenience" of the consumer at the cost of the safety and livelihood of the worker, it will ultimately lose its soul. True progressivism means ensuring that the "cheap" services the public enjoys are not subsidized by the exploitation of the vulnerable.

What to Watch Next

  • Internal Purges: Watch for whether the party leadership takes concrete steps to remove those viewed as "cuckoos" or if the factional infighting deepens.
  • Policy Shifts: Keep an eye on upcoming legislation regarding labor rights in the gig economy; this will be the litmus test for whether the party truly prioritizes workers over corporate platforms.
  • Grassroots Engagement: Expect a rise in activist pressure as party members utilize their status as donors to demand greater accountability from high-ranking officials.

Ultimately, the message from the base is clear: the Democratic Party is at a crossroads. It must decide whether to continue serving the interests of a select few or to return to its roots as a representative of the common people. The party’s future depends not on the maneuvers of its current leadership, but on its ability to reconnect with the spirit of its founders and the people who keep it alive.
