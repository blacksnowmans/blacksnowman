The Battle for South Korea's Prosecution Reform: Personnel Crises, Political Scars, and Foreign Policy Dilemmas

September 21, 2026

When foreign observers look at South Korea's high-stakes political landscape, they often see a flurry of rapid legislative changes, dramatic protests, and fierce partisan bickering. But beneath the daily headlines lies a deeper, more structural struggle: an ongoing war over the soul of the state's investigative apparatus. Nearly two years after a tumultuous constitutional crisis gripped the nation, the machinery of South Korean governance is still straining under the weight of unfinished reforms, internal fractures, and external pressures.

Recent controversies surrounding the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—designed to strip the traditional prosecution service of its sprawling investigative powers—have laid bare the contradictions of South Korea's reform movement. Coupled with lingering bitterness from the short-lived martial law attempt under the previous conservative administration and a risky foreign policy entanglement in the Middle East, President Lee Jae-myung's government faces a critical test of its political survival and ideological coherence.

The Prosecution Reform Paradox and the Controversy Over the First Chief

At the heart of the current domestic turmoil is a bitter irony: the decision to appoint a former prosecutor as the inaugural head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. For decades, South Korea's prosecutors held near-monopoly powers over both investigating and indicting crimes, an authority that critics argue was routinely abused for political weaponization. The creation of the new agency was supposed to mark a decisive break from this legacy, cleanly separating investigation from prosecution.

Yet, the nomination of Kim Ji-yong, a career prosecutor with a questionable past regarding how he handled past institutional abuses, has sparked outrage among reform advocates. Critics point out that appointing someone steeped in the old prosecutorial culture to lead a body meant to dismantle it defies common sense. Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the country's primary progressive party—have voiced deep alarm, noting that even high-ranking presidential staff were apparently bypassed or left in the dark during the opaque vetting process. This "passing" of internal political handlers points to a dysfunctional decision-making pipeline within the administration.

The numbers reflecting the agency's rollout are equally grim. Out of a targeted 2,874 positions, only 1,761 have drawn applicants—just 61.3% of capacity—with former prosecutors making up a meager hundred or so of those. Public opinion reflects this institutional skepticism: polls show that over 53% of citizens oppose appointing a former prosecutor to head the agency, while a striking 56.4% believe that if prosecutors refuse to investigate, the organization should simply be downsized. The government is rapidly discovering that simply rebranding state institutions does not automatically wash away public distrust.

The Shadow of Martial Law and the Battle Over Historical Memory

Complicating the administration's reform agenda is a fierce, ongoing culture war over the memory of the martial law decree that shocked the nation. Conservative figures, most notably People Power Party lawmaker Han Dong-hoon—who served as justice minister under the previous conservative government—have attempted to rebrand themselves as defenders of democracy who helped thwart the martial law attempt.

Progressive lawmakers and ruling party figures have aggressively pushed back against this narrative, labeling it a historical forgery. They argue that Han and his allies operated at the heart of an administration that systematically weaponized investigative bodies against political opponents, pointing to leaked records showing prosecutors prioritizing targeted investigations against figures like then-opposition leader Lee Jae-myung over routine regulatory matters like food and drug safety. To progressive critics, the attempt to spin the martial law episode into a political marketing tool insults the citizens who took to the streets and the lawmakers who fought through the night to rescind the decree.

This battle over historical memory is not merely academic; it dictates who controls the narrative of state legitimacy. As long as the apparatus built during the previous administration remains intact and unpurged, the Lee Jae-myung government will find its legislative and reform agenda throttled by institutional resistance and partisan counterattacks.

Stuck in Washington's Wake: South Korea's Middle East Dilemma

Beyond domestic institutional battles, South Korea's progressive government is facing a severe foreign policy test over the escalating conflict in the Middle East. With the United States launching unilateral military operations against Iran—marking a rare global conflict waged largely without broad international backing—Seoul finds itself caught between traditional alliance commitments and core national interests.

Commentators and regional experts have pointed out the deep hypocrisy of South Korea blindly following Washington into what many international legal scholars classify as an aggressive war. As a divided nation whose economy relies heavily on stable energy imports and global shipping routes—including the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz—South Korea has everything to lose and little to gain by endorsing foreign military adventures.

Instead of passively bowing to American pressure or contemplating controversial troop deployments, critics argue that Seoul should demand accountability and compensation for the economic damage inflicted on its energy-dependent economy. For a middle power aspiring to act as a bridge for peace, bending to humiliating diplomatic compliance risks passing severe financial and geopolitical burdens onto future generations.

Navigating Sliding Approval Ratings and Internal Division

These overlapping crises—bumbled institutional appointments, historical revisionism by the political opposition, and foreign policy overreach—have naturally taken a toll on the administration's political standing. President Lee Jae-myung's approval ratings have experienced a steady, structural slide, driven largely by voters who once supported the progressive bloc but are now withdrawing their backing out of frustration with internal bickering and slow policy delivery.

Yet, blaming the public or dismissing falling poll numbers as the work of hostile actors misses the point. Approval ratings are a thermometer, not the disease. The current friction between the Democratic Party and its conservative rivals mirrors a deeper anxiety among the electorate: whether the coalition that rose to power on the promise of sweeping away corrupt authoritarian practices can maintain the discipline to govern effectively.

To reverse this trend, ruling party leaders argue that the progressive camp must recapture the desperate, unified spirit of the winter night when citizens mobilized to block the martial law decree. Healing internal emotional rifts and refocusing on tangible livelihood issues and thorough institutional reform will be essential if the government is to restore the public trust that brought it to power.

What to Watch Next

  • The Serious Crimes Investigation Agency Hearings: Watch for parliamentary confirmation hearings and personnel adjustments regarding Chief Kim Ji-yong, which will signal whether the administration is willing to bow to public pressure on prosecution reform.
  • Internal Party Realignments: Look for signs of reconciliation or deepening factional splits within the Democratic Party as leadership attempts to consolidate support ahead of upcoming legislative battles.
  • Foreign Policy Stance on the Middle East: Monitor official Ministry of Foreign Affairs briefings for any concrete pushback or cautious distancing from U.S.-led military operations in the Gulf, particularly regarding energy security and shipping lane protections.

South Korea remains a democracy forged in crisis, moving forward through friction and fierce public debate. Whether the current administration can convert these compounding growth pains into lasting institutional renewal will depend entirely on its willingness to listen to the citizens who brought it to power.
