How a Routine Business Dispute and a Viral Photo Became a Korean Witch Hunt

September 15, 2026

Scroll through Korean YouTube or political commentary channels on any given day, and you will eventually stumble across thumbnails designed to make you rub your eyes in disbelief. A recent flashpoint centers on Yang Su-jin, an entrepreneur whose life and career have been picked apart by a coalition of aggressive digital creators, mainstream outlets, and state prosecutors. To watch the coverage is to witness a masterclass in how modern Korean sensationalism can manufacture a public scandal out of thin air, weaponizing state power and internet rumors to grind an individual’s reputation into dust. But beneath the lurid headlines lies a much more troubling story about how investigations are run, how gender bias operates in business, and how easily the public can be misled.

The Anatomy of a Flawed Prosecution

The handling of the legal case against Yang reveals systemic issues within South Korea's investigative apparatus. According to accounts of the proceedings, the Seoul Western District Prosecutors' Office struggled to build a coherent case, culminating in an extraordinary moment where prosecutors reportedly dismissed the investigation with a casual "You're cleared of charges" after failing to substantiate any wrongdoing. To a casual observer, an acquittal should bring relief. Instead, it exposes a reckless approach to law enforcement: launch a sweeping, fishing-expedition style investigation first, tear apart a person's life, and worry about finding actual evidence later.

This aggressive style of probing—often colloquially referred to in Korea as "dust-sifting"—leaves targeted individuals with ruined reputations while the investigators face zero accountability for their errors. Critics point out that the political timing of these investigations often tracks shifts in political winds. The probe gathered momentum during the tenure of Han Dong-hoon, then serving as Minister of Justice under the conservative administration. Observers and legal critics have alleged that the case became a matter of special interest to the leadership, leading to disruptive changes within the investigative teams and a push for indictments that strained legal logic. When the state's investigative machinery operates to serve high-level political interests rather than neutral justice, it ceases to be a safeguard and becomes a political weapon.

The Danger of Politicized Investigations: When state prosecutors target individuals under the banner of political interest, the rule of law gives way to state-sponsored harassment, eroding public trust in the entire legal system.

How Ordinary Restaurants Were Framed as Vice Establishments

While the legal case floundered, the court of public opinion was being poisoned by deliberate mischaracterizations of Yang's past business ventures. Commentators and sensationalist YouTubers seized upon two establishments Yang had previously operated: a general restaurant called Yagiba, and a seafood-focused eatery named Frydmerke Chicken Yagi. Both were legally registered general restaurants located near regular schools—specifically, in the vicinity of Yeongdong High School.

Legally and practically, these were ordinary dining establishments paying regular taxes. Yet, within the ecosystem of gossip-driven media, mundane details were stripped away and replaced with toxic innuendo. Because Yagiba employed female bartenders without providing hostesses—a common configuration under South Korean entertainment licensing laws—commentators aggressively branded the venue as an illicit room salon or adult entertainment establishment. The distinction between a normal dining business and a high-end hostess bar was intentionally erased to feed the algorithms of outrage.

  • Yagiba & Frydmerke: Legally registered general restaurants operating near local schools.
  • The Distortion: Standard licensing details were weaponized to paint legitimate businesses as illicit nightlife venues.
  • The Motive: Generating clicks and satisfying public appetite for scandal over factual accuracy.

The Weaponization of a Presidential Event Photo

The controversy reached a fever pitch due to an unlikely piece of evidence: a photograph from an official state-sponsored event held in May 2023. Yang had attended a large-scale gathering for small and medium-sized enterprises hosted by the presidential office, an event that brought together some 500 prominent business figures, including Samsung Electronics Chairman Lee Jae-yong. Because Yang appeared in the frame alongside corporate heavyweights, internet sleuths and hostile commentators treated the photo as smoking-gun proof of shady political patronage.

The reality was far more mundane. Yang was invited in her capacity as a female entrepreneur attending a major national economic conference. Yet in the fevered imaginations of online commentators, a routine seating arrangement at a massive business convention was twisted into a grand conspiracy. This specific angle exposed a deeply ingrained bias within Korean digital discourse: while successful male entrepreneurs are routinely praised for their vision and networking savvy, successful women in business are frequently subjected to immediate suspicion, hyper-sexualized rumors, and conspiracy theories about how they attained their positions.

Independent Media Pushback and the Fight for Facts

As mainstream outlets and gossip channels chased high view counts by amplifying unverified claims, alternative and independent media outlets stepped in to provide a counter-narrative. Platforms such as the News Factory and the Independent Media Network (KINN) conducted interviews allowing Yang to directly address the allegations, questioning why prosecutors repeatedly reversed course and why ordinary business operations were deliberately mislabeled.

Without these independent platforms, the public would have remained entirely trapped inside a digital echo chamber designed to validate a predetermined narrative of guilt. The episode serves as a stark reminder of the fragility of modern information ecosystems, where sensationalism routinely crowds out verification.

What to Watch Next

As this case continues to ripple through Korean public discourse, observers should watch for several concrete developments:

  • Legal Accountability for Defamation: Monitor whether civil or criminal defamation suits are filed against the media outlets and digital creators who pushed unverified "room salon" narratives.
  • Institutional Oversight of Prosecutors: Look for legislative or civic oversight inquiries into how high-profile, politically sensitive investigations are assigned, altered, and closed without charges.
  • Shifts in Digital Media Regulation: Pay attention to ongoing debates in South Korea regarding the regulation of monetization on gossip-focused YouTube channels that profit from unverified personal attacks.
