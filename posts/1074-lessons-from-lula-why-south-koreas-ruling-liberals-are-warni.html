Lessons from Lula: Why South Korea’s Ruling Liberals Are Warning Themselves Against Compromise

October 13, 2026

Following a grueling election cycle half a world away, Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva took to Facebook not to revel in victory, but to deliver a strikingly blunt self-assessment. Rather than blaming the far-right for its aggressive rise, Lula asked a painful question: Did the right grow strong because of its own brilliance, or because of the governing left’s incompetence and willingness to compromise?

For political strategists and observers in Seoul, Lula’s confession felt less like a distant commentary on Latin America and more like an urgent warning aimed directly at South Korea’s current political landscape. Following a turbulent period that saw the impeachment, removal, and ultimate imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, South Korea installed President Lee Jae-myung and his center-left Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) into power. Yet, as the new administration attempts to navigate deep structural reforms, progressive commentators are highlighting Lula’s warnings as a playbook of what *not* to do when holding power.

At the heart of this discussion lies a recurring historical trap: when reform-minded governments take office, they often attempt to placate the political establishment, conservative media, and financial markets in the name of stability. In doing so, they risk alienating the very public that elected them, leaving structural inequalities unaddressed and opening a wide door for conservative or radical right-wing revivals.

The Far-Right’s Mechanism: Feeding on Left-Wing Hesitation

Lula’s post-election introspection centered on the failures of neoliberal economic management. For decades, global economic projects promised that rising market tides would lift all boats. Instead, economic polarization widened, leaving working-class citizens, vulnerable workers, and young people feeling abandoned by the state. When progressive parties took power, they frequently focused on managing the existing neoliberal order rather than tackling its core contradictions.

This dynamic created a dangerous political vacuum. As the governing left prioritized institutional stability and market confidence over aggressive wealth redistribution, the far-right deftly disguised itself as an anti-establishment force. Right-wing populists weaponized the genuine economic pain and frustration of the public, redirecting widespread anger away from capital and systemic inequality toward social minorities, immigrants, and progressive movements.

Key Takeaway: The rise of far-right populism is rarely just a victory of conservative strategy. It is often the direct consequence of progressive leaders failing to deliver transformative economic relief, choosing instead to manage the status quo.

When political leaders abandon bold public interventions to satisfy market efficiency, society degenerates into a competitive free-for-all. For South Korean political analysts, this diagnosis serves as a sobering reminder: if a government claiming the mandate of reform hesitates out of fear of conflict, the public’s frustration will eventually curdle into cynicism and political backlash.

The Trap of Appeasing the Establishment

Why do progressive administrations consistently fall into this cycle? The answer lies in the intense pressure governing parties face the moment they step into executive power. Conservative media outlets, corporate cartels, and legacy bureaucratic institutions immediately raise alarms about economic instability, polarization, and policy overreach. In response, reformist governments frequently soften their stances, attempt to appease their fiercest critics, and slow the pace of promised changes.

This desire to satisfy political opponents almost always backfires. While the establishment remains fundamentally hostile to meaningful reform regardless of concessions, the administration’s core supporters begin to feel betrayed.

  • Loss of Core Trust: Voters who took to the streets and mobilized for systemic change watch their leaders prioritize elite consensus over public welfare.
  • Erosion of Momentum: Timid policy execution gives entrenched interest groups time to organize resistance and dilute legislative proposals.
  • Vulnerability to Attack: Once a government loses the enthusiastic backing of its base, it loses its primary shield against opposition attacks, leaving it fragile during political crises.

In Lula’s analysis, attempting to comfort financial markets and hostile media is a fatal mistake for any government elected on a mandate of transformation. When a administration spends its energy defending timid compromises rather than championing bold solutions, it loses its reason for existing in the eyes of the electorate.

Prosecutorial Reform and Wealth Disparity: Seoul’s High-Stakes Mirror

This global analysis hits close to home in South Korea, where the stakes of political reform are exceptionally high. The nation is still adjusting to the dramatic political shifts that brought down the previous conservative order. Following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the governing Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung assumed responsibility for addressing long-standing institutional grievances.

Chief among these challenges is the reform of South Korea’s powerful prosecution service—the Supreme Prosecutors' Office—an agency historically criticized for holding sweeping, unchecked powers over both investigative and prosecutorial functions. For years, progressive voters have demanded a thorough overhaul of the judicial and prosecutorial apparatus to end political prosecutions and dismantle entrenched bureaucratic cartels.

At the same time, South Korea faces severe socio-economic friction points:

  • Real Estate and Housing Inequality: Skyrocketing property values and rent pressures in the Seoul metropolitan area have priced out young families and widened the wealth gap between property owners and renters.
  • Financial Inequity: High household debt burdens and concentrated corporate power continue to strain working-class households.
  • Media Environment: Powerful conservative news syndicates actively frame structural reforms as dangerous radicalism or partisan revenge.

Korean commentary drawing on Lula’s message stresses that President Lee Jae-myung and the DPK cannot afford to play defense. Every time reform leaders pause prosecutorial restructuring or soften housing regulations to avoid bad headlines in conservative newspapers, they risk repeating the mistakes made by left-leaning governments abroad. If the government appears timid in the face of resistance from entrenched cartels, it forfeits the trust of citizens who demanded a fundamental break from the practices of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure.

What to Watch Next

As the Lee Jae-myung administration pushes forward with its legislative agenda, international observers should look beyond surface-level approval ratings and focus on specific structural indicators to evaluate whether South Korea’s ruling party is heeding these lessons:

1. Prosecutorial Restructuring Legislation: Watch how decisively the DPK-controlled National Assembly passes laws separating investigative and prosecutorial powers. Any dilution of these measures to seek consensus with the opposition People Power Party (PPP) will signal a pivot toward compromise.

2. Housing and Financial Mandates: Track whether the administration enacts direct, aggressive interventions to curb real estate speculation and support low-income borrowers, or whether it relies on modest, market-friendly tweaks that leave underlying price structures intact.

3. Strategic Narrative and Communication: Observe whether President Lee and the DPK leadership engage directly with their grassroots base to justify tough reforms, or if they alter their policy trajectory in response to pressure from major media conglomerates.

History shows that political reform is rarely achieved by seeking permission from the status quo. For South Korea’s ruling liberals, Lula’s post-election reflection serves as a stark reminder: genuine political survival depends not on soothing one's opponents, but on fearlessly delivering for the people who provided the mandate to govern.
