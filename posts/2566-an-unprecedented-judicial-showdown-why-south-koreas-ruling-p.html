An Unprecedented Judicial Showdown: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Moving to Impeach the Chief Justice

November 21, 2026

On November 20, the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly in Seoul descended into an extraordinary political crisis. During a tense general meeting of its lawmakers, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) formally adopted a resolution to impeach Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Korea. DPK leaders did not merely float the idea—they made it official party policy and pledged to push the impeachment motion through Parliament before the end of the year.

For international observers accustomed to standard legislative debates, the sight of a ruling party attempting to remove the head of the nation's judiciary is startling. In South Korea's constitutional system, targeting the top judge of the Supreme Court is considered a drastic measure reserved for serious misconduct. Yet, by the evening of November 20, the DPK embarked on a fast-track timetable to do just that, accusing Chief Justice Cho of destroying public trust in the judicial system through recent court decisions. The main opposition party, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), reacted immediately with outrage, calling the move "sheer madness," a "constitutional outrage," and an explicit act of political retaliation.

The sudden escalation marks a dangerous new chapter in South Korea's political landscape. What triggered this unprecedented clash between the legislative majority and the judiciary, and what is at stake for the country's democratic norms?

The Ruling Party’s High-Stakes Strategy: Fast-Tracking Impeachment Before Year-End

The DPK’s decision on November 20 transformed months of bubbling frustration over judicial rulings into formal parliamentary action. Major domestic news outlets reported that the DPK reached a consensus during its afternoon general meeting, framing the impeachment as a necessary step to hold the head of the court accountable for failing the public.

Following the meeting, DPK leadership signaled that they were entering a "speed battle." DPK spokespersons indicated that the party intends to submit and vote on the impeachment motion within the current legislative session, ensuring it passes before the calendar year closes. Reports from major networks highlighted that DPK leaders believe their patience with the Supreme Court has officially expired.

However, the decision to launch the impeachment train was not entirely unanimous, revealing subtle rifts within the ruling party over the political wisdom of targeting the head of the judiciary.

  • The Hardline Faction: Prominent DPK lawmaker Choo Mi-ae delivered scathing remarks targeting Chief Justice Cho, referring to him as a "factory manager for the judicial laundering of insurrectionists" and arguing that he should have been impeached much earlier. External activist groups, such as the National Emergency Conference, echoed this aggressive posture, alleging that recent Supreme Court decisions effectively minimized official accountability for state crisis events.
  • Internal Skepticism: Conversely, moderate voices within the DPK expressed deep anxiety over a potential public backlash. DPK Lawmaker Kwak Sang-eon publicly voiced his opposition during the internal discussions, asking plainly, "How will the public view this?" Concerns linger among some party members that weaponizing impeachment against the Supreme Court could be interpreted by independent voters as legislative overreach, triggering a political blowback.

Controversial Verdicts Under Scrutiny: The Legal Grounds for Impeachment

To understand why the DPK is taking such a drastic step, one must look at the specific legal rulings that ignited the party's anger. Reports indicate that the DPK’s special task force on impeachment is assembling its case around two high-profile Supreme Court remand decisions that altered lower court verdicts.

First, the DPK explicitly included the Supreme Court’s decision regarding the legal cases of President Lee Jae-myung as a primary ground for impeachment. The party claims that the judicial reasoning applied in President Lee's remanded trials reflected institutional bias and undermined legal stability.

Second, the DPK task force is examining the Supreme Court's remand decision regarding former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. Critics within the ruling party and civil society argue that the Supreme Court's handling of the Han case effectively narrowed the scope of legal responsibility regarding the December 3 martial law declaration, an event that led to the impeachment and imprisonment of former official Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

This approach presents a profound legal challenge. Under South Korea's Constitution, impeaching a public official requires proving that the official violated the Constitution or specific statutes in the execution of their official duties. Disagreeing with the outcome of a court ruling is generally not considered legal grounds for impeaching a judge. Consequently, legal analysts note that the DPK’s primary hurdle will be constructing a rock-solid case proving that Chief Justice Cho committed explicit illegalities, rather than simply issuing decisions that angered the ruling party.

The Opposition Hits the Streets: Accusations of ‘Madness’ and Judicial Retaliation

The reaction from the opposition People Power Party was immediate and fierce. PPP leaders framed the DPK's impeachment push as an assault on the separation of powers and an attempt to intimidate the judicial branch.

In statements issued throughout the day on November 20, PPP leadership collectively characterized the move as "madness," a "national tragedy," and "retaliatory impeachment." PPP spokespersons argued that the DPK, despite holding executive power under President Lee Jae-myung and maintaining a strong parliamentary majority, is attempting to manipulate the judicial landscape to serve political ends.

Prominent conservative figures launched a barrage of public criticisms:

  • Han Dong-hoon condemned the impeachment plan in harsh terms, describing it as a "self-coup and an act of rebellion," going so far as to compare the ruling party's actions to "provoking the Imjin War."
  • Rep. Kim Min-jeon challenged the underlying narrative, questioning the legal classification of the December 3 martial law decree and opposing the DPK's attempt to fault the Supreme Court for its rulings on the matter.
  • Legal commentator Kang Dae-gyu called the effort an abuse of parliamentary power, suggesting humorously that if the Constitutional Court ultimately throws out the impeachment, the lawmakers who voted for it should have their salaries docked.

Refusing to limit their response to parliamentary speeches, the PPP took its protest directly to the public. On the evening of November 20, PPP lawmakers and supporters launched street marches through Seoul, carrying banners denouncing "retaliatory impeachments" and chanting slogans against what they termed DPK tyranny. By combining legislative resistance with street demonstrations, the opposition aims to rally conservative base supporters and pressure moderate voters against the DPK's aggressive timeline.

What to Watch Next: Parliamentary Votes and Constitutional Hurdles

As South Korea enters the final weeks of the year, the showdown over Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has plunged national politics into a deep freeze. With both parties dug into uncompromising positions, several key indicators will determine how this constitutional crisis unfolds:

  • The Parliamentary Vote: The DPK holds enough seats in the National Assembly to pass an impeachment motion independently. The immediate test will be whether DPK leadership can maintain strict party discipline or if internal dissenters like Kwak Sang-eon reflect a broader, quiet hesitation within the party ranks.
  • Suspension of Powers: If the National Assembly passes the impeachment bill, Chief Justice Cho will be immediately suspended from his official duties pending a final verdict by the Constitutional Court. This would leave South Korea’s highest court without its administrative head, creating unprecedented judicial paralysis.
  • The Legal Burden at the Constitutional Court: Should the motion pass Parliament, the case will move to the Constitutional Court. The court will have to determine whether the DPK’s grounds—specifically, dissatisfaction with court rulings regarding President Lee Jae-myung and former PM Han Duck-soo—constitute impeachable violations of the law. If the Constitutional Court rejects the motion, the political backlash against the ruling party could be severe.
  • Public Sentiment: With the PPP mobilizing street protests and framing the event as a threat to judicial independence, public opinion polls in the coming weeks will offer crucial clues as to whether the electorate views this as necessary judicial accountability or an overreach of legislative power.
