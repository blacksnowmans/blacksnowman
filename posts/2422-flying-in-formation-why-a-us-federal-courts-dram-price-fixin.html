Flying in Formation: Why a US Federal Court’s DRAM Price-Fixing Ruling Threatens South Korea’s Chip Giants

November 17, 2026

For decades, the global memory chip industry has lived and died by the rhythm of the semiconductor cycle. When oversupply crushes chip prices, manufacturers pull back on wafer starts and slow investments until demand catches up. When shortages hit, margins soar, capital pours back into state-of-the-art fabrication facilities, and the boom begins anew. It is a brutal, capital-intensive dance mastered by a tight global oligopoly: South Korea’s Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, alongside their American counterpart Micron Technology.

Now, that fundamental business practice is being treated not as standard industrial economics, but as potential criminal conspiracy. A US federal court has formally rejected a joint motion to dismiss a sweeping consumer class-action lawsuit alleging that Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron colluded to artificially suppress DRAM supplies and inflate market prices. By advancing the case to trial on the merits, the court has stripped the three chipmakers of their primary legal shield, plunging South Korea’s top tech heavyweights into a protracted legal battle inside the American judicial system.

The Decision That Pierced the Oligopoly’s Legal Shield

The decision to deny the motion to dismiss marks a major setback for the three memory makers, which together control the overwhelming majority of the worldwide DRAM market. When the lawsuit was initially filed, the defendants aimed for a swift, procedural exit—a common strategy designed to choke off antitrust class actions before they reach the expensive and intrusive phase of formal evidence gathering.

Instead, the presiding US federal judge found that the plaintiffs’ arguments possessed enough legal plausibility to move forward into a full trial on the merits. While the court did weed out several peripheral claims brought by the plaintiffs, it explicitly accepted five core allegations as legally credible. By allowing those five central claims to proceed, the court effectively dismantled the chipmakers’ attempt to secure a clean break from the litigation.

This procedural shift fundamentally alters the leverage in the dispute. Moving past the dismissal phase means entering deep discovery, during which the companies may be compelled to hand over internal correspondence, fab scheduling data, capacity forecasts, and executive meeting notes. For Samsung Electronics, South Korea’s largest conglomerate, and SK Hynix, the country’s second-largest memory manufacturer, the ruling ensures that their operational strategies will be picked apart under the microscope of American antitrust law for months, if not years, to come.

'Hawks Flying in Formation' and the War Over Production Cuts

At the center of the dispute is a vivid metaphor cited in the court proceedings: "hawks flying in formation." The plaintiffs argue that the synchronized actions of Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron cannot be explained as mere coincidence or isolated reactions to market pressure. Instead, they allege the three companies moved like predatory birds maintaining tight formation in midair, adjusting their flight paths in tandem to choke off the supply of DRAM and force chip buyers to swallow inflated prices.

The legal clash strikes directly at the heart of recent industry decisions. Over the past several quarters, memory makers executed deep, deliberate production cuts to stop a historic price collapse. The plaintiffs point to these wafer-input reductions—described in the legal proceedings as "irreversible DRAM production cuts"—as active evidence of coordinated restraint of trade designed to manipulate pricing benchmarks.

The controversy is further complicated by the industry’s aggressive pivot toward High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), the advanced, stacked chips essential for running artificial intelligence accelerators. The chipmakers maintain that reallocating existing fabrication lines from conventional DRAM to HBM was a necessary and rational commercial response to an unprecedented wave of AI demand. However, the plaintiffs have weaponized that very shift in court, framing the HBM migration not as a neutral technological evolution, but as a calculated maneuver to starve the market of standard consumer RAM and drive spot prices higher.

The central question before the court is whether adjusting fab allocation and cutting wafer starts constitutes an autonomous commercial necessity or an illicit, synchronized restraint of global trade.

This creates an extraordinarily dangerous precedent for the memory sector. If a judge or jury determines that shifting manufacturing lines toward higher-margin, next-generation silicon is evidence of anticompetitive intent, normal capacity management across the entire tech sector could suddenly fall under antitrust suspicion.

Beyond Damages: The Chilling Threat of Production Restraints

While the prospect of astronomical financial penalties in a US consumer class action is daunting, the financial liability is actually not the most alarming threat facing the defendants. The far more disruptive danger lies in the possibility of structural or behavioral injunctive remedies handed down by the court.

If the court concludes that the companies’ supply adjustments crossed the line into illegal market coordination, the judicial system could seek remedies aimed at "correcting" the supply chain. Such interventions could take the form of judicial oversight over fab expansion schedules, mandated minimum production quotas, or strict restrictions on how the companies pace their capital expenditures. For an industry whose survival depends on nimble, counter-cyclical flexibility, having production lines subjected to court-supervised constraints would severely cripple executive autonomy.

The operational risks extend far beyond the courtroom door:

  • Exposure of proprietary operational data: Discovery procedures in US civil antitrust litigation are famously exhaustive. Submitting wafer-start logs, equipment installation schedules, and yield trajectories exposes sensitive commercial secrets to heightened scrutiny.
  • Loss of cyclical responsiveness: If semiconductor executives must clear capacity reductions or line conversions through legal compliance teams to avoid antitrust lawsuits, their ability to react quickly to abrupt demand drops will be compromised.
  • Precedent for enterprise litigation: A consumer class action advancing to trial opens the door for enterprise customers, including major cloud providers and PC manufacturers, to mount copycat antitrust challenges of their own.
  • Heightened geopolitical vulnerability: For South Korean manufacturers balancing high-stakes investments amid US-China tech frictions, facing an aggressive antitrust challenge on American soil adds an entirely new dimension of regulatory friction.

The blurring boundary between legitimate capacity management and unlawful collusion threatens the predictability of the global semiconductor supply chain. In an industry where miscalculating demand by just a few months can wipe out billions of dollars in operating profit, treating standard production discipline as a legal liability forces executives to operate with their hands tied behind their backs.

What Comes Next for Seoul's Tech Flagships

With their dismissal motions formally rejected, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix must now re-engineer their legal and operational defense strategies for a protracted American courtroom campaign. The immediate burden rests on their legal teams to produce irrefutable, objective evidence showing that every wafer adjustment, fab line conversion, and capital expenditure cut was arrived at through independent internal modeling rather than backchannel industry signals.

In the coming months, observers should closely monitor the discovery timeline established by the federal court. Key indicators of how deep the crisis will cut include whether the court orders broad depositions of senior manufacturing and pricing executives, and whether foreign-language internal communications from Seoul and Icheon will be subjected to sweeping disclosure orders.

Equally critical will be how Samsung and SK Hynix manage their public disclosures regarding future DRAM and HBM investments. Investors should watch for changes in how both companies communicate their production outlooks during quarterly earnings calls. The era of transparent, forward-looking commentary regarding shared industry "discipline" is effectively over; in its place will come strictly vetted, cautious language aimed at starving antitrust plaintiffs of their next batch of legal exhibits.

For South Korea's memory champions, the challenge is no longer merely out-engineering competitors or winning the race to supply AI accelerators. It is proving to an American jury that when market conditions turn cold, choosing to slow down production is an act of economic survival, not an illegal pact among hawks.
