The Transaction Cliff: South Korea’s Property Market Enters a Cooling Phase Amid Political Turmoil

November 13, 2026

Walking through the real estate offices in Seoul’s affluent Gangnam district these days is a surreal experience. The windows are plastered with listings for high-end apartments, many with prices slashed by hundreds of thousands of dollars compared to last year. Yet, the desks inside are empty of customers. The phones aren’t ringing. This phenomenon, which locals call a "transaction cliff," has become the defining characteristic of the South Korean housing market in late 2024. It is a standoff between sellers who have finally blinked and buyers who are convinced the bottom is still miles away.

For years, the South Korean property market was viewed as an unstoppable engine of wealth, where "Gangnam Bulpae"—the myth that Gangnam prices never fall—governed investment strategies. Today, that myth is being tested. Hong Ji-sun, the Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, recently signaled that the market has reached a definitive turning point. In a recent press briefing, she characterized the current environment not just as a temporary dip, but as a "clear signal of a downward trend." However, while the market cools, the political temperature in Seoul is reaching a boiling point. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is currently embroiled in a civil war over how to manage this decline, while the opposition is using the cooling market as a weapon to attack the administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

Market Signals: When Inventory Piles Up and Buyers Vanish

To understand why Minister Hong is sounding the alarm, one must look at the divergence between supply and demand. In a healthy market, a drop in price usually stimulates buying. In Korea, the opposite is happening. Minister Hong noted that while the number of apartments listed for sale is rising steadily, the volume of actual transactions is plummeting. This "lock-up" suggests that the market is paralyzed by high interest rates and a psychological shift among the public.

The "Gangnam 3"—the prestigious districts of Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa—have long been the bellwether for the national market. When these areas sneeze, the rest of the country catches a cold. According to recent data from November 12, even these "invincible" neighborhoods are seeing price contractions. Minister Hong pointed out that the pace of price increases in the outskirts of Seoul has slowed significantly, and the core districts are officially entering negative territory. This is largely attributed to the government's aggressive credit restrictions and targeted measures against high-priced housing.

However, the minister’s diagnosis comes with a warning: interest rates remain the "great unknown." Should the central bank raise rates further, the downward pressure on housing could turn from a controlled descent into a crash. For the "Young-ggeul" generation—a term referring to young people who "pulled every last bit of soul" to take out massive loans for home purchases during the boom—this is a terrifying prospect. The government is now walking a tightrope, trying to achieve a "soft landing" for the market without triggering a wave of personal bankruptcies that could destabilize the broader economy.

The Battle Over Real Estate Taxes and Betterment Levies

While the market responds to economic forces, the legislative halls of the National Assembly are focused on two specific, highly controversial policies: the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (CRT) and the Reconstruction Excess Profit Recovery Act.

The Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (CRT) is a progressive wealth tax levied on owners of high-value properties. It has long been a centerpiece of the Democratic Party’s platform to redistribute wealth and curb speculation. However, as property values surged over the past few years, more middle-class families found themselves swept into the tax's net. This has led to a fierce internal debate within the ruling party. Some pragmatists within the DPK have proposed raising the tax threshold to 1.4 billion won (about $1 million) to provide relief to one-home owners. Yet, the party leadership, including Representative Kim Min-seok, has pushed back, insisting that the government must maintain "consistency" in its policy to avoid sending a signal of weakness to speculators.

Even more contentious is the Reconstruction Excess Profit Recovery Act, often referred to as the "betterment levy." This law requires homeowners' associations to pay the government a portion of the "excess profit" gained when an old apartment complex is rebuilt into a modern high-rise. Critics argue that this is a tax on "unrealized gains"—money the homeowner hasn't actually received yet. Minister Hong recently admitted that while the levy is effective at stabilizing prices, it may infringe on private property rights. Her call for a "comprehensive review" marks a significant departure from the government’s previous hardline stance.

"The government cannot remain trapped in the dogma of price stabilization while ignoring the legitimate property rights of citizens," one analyst noted. "The lack of clarity in how these levies are calculated has destroyed market predictability."

A House Divided: Internal Friction Within the Democratic Party

The political stakes of these housing policies are immense. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, real estate has become an "Achilles' heel." Recent polling by the National Barometer Survey (NBS) shows a worrying trend for the administration: the opposition People Power Party (PPP) has overtaken the DPK in public trust regarding housing policy, leading 34% to 29%. Furthermore, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dipped to 44%, with housing dissatisfaction cited as a primary driver.

This political pressure has birthed a "Real Estate Pragmatism Group" within the DPK, consisting mostly of lawmakers representing districts in the Seoul Metropolitan Area. These lawmakers are on the front lines, hearing daily complaints from constituents who feel squeezed by taxes and rising monthly costs. They are calling for a retreat from the party’s more radical regulatory stances. However, this has led to public clashes with party hardliners who argue that any "policy retreat" would be a betrayal of their core supporters.

This internal "discord" (as the Korean media describes it) creates a dangerous vacuum. When the ruling party cannot agree on whether to ease or maintain regulations, market participants simply stop moving. Potential sellers wait to see if taxes will be cut, and potential buyers wait to see if prices will fall further. This uncertainty is precisely what is fueling the "transaction cliff" that Minister Hong is so concerned about.

The Opposition Strikes Back: Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s "Catastrophe" Label

While the ruling party bickers, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent member of the opposition People Power Party, has gone on the offensive. He has explicitly labeled the current administration’s housing policy a "catastrophe." His primary target is the "actual residency requirement"—a regulation that forces homeowners to live in their properties for a certain period to be eligible for tax benefits or to sell.

Mayor Oh argues that this policy has backfired spectacularly. By forcing owners to live in their homes, the government has effectively removed thousands of rental units from the market. In the unique Korean context of Jeonse (a system where tenants provide a large lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent), this has caused a supply shock. As Jeonse units disappear, prices for the remaining units skyrocket, or tenants are forced into monthly "Wolse" contracts that eat away at their disposable income.

In response, Mayor Oh is positioning Seoul as a laboratory for an alternative approach. While the central government focuses on "regulation," the Seoul Metropolitan Government is focusing on "supply." Oh has promised to bypass certain federal hurdles to accelerate reconstruction projects and has pledged direct subsidies for young people struggling with monthly rent and utility bills. This creates a sharp political contrast: a central government seen as punishing owners, versus a city government seen as helping renters and increasing supply.

  • The Central Government's Stance: Focus on suppressing demand through taxes (CRT) and levies (Reconstruction Recovery) to force prices down.
  • The Seoul City Stance: Focus on increasing supply by easing reconstruction rules and providing direct financial aid to vulnerable tenants.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves into the final months of the year, the housing market remains the most potent variable in the country’s political and economic landscape. The cooling of prices may be a victory for the administration’s stated goals, but if it comes at the cost of a frozen economy and a fractured political base, the price may be too high.

In the coming months, keep a close eye on these three developments:

  • The DPK’s Internal Tax Vote: Will the pragmatists win out and secure a threshold hike for the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax? If they do, it could signal a broader pivot toward the center for President Lee Jae-myung’s administration ahead of future elections.
  • Interest Rate Movements: If the Bank of Korea continues to mirror global trends and maintains or raises high rates, the "transaction cliff" could turn into a "price collapse," putting immense pressure on the banking sector and highly leveraged households.
  • The "Oh Se-hoon Effect": Watch whether Seoul’s supply-side policies actually lead to more construction starts. If Mayor Oh can demonstrate that easing regulations stabilizes the rental market, it will provide a powerful blueprint for the opposition's national platform.

Real estate in Korea has always been more than just a matter of shelter; it is a battleground for social mobility, generational fairness, and political survival. Minister Hong Ji-sun may have identified the signals of a market downturn, but the question remains whether the government has the political unity to manage the landing without crashing the house entirely.
