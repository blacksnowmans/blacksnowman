The Guardians Who Betrayed the Law: Unpacking the Allegations Against Kim Ji-yong and Lee Hui-dong

October 16, 2026

Imagine a legal system where the very individuals tasked with upholding the constitution are the ones systematically dismantling it from within. In South Korea, the prosecution has long been viewed as a "state within a state"—a powerful, insular organization with the exclusive power to both investigate and indict. For decades, this concentration of power has led to accusations of a "prosecution cartel," where senior officials protect their own while weaponizing the law against political rivals. Today, as the administration of President Lee Jae-myung (of the liberal Democratic Party) seeks to entrench deep-seated reforms, two names have emerged as symbols of this alleged rot: Kim Ji-yong and Lee Hui-dong.

The allegations against these two men—one involving the cover-up of sexual misconduct and the other involving the fabrication of investigation reports—are not merely stories of individual malpractice. They represent the "black box" of the South Korean prosecution, a culture where loyalty to the organization often supersedes the duty to the public. To understand why these cases are currently shaking the foundations of Korean trust in the judiciary, one must look past the legal jargon and into the history of how the prosecution has protected its own power, even at the cost of basic human rights and democratic integrity.

The Shadow of Kim Ji-yong: When Inspectors Become Enablers

Kim Ji-yong, who currently serves as a head of a trial department within the prosecution, finds himself at the center of a brewing storm regarding his past conduct. The core of the controversy dates back to his time as an inspector—a role that is supposed to be the internal "police" of the prosecution service. However, critics argue that during his tenure, Kim acted more as a shield than a sword, particularly in the infamous case of Ahn Tae-geun.

To a foreign observer, the name Ahn Tae-geun might not ring a bell, but in South Korea, he is a central figure in the country’s #MeToo movement. Ahn, a former high-ranking official in the Ministry of Justice, was accused of sexually harassing a junior prosecutor, Seo Ji-hyun, at a funeral in 2010. The case only came to light years later, sparking a national reckoning. The allegation against Kim Ji-yong is that, while serving as an inspector, he was fully aware of the misconduct but chose to remain "lukewarm" in his response. Internal records and media reports from the time suggest that Kim claimed a formal investigation was difficult because the "victim could not be specified"—a justification that many now view as a convenient excuse to protect a powerful superior.

"The issue isn't just a single mistake," observers noted during recent parliamentary discussions. It is about a systemic tendency to "shush" crimes committed within the walls of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office (SPO). By failing to act decisively, Kim is accused of participating in a structural cover-up. In a hierarchy as rigid as Korea’s prosecution, protecting a senior official like Ahn wasn't just a personal choice; it was a survival strategy for those looking to climb the ladder. For Kim to remain in a high-ranking position today is, for many, a slap in the face to the victims of sexual violence who looked to the law for protection and found only silence.

The Prosecution as a Private Fortress

The criticism of Kim Ji-yong extends beyond the Ahn Tae-geun case. There is a growing sentiment that his career trajectory represents the "privatization" of prosecutorial power. When a prosecutor uses their authority to decide which crimes are worth investigating and which should be buried, they are no longer a public servant; they are a gatekeeper for a private interest group. The phrase "Je-shik-gu-gam-ssagi"—literally "protecting one's own family"—is frequently used in Korean media to describe this phenomenon. It suggests that as long as you are part of the "inner circle," the law will bend to accommodate you.

If the allegations hold true, Kim Ji-yong did not just neglect his duty; he arguably became an accomplice to the crimes he was supposed to investigate. This narrative is particularly potent in the current political climate, where the Lee Jae-myung government is making the dismantling of this "prosecution cartel" a primary objective. For the public, Kim serves as a living example of why internal inspections are insufficient and why external oversight is a necessity.

Lee Hui-dong and the "Instigated Complaint" Scandal

While Kim Ji-yong represents the internal rot of the prosecution’s culture, Prosecutor Lee Hui-dong represents its alleged external interference in the democratic process. Lee is currently under fire for his role in the "Instigated Complaint" (or Gobal Saju) scandal—perhaps one of the most disgraceful episodes in the history of the South Korean judiciary. This scandal involves allegations that high-ranking prosecutors, during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former leader who was subsequently impeached and is currently imprisoned), drafted legal complaints against political opponents and "instigated" members of the then-opposition party to file them.

The gravity of this cannot be overstated. It suggests that the prosecution was not merely an observer of politics but an active participant, using its specialized knowledge to orchestrate legal attacks on critics of the establishment. Lee Hui-dong’s specific alleged role is even more granular and disturbing: the fabrication of investigation reports to cover the tracks of this operation.

"Lee Hui-dong did this during the instigated complaint case. He wrote reports claiming he had interviewed investigators when he hadn't even called them in,"

This testimony, highlighted in recent reports, points to a blatant disregard for the truth. If a prosecutor can write a report about an interview that never happened, then the entire paper trail of the South Korean justice system becomes suspect. In Lee’s case, the goal was not to find the truth, but to reach a predetermined conclusion that would shield his colleagues and his superiors from accountability. It was a "scripted" investigation, where the ending was written before the first witness was even called.

The Failure of the Promotion System

The fact that Lee Hui-dong continued to thrive within the organization despite these clouds of suspicion points to a fundamental flaw in the prosecution’s human resources system. In a healthy organization, a whiff of report fabrication would lead to an immediate suspension. In the SPO of the recent past, however, such actions were often rewarded as signs of "loyalty" to the leadership. The outcry of "Why aren't we getting rid of people like this?" is a common refrain among reformists.

Under the previous era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, critics argue that the criteria for promotion shifted from legal excellence to political utility. Prosecutors who were willing to "dirty their hands" to protect the leadership were fast-tracked, while those who insisted on independence were sidelined. This has created a generation of "political prosecutors" like Lee Hui-dong, who view the law as a tool for leverage rather than a standard for justice. Removing these "unfit" individuals is now seen as the first step in restoring the public’s trust in the law.

Breaking the Cartel: The Stakes of Reform

The cases of Kim Ji-yong and Lee Hui-dong matter because they provide the "smoking gun" for why the current administration’s prosecutorial reforms are so urgent. For years, the prosecution argued that they were the only ones capable of maintaining order. However, the revelation that they were allegedly covering up sex crimes and fabricating political documents has shattered that myth. The "Prosecution Cartel" is no longer a conspiracy theory; to many Koreans, it is a documented reality.

The Democratic Party, now the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, is pushing for a complete separation of the prosecution’s power to investigate and its power to indict. The logic is simple: if the same person who finds the evidence also decides whether to go to court, there is no check on their bias. By moving investigative powers to agencies like the National Investigative Headquarters (the Korean equivalent of the FBI), the government hopes to ensure that prosecutors like Lee Hui-dong can no longer "cook the books" in secret.

Furthermore, the demand for accountability is a generational one. For younger Koreans in their 20s and 30s, the idea that a senior official can escape a sexual harassment charge because his friend is the inspector is an intolerable relic of a corrupt past. They see the values of democracy and fairness—values championed by figures like the late President Roh Moo-hyun—being trampled by a group of elites who believe they are above the law.

A Crisis of Data and Trust

There is also a technical dimension to this crisis. The prosecution’s reliance on internal data and self-generated reports means that if the input is corrupted (as Lee Hui-dong is accused of doing), the output is worthless. When the gap between "reports written" and "investigations actually conducted" becomes a chasm, the statistical reliability of the entire justice system drops to zero. This isn't just a legal problem; it's an information problem that makes it impossible for the public to know if crime rates are actually falling or if the prosecution is simply choosing not to report them.

What to Watch Next

The battle over the souls of Kim Ji-yong and Lee Hui-dong’s careers is far from over. As the Lee Jae-myung administration moves into its next phase, several concrete developments will determine if real change is occurring:

  • Parliamentary Inquiries: Watch for the Democratic Party-led National Assembly to call Kim and Lee for testimony. Their responses (or lack thereof) will be a bellwether for how much the "wall of silence" within the SPO has cracked.
  • External Investigations: The role of the National Investigative Headquarters and the Gongsu-che (the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials) will be critical. If they can successfully prosecute a sitting or former prosecutor for report fabrication, it will be a historic first.
  • The "Purge" of Political Prosecutors: The year-end reshuffling of personnel within the Ministry of Justice will show whether the government is serious about removing those tied to the "instigated complaint" era.
  • Public Sentiment: Keep an eye on protest movements and civic group statements. In Korea, the "power of the candle" (mass peaceful protests) often provides the political capital needed for the government to take on entrenched interests like the prosecution.

The shadows cast by Kim Ji-yong and Lee Hui-dong are long, but they are finally being brought into the light. Whether the prosecution has the courage to "cut out its own rot" remains to be seen. But one thing is certain: the era of the untouchable prosecutor is coming to an end, and the truth, however long it has been hidden, is beginning to surface.
