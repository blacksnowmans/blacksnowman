Will South Korea's Sweeping Prosecution Reform Truly End Eight Decades of Imperial Power?

October 02, 2026

October 2026 began with a strange dissonance in Seoul. Watching the annual Armed Forces Day broadcast, it was hard not to feel a bitter flash of irony. Just two years prior, under the increasingly erratic administration of former President Yoon Suk Yeol, the military parade had taken on a chilling, aggressive tone—a display of state force that foreshadowed the democratic crisis to come. That arrogance culminated in the dramatic December 3 martial law declaration of 2024, an attempt to override the constitution that nearly shattered South Korea’s hard-won democratic institutions.

When the progressive government of President Lee Jae-myung took office promising a break from the past, many hoped South Korea had finally crossed the river of amnesia into stable, common-sense governance. Yet history rarely lets go so easily. Barely a year later, the political landscape is churning once again with familiar reactionary currents. At stake is a fundamental question of democratic republicanism: how a citizenry can democratically control a state apparatus that holds a monopoly on violence. To understand how South Korea arrived at this fragile juncture, we have to look at the intersecting battles over judicial reform, historical memory, and accountability.

The Return of the Old Guard: Revisionism and the People Power Party

To understand the current friction, one must examine figures like Lee Jin-sook and Kim Tae-gyu, both of whom secured seats in the National Assembly for the conservative People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) via by-elections. During the Yoon administration, Lee headed the Korea Communications Commission (KCC), while Kim served as its vice-chairman. Their tenure was defined by relentless controversy. Lee was eventually suspended by an impeachment motion in parliament, and courts repeatedly ruled that the KCC's rushed "two-person decision-making system" was legally flawed. Yet, rather than facing political exile, they traded their bureaucratic titles for parliamentary badges, transforming from architects of administrative overreach into lawmakers.

Their legislative immunity has emboldened a broader push to rewrite South Korea’s democratic history. In August 2026, lawmaker Lee Jin-sook hosted a forum in the National Assembly titled "Re-evaluating the May 18 Democratic Movement." The guest list read like a provocation: conservative academic Young-hoon Lee—a prominent figure associated with the controversial "New Right" historical movement—delivered a presentation, while Huh Hwa-pyung, a key aide to military dictator Chun Doo-hwan during the 1979 military coup and the subsequent brutal suppression of the Gwangju uprising, was in attendance. Lee claimed that the May 18 massacre "should not become the property of a specific political faction," standing historical accountability on its head.

The revisionism goes deeper. Huh Hwa-pyung recently claimed in a broadcast interview that the Chun Doo-hwan dictatorship actually "laid the groundwork for democratization." Such arguments echo the fringe "colonial modernization theory" pushed by New Right historians, which suggests Japanese colonial exploitation provided the foundation for Korean independence. According to data from the May 18 Memorial Foundation, online distortion and mockery of the 1979 Gwangju democratic uprising spiked threefold over the past year, moving from crude falsehoods to sophisticated ridicule of victims and their families. Because current special laws target only the dissemination of direct falsehoods, these malicious campaigns often operate in a legal grey area, demanding stronger legislative penalties for historical defamation.

Challenging the Judiciary: The Defense of Martial Law and the Fall of Choo Kyung-ho

If Lee Jin-sook's antics focus on cultural revisionism, her allies are targeting the judiciary itself. Kim Tae-gyu recently declared on a political YouTube broadcast that former President Yoon Suk Yeol "did not commit insurrection," going so far as to call for a re-evaluation of the landmark 1997 Supreme Court ruling that convicted Chun Doo-hwan of treason. For a former judge to openly dismiss established case law and decades of judicial precedent is a direct assault on the constitutional order, designed to shield historical dictatorships from accountability.

Meanwhile, the legal reckoning for those who enabled the December 3 martial law attempt is reaching a crescendo. In the Seoul Central District Court, independent counsel Cho Eun-seok has demanded a 20-year prison sentence for Choo Kyung-ho, a People Power Party lawmaker and former floor leader, on charges of playing a primary role in the insurrection. Prosecutors point to phone logs showing heavy coordination with Yoon Suk Yeol the night martial law was declared, which resulted in 90 out of 108 ruling party lawmakers boycotting the parliamentary vote to lift it.

Choo’s defense insists there was no direct physical blocking of the legislature, attributing changing assembly locations that night to mere logistical confusion. But prosecutors argue that the rapid shifting of meeting points between the party headquarters and the National Assembly was an intentional alibi operation designed to stall lawmakers until it was too late. The trial is becoming a historic test of whether political leaders who subvert constitutional duties during a national crisis will face proportional consequences.

Dismantling the 80-Year Prosecution Regime: The Birth of the Investigation and Prosecution Offices

Against this backdrop of political and historical friction, South Korea is enacting the most radical structural overhaul of its criminal justice system in generations. On October 2, 2026, the state officially launched the new Prosecution Service (focusing solely on indictments) and the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (handling investigations), effectively ending the nearly 80-year monopoly held by the traditional Prosecutors' Office.

For decades, the Korean prosecution wielded unchecked, politically pliable investigative and indicting powers that frequently drew accusations of weaponizing the law. The complete separation of investigation and prosecution is meant to cure this systemic abuse. However, the reform is not without anxieties. Critics worry about shifting massive power to police and newly minted agencies, risking a replication of the old prosecutorial overreach under a different name.

The success of this monumental institutional shift hinges heavily on its leadership. Yet, the nomination of Kim Ji-yong to head the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency has sparked immediate friction. Critics question whether Kim—viewed by many as a "pro-Yoon" prosecutor—possesses the reformist zeal required to dismantle entrenched bureaucratic power. While the presidential office has defended the appointment and urged swift confirmation hearings, the controversy highlights the administration’s difficult tightrope walk: pushing revolutionary structural reforms while trying to avoid the pitfalls of political patronage.

Building a Republic of Citizens Against the Tides of Amnesia

History suggests that institutional changes alone cannot preserve a democracy. During the French Revolution, the nascent republic survived hostile foreign coalitions not merely through military tactics, but because ordinary citizens viewed the state as their own creation to defend. In a true democratic republic, using state power to subvert the public will—whether through a midnight martial law decree, judicial revisionism, or bureaucratic self-preservation—is treated as the ultimate betrayal.

South Korea is currently navigating two dangerous rivers: the river of historical revisionism, which seeks to sanitize past insurrections, and the river of amnesia, where the public is pressured to forget the trauma of recent political crises in the name of political fatigue. The persistent pushback from figures defending the old order demonstrates that the battle for South Korea’s democratic identity is far from finished.

The end of the 80-year prosecution era is not an endpoint, but a beginning. As the new investigative agencies find their footing and the courts rule on the architects of martial law, the ultimate safeguard of South Korea's democracy remains an attentive, unyielding citizenry. When institutions wobble under the weight of political opportunism, it is the public's refusal to forget that keeps the democratic republic anchored.

Crossing the river of amnesia requires a public that refuses to unlearn its own history. 🌊
