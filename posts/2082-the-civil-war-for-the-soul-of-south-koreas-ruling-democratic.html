The Civil War for the Soul of South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

November 07, 2026

For observers outside South Korea, the country's progressive camp might appear to be operating from a position of unassailable strength. The ruling Democratic Party holds executive power under President Lee Jae-myung, who currently enjoys approval ratings comfortably above 60 percent. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former conservative president whose administration collapsed under impeachment, is behind bars. The conservative People Power Party sits on the defensive as the primary opposition, frequently divided and struggling to articulate a coherent national alternative.

Yet inside the progressive camp, an intense and increasingly bitter ideological civil war is brewing. The fight is not being waged between the ruling party and the conservative opposition, but within the Democratic Party itself. A growing, vocal faction of traditional progressive activists, independent progressive media outlets like SisaTapa TV, and long-standing party stalwarts are sounding an existential alarm: they argue that under the banner of President Lee, an opportunistic faction they dub the "New Lee Jae-myung faction" is systematically stripping the Democratic Party of its historical soul, turning South Korea's premier progressive institution into an ideologically hollow vehicle for patronage and personal survival.

The Rhetoric of "Shared Growth" Versus Corporate Tax Breaks

At the center of grassroots discontent is a widening chasm between the Lee administration's campaign promises and its actual governance. Since taking office, President Lee has framed his national economic agenda around two flagship slogans: "fairness" and "shared growth." The presentation is polished and modern, pitched directly at a public exhausted by soaring living costs and deep socio-economic polarization. Yet when traditional progressives scrutinize the underlying numbers, they see an economic approach that looks uncomfortably conservative.

The government's proposed national budget for next year illustrates this tension clearly. Total government expenditure is set to rise by 12.8 percent to an unprecedented 820.9 trillion won (approximately $610 billion). Yet the budget allocated to the Ministry of Health and Welfare will grow by just 8.2 percent to around 148.7 trillion won (about $110 billion), with purely redistributive welfare spending inching up by only 9.3 percent. For long-time progressives, this disparity is a red flag. If overall state spending expands by nearly 13 percent while basic welfare and social safety-net growth trails far behind, redistributive equity has effectively been downgraded in the administration's policy queue.

Even more jarring for the party's traditional base is the administration's tax policy. The government is advancing broad tax cuts aimed at large conglomerates, justifying them as essential incentives to stimulate investment in strategic industries such as semiconductors and artificial intelligence. In practice, critics argue, this is nothing more than classic trickle-down economics—the very doctrine the Democratic Party spent decades opposing—rebranded as progressive industrial strategy.

Compounding this alienation are the administration's rhetorical forays into cultural debates. Traditional progressives were stunned when rhetoric echoing hard-right tropes—including remarks questioning reverse discrimination concerning multicultural families and foreign migrant laborers—surfaced from the presidential office. When conservative heavyweights like Na Kyung-won, Han Dong-hoon, and Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon end up nodding along with policies and statements coming from the Blue House, traditional supporters begin asking who, exactly, the president is governing for.

Enter the "Blue People Power Party": The Rise of the New Lee Faction

If economic compromises are the symptom, grassroots activists argue the root disease is structural: the consolidation of power by the so-called "New Lee Jae-myung faction." This cadre of lawmakers and apparatchiks surrounds the president, deriving their authority not from grassroots organizing or distinct ideological commitments, but entirely from personal loyalty to Lee's brand.

Critics describe this emerging political machine as a "Blue People Power Party"—a group wearing the Democratic Party's signature blue colors while governing like the red-branded conservative establishment. Prominent lawmakers within this orbit—including Kim Min-seok, Han Jun-ho, Kang Deok-gu, Park Sun-won, Kim Dong-ah, and Kim Nam-hee—stand accused of exploiting the loyalty of progressive voters who routinely choose the Democratic Party as the "lesser of two evils" in elections, taking their ballots for granted while dismantling the party's founding values.

"If the New Lee faction wins legislative nominations and wears the lawmaker badge again, it will mean the reconstruction of the Democratic Party is impossible. They are trading the party's historic identity for political survival."

This dynamic was made starkly evident during debates over a proposed merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the progressive minor party founded by former justice minister Cho Kuk. Factional operators who fiercely opposed mergers during past leadership tenures under figures like Jung Chung-rae are now seen attempting to engineer an absorption of the Rebuilding Korea Party—not as a coalition of shared values, but as a deliberate maneuver to box out internal dissent, brand potential rivals as "anti-Lee," and assign blame to Cho's party should negotiations falter.

Perhaps most alarming to veteran party organizers is what looks like an intentional campaign of "supporter replacement." The Democratic Party's historic base, rooted in the political lineages of Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in, is increasingly sidelined and disparaged online by pro-leadership factions. In its place, the New Lee faction appears intent on remodeling the base into a transactional coalition of centrist pragmatists and unquestioning fandom culture, viewing deep-seated ideological critiques not as legitimate internal debate, but as treacherous "friendly fire."

Parliamentary Inquiries and the Avoidance of Hard Accountability

The party establishment's political choreography in the National Assembly has only deepened voter cynicism. While Democratic leaders project an aggressive stance against institutional misconduct through high-profile parliamentary investigations (국정조사), grassroots activists view these exercises as theatrical time-wasting designed to deflect militant calls from the street for sweeping constitutional and legal reckonings.

A recent 40-day parliamentary probe launched in December and running into mid-January provides a case in point. Critics point out that its schedule conveniently wraps up right before critical political milestones in the spring, including the recommendation committee for succeeding Supreme Court justices in March. By channeling public anger into iterative legislative hearings over issues already examined by special prosecutors, party managers stall momentum, buy time for the executive branch, and avoid risky judicial showdowns that could destabilize the current governing consensus.

A similar opacity surrounds internal party governance. When explosive allegations emerged that large numbers of members from Shincheonji—the controversial religious movement that has previously faced intense political scrutiny in Korea—had registered en masse into the Democratic Party, the leadership's response drew immediate suspicion. Lawmakers prepared to brush off the controversy during general caucus meetings by disclosing superficial enrollment numbers rather than conducting a genuine forensic audit of how, when, and why these registrations occurred.

Worse, figures hostile to the party's "one person, one vote" democratic internal voting reforms have seized on the Shincheonji controversy to argue that rank-and-file voting power must be curtailed. Activists see this as a pretext: by pointing to external infiltration, party managers seek an excuse to suppress the voting leverage of reformist, dues-paying members, ensuring the New Lee faction maintains an iron grip on the candidate selection process for future elections.

Data Leaks, Broken Trust, and the Brewing "Purification Movement"

The growing rift reached a boiling point over the apparent compromise of the party's internal voter registry. Concerns erupted when lawmakers closely tied to the New Lee faction, such as Kim Nam-hee (representing the Gwangmyeong-eul district), sent unsolicited mass campaign text messages directly to female party members nationwide—and in many cases, to male members as well.

A political party's member database is its most sensitive operational asset. The fact that district-level loyalist candidates gained nationwide access to member data—a registry strictly sealed off from reform-minded figures like lawmaker Moon Jung-bok—suggests the party's administrative machinery has been hijacked for factional patronage. Allegations that donor and member lists used during the national convention by Kim Min-seok have been recycled across factional networks have prompted calls for formal legal action under South Korea's Personal Information Protection Act, with grassroots leaders characterizing the leak as outright interference with fair internal operations.

In response, an active "party purification movement" (정풍운동) is taking shape at the grassroots level. Rather than simply venting on social media, activist groups are outlining actionable, hardline resistance strategies:

  • Documenting Data Violations: Gathering digital evidence of unauthorized campaign text messages to initiate formal complaints and criminal investigations against faction-backed candidates.
  • Primary Challenges: Mobilizing traditional grassroots members to systematically challenge and defeat New Lee faction incumbents during legislative nomination primaries.
  • Independent Challenger Parties: Laying the groundwork for an independent break-away progressive vehicle if internal primaries are rigged. Under Korean election law, registering a new political party requires just 1,000 verified members across five regional chapters—a threshold activists note can be met in under two weeks.
  • Targeted Electoral Defeat: Running alternative progressive candidates in competitive districts to deliberately sink New Lee faction nominees in the general election, with activists openly asserting that losing a seat to the conservative People Power Party is preferable to allowing an entrenched, unaccountable faction to hollow out the Democratic Party from within.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will demonstrate whether the Democratic Party can mend its fractures or whether it is heading toward an irrevocable rupture. Three concrete markers will reveal which way the momentum is swinging:

First, monitor the leadership's handling of the member database controversy. If independent audits or legal complaints proceed regarding the illicit distribution of regional membership data to preferred loyalist campaigns, it will force party headquarters into the open and accelerate calls for disciplinary action against key faction organizers.

Second, watch the procedural fate of internal party voting reforms, particularly the "one person, one vote" rule. If party elites use the Shincheonji enrollment issue to roll back rank-and-file voting rights, expect an immediate, public revolt among core dues-paying activists and progressive independent media creators.

Finally, track the delicate negotiations with the Rebuilding Korea Party. If the Democratic leadership attempts a heavy-handed, coercive takeover rather than an equal ideological coalition, it will solidify the perception among traditional progressives that the ruling party is no longer an engine for progressive governance, but a protective shield for an entrenched insider cartel.
