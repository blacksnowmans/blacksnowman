The 33.8% Crisis: Inside the Deepening Trouble Facing South Korea's Lee Jae-myung Government

September 22, 2026

When you sit down to look at South Korea's political news lately, a heavy sense of fatigue tends to set in. The latest numbers from a Realmeter survey show that President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating has plummeted to 33.8% by his 67th week in office. To put that in perspective, former President Moon Jae-in sat at a robust 56.3% at the exact same point in his term, while even his conservative predecessor, Yoon Suk Yeol, held steady at 37.6%. This is not simply the fading honeymoon phase of a new administration. Alarming sounds of panic and fracturing loyalty are echoing from deep within the president's own base.

It is no longer enough to blame macroeconomic headwinds or an aggressive conservative opposition. The core problem runs much deeper: a widening chasm between what the administration says and how it actually behaves, compounded by a malfunctioning state apparatus. While presidential aides and the leader's own public statements point in different directions, the public has quietly grown cold. Understanding why this matters requires looking past the daily talking points and examining the structural failure of governance currently playing out in Seoul.

A Fractured Base and the Illusion of Reform

The steep decline in approval ratings carries a specific warning that goes beyond a mere drop in numbers. Digging into the Realmeter data reveals that the proportion of respondents saying they "strongly approve" has crashed to just 15.3%. This is a sign that the bedrock of the administration's political support is fracturing. For a progressive government entering its second year, the sense of economic deprivation and widening asset inequality has created a wall of resentment among younger, unaffiliated voters who are ready to walk away.

Yet, what frustrates loyal supporters the most is not the opposition's attacks, but the friendly fire and internal vitriol festering within the liberal camp. While the president preaches unity, online supporters tear each other apart using vicious nicknames, and presidential aides follow and retweet these accounts, tolerating a toxic internal culture. A government that fails to police its own ranks on basic decency loses the moral authority to demand national unity.

Furthermore, the administration has allowed itself to become bogged down in consuming, ego-driven political agendas—such as talk of constitutional revisions, presidential term extensions, or legal pardons—that have nothing to do with everyday survival. Voters did not elect this administration to debate the preservation of power; they elected it to fix an economy where non-cash assets dictate a person's worth. Unless the president clearly draws a line under these self-serving distractions and returns to his core strength of pragmatic, detail-oriented public policy, the slide will continue.

The Prosecutor's Office Outsmarts the Reformers

Nowhere is the gap between rhetoric and reality more glaring than in the long-promised campaign to reform South Korea's powerful investigative agencies. The administration has repeatedly vowed to strip the prosecution of its sweeping powers and overhaul the system. Yet, when the government's latest organizational restructuring proposal was published, the total number of state prosecutors remained stubbornly unchanged at 2,292.

While the country was distracted by a series of political battles and cabinet reshuffles, the prosecution quietly preserved its core staffing, investigative footprint, and institutional reach. This pattern of institutional self-preservation was further highlighted by the controversy surrounding Kim Ji-yong, a nominee to head the newly proposed Serious Crime Investigation Agency. Despite his past involvement in contentious indictments during previous administrations, the Ministry of the Interior and Safety attempted to wave away his record by arguing he was "not the final decision-maker."

This excuse sounds remarkably similar to the defenses offered by figures from past administrations during major political scandals. When officials who spent years executing aggressive prosecutorial commands under previous conservative leaders are suddenly placed in charge of a reform agency under a progressive government, it ceases to be a personnel misstep and becomes an act of institutional self-sabotage. Prosecutors are famously persistent; if a reform-minded administration fails to understand how deeply entrenched bureaucratic structures protect their turf, any attempt at structural overhaul will exist on paper only.

The Collapse of the Ruling Party's Backbone

A government cannot function when its internal communication channels completely break down. Inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the country's main liberal political force—the atmosphere has shifted from fiery debate to fearful silence. During previous assemblies, general meetings of party lawmakers would routinely run for over an hour as members argued passionately over policy and risk management.

Today, those same meetings wrap up in 15 or 20 minutes. Lawmakers keep their heads down, nobody is willing to take the political risk of speaking truth to power, and the entire weight of public backlash falls squarely on the president's shoulders. Meanwhile, the presidential secretariat—a group whose primary job is to absorb early blows, test public sentiment, and manage political risk—has vanished from the frontline. The traditional Sunday consultative meetings between the party, the government, and the Blue House have evaporated, leaving the administration in a state of isolated rule.

This lack of communication extends across the entire public sector. While all eyes remain fixed on the prosecution, other vital state functions are quietly fraying. For instance, the National Police Agency has been left without a permanent commissioner for an extended period following the dismissal of Cho Ji-ho. Amid mounting public distrust over police investigations, the executive branch has offered little explanation or guidance. Leaving basic public safety and law enforcement rudderless while chasing grand legislative battles is a glaring administrative failure.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will test whether the current administration has the political will to arrest its decline. Several concrete developments will signal whether a genuine course correction is underway:

    The Presidential Press Conference: Watch closely to see whether the president addresses the approval rating drop and internal party fractures with direct accountability, or relies on standard administrative talking points and policy achievements.

    Staff Realignments: Look for tangible changes within the presidential secretariat and the party leadership. If the same advisors who insulated the executive branch from reality remain in place, institutional reform is unlikely.

    Legislative Handling of Investigative Reforms: Monitor whether the ruling party pushes through genuine structural downsizings of investigative agencies or simply accepts bureaucratic compromises that preserve old power structures.

    Public Safety Leadership: See if the government finally moves to normalize command structures in critical administrative arms, such as appointing a permanent head to the rudderless national police.

Ultimately, a government cannot govern through messaging alone. If the administration continues to substitute public relations for decisive, transparent action, no amount of political spin will reverse the loss of public trust.
