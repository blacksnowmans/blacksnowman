South Korea’s New High School Credit System Promised Educational Freedom. Instead, It Triggered Parent Panic and Teacher Protests.

November 27, 2026

Inside an auditorium hosted by the Gangnam-Seocho Education Support Office—the public education authority covering Seoul’s most affluent and academically obsessive southern districts—800 parents packed into every available seat. They did not gather for a standard orientation. They came looking for answers to a structural shift that is throwing South Korea's secondary education system into turmoil.

Across the country, from Daejeon in the central region to rural counties like Geochang in the south, identical scenes are playing out. South Korea is preparing for the nationwide, full-scale implementation of the High School Credit System (Gogyohakjeomje). Designed to mimic college-style course selection, the initiative allows students to pick specialized elective subjects tailored to their career aspirations rather than following a standardized, rigid high school curriculum.

On paper, the policy represents a modernized, student-centered reform. In reality, as the rollout nears, it is creating severe administrative bottlenecks, exacerbating educational inequality between schools, and sparking open revolt among classroom teachers. What was pitched as a system of empowerment is turning into a chaotic struggle where students are forced to gamble on high school assignments without knowing whether their desired courses will even exist.

The Broken Promise of Student Choice

To understand the anxiety spreading through Korean households, one must understand how high school subject choices directly feed into South Korea’s hyper-competitive university admissions process. Under the High School Credit System, a student aiming for an engineering major at a top university is expected to take advanced mathematics and physics electives during high school. Taking the wrong sequence—or failing to take prerequisite courses—can derail a college application before it even begins.

However, an inherent contradiction lies at the heart of the government’s policy: schools are legally required to grant students course selection rights, but many lack the resources, faculty, or funding to actually offer those courses.

Consider a recent case in a Korean high school where 15 students registered for high-level Geometry (Giha), a key requirement for prospective STEM majors. Despite reaching what would normally be considered a viable class size, the school abruptly canceled the course due to a shortage of qualified teaching staff and budget constraints. 15 students were left stranded without the foundational math credits they needed for their target university tracks.

Making matters worse is a complete lack of administrative transparency. Under the current high school assignment system, ninth-grade students (middle school seniors) must select which high schools to apply to without knowing whether those schools actually deliver on their curriculum promises. School historical data regarding course cancellations or under-enrollment dropouts is hidden from the public. Families are effectively forced to make blind decisions, committing to a three-year high school track without knowing if essential subjects will end up on the chopping block.

Teachers Push Back Against Government Self-Praise

The gap between the Ministry of Education’s public rhetoric and the reality inside classrooms exploded into public view during a recent government conference. As officials gathered for a high-profile "performance-sharing event" to mark the second year of pilot operations for the credit system, three major South Korean teacher unions staged a joint demonstration outside the venue.

The teachers’ message was blunt: stop broadcasting promotional success stories while schools on the ground are crumbling under the weight of flawed execution.

"Schools are groaning in pain, so what exactly are the Ministry of Education and the evaluation boards celebrating?" educators demanded during the protest.

The burden on teaching faculty has multiplied exponentially under the new framework. Rather than teaching one or two standard courses, high school teachers are now forced to prepare and grade multiple distinct elective subjects simultaneously to accommodate student choices—all without a corresponding increase in teaching headcount. This multi-subject load has led to severe burnout and administrative fatigue.

Teacher organizations argue that the government has prioritized structural optics over operational feasibility. By rushing the rollout to claim political credit for educational reform, the administration has left educators to absorb the fallout of understaffing, unfulfilled course requests, and chaotic scheduling.

Grassroots Experiments and Local Government Interventions

While structural policy fractures continue to plague the nationwide rollout, local municipalities and individual schools are scrambling to build their own safety nets.

In Seoul’s western district of Mapo-gu, the local district office has taken matters into its own hands, bypassing standard school announcements to organize one-on-one custom college admissions strategy sessions and briefings for the upcoming college entrance cycles. Because high school course selection now directly influences regular and early-admissions university evaluation criteria, municipal authorities are filling the guidance vacuum by providing tailored counseling directly to families.

Education support offices in regional provinces are following suit. In Jeonbuk Province and Yecheon County, administrative bodies have launched intensive training programs for middle school teachers. The goal is to train middle school faculty into effective career counselors who can guide rising high schoolers through complex course selection trees before they step foot on a high school campus. In Uijeongbu, north of Seoul, local officials initiated the "Child’s Polaris" project, a specialized parent-education program designed to prevent confused parents from pressuring children into misaligned course choices.

Beyond municipal workarounds, a small number of schools are demonstrating what the system could look like if properly supported. Daejeon Hanbit High School, located in South Korea’s central tech hub, has gained attention for redesigning its curriculum around long-term life planning rather than narrow, rote university prep. By offering structured, interdisciplinary pathways that connect student interests with actual course availability, the school has created a working model of credit-based education. However, policy experts note that relying on the extraordinary dedication of individual pilot schools is not a sustainable nationwide strategy.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward the full implementation deadline for the High School Credit System, the educational landscape faces a critical turning point. The system's success or failure will depend on whether policymakers can resolve the mismatch between administrative promises and school-level capacity.

In the coming months, observers of South Korean policy and education should track three key indicators:

  • Course Opening Transparency: Watch whether the Ministry of Education yields to public pressure and mandates full public disclosure of course cancellation rates and actual subject offerings across individual high schools via public databases like School Info (Hakgyo Allimi).
  • Teacher Resource Allocation: Monitor whether the government provides actual budgetary support to expand teacher headcounts or compensate educators carrying multi-subject teaching loads, as demanded by the unified teacher unions.
  • Inter-School Cooperative Curricula: Pay attention to the expansion of joint credit programs (Gongdong Gyo-yuk Gwagyeong). If smaller or underfunded high schools cannot open specialized electives independently, their survival will depend on whether students can seamlessly take shared credit courses at neighboring schools or through regional online credit hubs.

For South Korea's prospective high school students and their parents, navigating this transition requires cold realism over optimism. Until structural reforms catch up with systemic ambitions, the burden of verifying a school's actual operational capacity will fall entirely on individual families.
