Beyond the Numbers: The Dilemmas Haunting South Korea’s Presidential Approval Ratings

October 26, 2026

Turn on any South Korean cable news network on a weekday morning, and you will almost certainly be greeted by a flurry of animated bar charts. Every Monday and Thursday, polling numbers measuring public approval of President Lee Jae-myung flash across screens, accompanied by urgent headlines proclaiming a breakthrough or a deepening crisis. One day a poll climbs three points, the next it drops four, feeding a relentless cycle of horse-race political commentary that leaves voters bewildered and politicians on edge.

Yet focusing on these marginal weekly oscillations misses the broader, more tectonic shifts occurring underneath South Korea's political landscape. While South Korean headlines treat presidential approval ratings as a spectator sport, the data over recent months tells a much more sobering story: a consistent structural erosion in public confidence, a hardening of partisan battle lines, and an intensifying debate within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea over whether to double down on ideological purity or pivot toward the political center.

The Anatomy of the Trend: Polling Mechanics and Shifting Grievances

To make sense of the polling barrage in Seoul, an outside observer must first understand the split in how public sentiment is captured. Different research firms employ markedly different tools. Polling Agency Kkot (Yeoronjosa Kkot), Gallup Korea, and Realmeter produce varying results in part because they alternate between automated response systems (ARS)—pre-recorded robocalls that tend to capture highly motivated, partisan respondents—and computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI), where human interviewers often elicit more cautious, moderate responses. But despite technical discrepancies between sample frames, every major survey from May through the second week of October points to the same macro-trend: positive evaluations of President Lee have followed a downward slope, while negative evaluations have steadily climbed.

Crucially, political pollsters note that the primary reasons voters offer for disapproving of the administration rarely change from week to week. Dissatisfaction typically solidifies around established grievances, meaning that the true analytical value lies in identifying what new flashpoints break into the rankings at any given moment.

  • January to February: The administration’s Achilles' heel was real estate policy, as frustration over housing costs and market regulations dominated voter discontent.
  • April to May: Backlash mounted over universal welfare and livelihood relief stipends, which fiscal conservatives and critics attacked as excessive populist handouts.
  • June: Following nationwide local elections, public disapproval generalized into vague impressions of broad underperformance, compounded by allegations of heavy-handed governance, ethical controversies, and persistent criticism surrounding President Lee’s ongoing status as a criminal defendant and accusations of avoiding court trials.
  • August: National security and defense policy entered the list of concerns, accompanied by intense friction over efforts to rein in the powers of the state prosecution service and controversial cabinet appointments, driving negative ratings to an acute peak on August 9.

These recurring complaints reflect an increasingly rigid divide. South Korea’s traditional progressive heartland in the southwestern Honam region—encompassing Gwangju and the Jeolla provinces—and voters in their forties and fifties remain the demographic backbone of the president's positive ratings. Yet opinions on both sides are deeply calcified. The country is split into entrenched camps whose reasons for loyalty or opposition have hardened into an immovable baseline.

The Dilemma of the Core Base: Lessons from Progressive History

Whenever an approval curve sags toward uncomfortable territory, political strategists in Seoul invariably invoke the concept of the "core base." What constitutes this core, however, is a matter of fierce debate. Veteran progressive author and commentator Rhyu Si-min once described political support as an intersection of ideological purists who share core values and pragmatic groups pursuing material interests. By contrast, influential progressive media figures like Kim Eo-jun view the core base as an organized collective movement—a unified force marching behind a succession of progressive leaders from Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun to Moon Jae-in, and now Lee Jae-myung.

Yet President Lee operates from a distinctly vulnerable starting point compared to his predecessors. He did not rise through the traditional, tightly knit factions that defined the pro-Roh or pro-Moon establishments. Because his institutional roots within the Democratic Party machinery are comparatively shallow, any slight tremor within the core base sends shockwaves of panic through the presidential office and the parliamentary leadership.

Historical precedent offers reason for anxiety. The last time a progressive South Korean president suffered a catastrophic fracture of his core base was in 2003, during the early months of the Roh Moo-hyun administration. When President Roh opted not to veto a special prosecutor bill investigating illicit inter-Korean financial transfers—a legacy of his predecessor, Kim Dae-jung—the traditional progressive coalition split violently. The resulting fallout shattered the old ruling party and led to the creation of the Uri Party, permanently altering South Korea’s political trajectory.

President Lee faces a different, yet equally perilous, erosion. According to Gallup Korea surveys, the president’s positive approval rating plummeted from 64 percent on May 25, 2022, down into the 39 percent range. In Honam, where progressive support once reached an overwhelming 89 percent, backing has dwindled to roughly 65 percent. Progressive regulars and moderate swing voters have drifted away in tandem, creating a dual-front crisis for the administration.

Economic Friction and the "Structural Minority" Trap

While partisan battles over institutional reform consume parliamentary debate, ordinary voters are reacting primarily to their pocketbooks. The ultimate trigger for falling approval has been policy turbulence in real estate and the stock market. During periods of financial exuberance, the administration was quick to claim rising asset values as a governance victory. But when speculative trading and overleveraged retail investments unwound, public disillusionment snapped back onto the presidential office.

Compounding the economic fatigue was the administration's early posture toward its own errors: instinctively defending disputed cabinet nominees and controversial policies rather than acknowledging missteps. This defensive stance alienated moderate voters who were already weary of prolonged clashes over prosecutorial reform and anxious about regional security uncertainties.

Within the pro-Lee faction, hardliners argue that the government must rally its hardcore loyalists and press forward with sweeping structural reforms. Yet President Lee himself has repeatedly articulated the geometric reality of South Korean electoral politics: insular tribalism is a dead end.

"If we only band together among ourselves, we end up as a structural minority. There are always people who shout, 'Let's only stick together, we can do it alone, anyone else is a traitor.' But that approach isolates us."

In a closely divided democracy, progressive loyalty alone cannot assemble a governing majority. When an administration allows its supporters to brand moderate internal critics as "extremists" or "traitors," it constructs an ideological fortress that keeps critics out while trapping itself inside. Navigating between satisfying the demands of activist loyalists and offering pragmatic policy relief to middle-class voters remains the central, unresolved tension of Lee’s tenure.

Governing Live: Media Reform and the Presidency on YouTube

Unable to rely on favorable traditional media coverage, the Lee Jae-myung administration has pursued an aggressive overhaul of South Korea’s information ecosystem. Over the past year, the government passed historic amendments to the Broadcasting Act, restructuring the governance of public broadcasters. Rather than allowing the ruling and opposition parties to carve up board appointments through the Korea Communications Commission, the new framework disperses nomination authority across viewer committees, media union workers, academic societies, and legal institutions.

Alongside structural reforms, the administration introduced amendments to the Information and Communications Network Act, establishing punitive damages of up to five times actual financial losses for spreading fabricated or manipulated information. While framed as an overdue check against malicious disinformation, the measure has sparked intense concern among journalists, press freedom advocates, and high-profile independent creators. Because the statute explicitly encompasses large YouTube channels with over 100,000 subscribers, critics fear it could easily be weaponized by public officials to silence aggressive investigative reporting through ruinous lawsuits.

Simultaneously, the administration has introduced what it terms a "live government." Bypassing the editorial filters of established newsrooms, the Blue House has taken to broadcasting entire official proceedings directly to the public via YouTube: presidential press conferences, cabinet debates, and ministerial policy briefings are streamed unedited in real time. It is the most radical experiment in direct presidential communication since the early 2000s.

President Lee has paired this livestreaming culture with direct, confrontational engagement on social media. The president regularly shares screenshots of news articles to publicly challenge reporters, at one point openly asking on social media why mainstream outlets appeared to defend speculative property investments. Conservative editorial desks in Seoul reportedly scan the president's social accounts each morning, bracing for digital confrontations. When government critiques forced the Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry to issue a public apology for an inaccurate press release, supporters cheered it as a triumph of accountability over sloppy reporting.

Yet this direct combat carries clear hazards. Detractors warn that when a sitting president publicly targets individual journalists or media institutions, it serves as an implicit instruction for hyper-partisan online supporters to swarm opponents—a practice known domestically as "targeting coordinates" (jwa-pyo jjikgi). What the presidential office frames as democratic self-defense can easily look like state-sponsored intimidation that chills independent press scrutiny.

What to Watch Next

As the administration navigates the remainder of its term, watching the topline polling numbers change week to week will offer little insight. Instead, observers should monitor three concrete indicators that will reveal whether the government can stabilize its standing:

  • Implementation of Media Regulations: How aggressively state regulators and public figures invoke the fivefold punitive damages law against critical newsrooms and independent political commentators will indicate whether media policy becomes a tool for administrative discipline.
  • Pragmatism Versus Factional Purity: Watch whether the Blue House accommodates moderate dissent on economic and real estate policy, or yields to demands from party hardliners for uncompromising judicial and institutional showdowns.
  • Honam and Moderate Reclamation: The true test of political recovery is regional and generational. If approval figures among voters in their thirties, moderates, and the traditional southwestern heartland of Honam fail to rebound from their current sub-70 percent levels, the administration’s legislative leverage will face persistent paralysis.
