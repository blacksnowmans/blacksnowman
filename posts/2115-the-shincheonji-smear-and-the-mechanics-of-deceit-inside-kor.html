The Shincheonji Smear and the Mechanics of Deceit Inside Korea's Ruling Party

November 08, 2026

In South Korean politics, few weapons are as toxic as an accusation of ties to Shincheonji, a controversial fringe religious sect long viewed by the public with deep suspicion. To level such an accusation against a colleague during an internal party election is the political equivalent of pulling the pin on a grenade. Yet, when Park Sun-won, a lawmaker and member of the Supreme Council of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, was pressed live on air to substantiate his explosive claim that an internal rival was linked to the sect, his answer was staggering: he told viewers and party members to look it up on Namuwiki, a crowdsourced, user-edited Korean equivalent of Wikipedia.

The real drama, however, played out away from the cameras. As the fallout escalated, Park allegedly placed a desperate private phone call to former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook, pleading through tears for political salvation: "I will never bring up Shincheonji again, so please, save me." When asked who orchestrated the groundless smear, Park reportedly surrendered to panic, admitting he did not even know why he had thrown the bomb in the first place. The exposure of this exchange has pulled back the curtain on the cutthroat, backstage maneuvering gripping South Korea's governing party under President Lee Jae-myung, exposing a political culture where baseless character assassination is deployed without consequence and repented only when the architect faces ruin.

A Manufactured Smear and a Desperate Midnight Plea

To understand why this episode has resonated so heavily across South Korean political circles, one must understand the currency of factional warfare in Yeouido, Seoul's political district. In the high-stakes environment of a party convention, securing a seat on the Supreme Council—the leadership body that steers party decisions and controls future nominations—is a prize worth fighting for. But the nature of Park Sun-won's offensive crossed an unwritten line.

Without presenting verified documents, intelligence, or objective facts, Park floated the Shincheonji conspiracy to taint a fellow party contender. In South Korea, where Shincheonji has faced intense public hostility—particularly after its involvement in early COVID-19 superspreading events—any association can fatally poison a politician's standing with the progressive base. Yet when confronted by debate panelists and rank-and-file party members to provide a single shred of proof, Park could only point to unverified forum entries and wiki pages.

The turning point arrived when former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook laid bare the private telephone call that followed the broadcast. According to Choe, Park’s hubris vanished the moment public condemnation erupted:

"I don't even know. I'm going crazy. I don't know why on earth I am doing this right now."

Choe’s disclosure of the call was not a casual leak; it documented specific times, records, and corroborating witness accounts. The dilemma for Park is absolute. While Choe has virtually nothing to gain politically from recounting the embarrassing plea, Park’s admission of the call would permanently shatter his credibility as a national leader. Consequently, Park has chosen tactical silence. But as critics within the party have noted, when a politician hurls a devastating allegation they know to be false, only to beg behind closed doors for their own survival, it is no longer political competition—it is extortion and defamation disguised as debate.

The Dangerous Habit of Invoking Presidential Will

The Shincheonji dispute is far from an isolated mishap. Rather, it mirrors a recurring pathology within the Democratic Party: weaponizing the phantom "will of the President" to eliminate political rivals.

A nearly identical playbook was deployed recently when party leader Jung Chung-rae proposed an initiative for broader center-left political integration. Almost immediately, rival factional figures began circulating rumors through conservative media and political whisper networks that Jung was acting out of runaway personal ambition and that his plan ran counter to the wishes of the presidential office. Jung was painted as an obstinate maverick disrupting state governance.

That narrative collapsed within days when it was revealed that Jung’s integration roadmap had been formulated in close consultation and consensus with President Lee Jae-myung and his key aides. Yet the operatives who manufactured the whisper campaign offered neither an apology nor a retraction. They simply melted into the background, banking on the news cycle to wash away their deceit.

This dynamic drew an extraordinary, scathing rebuke from Rhyu Si-min, one of South Korea's most influential progressive intellectuals and commentators. Breaking his customary detachment, Rhyu addressed the Supreme Council member directly on a public broadcast:

"Mr. Park Sun-won, you should not act like that. It is utterly appalling that someone like that sits on the Supreme Council."

Rhyu's outrage captured a broader sentiment among party loyalists. Under Article 25 of the National Assembly Act and the legislature's code of ethics, lawmakers are explicitly barred from conduct that degrades the dignity of the parliament, including smearing individuals with unverified fabrications. In practice, however, the National Assembly's Special Committee on Ethics has long been neutralized by partisan gridlock, while internal party watchdog units routinely turn a blind eye to top leadership. The result is a moral hazard: the potential payoff of a successful lie is massive, while the institutional penalty for being caught is virtually nonexistent.

Performative Loyalty and the Chameleons of Yeouido

Beyond active smears, the ongoing controversy has highlighted another fixture of Yeouido survival: theatrical loyalty campaigns paired with behind-the-scenes evasion. A case in point occurred during a recent appearance by veteran lawmaker Park Jie-won on the popular progressive YouTube channel Humility is Hard - News Factory, hosted by Kim Eo-jun. In a tense exchange, the seasoned politician publicly chided the host, demanding to know why he would "shake the Lee Jae-myung administration."

While framed as an act of dedicated defense, many viewers perceived it as an unhelpful display of performative loyalty. Appointing oneself as the President's personal bodyguard on camera often does more to stifle constructive internal debate than protect the administration. Worse, it distracts from the lawmakers who practice outright duplicity.

Within party corridors, figures such as lawmakers Lee Won-ju and Han Jun-ho have faced quiet criticism for practicing a two-faced political rhetoric. Behind closed doors with reform-minded colleagues, such figures nod in passionate agreement, endorsing aggressive policy directions. Yet, when seated before television cameras or among cautious factional peers, their tone shifts entirely—or they vanish altogether when high-stakes political battles demand sacrifice.

This chameleon behavior is particularly glaring on the issue of judicial and prosecutorial reform. During both the 21st and 22nd general election cycles, virtually every progressive candidate campaigned on promises to rein in prosecutorial overreach. No candidate dared tell voters that reform should be delayed or watered down to appease bureaucratic interests. Yet once safely ensconced in the National Assembly, several of these same figures have dragged their feet, using legislative subcommittees to stall structural overhaul bills. They rode into office on the voters' appetite for sweeping reform, only to place fresh stumbling blocks in its path.

The party has seen this trajectory before. The rapid rise and subsequent disenchantment surrounding former judge-turned-lawmaker Lee Su-jin, alongside the shifting stances of former floor leader Park Hong-keun, served as harsh lessons for progressive voters on how quickly campaign fervor can degenerate into bureaucratic self-preservation once power is attained.

What to Watch Next

The exposure of the Shincheonji controversy marks a critical test for the ruling Democratic Party as it balances legislative dominance with the executive agenda of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Moving forward, observers should monitor three concrete fault lines:

  • Supreme Council Accountability: Watch whether Park Sun-won is forced to make a formal public explanation or step back from front-line party leadership duties ahead of upcoming policy drives, or whether the party establishment chooses to sweep the recorded plea under the carpet.
  • Discipline on Internal Disinformation: Keep an eye on whether the Democratic Party’s leadership institutes actual disciplinary procedures against lawmakers who leverage unsubstantiated smears against internal colleagues, particularly as local and interim election nomination battles heat up.
  • Legislative Velocity on Core Reforms: Monitor whether the lawmakers who aggressively courted the party's base during the general election actively sponsor and vote for judicial and institutional reform packages, or whether procedural delays persist within key standing committees.

For South Korea's governing party, the danger does not stem from external opposition alone. It lies in tolerating a breed of politics where internal slander is treated as an acceptable shortcut to power, and where the architects of deception can plead for mercy in private while retaining their mandate to lead.
