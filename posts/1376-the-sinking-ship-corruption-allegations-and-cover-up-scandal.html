The Sinking Ship: Corruption Allegations and Cover-up Scandals Threaten Seoul’s Han River Bus Project

October 21, 2026

Imagine standing on the banks of Seoul's iconic Han River, watching sleek new passenger ferries glide across the water. For Mayor Oh Se-hoon, this ambitious water-bus transit system was supposed to be a crowning achievement—a modern, eco-friendly commuting marvel for a megacity choked by traffic. But beneath the shiny surface, the project is rapidly capsizing. What began as an innovative transit initiative has morphed into a full-blown political scandal, complete with untraceable six-figure payouts, missing contract clauses, alleged document shredding by municipal auditors, and a vicious smear campaign against a whistleblower.

For international observers watching South Korea's high-stakes political theater, this controversy offers a sobering look at how local administration can derail when high-profile urban projects collide with backroom politics. As Seoul grapples with these revelations, the scandal has exposed deep fractures in the city’s governance under Mayor Oh, drawing sharp contrasts with the transparency demanded by President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. To understand why this matters, we have to look past the PR and examine the murky paper trail left in the wake of the Han River bus.

The Ghost Commission: Unexplained Payments and the 320 Million Won Question

At the heart of the financial scandal is a shipbuilding contract with Gadeok Heavy Industries, one of the primary private partners building the ferries. When legal experts, including attorney Lee Jung-il, scrutinized the contract, they uncovered a glaring anomaly: nowhere in the official paperwork is there any mention of "advisory fees" or "brokerage commissions." Yet, financial records show that about 320 million Korean won (approximately $230,000) was quietly funneled out of the project budget.

In any public works project utilizing taxpayer money, moving hundreds of thousands of dollars outside of a binding contract is a fundamental breach of administrative law. Instead of a minor accounting error, critics point to a stark discrepancy: indirect expenses that should have been capped at around 27 million won ($19,000) per vessel inexplicably ballooned. When pressed during a parliamentary audit by the National Assembly's Land, Infrastructure, and Transport Committee, Mayor Oh defended the payout, calling it a "brokerage commission" rather than an illegal rebate, and noted that a standard 2% rate was being investigated.

Far from clearing the air, Oh’s defense only deepened suspicions. Labeling an uncontracted fee as a "commission" after the fact looks less like a standard business practice and more like a retroactive attempt to legitimize a slush fund. Compounding these fears, testimony from a Gadeok Heavy Industries executive revealed that external brokers had approached the firm waving project manager business cards to demand kickbacks. For a project heavily funded by public coffers, the existence of unregistered brokers skimming cash off the top points directly to potential lobbying and corruption.

Shooting the Messenger: The Campaign Against the Whistleblower

When an internal whistleblower stepped forward to flag these financial irregularities, the Seoul Metropolitan Government’s response was swift, brutal, and entirely predictable: they attacked the messenger.

Rather than investigating the missing 320 million won, the city administration and Mayor Oh’s allies launched a concerted campaign to character-assassinate the employee. During the parliamentary audit, Oh publicly dismissed the whistleblower not as a public-interest informant, but as a disgruntled worker whose internal transfer was merely the result of "unpleasant interpersonal conflicts" with colleagues. By framing the whistleblower's actions as petty personal revenge or workplace maladjustment, the administration attempted to neutralize the substantive allegations.

This tactic is a classic authoritarian playbook maneuver: shift the focus from the crime to the morals of the person reporting it. The whistleblower was subsequently hit with a transfer order on July 30, right after initiating contact with internal auditors—a timing that local insiders view as textbook retaliatory punishment. Under South Korea's Public Interest Whistleblower Protection Act, municipal authorities have a legal duty to shield informants. Instead, Seoul's leadership has created a chilling internal atmosphere, signaling to other potential whistleblowers that raising concerns will result in professional isolation and public disgrace.

Shredding the Evidence: The September 30 Paper Trail Cover-Up

Perhaps the most damning aspect of the scandal is not just the missing money, but the deliberate attempt to erase the paper trail. On September 30, as scrutiny over the Han River bus project intensified, a directive was reportedly handed down within the Seoul Metropolitan Government’s Audit Committee: destroy all related files.

Administrative audits exist to preserve records and hold power to account. Ordering the deletion of investigation files ahead of a potential police referral is an act of self-incrimination. A recovered internal memo dated September 12 at 8:46 AM provides a smoking gun, explicitly instructing staff to "conduct audit claims after regular operations begin and coordinate the timing of the police referral while maintaining strict security." In other words, the city leadership was well aware of the corruption risks but actively managed the timeline of the investigation to minimize political fallout.

Faced with illegal orders from above, career civil servants—often referred to in Korea as *neul-gong* (permanent public officials)—found themselves caught in a Kafkaesque trap. Desperate to protect themselves from future criminal liability when a new administration eventually takes over, some officials resorted to covert operations, locking investigation drafts behind digital passwords and hiding physical originals. The fact that government employees had to treat their own administrative records like state secrets to protect them from their superiors underscores a grim reality: Seoul's municipal governance under Oh has drifted dangerously close to backroom authoritarianism.

What to Watch Next

As the Han River bus project barrels toward full commercial operation, the political and legal fallout is reaching a critical juncture. Here is what to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • Independent Investigations: Watch whether law enforcement agencies push past Seoul’s internal audit roadblocks to subpoena the unredacted financial records and trace the exact destination of the 1.5 billion won ($1.1 million) in disputed project expenses.
  • Whistleblower Protections: Look for legal challenges from the whistleblower's defense team regarding the retaliatory July 30 transfer and potential violations of the Public Interest Whistleblower Protection Act.
  • Political Accountability: As the ruling Democratic Party applies pressure from the national level, observe how Mayor Oh navigates upcoming municipal assemblies where opposition lawmakers are demanding a full, independent audit of the transit initiative.

Mayor Oh can attack messengers and delay audits until the ferries are officially afloat, but physical ships cannot sail away from a financial paper trail. If the Seoul Metropolitan Government continues to stonewall, the Han River bus may well become remembered not as a transit triumph, but as the vehicle that sank an administration's credibility.
