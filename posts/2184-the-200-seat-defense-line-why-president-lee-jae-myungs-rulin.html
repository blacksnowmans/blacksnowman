The 200-Seat Defense Line: Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Ruling Party Is Stumbling Toward the Next Election

November 10, 2026

Inside the halls of South Korea’s presidential office and the headquarters of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, an existential anxiety looms over every policy briefing and closed-door caucus. It is not merely the customary pressure to maintain a legislative majority in the upcoming National Assembly election. It is the terrifying math of South Korea’s constitutional supermajority: 200 seats out of 300. Should the conservative opposition, led by the People Power Party, capture two-thirds of the legislature, the barrier guarding President Lee Jae-myung against impeachment dissolves overnight. In Seoul’s high-stakes political theater, the upcoming legislative vote is not simply a midterm verdict; it is an existential battle for survival.

Yet, despite the gravity of the stakes, the mood inside the presidential administration remains oddly detached. Observers and party insiders point to a widening rift between the presidential office and the Democratic Party leadership under Chairman Kim Min-seok. Instead of taking swift, decisive command of a volatile political landscape, the administration has fallen back on a vertical hierarchy, treating the ruling party as a subordinate messaging arm while adopting a wait-and-see posture. With presidential approval ratings stuck in place and centrist voters drifting away, the ruling bloc appears trapped between the president’s personal political instincts and a party apparatus consumed by internal factional feuds.

The 200-Seat Impeachment Threat and the Mirage of Economic Turnarounds

To understand the current paralysis, one must examine President Lee’s political calculus. The immediate strategic imperative is keeping the opposition comfortably below 200 seats. In South Korea, passing an impeachment motion against a sitting president requires the assent of two-thirds of the National Assembly. If the opposition secures that threshold, the presidency effectively loses its shield, exposing the administration to relentless investigative committees and constitutional trials. For Lee, a parliamentary majority is the difference between governing and fighting for legal survival.

Despite this stark reality, the presidential office has responded with rigid complacency. Rather than aggressively intervening to reset political narratives or overhaul his inner circle, Lee has exhibited a recurring pattern from his past crises: retreating into a passive observation mode, waiting for shifting public sentiment and factional currents to settle before committing to a move. The administration appears convinced that time is on its side. The operating assumption is that once new government spending packages roll out, the economy will steadily rebound, consumer confidence will stabilize, the domestic stock market will rally, and voters will naturally gravitate back to the ruling party by election day.

This optimism, however, rests on a dangerous psychological trap. Lee’s inner circle continues to view national politics through the prism of his past tenure as the Governor of Gyeonggi Province, the country’s most populous province encircling Seoul. In Gyeonggi, Lee repeatedly transformed dire political predicaments into resounding personal victories by delivering swift, tangible administrative results—such as targeted local stimulus vouchers and decisive crackdowns on illegal stream bed commercialization. His advisers remain convinced that tangible governance will always outshine political noise.

National governance, however, does not mirror local administration. An executive governor wields direct budgetary and administrative enforcement powers that can generate immediate, localized impact. A national president, by contrast, operates in an environment governed by complex macroeconomic headwinds, intense diplomatic pressures, a fractured legislative branch, and constant judicial exposure. Vague expectations that public sentiment will automatically warm as budget funds trickle down ignore both policy transmission lags and the rapid speed at which disillusioned voters abandon an incumbent administration. Publicly expressing frustration during Cabinet meetings that the National Assembly is uncooperative solves nothing; it merely signals an administration struggling to command its own political environment.

The 2012 Playbook vs. Today’s Stagnant Democratic Party

To highlight the ruling camp’s strategic drift, political strategists frequently point to one of the most remarkable comebacks in modern Korean political history: the 19th general election in 2012. Months before that vote, conservative President Lee Myung-bak was an electoral liability, suffering from abysmal approval ratings hovering around 27 percent. Defeat for the ruling Saenuri Party (a predecessor to today’s People Power Party) seemed preordained.

Yet the ruling conservatives shocked the nation by winning a 152-seat outright majority. They did so through brutal, uncompromising internal transformation led by Park Geun-hye, who took over as chair of the party’s emergency leadership committee. Park’s team deliberately created public distance between the party and the unpopular sitting president, boldly stripped entrenched factional stalwarts of their nominations, and recruited fresh, moderate faces capable of capturing the critical political center. The party endured excruciating internal pain to offer the electorate a credible break from the status quo.

The Democratic Party today exhibits none of that urgency. There are no signs of painful nomination reforms or willingness to prune entrenched incumbents to woo moderate voters. Instead, the presidential office and party headquarters oscillate between stopgap public-relations patches and contradictory public communications.

This messaging confusion has severely alienated non-partisan voters. While seasoned lawmakers holding five or more terms float delicate discussions about party mergers and legislative realignments, President Lee frequently takes to his personal X account (formerly Twitter) to publish sharp, cryptic jabs aimed at specific intra-party factions. When a president bypasses formal institutional channels to vent personal grievances or signal factional displeasure on social media, it sows confusion among party rank-and-file and signals to the wider public that the administration lacks coherent message discipline. Moderate voters do not care about factional score-settling; they care about rising living costs and economic stability.

Factional Hypocrisy and Paralyzed Leadership under Kim Min-seok

At the center of the ruling party’s organizational troubles sits party leader Kim Min-seok. Public opinion polls repeatedly rank Kim among the politicians with the highest unfavorability ratings in South Korea. Yet, shielded within an insular party structure, the leadership team operates with a tone of self-satisfied denial, ignoring warning signals flashing across polling data.

This dysfunction is compounded by the contradictory posture of the so-called "New Lee Jae-myung" faction. Claiming fiercely loyal allegiance to the president, these cadres nevertheless remain silent when party policy drifts away from the administration’s core agenda, or when Kim Min-seok falters in guiding legislative priorities. A glaring case in point is the ongoing clash over the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (known in Korea as Jongbuse), an emotionally charged levy on high-value properties. While pragmatists argue for tax relief to stop affluent middle-class homeowners from defecting to the conservative opposition, traditional progressive hardliners insist that watering down property taxes constitutes a betrayal of core values. Party leadership has remained paralyzed, unwilling to make an authoritative choice.

Rather than mediating these platform contradictions to protect the president’s legislative standing, self-styled loyalist groups spend their energy fighting lingering rivalries against traditional pro-Moon Jae-in factions and clashing with prominent party hardliners such as lawmaker Chung Cheong-rae. The dynamic resembles an unhealthy personality-driven fandom rather than an ideological political party. By prioritizing the purge of internal rivals over building a coherent policy platform, these factions leave the party vulnerable to sweeping electoral retribution.

The Judicialization Trap and What Lies Ahead

Perhaps the most destructive dynamic gripping the Democratic Party is its complete loss of self-cleansing capacity, culminating in what Korean commentators term the "judicialization of politics." When internal corruption scandals and ethical allegations have surfaced—ranging from controversial dealings tied to state-run lenders like the Industrial Bank of Korea to opaque private-sector commercial ties and contentious religious allegations—the party’s internal fact-finding panels have proven entirely ineffective.

Dominated by close associates of party leader Kim Min-seok, internal review committees have repeatedly engaged in foot-dragging, attempting to sweep awkward controversies under the rug rather than conducting transparent investigations. By refusing to let uncompromising internal watchdogs carry out deep organizational purges, the party has ceded its political autonomy. When a governing party cannot police itself through political compromise and internal discipline, every dispute inevitably lands directly in the hands of state prosecutors and police investigators.

The Cycle of Judicialization: When politicians abdicate their responsibility to resolve wrongdoing through transparent internal ethics panels, they surrender national politics to the jaws of criminal investigations, destroying any possibility of bipartisan compromise or public trust.

To ordinary voters struggling with price inflation, high borrowing costs, and household debt, the spectacle of ruling-party figures endlessly feuding over internal probe findings and portraying themselves as victims strikes a tone of profound hypocrisy. Claiming victimhood while walking straight into prosecutorial investigations proves that the party’s political instincts have atrophied.

In the coming months, two key indicators will determine whether the Democratic Party can avert disaster: First, watch whether President Lee initiates a genuine overhaul of his Cabinet and presidential staff, signaling a willingness to break out of his insular comfort zone and abandon top-down management of the legislature. Second, observe whether the Democratic Party’s candidate nomination committee dares to purge controversial factional loyalists in competitive metropolitan swing districts in favor of credible, policy-oriented moderates. If the presidential office instead doubles down on its wait-and-see strategy while delegating its political battles to the courtroom, the path toward an opposition supermajority will transform from a theoretical nightmare into a devastating reality.
