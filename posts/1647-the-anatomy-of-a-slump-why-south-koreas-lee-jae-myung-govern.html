The Anatomy of a Slump: Why South Korea’s Lee Jae-myung Government Is Losing Ground

October 29, 2026

In South Korean political lore, an ancient maxim often resurfaces whenever an administration runs into rough seas: "The ruler is the boat, and the people are the water." Water can carry a vessel to great destinations, but it can just as easily overturn it. For President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, those waters are growing increasingly turbulent. After enjoying a commanding mandate following the dramatic collapse of the previous conservative administration, the current government finds itself grappling with public approval ratings stalled in the mid-40% range—and in some surveys, falling much lower.

For a foreign observer, a mid-40s approval rating might seem unremarkable, perhaps even respectable by the standards of Western democracies. But in the hyper-polarized hothouse of Seoul politics, polling numbers are more than just popularity metrics; they are the literal buoyancy of executive power. Without strong numbers, an administration's reform agenda stalls, the civil service grows cautious, and opposition forces—led by the conservative People Power Party—seize the offensive. Worse yet for President Lee, the warning lights are flashing not only on the charts of pollsters, but within his own progressive coalition.

The Poll Numbers Tell a Bleak Story

To grasp why political strategists in Seoul are sounding the alarm, one must look at the historical baseline. In recent Gallup Korea polling, President Lee’s positive evaluation sits at approximately 46%. While that might not immediately register as a collapse, it represents a steep drop compared to past precedent. When former President Moon Jae-in led a previous Democratic Party administration, his approval rating stood at a soaring 75% at the exact same juncture. That massive reservoir of public goodwill provided Moon with the political capital to push forward ambitious changes to law enforcement agencies and pursue high-stakes diplomatic initiatives with North Korea. A rating of 46%, by contrast, leaves little room to maneuver when facing determined legislative resistance.

A closer look at the methodology behind South Korean polling reveals an even more troubling trajectory. South Korean pollsters primarily rely on two methods: telephone interviews conducted by human operators (CATI) and automated response system (ARS) robocalls. Both are flashing red:

  • Human telephone interviews (CATI): Between March and May of 2026, President Lee commanded approval figures in the mid-70% range. However, starting in September, negative evaluations overtook positive ones—a trend that has persisted for over a month.
  • Automated polling (ARS): These surveys recorded a "dead cross"—the point at which negative marks surpass positive sentiment—beginning in August after an initial dip in July 2026. The latest ARS figures show disapproval climbing to 57.7%.
  • Realmeter tracking: The numbers published by Realmeter are bleaker still, with President Lee’s approval sinking to 35% against a disapproval rating of 62%.

This 35-to-62 dynamic evokes unsettling memories across the political spectrum. It mirrors the exact polling rut that plagued the conservative administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, approval plunged to 35% within his first 100 days and remained trapped under a 60% disapproval ceiling until his presidency ended in impeachment, removal from office, and eventual imprisonment. To see similar numbers appearing under a Democratic administration is a profound psychological shock to the ruling base.

Beyond Policy: The Problem of Presidential Demeanor

Policy disagreements alone rarely account for such a sustained erosion of trust. Prominent political commentators, including progressive author Rhyu Si-min and poll analyst Park Si-young, argue that the core issue lies in the public's perception of President Lee's personal demeanor. Politics remains an emotional transaction. Voters expect an effective administrator, but in times of economic fatigue and national strain, they also expect humility, empathy, and an instinct for listening.

President Lee built his political reputation as an uncompromising, results-oriented executive during his days in local and provincial government. Yet the very traits that fueled his rise—his relentless drive and assertive rhetoric—risk being perceived as arrogance when wielded from the presidential office. Critics note that the administration has frequently deployed the unyielding "language of victors" rather than adopting an empathetic posture toward public hardship. When an executive appears dogmatic, even well-designed policies are rejected by voters who feel talked down to.

In South Korea’s presidential system, state administration almost inevitably converges toward the character of the individual occupying the Blue House. The president’s personal intellectual habits, interpersonal boundaries, and willingness to tolerate dissent dictate the performance of the entire executive branch. If the head of state isolates himself within a narrow inner circle or brushes aside expert counsel, the government loses its institutional flexibility. The prevailing concern among analysts is that the machinery of state is shrinking to match the personal limitations of one individual.

The Mechanics of Power: Incentives and Value Clashes

The current impasse can be broken down using a set of fundamental political axioms:

  • Axiom 1: People respond to tangible incentives. Citizens support leaders who tangibly improve their everyday lives and turn away when government initiatives bring perceived burdens. If state reforms fail to deliver concrete economic or personal benefits to the general public, loyalty evaporates.
  • Axiom 2: Every citizen possesses their own moral yardstick. When a government insists that its policies are inherently "just" without demonstrating respect for the public's prevailing sense of fairness, friction is inevitable. Voters will not simply accept moral declarations from on high; they constantly evaluate whether an administration's actions align with their common sense.
  • Axiom 3: Power seeks to perpetuate and demonstrate itself. Political leaders naturally wish to preserve their authority and show that they can enact their will even against opposition. However, when an administration pushes contentious policies purely to demonstrate authority or appease a narrow activist faction, the wider electorate interprets this not as leadership, but as overreach.

The growing gap between the ambitious promises made on the campaign trail and the reality of governing reflects this dilemma. Attempting to force unpopular measures through sheer executive willpower inevitably activates Axiom 2, triggering deep skepticism among the moderate electorate.

Fracturing from Within: The Unraveling Coalition

The most dangerous symptom of the administration’s declining approval is not the hostility of the conservative opposition, but the fragmentation of its own camp. Influential progressive media figures and commentators—including broadcaster Kim Ou-joon, political consultant Lee Hae-yeon, and analyst Park Si-dong—have warned that the core progressive coalition is fracturing in public view.

Rather than presenting a united front, prominent voices within the ruling camp have increasingly turned on one another. High-profile progressive figures and commentators such as Hwang Hee-doo, Kim Young-hwan, Choe Kang-wook, and Park Ye-seul have clashed over ideological purity, strategic priorities, and the direction of the party. When internal factions spend their energy attacking intra-party rivals instead of addressing the nation's problems, supporters experience acute fatigue. The phrase "friendly fire" has become a routine descriptor of the party's internal discourse.

This dynamic has produced a telling reaction among regular Democratic Party supporters: calls for party representatives to simply "stay silent." The logic, while paradoxical, reflects desperation: many believe that whenever party elites speak, they only aggravate public weariness and drive ratings down further. A divided coalition strips the president of his natural defensive perimeter, leaving his office exposed to legislative blockades and outside scrutiny.

What to Watch Next

A presidency operating with approval ratings in the mid-40s—or lower—faces an uphill climb in South Korea's unforgiving political arena. The critical question moving forward is whether President Lee Jae-myung will interpret these poll numbers as statistical noise or as an urgent call for course correction. In the weeks ahead, several indicators will signal the administration's trajectory:

  • Tone and Communication: Watch for whether President Lee shifts his public rhetoric away from assertive declarations toward open forums, town halls, or direct press conferences aimed at demonstrating humility.
  • Party Discipline: Observe whether the leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea can quell factional skirmishes among high-profile surrogates and establish a coherent legislative agenda.
  • Polling Ceilings: Pay close attention to whether disapproval numbers settle permanently above the 60% mark across both Gallup and automated tracking. If that ceiling hardens, pushing contentious legislation through the National Assembly will become nearly impossible.

Polling numbers do not lie about sentiment. Behind the percentages are ordinary citizens sending an unmistakable signal: governing a democracy requires more than executive willpower. It demands constant listening and an enduring respect for the water beneath the boat.
