Why South Korea's 30- and 40-Somethings Are Turning Away From President Lee Jae-myung

September 05, 2026

When you sit down to look at the state of South Korean politics these days, a heavy sense of exhaustion tends to settle in. This was supposed to be an administration built on the promise of safeguarding the nation and carefully nurturing the everyday lives of ordinary citizens. Instead, the country is staring down a grim reality: President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating has sunk to 34.7%, while his disapproval rating sits at an alarming 61.9%. Looking at those numbers doesn't just breed frustration; it triggers a deep, hollow sense of futility.

Yet the real danger is not merely a low polling number. The true peril lies in the bitter, exhausted cries of ordinary citizens embedded within those statistics. Most telling is the severe disapproval coming from voters in their 30s and 40s—demographics that recorded negative ratings of 70% and 66.6%, respectively. For a government that once staked its identity on representing the working public, these figures serve as a glaring warning sign: the core of South Korea’s economic workforce feels that their very survival is under threat.

The Reality of Everyday Survival and Economic Strain

The profound anger radiating from South Koreans in their 30s and 40s is far from accidental. This generation represents the backbone of society, the demographic expected to be driving economic activity at its peak. Yet right now, their bank accounts are running dangerously dry. The housing market remains a labyrinth of frustration where buying a home feels increasingly like an impossible dream, while volatility in the stock market has translated directly into severe asset losses for everyday retail investors.

The core issue here is simple, yet easily forgotten by those sitting in the halls of power: when politics fails to solve the basic need to make a living, the moral legitimacy of a ruling administration evaporates practically overnight. Somewhere along the way, lofty political promises appear to have been overshadowed by institutional friction. What ordinary people need right now is not grand historical rhetoric; they need the basic peace of mind that comes with knowing they won't have to stress over next month's rent.

Looking back at historical benchmarks, an approval rating hovering in the 30% range during a president's second year in office is a flashing red light. It mirrors the painful troughs once experienced by past administrations, such as those of former presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Park Geun-hye, both of whom saw their support bases fracture during similar phases of their tenures. While every political context carries its own unique variables, public approval remains the most brutally honest report card a government can receive. The steep, downward slope of public trust suggests that the current administration is severely misreading the pulse of the electorate.

Procedural Missteps and the Dangers of Isolated Power

Beyond economic anxiety, a major driver of this discontent is how power is being exercised within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's primary liberal political force. Recent moves by party leadership—such as aggressively restructuring party rules and regulations to tighten control over candidate nominations, and deploying internal ethics watchdogs to sideline dissenting factions—have alienated voters who once championed progressive democratic norms.

Internal party democracy is the bedrock of any healthy political organization. When candidate selection processes are perceived as fair and transparent, public support naturally follows. However, when institutional rules are treated as malleable instruments for tightening political control, the party's broad appeal shrinks, pushing even loyal supporters away.

Critics argue that President Lee has increasingly projected an authoritarian demeanor rather than fostering genuine communication with his base and party members. When a leader who once promised to act as a humble "instrument" for the public instead attempts to rule from above, the inevitable result is widespread disillusionment. Politics fundamentally belongs to the public. Without inclusive leadership that integrates diverse internal voices, centralized authority quickly finds itself isolated in an echo chamber.

The Imperative of Restoring Trust and Genuine Empathy

Reversing this downward trajectory requires more than superficial public relations adjustments. The administration and ruling party leadership face a clear imperative: halt heavy-handed nomination strategies, establish genuine channels for internal dialogue, and completely reevaluate the policy approaches driving economic hardship.

Approval ratings can theoretically recover, but rebuilding eroded public trust requires an exponential effort. This is a critical juncture for the administration to steady the ship before social divisions deepen further. The message sent by a 34.7% approval rating is stark: without immediate and substantive change, the future remains deeply uncertain.

Ultimately, the citizens who supported this administration were not asking for miraculous, unattainable economic miracles. They were hoping for a fair society where fewer people feel cheated, where individuals receive just treatment, and, above all, where politics involves actual conversation rather than top-down edicts. True supporters are often the first to feel a deep sense of betrayal when power fails to protect everyday livelihoods, and their sharp criticism stems from a desperate hope that politics can still course-correct. For the administration, the path forward requires returning to the original promise of serving the public, lest the very authority they fought to secure slips entirely through their fingers.

What to Watch Next

  • Party Rule Revisions and Nominations: Monitor how the Democratic Party handles ongoing internal resistance regarding candidate selection rules and party regulations ahead of upcoming local or legislative dynamics.
  • Economic and Housing Policy Shifts: Watch for concrete policy adjustments from the Blue House aimed at stabilizing household financial pressures, particularly measures addressing rental costs and retail investment stability for 30- and 40-something demographics.
  • Polling Trends in the Autumn Quarter: Track whether subsequent public opinion surveys show stabilization above the 40% threshold or continued erosion, which would signal a deeper structural crisis for the administration's second-year agenda.
