When Critics Keep Their Distance: The Ruy Si-min Affair and South Korea's Crisis of Power

September 24, 2026

Picture the scene: a government sliding in the polls, its once-fervent base beginning to fray, and the president’s inner circle desperately reaching out to a prominent public intellectual for a quiet sit-down. It is a classic playbook move designed to shore up flagging support with a show of unity. But what happens when the intellectual says no?

This was not a hypothetical dilemma in South Korean politics recently. Ruy Si-min, a veteran progressive writer, commentator, and former politician, publicly turned down a meeting with South Korean President Lee Jae-myung. The refusal immediately sparked intense debate across the country. Yet, as political observers quickly realized, the story is not about a snub. It is about a deeper, more unsettling question for South Korea’s progressive camp: what happens when the boundary between political support and blind worship collapses, and when those who once championed a leader are forced to choose between loyalty and honesty?

Cracks in the Core: How the Disenchantment Began

To understand why Ruy’s refusal matters, one has to look back at how the relationship between South Korea's progressive intellectuals and the current administration soured. According to Ruy's recent media interviews, the warning signs began appearing as early as September. Surrounded by ardent supporters of President Lee, Ruy noticed a subtle shift. People who had once cheered unconditionally started asking hesitant, troubled questions: "Is the person you told us about really who we thought he was?"

For a movement built on high expectations of political efficacy—the promise that progressive governance would tangibly improve ordinary lives—such doubts are corrosive. Ruy did not dismiss these murmurs as attacks from political adversaries. Instead, he recognized them as the early fraying of the administration’s core support base. When he voiced these concerns, critics accused him of fanning the flames. But Ruy argued the opposite: he was merely naming a fire that was already burning.

By December, the warnings grew sharper. Rather than attacking the president directly, Ruy began critiquing the missteps of the administration’s inner circle—prime ministers, presidential chiefs of staff, and cabinet ministers. Yet the presidential office fundamentally misread these critiques. Instead of taking them as policy warnings, the administration viewed them as an attack on subordinate staff, retreating into a defensive posture.

This dynamic bears an uncomfortable resemblance to past political missteps in South Korea, where leaders walled themselves off from public sentiment, treating criticism as hostility rather than vital feedback. When an administration views its own staff solely as targets for protection, debate dries up and truth gets buried.

The Optics of Rejection and the Fall in the Polls

As the administration's approval ratings dipped—with recent data from pollsters like Realmeter showing sharp double-digit drops among crucial demographics, including voters in their fifties and the traditional progressive stronghold of the Jeolla provinces—the pressure on the government intensified. Entering the 30% approval danger zone is historically a tipping point for South Korean administrations, accelerating lame-duck dynamics and freezing bureaucratic momentum.

Instead of addressing the policy failures driving the slide, however, pro-government circles spun a narrative of personal friction. Leaks to the press painted Ruy as an arrogant, cold intellectual who stubbornly rebuffed an earnest, reaching-out president. It was a classic framing exercise designed to shift blame for the government's communication breakdown onto an independent critic.

Ruy’s refusal to meet, however, was rooted in a simpler, firmer principle: he is neither a presidential campaign worker nor a crisis-management messenger. In his view, a public intellectual’s job is not to act as a private troubleshooter or to participate in political theater designed to shore up numbers. Meeting with the president behind closed doors, he suggested, would risk compromising his independence and turning a public critic into a private accessory to power.

The Burden of the Public Critic

At the heart of this episode lies a fundamental tension in modern democratic politics: the relationship between power and its critics. In South Korean political culture, intellectuals and writers who lean progressive have often felt a strong pull to align closely with incumbent administrations to protect shared political gains from conservative opposition. But Ruy’s stance serves as a stark reminder that unconditional loyalty can easily curdle into complicity.

"I supported him, but I did not worship him," Ruy has maintained, drawing a sharp line that modern political discourse often erases. In a healthy democracy, supporters must be allowed to applaud victories and voice sharp dissent over failures. When internal criticism is treated as treason, organizations rot from within.

By keeping his distance, Ruy has provided a masterclass in the aesthetics of political detachment. He demonstrated that true intellectual independence requires refusing the velvet trap of proximity. Power always wants to meet—sometimes to persuade, sometimes to co-opt. But a critic whose voice can be bought or neutralized by an invitation is no longer a critic at all.

What to Watch Next

As this political drama continues to unfold in Seoul, several key indicators will determine where the administration goes from here:

    The handling of further dissent: Watch whether the administration doubles down on attacking internal critics or opens genuine channels for policy correction. Approval rating floors: Monitor whether core demographics continue to drift away or if sliding poll numbers force a major cabinet reshuffle. The evolution of public discourse: Pay attention to whether other prominent intellectuals follow Ruy’s lead in carving out an independent space for public criticism, free from partisan capture.

The Ruy Si-min affair is more than a personal disagreement between a writer and a presidential office. It is a stress test for how South Korea's political sphere handles accountability. Ultimately, politics should not be about worshipping leaders, but about constantly questioning them in pursuit of a better tomorrow. Whether this administration heeds the warning or dismisses it as background noise will shape the political landscape for months to come.
