South Korea Court Increases Prison Sentence for Former Minister Over Martial Law Order

September 09, 2026

The atmosphere inside Seoul High Court's courtroom carried a profound sense of historical weight as the presiding judge instructed the defendant to rise. When the court handed down a nine-year prison sentence, ruling that a lower court's previous punishment was inappropriately lenient, it marked another critical milestone in South Korea's ongoing reckoning with the extraordinary and chaotic events of last winter's short-lived martial law declaration.

The case centers on Lee Sang-min, who served as the Minister of the Interior and Safety—the high-ranking cabinet official overseeing internal security, disaster management, and local government administration. Following an appeal by the special counsel team led by Cho Eun-seok, an independent investigative body established to probe the insurrection, the appellate court overturned a lower court's seven-year sentence. The original trial had found Lee guilty of playing a key role in an insurrection, but prosecutors had originally demanded 15 years, arguing that the initial punishment failed to reflect the gravity of subverting the constitutional order.

To foreign observers, the legal terminology of "insurrection" and "important duties" may sound archaic, but in South Korea's democratic history, they represent the absolute legal red lines protecting the nation from military coups and authoritarian overreach. Understanding why an appellate court decided to add two extra years to a former minister's sentence requires looking past standard judicial metrics and examining how a high-ranking civilian official became a willing instrument in an assault on South Korea's democracy.

The Collapse of the Lenient Defense and the Weight of Elite Responsibility

During the initial trial, Lee's legal defense relied heavily on the argument that he was not a primary planner of the martial law decree issued by former President Yoon Suk Yeol and Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun. His lawyers pointed out that no evidence suggested Lee had participated in plotting the martial law before its announcement, nor was he the mastermind behind specific coercive measures. Furthermore, they emphasized his lack of prior criminal convictions and argued that his overall involvement in the political turmoil was relatively minor.

The Seoul High Court dismantled this defense by drawing a sharp distinction between an ordinary citizen and someone occupying the upper echelons of state power. The appellate judges ruled that a clean criminal record should be given extremely limited weight when evaluating crimes committed by high-ranking government officials who abuse their authority to dismantle the constitution. The law cannot apply the same yardstick to a civilian as it does to a state official entrusted with upholding the democratic framework.

This reasoning goes to the core of executive accountability. Even if Lee was not in the initial room where martial law was hatched, his role as a senior cabinet minister made his compliance far more damaging. When an unlawful order cascades down from the highest office, cabinet members are supposed to act as institutional firewalls. Instead, Lee chose compliance over resistance, turning systemic executive authority into a tool of subversion. The appellate court rightly judged that the original bench's leniency missed the mark of true judicial justice.

Defiance and Evasion: How Bad Behavior Backfired in Court

A decisive factor in the court's decision to increase the sentence from seven to nine years was Lee's conduct throughout the investigative and judicial processes. From the moment he faced questioning, Lee displayed an evasive attitude, showing virtually no remorse and failing to distance himself from the justification of the martial law declaration.

Even if he privately harbored reservations, his legal culpability remained undiminished because of the specific actions he took once the decree was issued. As interior minister, he possessed the administrative authority to evaluate orders and protect public institutions. Instead, when faced with a critical moment for democratic choice, he chose submission. Worse still, during the legal proceedings, court records showed he attempted to obscure the truth, waiving certain rights not to self-incriminate only to engage in evasive testimony and shield his actions.

In South Korea's penal system, post-crime conduct—such as showing genuine remorse versus attempting to cover up tracks—plays a substantial role in sentencing. Lee's ongoing refusal to acknowledge the gravity of his actions proved to be a fatal miscalculation, cementing the court's view that a harsher sentence was necessary to reflect his unrepentant stance.

Targeting the Press: The Plan to Silence Independent Media

Among the most alarming specifics of Lee's conviction was his involvement in ordering utility cutoffs—specifically electricity and water supplies—to targeted media outlets critical of the administration. This directive, transmitted down to the head of the National Fire Agency, was an aggressive assault on the freedom of the press, which serves as a foundational pillar of South Korean democracy.

Utilizing public infrastructure and emergency service agencies to blind and silence independent journalism echoes the darkest tactics of past authoritarian regimes. Had the fire agency officials blindly followed the minister's unlawful instructions, the country would have witnessed a terrifying regression into darkness, with news organizations forced off-air while executive power sought to suppress public scrutiny.

The fact that the media blackouts were ultimately prevented was not due to any change of heart by Lee. Rather, the plan failed because the martial law decree collapsed too quickly, and because lower-level career officials—specifically the head and deputy head of the fire agency—recognized the blatant illegality of the command and deliberately stalled or bypassed execution. The court's ruling forcefully rejected the idea that a failed crime reduces the guilt of the person who ordered it.

Traumatizing Frontline Emergency Workers

The fallout from Lee's directives also inflicted severe professional and psychological damage on South Korea's emergency personnel. Firefighters and rescue workers, who spend their lives risking physical safety to protect the public from disasters, suddenly found themselves questioned as suspected accomplices in an insurrection simply because their agency head received an unconstitutional order from the interior ministry.

Forcing civil servants dedicated to public safety to act as unwitting conduits for political subversion represents a complete breakdown of administrative integrity. The appellate court's verdict recognized that the mental anguish and institutional damage inflicted upon the fire service were direct consequences of Lee's willingness to use his portfolio as a shield for unlawful political behavior. By turning emergency management infrastructure into an arm of political suppression, he betrayed not only the constitution but the very people working under his ministry.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The Seoul High Court increased former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min's sentence from 7 years to 9 years in prison, ruling the initial punishment too lenient.
  • His directive to cut utilities to media outlets via the fire agency constituted a grave violation of insurrection laws and press freedom.
  • His unrepentant attitude, evasive testimony, and failure to show remorse throughout the trial were decisive factors behind the harsher sentence.

What to Watch Next

As this high-profile case moves inevitably toward a final appeal at South Korea's Supreme Court, several critical developments warrant close monitoring by observers of the country's political and legal landscape:

  • The Supreme Court's Legal Review: The final verdict will set a definitive legal precedent regarding the precise boundaries of ministerial liability during unconstitutional executive actions. Watch for how the top court treats the interplay between a subordinate's refusal to obey and a superior's intent.
  • Wider Trials of Other Officials: Lee's ruling does not exist in a vacuum. The courts are concurrently processing cases against other key architects and executors of the martial law decree, including former President Yoon Suk Yeol and former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun. The consistency of these sentences will indicate how firmly South Korea's judiciary is drawing the line against executive overreach.
  • Institutional Reforms in Public Administration: Lawmakers in the National Assembly are actively discussing legislative safeguards to ensure that cabinet ministers and agency heads cannot unilaterally leverage emergency apparatuses, such as the fire or police services, for partisan political objectives.

South Korea's legal system has demonstrated that holding high-ranking architects of political instability accountable is a slow, methodical, but ultimately resolute process. While the appellate decision leaves some room for debate over whether a nine-year term fully matches the historical damage of an attempted insurrection, it sends an unmistakable message: in a mature democracy, executive power is never absolute, and those who weaponize the state against its citizens will face the full weight of the law.
