The Grim Reality of Property Auctions: How to Handle Uncooperative Occupants

September 08, 2026

For many international observers, the Korean real estate market is often viewed through the lens of soaring apartment prices in Seoul’s Gangnam district. However, behind the glossy headlines lies a far grittier reality: the world of property auctions. For investors, winning a bid is only the beginning. The real battle begins the moment you step onto the property to claim what you have legally purchased, only to find the previous owner still sitting inside, refusing to budge.

As of August 2025, I have successfully won four property bids this year alone. Each time, I am reminded of just how fractured South Korea’s jeonse system—the unique, massive lump-sum deposit rental model—has become. When the math doesn't add up and a landlord can no longer afford to return a tenant's deposit, the state-backed Korea Housing and Urban Guarantee Corporation (HUG), South Korea's state-run agency that insures housing deposits, often steps in. But in many cases, landlords exploit this, handing over their keys to HUG while squatting in the property themselves, hoping to squeeze a "moving fee" out of the next buyer. It is a cycle of professional victimhood that tests the patience of even the most seasoned investor.

The Anatomy of a "Stubborn" Occupant

In my most recent case, the previous owner’s audacity was staggering. After the property was put up for auction, the owner had already surrendered the door passcode to HUG, yet they continued to occupy the home. They weren't just staying there; they were holding the property hostage, despite having no legal claim to remain. They were effectively waiting for a buyer like me to arrive so they could demand a payoff to vacate.

The numbers behind these situations are often bleak. In this specific case, the original jeonse deposit was about $120,000 (165 million KRW), while the market value hovered around $130,000 to $135,000. When the housing market cooled, the landlord couldn't return the deposit, the property was registered as "distressed" by the tenant, and the cycle of foreclosure began. Now, as the new owner who has already invested about $160,000 (220 million KRW) including repairs, legal fees, and administrative costs, I am the one tasked with cleaning up the mess.

The previous owner attempted to control the situation through "text message warfare," using vague excuses—claiming to be in Gangneung or scheduling meetings only to cancel them at the last minute. This is the "manual" for malicious occupants: use emotional manipulation and delay tactics until the legal pressure becomes too great to ignore.

Negotiation in the Real World: Why Law Beats Emotion

My meeting with the occupant finally took place in early September 2025. Predictably, the conversation was a masterclass in obfuscation. The previous owner attempted to play the victim, citing the "unfairness" of the auction and how they had once been an experienced investor themselves. They tried to use their past "expertise" to guilt me into paying a high moving fee. They demanded roughly $1,500 (2 million KRW) just to leave the property.

Many novice investors would crumble here, intimidated by the prospect of a long-term fight. However, the secret to successful "myeongdo" (the process of vacating a property after an auction) is simple: you must hold the legal high ground. I made it clear that while I was willing to discuss a small moving fee, my timeline was non-negotiable. I explained that every day they delayed beyond my final payment date, their financial liability—and the costs associated with a forced eviction—would only grow. Faced with the cold, hard reality of legal consequences, the "stubborn" occupant suddenly became quite reasonable. We reached an agreement for the $1,500 fee, and they vacated the property within a week.

The Non-Negotiable Rules of Auction Investing

If there is one thing to learn from the chaotic landscape of Korean property auctions, it is that there is no "secret trick" to speed up the process. There is only the system. To survive and thrive in this environment, investors must abide by three core principles:

  • Never skip the "Order of Possession" (Indo-myeongryeong): Even if you are on friendly terms with the occupant, you must file for an order of possession immediately upon payment. Never assume a handshake deal will hold; if the occupant changes their mind, you need the legal machinery already in motion to protect your investment.
  • Respect the Law of Trespass: Even if a house looks abandoned, do not force entry. Doing so, even on your own property, can lead to criminal charges for breaking and entering or property damage. Always involve court enforcement officers to handle forced evictions legally.
  • Mind the Tax Trap: Many investors make the mistake of failing to properly document their "myeongdo" expenses. Whether a payout to an occupant qualifies as a "necessary expense" for tax deductions is a gray area. Without a clear, documented legal argument, you risk having your deductions denied by the tax office later.

What to Watch Next

As we move into late 2025, the volume of distressed residential properties in Korea remains high. The key factor to watch is the tightening of HUG’s recovery processes and how local courts handle the backlog of eviction orders. For foreign investors or those new to the Korean market, the message is clear: Success in property auctions is not about finding the cheapest deal; it is about having the mental fortitude to manage the aftermath.

The legal system is heavily weighted in favor of the successful bidder. If you are prepared, follow the procedures to the letter, and maintain a cold, analytical distance from the emotional drama of the previous owners, the auction process is not a nightmare—it is simply a predictable administrative procedure.

If you are looking to enter the market, remember: fear is a product of ignorance. By mastering the legal steps of property transition, you turn a high-risk situation into a calculated investment. Do not let the "malicious occupants" dictate the tempo of your business; let the law do that for you.
