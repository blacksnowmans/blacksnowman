Behind the Telegram Screen: How a Cult Conspiracy Rumor Exposed Factional Warfare in Korea’s Ruling Party

October 18, 2026

When high-powered telephoto lenses catch the glowing smartphone screens of politicians on the floor of South Korea’s National Assembly, the resulting snapshots rarely bring good news. But the text messages photographed over the shoulder of Representative Park Sun-won on September 1 went far beyond typical parliamentary gossip. They offered a raw, unvarnished look at backroom plotting inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—complete with demands to "seize control" of the party’s internal ethics watchdog to neutralize rival factions.

That snapshot was merely a prelude to a far more bizarre and damaging spectacle. In mid-October, a bitter public feud erupted between Park, a member of the DPK’s Supreme Council and a former top intelligence official, and Choe Kang-wook, an outspoken former lawmaker from the same progressive camp. At the center of their quarrel is a sensational conspiracy theory: that Shincheonji, a secretive fringe religious sect, had orchestrated an underground takeover of the ruling party’s leadership election. What began as an alarming claim of religious infiltration has rapidly collapsed into an embarrassing squabble over call logs, internet searches, and private Telegram chats, raising serious questions about governance within the camp of President Lee Jae-myung.

A Midnight Phone Call and a Scramble over Namuwiki

The latest round of recriminations was ignited on October 16, 2026, during an appearance by former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook on the popular YouTube political commentary program Hong Sa-hoon Show. Choe broke months of silence to recount an extraordinary phone conversation that allegedly took place during the DPK’s national party convention.

At the time, Park Sun-won was campaigning for a seat on the party’s Supreme Council. To rally support, Park went public with explosive claims that Shincheonji—a controversial Christian-derived sect infamous in South Korea for clandestine proselytizing—was systematically infiltrating the DPK. The sect had previously been embroiled in political scandal during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, South Korea's former president who was subsequently impeached and is now serving a prison sentence. Conservative primary races during that era were dogged by persistent allegations that some 56,000 Shincheonji adherents had mobilized to sway candidate selections in favor of the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party. By claiming that the sect had now issued a "blue ribbon" directive—ordering its followers to don the DPK’s signature blue and infiltrate progressive ranks—Park suggested that the ruling party’s internal democracy was actively being subverted.

The backlash inside the party was immediate and furious. Suspecting that the rumor was a clumsy smear designed to discredit rival convention candidates, colleagues demanded hard proof. According to Choe, a panicked Park phoned him the very next day, practically begging for help to contain the fallout. Choe recounted pressing Park for the origin of the allegation:

"Never mind that—who on earth told you to bring up Shincheonji? Where did that story come from? Just tell me that."

Park’s alleged reply was startling in its emptiness:

"Ha, even I don't know. It’s driving me crazy."

For party insiders, the admission was staggering. Park is not an inexperienced political novice; he previously served as the first deputy director of the National Intelligence Service (NIS), South Korea's primary foreign and domestic intelligence agency. Yet when pressed by journalists and fellow party members for concrete evidence—bank transfers, directives, or member rosters—Park offered neither intelligence dossiers nor whistleblowers. Instead, he publicly told reporters to look up the claims on Namuwiki, South Korea’s open-access, crowdsourced wiki equivalent to Wikipedia. Behind evocative labels like the "Pilates project" and "blue and red ribbons," there was no verifiable trail of organizational meddling. Even Park was eventually forced to admit that, as of the convention, no actual operational links had been confirmed.

"Hehehe, I Should Sue": Cynicism Replaces Accountability

Rather than providing clarity or de-escalating the controversy, Park’s response to Choe’s broadcast turned an internal party embarrassment into an outright farce. Shortly after the broadcast aired, a supporter forwarded a real-time summary of Choe’s remarks to Park via the encrypted messaging app Telegram. Park’s written reply was promptly leaked to online political forums.

Dismissing the former lawmaker with contempt, Park wrote back: "I don't even have Choe Kang-wook’s phone number, and that fellow is not in any relationship to say such things to me." When the supporter warned that Choe had threatened to disclose even more damning details if pushed, Park laughed it off with a flippant message: "Hehehe, I guess I should sue."

The spectacle of a senior ruling party leader using dismissive language against a former legislative colleague while hiding behind the threat of defamation lawsuits has left supporters dismayed. In South Korea's legal environment, whether two political figures spoke on the phone is a matter of black-and-white cell carrier records, easily settled within days. Choe, undeterred, doubled down immediately, stating that if Park filed a complaint, he would reveal the rest of the suppressed details in court.

To many observers, the posture adopted by Park reflects a troubling retreat from accountability. Rather than answering why an unverified cult conspiracy was weaponized in an internal election, a member of the party’s highest decision-making body appeared content to treat the controversy as a matter of partisan gossip, laughing behind encrypted chats.

The Leaked Screen: How Party Ethics Became a Tool for Factional War

The Shincheonji dispute cannot be separated from the deeper institutional malaise plaguing the Democratic Party. The phone dispute took on an even darker cast because it followed the September 1 photo scandal involving the DPK’s Ethics and Inspection Team—the very organ tasked with rooting out misconduct and maintaining integrity within party ranks.

On that afternoon, cameras captured a Telegram message sent directly to Park by Kang Min-gu, the deputy director of the ethics team. Kang’s text read like an instruction memo from a factional war room:

"It looks like Chung Chung-rae’s faction is moving against Lawmaker Kim Ki-pyo. I haven't received word from central party headquarters to report to work. Could you speak to the Secretary-General? ... I think I need to get there quickly and seize control."

The implications were unmistakable. An official charged with independent oversight was actively lobbying a Supreme Council member for an appointment, framing the internal ethics apparatus not as a neutral arbiter, but as an operational weapon to be "seized" against rival factions loyal to heavyweight figures like Chung Chung-rae.

DPK leader Kim Min-seok moved swiftly to contain the damage, dismissing Kang from his post almost immediately. Yet the structural damage was already done. Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee publicly warned that cosmetic personnel changes would not restore trust among lawmakers or party members who now feared they could be subjected to targeted, politically motivated audits. When internal watchdogs are viewed as private hit squads for factional bosses, fair competition inside the party ceases to exist.

What This Means for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

This escalating drama comes at a critical juncture for South Korea’s political landscape. President Lee Jae-myung’s administration came to power following an unprecedented constitutional crisis that saw the conservative administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol collapse under impeachment and criminal prosecution. The ruling party inherited a difficult national agenda, marked by persistent domestic inflation, a sluggish labor market for young Koreans, and the daunting challenge of restoring public trust in state institutions.

Yet rather than acting as a disciplined legislative partner, elements of the ruling party appear trapped in recursive factional combat. By borrowing the conspiratorial playbooks of their predecessors—branding internal rivals as agents of shadowy religious cults without a shred of proof—lawmakers risk alienating the moderate voters who backed the transition to the current administration.

As the fallout from the Choe-Park clash unfolds, observers should watch several concrete developments:

  • The carrier records and legal threats: Whether Park actually follows through on his threat to file a criminal complaint against Choe. A formal legal dispute will compel the disclosure of telecommunications metadata, conclusively proving whether the convention phone call took place.
  • Choe Kang-wook's promised follow-up leaks: If pressured, Choe has explicitly signaled he holds additional records regarding who orchestrated the Shincheonji whispering campaign during the leadership race.
  • Party reform under Kim Min-seok: How the DPK leadership handles the fallout from the Ethics and Inspection Team scandal. Replacing a single deputy director will not satisfy critics unless structural safeguards are instituted to prevent the party's disciplinary apparatus from being used in factional vendettas.

South Korea’s voters granted the Democratic Party power to stabilize a disrupted republic, not to engage in internet-sourced smear campaigns. If ruling party leaders continue to substitute Namuwiki searches for verified facts and meet legitimate internal scrutiny with cynical Telegram quips, the political capital needed to sustain the Lee Jae-myung administration's reform agenda may evaporate before it has even had a chance to yield results.
