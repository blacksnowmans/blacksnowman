When the Scales of Justice Only Weigh for Some: South Korea's Crisis of Selective Law Enforcement

October 12, 2026

Imagine standing in a high-rise stairwell in Seoul, the air thick with construction dust, only for a routine day to end in unthinkable tragedy. Now imagine, months later, that the paperwork clearing your loved one's death of any criminal responsibility was mailed not to you, but mechanically addressed to the deceased — leaving you to find out about the closed case by watching a televised parliamentary audit. This is not the plot of a dystopian drama. It is the grim reality facing grieving families in South Korea today, where the machinery of the state often grinds forward with heartless bureaucratic indifference for ordinary citizens while treading with extreme caution around the powerful.

Public cynicism in South Korea has moved past simple fatigue and settled into a deep, bone-weary resignation. The country's political and investigative arenas are increasingly defined by what critics call "selective blinking" — the artful ability of prosecutors and police to keep their eyes wide shut when an investigation touches those connected to power, while bringing the full fury of the state down on others. Two recent controversies have laid bare the hollow core of South Korea's vaunted rule of law: a superficial police probe into a high-profile figure's self-published textbook sales, and the callous dismissal of a fatal workplace safety accident. Together, they expose a justice system that acts as a plush velvet shield for the strong and a sharp, cruel blade for the weak.

Following the Digital Money Trail: The Amazon Textbook Controversy

The controversy centers on a seemingly obscure yet fiercely contested battle over whether educational materials sold on Amazon by former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon involved a profit-seeking motive. During a parliamentary audit, Representative Yang Bu-nam of the ruling Democratic Party — the country's main liberal political force — pressed police officials on the matter. The police offered a remarkably convenient conclusion: because the Amazon listings were priced at just $0.99 and fees reportedly absorbed the total amount, there was no commercial intent. Investigators conveniently shrugged off the matter by claiming international judicial cooperation was too difficult to pursue.

To anyone familiar with digital publishing, the police explanation evaporates under minimal scrutiny. Yang pointed out that Han had previously mentioned registering the material through Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP), Amazon’s self-publishing platform. A thirty-minute search of Amazon's public policy pages reveals a standard option: authors can receive a royalty rate of approximately 35% of the list price, provided pricing requirements are met. Yet South Korea's investigative agencies somehow failed to find what an opposition lawmaker's junior aide uncovered in half an hour. Critics argue this was not a failure of research, but a deliberate choice to look the other way.

This kid-glove treatment stands in glaring contrast to past aggressive prosecutions. Observers vividly recall the exhaustive, dragnet-style raids — involving dozens of search warrants and minute-by-minute tracking of internship hours — directed at Cho Min, the daughter of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. When investigations brush against conservative power brokers or prominent political figures, however, the state's investigative drive suddenly stalls. A similar pattern emerged regarding allegations surrounding the political interference scandals of the past, specifically during the administration of now-imprisoned former leader Yoon Seok-yeol, where mountains of communications records ultimately yielded limp non-indictment decisions. The law, it seems, remains remarkably gentle with the strong.

Fatal Oversight: The Elevator Inspector's Death and Administrative Cruelty

While political elites wrangle over digital royalties and public image, the quiet tragedies of ordinary workers are met with a chilling lack of accountability. On February 14, 2025, Kim Kyung-tae, an elevator safety inspector, fell to his death from the 24th floor while on the job. Left to navigate an incomprehensible grief, his family filed criminal complaints against two supervisors for professional negligence resulting in death, pleading for a thorough investigation.

Instead, the police delivered another blow. Despite receiving supplementary opinion papers and petitions from the grieving family over the summer and autumn, investigators quietly dropped the charges against both supervisors in early 2025. A man had plummeted to his death, yet no one was held responsible. Worse still, when police issued their official non-indictment notices, they mailed the documents directly to the deceased victim's home address, operating on a blind, automated adherence to registry databases. The family only discovered the case had been quietly closed when the issue was raised during the national audit.

This bureaucratic gaffe goes far beyond a simple administrative error; it constitutes a profound violation of surviving family members' right to know and amounts to secondary trauma. When a state apparatus is so devoid of basic empathy that it mails a dismissal notice to a dead man and logs the task as "completed," it forces the public to question the fundamental purpose of the institution. For a worker who sacrificed his life in a hazardous industry, the state offered neither protection in life nor transparency in death.

The Erosion of Judicial Trust and What Lies Ahead

These parallel crises — one involving political privilege and digital commerce, the other concerning industrial safety and administrative apathy — boil down to a single, urgent question: Whom does South Korea's legal system actually serve? When investigative depth shifts according to political station, and when an elite figure's publishing profits are met with institutional blindness while a dead worker's family is locked out of the truth, the foundational premise of equality before the law collapses.

A society cannot maintain social cohesion when its judicial tools function as partisan protectors rather than instruments of neutral equity. If legal outcomes depend on one's social standing or political alignment, the nation risks sliding from a constitutional democracy into an environment governed by institutional impunity. What citizens are demanding is not vengeance, but consistency — the simple assurance that rules apply universally.

Editor's Note: In the shadow of grand political theater and powerful figures dodging accountability, the quiet grief of ordinary citizens is routinely erased. While legal experts bend over backward to parse the digital royalties of an Amazon ebook, a family learns of their son's closed case only because a lawmaker brought it to a televised hearing. True fairness cannot take root until the state abandons its selective blindness toward the powerful and its cold disregard for the vulnerable.

Moving forward, several concrete developments bear watching to gauge whether South Korea's justice system can reclaim its integrity:

  • Mandatory Direct Notification Reforms: Watch for legislative proposals requiring investigative agencies to communicate directly with surviving families in workplace fatality cases, closing loopholes that allow bureaucratic notifications to bypass actual stakeholders.
  • Potential Re-investigation Pressure: Monitor whether public outcry and political pressure force police to reopen inquiries into digital publishing and copyright matters where initial evidence was summarily dismissed.
  • Oversight of Investigative Compliance: Pay close attention to upcoming parliamentary committee reviews scrutinizing police handling of industrial deaths and non-indictment decisions.

Truth has a way of refusing to stay buried, no matter how thickly wrapped it is in bureaucratic red tape or political excuses. For South Korea's investigative agencies, restoring public trust will require more than defensive press releases; it will demand a willingness to look squarely at the evidence they have spent too long ignoring.
