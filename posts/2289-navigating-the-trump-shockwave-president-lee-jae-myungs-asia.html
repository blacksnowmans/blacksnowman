Navigating the Trump Shockwave: President Lee Jae-myung’s Asia Tour and a Fracturing Global Order

November 13, 2026

Diplomatic calendars rarely offer the luxury of calm, but the itinerary awaiting South Korean President Lee Jae-myung this November comes at an exceptionally volatile moment. As the aftermath of the recent United States presidential election sends ripples across global markets and security architectures, Seoul finds itself balancing delicate supply chains, regional security dilemmas, and deep geopolitical realignments. Beginning November 16, President Lee embarks on a high-stakes tour through the Philippines and China, attending a sequence of multilateral summits designed to steady South Korea’s footing on the world stage.

For international observers, understanding Seoul's current diplomatic posture requires appreciating its recent domestic transition. President Lee Jae-myung, representing the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, took office following the dramatic impeachment, removal, and subsequent imprisonment of his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The current administration has framed its international agenda around pragmatic statecraft—deliberately seeking to repair regional diplomatic fissures and rebuild international standing that suffered during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office. Yet South Korea’s renewed diplomatic push does not occur in a vacuum; it unfolds against a backdrop of fierce disputes within Donald Trump’s Washington, dangerous flashpoints in the Middle East, and widespread social unrest in Europe.

President Lee’s Diplomatic Marathon: Rebuilding Alliances and Supply Chains

President Lee’s four-day regional mission begins in Manila on November 16, where he will take part in both the Korea-ASEAN Summit and the Korea-Mekong Summit, followed by the ASEAN+3 and the East Asia Summit (EAS) on November 17. The visit to the Philippines underscores Southeast Asia’s growing strategic value for South Korea. ASEAN is not merely a major export destination; it functions as an essential strategic buffer for mid-sized powers trying to avoid getting crushed between Washington and Beijing.

The agenda in Manila and during the broader tour centers on securing broader regional backing for the Korean Peninsula peace process and safeguarding trade flows amid escalating protectionism. On November 17, the ASEAN+3 and EAS sessions will provide an important venue to recalibrate trilateral coordination between South Korea, China, and Japan—a framework that has experienced considerable friction in recent years. President Lee’s diplomatic team is promoting a policy of strict national interest, designed to maintain practical ties across ideological divides rather than locking the country into rigid geopolitical camps.

The tour shifts to China on November 18 and 19 for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) Economic Leaders' Meeting, where Lee will share the stage with leaders from the United States, China, and Russia. For South Korea’s trade-reliant economy, the discussions here are existential. Central to the working table are secure access to critical minerals and semiconductor supply chains. Having repeatedly felt the sting of over-reliance on single partners for key industrial inputs, Seoul’s objective at APEC is clear: protect room to maneuver, diversify supply lines, and prevent South Korea’s advanced manufacturing sectors from becoming collateral damage in major-power disputes.

  • November 16–17: Manila, Philippines — Korea-ASEAN, Korea-Mekong, ASEAN+3, and East Asia Summit.
  • November 18–19: China — APEC Summit, focusing on multilateral negotiations with the US, China, and Russia.
  • Core Priorities: Supply chain stability for semiconductors and critical minerals, practical economic cooperation, and securing international backing for Korean Peninsula peace.

A "Shouting Match From Hell": The Trump-Netanyahu Rupture

While Asian capitals prepare for summitry, an acute crisis has erupted in Washington’s Middle East policy. Reports from international outlets, including the Financial Times, reveal an increasingly desperate campaign by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. According to these accounts, Netanyahu has been placing near-daily telephone calls to President Donald Trump, pressing the US administration to launch renewed military strikes against Iran.

Netanyahu’s insistence is widely viewed by analysts as the maneuver of an embattled leader cornered at home. Straining under intense domestic opposition, shaky coalition dynamics, and looming corruption trials that could lead to imprisonment if he is removed from power, the Israeli premier appears intent on extending military operations to ensure his own political survival. By pushing for direct conflict with Tehran, Netanyahu hopes to draw Washington into an open military confrontation—a gambit that has drawn severe international condemnation.

That gamble, however, has run into fierce resistance from Trump himself. Reporting from the Israeli daily Yedioth Ahronoth revealed that a recent call between the two leaders degenerated into an extraordinary confrontation described as a "shouting match from hell." Trump reportedly vented intense fury over Netanyahu’s persistent demands for military action against Iran. Trump attributes much of his past political vulnerability to heightened tensions with Iran and Middle Eastern security crises, viewing Netanyahu’s attempts to drag the US into regional war not as an allied request, but as an act of political betrayal. This rupture exposes deep fissures within the traditional US-Israel partnership, showing a White House focused on transactional self-interest clashing head-on with a foreign ally fighting for political self-preservation.

Impeachment Calculations in Washington: The Vance Contingency

Beyond foreign policy rows, Trump faces quiet yet serious domestic vulnerabilities within his own legislative branch. Under the US Constitution, a presidential conviction requires a two-thirds majority in the Senate—67 votes. With Senate Democrats currently holding 56 seats, the threshold for conviction sits just 11 votes away. While Trump maintained ironclad discipline over congressional Republicans during his first term, the underlying political arithmetic is shifting.

Political pollster Rich Baris of Big Data Poll has pointed out an uncomfortable reality for the White House: a notable contingent of elected Republicans privately believes their electoral prospects would improve dramatically if Vice President JD Vance were serving as president instead. Trump’s abrasive style and unpredictable governance have accumulated bitter personal resentments inside his own party over the years. According to Baris’s analysis, figures exist within the Republican establishment who harbor deeper animosity toward Trump than many Democrats do.

Should Trump’s public approval slide into the low 30% range, the instinct for political survival could easily override partisan loyalty. For lawmakers staring down tough re-election bids, switching allegiance to Vance presents a tempting, lower-volatility alternative. The requirement of just 11 Republican defectors transforms what once seemed an impossible prospect into a viable backroom calculation, creating an undercurrent of institutional fragility that foreign capitals must factor into their long-term planning.

Spain’s Housing Explosion: A Cautionary Tale for Korea

While high-level politics dominates the headlines, the pressures shaping world politics are fundamentally rooted in household survival. In Spain, an economic crisis has boiled over into the streets, sparking the country’s first nationwide general strike in 14 years. An estimated 4 million workers out of a national workforce of 23 million walked off the job, filling central squares across Madrid and Barcelona in protest over an unlivable housing market.

The numbers behind the Spanish crisis are staggering. The nationwide average monthly rent has climbed to €1,176 (approximately $1,280, or 1.74 million Korean won). Critically, that figure represents an astonishing 98.7% of the average monthly wage earned by young Spanish workers—leaving young people with virtually nothing to live on after paying for basic shelter. Compounding the issue is an acute physical shortage of homes. Bank of Spain estimates indicate a current deficit of 750,000 housing units, a shortfall projected to surpass one million by 2028.

Public outrage reached a boiling point following a tragedy in Madrid this past September, when an 87-year-old woman was forcibly evicted from the apartment she had lived in for decades after falling behind on rent; she died just two weeks later. The death galvanized the public, transforming quiet despair into mass mobilization. While Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s left-wing coalition government attempted to implement emergency restrictions on evictions, the legislation was blocked in parliament by the center-right People's Party and far-right factions, who rejected the measures as an infringement on private property rights. Sánchez has considered calling snap elections, but with public trust across the political spectrum severely eroded, electoral prospects remain grim.

For South Korea, Spain’s turmoil is an uncomfortably familiar warning. With Seoul and the surrounding capital region grappling with steep housing costs, real estate fraud, and severe economic pressures on young adults, the Spanish unrest demonstrates that housing cannot be treated solely as a market asset. When basic shelter becomes unattainable, the breakdown of the social contract quickly threatens broader political and economic stability.

What to Watch Next

As these cross-cutting crises develop, several concrete developments will reveal how effectively regional and global players can weather the storm:

  • APEC Trilateral Meetings: Watch for whether President Lee Jae-myung secures formal bilateral sessions with Chinese and American counterparts on the sidelines of the APEC Summit, specifically regarding exemptions or safeguards for South Korean semiconductor exports.
  • Middle East Containment: Monitor diplomatic readouts from Washington and Tel Aviv to see if Trump’s pushback forces Netanyahu to dial down public demands for military action against Iran, or if cross-border strikes escalate independently.
  • US Senate Alignment: Track Trump’s domestic approval ratings as legislative deadlines approach. If his polling sinks toward the low 30s, watch for public policy departures from senior Senate Republicans signaling support for Vice President Vance.
  • European Housing Reforms: Observe whether the Spanish parliament introduces revised compromise legislation on eviction controls and tenant protections before year-end, or if Prime Minister Sánchez formally dissolves parliament for an early vote.
