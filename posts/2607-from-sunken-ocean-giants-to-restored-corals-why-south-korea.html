From Sunken Ocean Giants to Restored Corals: Why South Korea and the Global Diving World Are Facing a Safety and Ecological Reckoning

November 22, 2026

Imagine slipping beneath the ocean’s surface into cold, slate-blue water, watching the hulking outline of a silent ocean liner take shape on the seabed. Whether it is the newly scuttled SS United States settling into its final resting place as an artificial reef off Florida’s Panhandle, or the eerie, 120-meter-deep wreck of the Britannic in the Aegean Sea, the allure of underwater exploration has never been stronger. Local charter boats are reporting unprecedented booking surges, and social media feeds are filled with serene footage of divers gliding past sunken steel structures and rare marine life. But beneath this aesthetic ideal lies a harsher reality: rapid commercialization, mounting diver safety hazards, and vulnerable marine ecosystems pushed to their limits.

For South Korea—a country whose modern economy was built on heavy coastal manufacturing, shipping, and rapid urbanization—leisure diving and marine conservation have moved from niche hobbies to major national policy topics. South Koreans have quickly become one of the most lucrative target markets for Southeast Asian dive tourism. At the same time, South Korea’s domestic coastline faces severe environmental pressure from sea surface temperature spikes and marine desertification. The intersection of global dive trends, regional tourism marketing, and South Korea’s domestic ocean initiatives reveals how leisure, safety regulations, and environmental policy are increasingly intertwined.

Regulatory Overhauls and the Deepening Risks of Wreck Diving

The scuttling of the SS United States off Florida represents a major milestone for artificial reef engineering and recreational wreck diving. By transforming a historic ocean liner into a sanctuary for sea turtles and fish, local authorities created an instant bucket-list destination. However, the commercial excitement highlights an ongoing dilemma in the diving community. Penetrating large sunken vessels requires advanced technical training, specialized gear, and strict gas management. When untrained divers rush into complex steel structures without technical certifications, visual disorientation from kicked-up silt and entanglement hazards can turn a thrill ride into a tragedy.

This tension between tourism appeal and diver mortality is driving a global shift toward government regulation. In the Mediterranean, the island nation of Cyprus has begun drafting strict legislation to control recreational diving following seven fatalities at the famous Zenobia wreck site. For years, lax enforcement permitted underqualified guides and unprepared tourists to venture into hazardous overhead environments. Cyprus’s legislative intervention marks a turning point: voluntary industry guidelines are being replaced by legal accountability.

A similar regulatory wave is taking shape in Southeast Asia. The Malaysian government announced that starting in April 2027, it will strictly enforce mandatory state licensing for all underwater dive guides. This move targets a long-standing industry loophole where unlicensed or minimally trained guides led groups into deep or high-current sites to cut operational costs. For international travelers—including the growing volume of South Korean tourists—these regulations provide an essential safety standard, even if they raise operating costs for local dive centers.

This regulatory shift coincides with a major leadership change at the world’s largest diver training agency. Drew Richardson, the long-serving head of the Professional Association of Diving Instructors (PADI), has stepped down. PADI appointed Chad Kuehn as its new CEO and Kristin Valette Wirth as President. As PADI prepares for its 60th anniversary—marked by an ambassador summit on Australia’s Great Barrier Reef—the incoming leadership faces mounting pressure from within the diving community. Critics have long argued that global certifying bodies prioritize card issuance over rigorous safety standards. The new executive team will be judged on whether it can restore educational rigor and enforce stricter safety and environmental standards worldwide.

Southeast Asia’s Race for Korean Divers and Eco-Tourism Realities

South Korea has emerged as one of the fastest-growing source markets for marine sports in the Asia-Pacific region. Driven by lifestyle shifts among younger generations, non-traditional aquatic sports like freediving (diving on a single breath) and "mermaid diving" have surged in popularity across Korean urban centers. Recognizing this trend, the Philippine Department of Tourism (DOT) has launched an aggressive campaign to capture Korean market share, actively participating in events like the PADI Freediving & Mermaid Festival 2026.

The commercial efforts extend directly to regional infrastructure and direct transport. A delegation from Misamis Oriental in the northern Philippines recently visited South Korea to negotiate direct commercial flights and charter routes, aiming to connect Korean travelers directly to lesser-known coastal sites. Meanwhile, a recent promotional tour featuring 21 diving experts made headlines after divers encountered six rare thresher sharks simultaneously off the island of Malapascua.

Yet this rapid expansion of dive tourism brings clear ecological risks. Thresher sharks utilize specific shallow cleaning stations off Malapascua, relying on cleaner wrasse fish to remove parasites. When excessive crowds of divers, heavy boat traffic, and bright underwater strobes flood these fragile habitats, the sharks often abandon the sites entirely. While expanded flight routes and festival marketing boost local economies, sustainable growth requires local authorities to enforce strict limits on diver numbers and establish low-impact underwater protocols.

Restoring the Seabed: South Korea’s Coastal Initiatives and Global Coral Funds

While South Korean divers travel abroad, South Korea's domestic marine ecosystems face critical challenges at home. Rising sea temperatures and coastal pollution have accelerated "sea desertification" (often referred to locally as baekhwa or ocean whitening), a process where lush kelp and seaweed forests die off, leaving barren, rock-like sea floors dominated by encrusting coralline algae. In response, seven key South Korean state and academic bodies—including the Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries—held a joint symposium in Ulsan to pool research data regarding the underwater ecosystem around Dokdo, a group of islets in the East Sea that serves as an important ecological indicator for temperate marine shifts.

To combat marine desertification, South Korea is relying on public-private partnerships. The Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries partnered with Hanwha Ocean, one of South Korea’s leading shipbuilders and defense contractors, to construct a 1.59-square-kilometer (about 390-acre) artificial ocean forest off Geoje Island. Hanwha Ocean’s involvement reflects a growing trend among South Korea’s industrial conglomerates (chaebol) to incorporate marine environmental, social, and governance (ESG) projects directly into their regional marine engineering operations.

On the regional level, local governments and civic groups are taking direct action:

  • Pohang's Homi Peninsula: Municipal authorities announced a 100.7 billion KRW (about $73 million) eco-investment initiative dedicated to restoring coastal eelgrass beds—vital blue carbon sinks—and building sea turtle protection zones.
  • Jeju Island Civic Cleanups: Non-governmental dive teams, including groups like Deepda Jeju, continue routine marine debris collection, recovering tons of discarded ghost fishing gear from nearshore reefs.
  • Inland Waters: In Jecheon, North Chungcheong Province, specialized volunteer divers performed underwater culling operations in Uirimji Reservoir to eradicate invasive largemouth bass that threaten indigenous freshwater fish species.

These domestic initiatives mirror broader international conservation efforts highlighted at the COP31 climate conference in Azerbaijan. There, the Global Fund for Coral Reefs (GFCR) and the British government pledged over $30 million in target funding to safeguard coral reef systems across 22 developing nations. On the ground, actionable projects are already moving forward: in Vietnam, marine scientists and dive teams off Con Dao Island launched a manual transplantation effort, descending 12 meters underwater to plant 12 endangered species of hard coral. However, conservationists emphasize that localized transplantation efforts will only succeed if global climate policies slow the rate of ocean warming.

Safety Warnings, Consumer Tech, and Youth Records

As water sports participation expands, consumer safety agencies are issuing sharp warnings regarding cheap, unvetted diving and snorkeling equipment. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and international marine safety publications issued emergency consumer advisories against integrated full-face snorkel masks—specifically targeting uncertified brands like Qing Song sold heavily through online platforms like Amazon and TikTok. These full-face masks can trap exhaled carbon dioxide inside the breathing chamber. Snorkelers rebreathing this trapped gas risk sudden hypercapnia, unconsciousness, and shallow-water drowning—a silent danger that highlights the vital need for certified equipment over low-cost online trends.

Simultaneously, underwater technology is becoming more accessible. South Korean underwater tech firm DIVEROID announced the December Kickstarter launch of its "WatchDive" system, a technology designed to convert standard consumer smartwatches into fully functional, decompression-calculating dive computers. While reducing the cost barrier for novice divers, industry experts caution that digital convenience should never replace a baseline understanding of decompression theory, nitrogen narcosis, and manual emergency procedures.

Finally, the competitive boundary of leisure diving was pushed further in India's Andaman Islands, where 10-year-old Ira Aditya Raut became the world’s youngest qualified scuba diver at the age of 10 years, 17 hours, and 35 minutes, setting six regional and global records in a single day. While her achievement was widely celebrated, medical specialists emphasize that youth diving mandates extreme caution. Children have lower thermal tolerances, smaller lung volumes, and developing physiology that makes them particularly sensitive to barotrauma and rapid pressure shifts, reinforcing the need for strict depth limits and physical safeguards.
