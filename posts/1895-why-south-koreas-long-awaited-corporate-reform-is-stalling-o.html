Why South Korea's Long-Awaited Corporate Reform Is Stalling on the Rocks

November 03, 2026

For millions of retail investors in South Korea, the stock market has long felt less like an investment vehicle and more like a rigged casino. Day after day, ordinary people pump their savings into domestic equities, only to watch corporate governance failures, controlling family maneuvers, and political foot-dragging wipe out their gains. While global markets celebrate artificial intelligence booms and economic milestones, South Korean equities remain haunted by the "Korea Discount"—a persistent undervaluation born of corporate structures that treat minority shareholders as an afterthought.

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, hopes had run high that structural normalization was finally within reach. Yet the benchmark Kospi index has stagnated in a frustrating 5,000-to-6,000 range, a far cry from the optimistic heights once whispered by market boosters. The frustration has boiled over into popular culture, spawning the grim joke that "escaping the Korean stock market is a sign of high intelligence." As retail investors flee to foreign markets like the United States, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the conservative opposition People Power Party are locked in a bitter struggle over the one thing that could actually save the market: sweeping commercial law reform.

The Illusion of Growth and the Reality of Structural Rot

To understand why South Korean investors are so cynical, one must look past the headline numbers. Not long ago, bullish forecasters pointed to explosive growth in semiconductors and artificial intelligence as proof that South Korea's economic fundamentals could push the Kospi toward 9,000 points. Instead, the market retreated into a painful, grinding correction. When the market surges, politicians from all sides rush to claim credit, branding the gains as proof of their economic competence. But when a downturn hits, the same officials retreat into silence, quietly blaming retail investors for lacking risk-management skills.

This blame-shifting exposes a deep-seated institutional failure. The problem with the South Korean market is not that domestic companies fail to turn a profit; it is that those profits are rarely distributed fairly. Corporate earnings are routinely siphoned off or trapped within convoluted holding structures that function more like private piggy banks for controlling families than transparent business entities. Without legal protections that force companies to respect ordinary shareholders, chasing arbitrary index targets is a fool's errand. Until transparency replaces political chest-beating, the market will remain permanently discounted.

How the Mandatory Tender Offer Was Drained of Its Teeth

One of the most egregious symptoms of this unequal system occurs during mergers and acquisitions. In a typical South Korean buyout, controlling shareholders pocket massive "management premium" payouts while minority shareholders are left holding the bag as share prices plunge. To fix this, reformers pushed for a mandatory tender offer rule—a regulation that would force corporate buyers to acquire shares from all minority holders at a fair, equal price when taking over a company.

The original vision was uncompromising: a 100% purchase request right that guaranteed everyday investors the exact same terms as the controlling elite. But as the legislation wound its way through the National Assembly, heavy pushback from the conservative People Power Party and industry lobbyists gutted the proposal. The resulting compromise was watered down to a meager 50% plus one share. By allowing buyers to bypass half of the minority shareholders, the revised rule effectively legalizes the very discrimination it was meant to eliminate. No foreign institutional investor will commit long-term capital to a market where minority owners are treated as disposable extras.

The Great Treasury Stock Shell Game

Another battlefield in this war over market fairness centers on treasury shares. In a healthy capitalist system, when a company buys back its own stock and incinerates it, the reduced share count boosts the value for remaining investors. In South Korea, however, companies hoard treasury shares under the guise of "defending management rights" or securing friendly voting blocs, turning what should be a shareholder-return mechanism into a weapon against hostile takeovers.

A recent analysis by the Corporate Governance Service and civic groups revealed a staggering level of corporate evasion: out of hundreds of listed companies scrutinized during the annual shareholders' meeting season, a massive share rushed to amend their articles of incorporation specifically to preserve treasury shares for management purposes. Prominent blue-chip asset-heavy firms, including industrial giants like Taekwang Industrial, have faced heavy criticism for deploying legal loopholes to dodge mandatory cancellation. When companies are allowed to sit on vast pools of treasury stock without retiring them, it acts as a permanent depressant on share value—a legal form of expropriation disguised as sound corporate strategy.

Commercial Law Revision as a Matter of National Survival

At the epicenter of the current legislative battle is an amendment to South Korea's Commercial Act that would expand directors' fiduciary duties. Under the current legal framework, corporate directors are bound to serve the "company" abstractly, leaving them legally unaccountable even when their decisions inflict heavy losses on shareholders. Updating the law to explicitly mandate that directors owe a duty of loyalty to shareholders—not just the corporate entity—would fundamentally alter how boardrooms operate.

This is not merely about padding retail portfolios; it is an urgent matter of national economic survival. When capital markets function fairly, household wealth grows, fueling domestic consumption and creating a virtuous cycle of sustainable investment. If the Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party fail to push these reforms past legislative roadblocks, the Korea Discount will become a permanent fixture of the nation's economy.

  • Mandatory Tender Offers: Watch whether lawmakers attempt to restore the original 100% purchase requirement or allow the watered-down 50%+1 rule to stand.
  • Treasury Stock Loopholes: Monitor ongoing shareholder activism targeting companies that amend their articles to protect unretired buybacks.
  • Commercial Act Progress: Track the National Assembly's Judiciary Committee for signs of whether the fiduciary duty amendments will survive industry lobbying.

South Korea’s capital market sits at a historic crossroads. The era of treating the stock market as a playground for a privileged few is running up against a wall of mounting public fury from millions of retail investors. Whether the political establishment has the courage to dismantle these entrenched privileges will determine not just the future of the Kospi index, but the credibility of South Korean capitalism itself.
