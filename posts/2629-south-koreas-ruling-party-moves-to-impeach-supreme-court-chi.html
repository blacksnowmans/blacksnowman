South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice in Unprecedented Clash

November 23, 2026

On November 22, the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly in Seoul became the epicenter of an extraordinary constitutional confrontation. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) officially launched a dedicated task force with a singular, aggressive goal: passing an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae of the Supreme Court of Korea before the end of December. The move marks the first time in South Korean constitutional history that a sitting head of the judiciary faces a formal removal attempt by the legislature, thrusting the country’s political landscape into an immediate crisis.

For foreign observers, the confrontation represents more than just routine partisan skirmishing; it is a high-stakes test of the balance of power between the legislative branch and the judiciary. The DPK’s push reflects deep-seated frustration with what the party describes as systemic trial delays and judicial bias. Meanwhile, opposition forces accuse the ruling party of mounting an open assault on judicial independence to shield the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. As both sides dig in, South Korea faces a December legislative session defined by bitter polarization, impending parliamentary paralysis, and historic legal uncertainty.

The December Countdown and the Mechanics of Impeachment

The DPK made its intentions clear during a press conference convened by its newly minted "Supreme Court Chief Justice Impeachment Promotion Task Force." Party leaders announced an unyielding timetable, confirming their strategy to force a floor vote on the impeachment motion in the National Assembly’s plenary session before the calendar turns to January. By transforming individual lawmakers' calls for impeachment into an official party initiative, the DPK leadership has elevated its pressure on the judiciary to the highest possible level.

Under South Korean law, impeaching the head of the judiciary requires the approval of a simple majority of all registered lawmakers in the National Assembly. Because the ruling DPK holds an absolute legislative majority, the party possesses the structural power to vote through an impeachment bill on its own strength. With the December deadline established, the parliamentary Legislation and Judiciary Committee is expected to expedite its internal investigation and draft the formal petition at breakneck speed.

Beyond parliamentary logistics, the timing carries intense political symbolism. Prominent figures within and alongside the DPK have explicitly linked the impeachment drive to the upcoming second anniversary of the controversial martial law declaration. By aligning the vote with this symbolic milestone, hardliners aim to frame the judiciary as overly lenient toward anti-constitutional forces.

Voices pushing for swift action have grown noticeably louder. DPK Lawmaker Lee Seong-yoon insisted publicly that Chief Justice Jo must be impeached without delay, arguing that no official—regardless of rank—should be immune from accountability. Externally, Kim Min-woong, head of the civic group Candlelight Action, echoed those sentiments during public demonstrations, accusing Chief Justice Jo of turning the law into an "emergency exit for insurrectionist forces." However, this rapid pace has sparked internal concerns that rushing the legal arguments could compromise the petition's quality when it eventually reaches the Constitutional Court of Korea.

Refining the Legal Strategy: Procedure Over Outcome

Recognizing the legal risks involved, the DPK task force executed a tactical pivot regarding the specific grounds for impeachment. Initially, much of the party's anger stemmed from a contentious Supreme Court remand decision involving former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. However, to blunt criticisms from conservative opponents and legal experts who accused the ruling party of attempting to overturn judicial verdicts through political force, the task force announced that it would explicitly exclude the substantive outcome of the Han Duck-soo ruling from the impeachment petition.

Instead, the DPK is focusing its legal spotlight strictly on alleged procedural defects and administrative missteps within the judicial system. The revised petition will center on four main pillars:

  • Irregularities in case assignments: Questioning whether high-profile cases were routed inappropriately within the court system.
  • Deliberate trial delays: Alleging that court leadership intentionally stalled proceedings in politically sensitive cases.
  • Abuse of administrative authority: Claiming that Chief Justice Jo overstepped his constitutional mandate in overseeing court administration.
  • Violations of procedural fairness: Arguing that systemic procedural flaws breached the constitutional guarantee of due process.

DPK Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok defended this strategic adjustment, emphasizing that separating the verdict’s content from the process is vital for building a watertight legal case. By targeting procedural misconduct rather than judicial opinions, the ruling party hopes to bypass claims that it is encroaching on judicial autonomy. Yet, establishing that procedural flaws rise to the level of grave constitutional violations remains a notoriously difficult hurdle under South Korean constitutional law.

Internal Friction and Firestorm from the Opposition

Despite the leadership’s public resolve, subtle fractures have emerged within the ruling party. DPK Lawmaker Kwak Sang-eon broke ranks with the aggressive push, publicly suggesting that the impeachment vote should not be enforced as a mandatory party line. Kwak argued that such a momentous decision ought to be left to the individual conscience of each lawmaker.

Moderate factions within the DPK harbor genuine anxiety about the political fallout. If the impeachment bill passes the legislature only to be rejected later by the Constitutional Court, the political backlash could severely damage the ruling party ahead of the nationwide local elections scheduled for 2026. Major media editorials have similarly urged restraint, cautioning that a failed legal challenge would hand a major political victory to the opposition while leaving the ruling party looking heavy-handed.

Predictably, the opposition has responded with fierce resistance. On November 22, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, mobilized its base in the conservative stronghold of Daegu. Holding a massive outdoor rally titled the "Rally to Denounce Manipulated Prosecution Special Counsel and the Impeachment of Jo Hee-dae," PPP leaders declared an all-out war against what they called an autocratic legislative tyranny.

"The real reason President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party are obsessed with impeaching Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae is to engineer a prison break for Lee Jae-myung. This is the real insurrection and a palace coup against our justice system."
— Rep. Jang Dong-hyeok, People Power Party

Other conservative figures launched scathing attacks of their own. PPP Lawmaker Jeong Jeom-sig dismissed the DPK’s legal petition as a "100% fake impeachment," labeling the ruling party as reckless political actors driven purely by partisan motives. Former PPP leader Han Dong-hoon directed harsh criticisms directly at the DPK leadership, while third-party leaders like Reform Party chief Lee Jun-seok slammed the move on social media, writing, "RIP Democratic Party, be ashamed of yourselves." Opposition leaders contend that suspending the Chief Justice is a deliberate ploy to freeze the court system and intimidate judges nationwide.

Historical Precedents and What to Watch in December

An attempt to remove the head of the judicial branch places South Korea in uncharted constitutional waters. While the National Assembly voted to impeach senior judge Lim Seong-geun in 2021, that motion was ultimately dismissed by the Constitutional Court because Judge Lim’s term had already expired. Never in the history of the republic has a sitting Supreme Court Chief Justice been impeached by Parliament.

If the DPK succeeds in passing the motion in December, Chief Justice Jo will be immediately suspended from all official duties until the Constitutional Court issues a final verdict. Such a suspension would create an unprecedented power vacuum at the top of South Korea’s judicial hierarchy, throwing administrative oversight and pending Supreme Court deliberations into disarray.

Compounding the political tension is the legislative calendar. December is traditionally reserved for passing the government’s annual budget bill. With the DPK insisting on putting the impeachment motion to a vote alongside contentious special counsel bills, a complete shutdown of parliamentary compromise appears inevitable. The opposition has already hinted at boycotting legislative proceedings and extending its outdoor protest campaigns.

As South Korea enters a volatile year-end political stretch, foreign observers and market analysts should keep a close eye on three critical developments:

  • The final language of the impeachment petition: Whether DPK legal experts can present concrete, verifiable evidence of procedural violations strong enough to withstand Constitutional Court scrutiny.
  • The DPK’s internal voting strategy: Whether party leadership officially adopts a binding party line or allows individual lawmakers a vote of conscience following Rep. Kwak Sang-eon’s public pushback.
  • Collateral damage on the national budget: How the standoff impacts the statutory deadline for approving the government’s budget, which could trigger broader fiscal and governance uncertainty.
