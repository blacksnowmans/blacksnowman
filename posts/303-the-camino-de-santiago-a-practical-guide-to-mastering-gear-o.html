The Camino de Santiago: A Practical Guide to Mastering Gear on the Trail

September 07, 2026

I still remember the peculiar tension I felt standing inside a Decathlon store in Madrid just days before a 40-day journey along the Camino de Santiago, the legendary network of pilgrimage routes across Spain. Preparing for the Camino isn’t just about packing a bag; it is about designing a survival strategy that will support your body for over a month. While many talk about the excitement of the journey, the reality on the trail is defined by cold, hard facts. Statistics show that the probability of injury skyrockets by 90% once your backpack exceeds 7 kilograms (about 15 pounds). I have witnessed countless pilgrims forced to abandon their journey early due to knee and ankle pain caused by overpacking. Ultimately, the brand of your gear matters far less than how precisely it fits your body.

The Backpack: Your Vital Survival Tool

On the Camino, your backpack is not just a bag; it is a weight that should be supported by your hips, not your shoulders. If your pack’s center of gravity is off, your trapezius muscles will scream, and your spine will inevitably suffer. A proper hip belt should firmly wrap around your pelvic bone, transferring the weight to your legs—this is the secret to maintaining a sustainable walking rhythm.

Back length is another critical factor often overlooked. If you are shorter and wear a size Large pack, the waist strap will sit against your stomach rather than your hips, causing the bag to sway and wasting your precious energy. Before purchasing, put on the pack and check yourself in a mirror. Does the waist strap perfectly cover your pelvis? When you tighten the shoulder straps, does the back panel stay flush against your body? This is where the local Decathlon—a major French sporting goods retailer ubiquitous in Europe—is a lifesaver. Their online-order-and-pickup system allows you to test the fit in-store, and if it doesn’t work, you can cancel the transaction immediately. It is the best way to ensure you don’t start your journey with gear that doesn't fit.

I suggest aiming for a 40-liter capacity. Anything less is insufficient for a long trip, but anything over 50 liters invites the temptation to pack unnecessary items. The unwritten rule among seasoned pilgrims is simple: keep your total pack weight under 10% of your body weight. That is the first law of the experts.

Pro-Tip: Balance over Capacity
Don't be fooled by "large-capacity" marketing. Once you cross that 7kg threshold, the Camino becomes a struggle rather than a journey. Eight out of ten pilgrims report injuries within the first three days because of poor weight distribution. Focus on finding the point where your physical fatigue is near zero, rather than worrying about the logo on your bag.

The Art of Layering for Temperature Shifts

The Camino is a land of extremes. You will bake under the sun by midday, but the mornings and evenings often carry a biting chill. A lightweight down vest—often available for around €15 ($16) at Decathlon—is a game-changer. These vests pack down to the size of a fist and are essential for those 5:00 AM starts in the dark. Instead of bringing one heavy jacket, use a "layering" strategy; it is significantly more effective at maintaining core temperature.

For rain, avoid expensive, heavy Gore-Tex jackets. A simple poncho costing around €10 ($11) that can cover both you and your backpack is far more practical. When the rain stops, you can simply bundle it up and strap it to the side of your bag. Also, avoid cotton at all costs; if it gets sweaty or wet, it stays wet and heavy. Invest in moisture-wicking synthetic shirts, which are easily found for under €10.

When it comes to pants, consider "convertible" trousers with zippers at the knees. They provide sun protection for your legs during the day to prevent burns, and the ability to unzip the lower portion is incredibly useful for ventilation or laundry.

Protecting Your Foundation: Feet and Essentials

Your shoes are your destiny on the Camino. It is not an exaggeration to suggest buying hiking shoes two sizes larger than your regular footwear, as your feet will inevitably swell after hours of walking. Pair these with thick wool socks and use petroleum jelly (Vaseline) between your toes to reduce friction and prevent blisters. Your shoes should never be brand new when you start; break them in thoroughly before you depart.

Finally, do not underestimate the small items. A wide-brimmed hat with a chin strap is vital, as Spanish winds can be surprisingly fierce. Additionally, wear lightweight hiking gloves. If you are using walking poles for 7 to 8 hours a day, your hands will swell and your skin will abrade. Gloves reduce the constant friction of the grips and significantly decrease hand fatigue.

What to Watch Next

If you are planning your own pilgrimage, stop worrying about "perfect" gear and start focusing on the "necessary" gear. The most important thing to watch is your own body's signals: pay attention to your feet, maintain your weight distribution, and don't be afraid to leave things behind as you go.

Things to watch for on your journey:

  • Local Retail Strategy: Instead of buying an expensive, full-set kit in your home country, consider the "local procurement" method. It saves you baggage fees and allows you to test gear in the actual environment you will be walking in.
  • Social Integration: While gear is the tool, the community is the fuel. Look for local activities like Padel—a popular Spanish racquet sport—if you want to take a break from the trail and connect with locals.
  • The Weight Paradox: Keep a close eye on your pack weight throughout the first week. If you find yourself struggling, discard what you don't need at your next stop. The lighter your pack, the more you will be able to experience the trail itself.

Ultimately, the Camino is not a test of your equipment; it is a test of your attitude. A high-end backpack won't make the pain disappear, but it can provide just enough comfort to help you endure it. Travel light, listen to your body, and enjoy the path.
