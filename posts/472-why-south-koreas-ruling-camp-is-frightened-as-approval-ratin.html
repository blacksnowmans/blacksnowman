Why South Korea's Ruling Camp Is Frightened as Approval Ratings Slide

September 08, 2026

Late at night, listening to politicians talk through a YouTube screen can leave a heavy feeling in the chest. Watching the current political landscape unfold in South Korea brings little more than sighs. Hearing Hong Ik-pyo, the senior presidential secretary for political affairs, on a popular political commentary program hosted by Kim Ou-jun, the thought immediately arose: it was not just one person feeling this annoyed. When approval ratings continue to erode, it usually means the presidential office feels an extreme sense of urgency. Yet the answers offered on that broadcast were nothing short of devastating. When the host kept digging and the answers fell flat, Hong stepped into the spotlight himself—only to pour fuel on the growing anger of the ruling camp's supporters.

Does the presidential office truly have a solution to heal this deep political fracture and alienation of public sentiment? Unable to answer properly, Hong introduced bizarre online slang terms and dismissed the crisis with a tired, "Everyone is at fault" style of false equivalence. It felt like watching a school bully mercilessly beat a victim, only for an indifferent bystander to walk up, pat everyone on the shoulder, and say, "Hey, both of you are equally wrong, so let's just make up." This irresponsible blurring of the lines between perpetrator and victim leaves one wondering who this attitude is really meant to serve.

The Fallback on False Equivalence and Disconnected Spin

A Desperate Appearance Driven by Plunging Approval Ratings

Hong Ik-pyo's appearance on the broadcast is itself proof of the profound crisis facing South Korea's administration. It reflects an underlying panic inside the presidential office as poll numbers threaten to slip into the 20% range. Even when sharp questions cut into the vulnerabilities of the administration's governance, Hong did not bring fundamental reflection or policy shifts. Instead, his remarks laid bare just how panicked the presidential office has become, confirming fears that approval ratings are actively bleeding.

When political conflict escalates, the role of presidential aides is not to blind the president, but to deliver unvarnished truths about public sentiment. Yet what was on display was an alarming level of political tone-deafness. Rather than soothing voters trapped in deep emotional polarization, attempting to blame both sides equally abandons the basic mechanics of representative democracy. Modern political research shows that voter choices are heavily driven by hostility and disappointment toward opposing forces; the presidential office appears utterly incapable of reading these massive waves of public sentiment.

Ultimately, this attitude only accelerates the defection of core supporters. While pretending to communicate, defense mechanisms that deflect responsibility are the only things actually operating. At a time when serious soul-searching is required to understand why support is collapsing, scrounging up strange excuses and sticking to evasion generates frustration that crosses over into anger. Left unchecked, the administration will enter massive political showdowns—such as upcoming local by-elections—completely defenseless.

The Violence of Erasing Victims and Perpetrators

The false equivalence Hong displayed on air was not mere political rhetoric; it bordered on political violence. How is this any different from claiming both parties are equally at fault when a bully delivers an unprovoked beating and the victim lets out a tiny squeak of pain? The centers of gravity between those holding power and those raising legitimate criticisms are entirely different, yet packaging this as mechanical neutrality is cowardly. Arbitrations declaring that "both sides are wrong so let's make up" do not resolve conflict; they simply legitimize unjust force.

Such equivalence ultimately muddies political accountability. It grants absolution to the wrongdoers while acting as a silent pressure campaign forcing critics to shut their mouths. Although the role of the presidential secretary for political affairs is to relay public sentiment without filter, deploying this kind of obfuscation to avoid upsetting the president is a dereliction of duty. Even the show's host found it difficult to stomach, posing sharp questions about the context of online attacks, yet watching the official dodge the core issue was both pathetic and disheartening.

Politics loses its trust the moment it turns away from truth and opts for expedient compromises. False equivalence invariably sides with the aggressor. The moment the voices of the vulnerable are erased and all political responsibility is locked inside a mutual mudslinging frame, democracy's self-correcting mechanism grinds to a halt. Amid all the words spoken, what emerged was not a will for reform, but a bureaucratic complacency desperate to dodge accountability.

Obsession with Phantom Memes and Online Noise

Summoning Obscure Slang for Political Convenience

The bizarre string of syllables brought up by Hong on the broadcast was sheer absurdity. Tracing the origin of this internet slang reveals it to be little more than a fleeting, trivial combination of syllables cooked up by a few users in niche online community boards as a mirror attack against rival factions. In reality, these compound terms were never used by the general public and evaporated almost immediately.

The chilling part is that a senior presidential secretary appeared on an official broadcast with a grave expression to dissect the discarded debris of an online forum that nobody else was sharing. This implies something profound: the presidential office is continuously and obsessively monitoring online communities, and even these insignificant, toxic forum posts are being reported in real-time to the highest echelons of power. It suggests key staff members are wringing their hands over anonymous internet chatter instead of managing the livelihoods of ordinary citizens.

It is the political equivalent of grabbing a few critical fragments from the vast ocean of public opinion and waving them like a badge of honor to claim, "You use hate speech too." Exaggerating a forgotten online term as if it were a grand conspiracy to justify false equivalence is a cheap spin tactic designed to dodge a regime's crisis. The gaze of power belongs on the sites of people's everyday struggles, not in the dark, anonymous corners of internet forums.

The Bitter Scars Left by Mirroring Hatred

It is true that the progressive camp largely abhors the politics of hatred and mockery. While some experimented with self-deprecating memes as a form of mirroring to counter low blows from opponents, those terms failed to survive because the camp ultimately detests that style. As the sentiment goes, ordinary supporters do not want a mud fight. They reject that culture, which is precisely why those phrases failed to catch on.

Despite this, core figures dug up expired memes to lecture that "both sides fuel hatred." This is no different from slapping a victim in the face and demanding to know if they always used polite language. Despair sets in when witnessing the low level of awareness within the presidential office, which drags up even the self-corrected traces of toxicity to use as a shield for absolution. When politics attempts to lecture citizens instead of embracing their wounds, a drop in approval ratings is a natural consequence.

The reliance of political elites on the sludge of online spaces exposes the current state of South Korean democracy. When embarrassing anonymous forum mockery replaces policy and vision, productive debate disappears and emotional exhaustion is maximized. What the presidential office truly needs to reflect on is not imaginary acronyms created by supporters, but its own past behavior defined by arrogance and the rejection of public voices.

📊 Power's Distorted View of Public Opinion

When those in power begin obsessing over anonymous forum comments and internet memes instead of people's livelihoods, that administration's vitality has reached its limit.

The Rise of Toxic Factions and Mockery Culture

Targeting Former Presidents Using Old Playbooks

Among the elements polluting the current political scene are factions styling themselves as a "New Lee Jae-myung" group. As correctly pointed out, many of these individuals are extensions of the exact same people who once stood on the front lines demonizing President Lee Jae-myung. What is astonishing is their playbook: they have turned their sights onto former President Moon Jae-in and his circle using the exact same formulas of hatred and demonization once deployed against Lee.

These groups are heavily equipped with a culture of mockery. Lacking the ability to persuade opponents through logic and vision, they can only establish an identity by trampling others with derisive language. Innumerable pieces of evidence have surfaced showing them posing as loyalists in closed chat rooms while actively instigating internal division. These variants mutate their logos subtly and revive endlessly; they are closer to cancer cells eating away at the progressive camp from within than they are to partners in reform.

Traditional supporters of the Democratic Party hold an intense aversion to this culture of mockery and hatred. The democracy they pursued was never one of trampling others to climb upward. Yet these factions cannot execute a single line of logic without derision. As they loiter around the party's mainstream and attempt to exercise influence, the Democratic Party's moral standing and public reach inevitably collapse. If the administration condones or politically exploits these toxic symptoms, the price will be the joint destruction of the entire party.

The Cowardice of Hiding Hatred Behind "Mirroring"

It is lamentable that the term "mirroring" is abused to justify political barbarism. The low-grade derision spewed by these factions is by no means healthy mirroring. It is not defensive resistance against the vulnerable, but the byproduct of a vicious struggle for hegemony by figures looking to secure privileges around the corridors of power. They act like hyenas sniffing out political scraps through the isolation and demonization of their targets.

The process by which these factions seize party hegemony paradoxically demonstrates how fragile the ruling camp's political foundation is. When crowds armed with raw hostility and mockery displace forces armed with solid philosophy and policy vision, the regime's future is predictable. As the insight notes, the grammar of destruction always pivots without sparing its targets.

Ultimately, true voices for reform suffocate inside this ocean of hatred. Even though the presidential office and party leadership should recognize these entities and draw a firm line, their passive or quietly approving attitude is pathetic. An administration that tolerates hatred will eventually burn in its flames. As long as mockery culture erodes the party's identity, finding a shred of hope becomes nearly impossible.

Silent Aides and Foreign Trips as Avoidance Tactics

The Silence on Military Deployments

Hong Ik-pyo's attitude toward sensitive national security issues, such as foreign troop deployments, was evasive. While playing down any concrete decisions, he casually let slip that information seemed to be leaking from security circles. Witnessing such casual treatment of a grave issue involving the nation's destiny and the lives of young soldiers reveals total laxity within the presidential office. Yet the scarier part is that this casual demeanor is not a slip of the tongue, but proof that internal decisions have already been made.

It is rational to assume that President Lee Jae-myung has already decided on deployment. The evidence lies in how specific leaked details have become. One must suspect that a certain degree of communication and practical alignment has already occurred with the United States. If the administration wished to use domestic opposition as leverage to boost negotiating power—as former President Roh Moo-hyun once did—fierce debates and deliberate leaks would have erupted domestically before details were sealed.

Instead, the most decisive and scientific proof comes from the reactions of pro-administration channels. If the president desired opposing public sentiment, key pro-regime figures would be shouting objections from broadcast studios. Yet neither they nor any New Lee-affiliated channels are raising voices against deployment. The absence of any opposition proves that the president's will is deployment itself.

The Cowardly Silence of the Inner Circle

Because the president's intent leans toward deployment, even those who once vocally championed peace have clamped their mouths shut. This cowardly silence from aides and prominent speakers mirrors the autocratic, top-down governance of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Beneath the surface, philosophy and justification have vanished, leaving behind only calculated political maneuvering.

Using the cold realities of international politics as an excuse to abandon principles while employing media management and enforced silence to minimize supporter backlash mirrors the playbooks of past conservative regimes. Supporters watch with heartbreak as the values of peace built by the progressive movement are so easily cast aside. When those who ought to voice dissent form a cartel of silence out of fear of power, democracy slowly suffocates.

Ultimately, the president's word becomes law, and aides are reduced to sunflowers turning toward power without offering a single critique. This stark reality leaves supporters letting go of their final threads of hope. Grounded in cold political calculation, this truth serves as a painful reminder that there is nowhere left to lean within this administration.

Fleeing Controversy and the Paralysis of Critical Dissent

Foreign Tours as an Escape from Accountability

When pressed on sensitive political issues, Hong offered evasive non-answers. Inquiries about schedules upon returning from European diplomatic trips were batted away with excuses about an intense autumn diplomatic calendar involving the United Nations and Mexico. This dynamic evokes memories of past administrations dropping massive reform bombs—such as sudden structural revisions to prosecution agencies—right before heading overseas, leaving subordinates to pick up the pieces.

Dropping policies likely to trigger fierce backlash and fleeing abroad while subordinates handle the fallout reveals a specific leadership style. It is hard to shake the rational suspicion that overseas trips are intentionally scheduled to let controversies cool down. Watching an administration that once promised open communication retreat into an impregnable fortress of non-communication generates profound disillusionment.

The presidential office reportedly implemented quiet bans on appearances on specific critical commentary programs during party conventions. When investigative journalists expose controversial political deals, the inner circle plunges into chaos. Because the current leadership has little tolerance for criticism, dissent is immediately crushed, freezing the internal atmosphere. The reality that aides only dared to send Hong on air during a foreign trip, after approval ratings crashed into the 20% range, speaks volumes.
