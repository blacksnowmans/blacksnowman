Is the U.S. Debt Cycle Approaching a Breaking Point?

September 05, 2026

Spend any time in South Korean investment communities or scanning the financial news lately, and you will inevitably stumble upon a recurring, ominous phrase: the “long-term debt cycle.” This isn’t the typical, low-stakes chatter about whether the central bank will hike or cut interest rates by a quarter-point next month. It carries a heavier, more existential tone, like the anxiety of coastal residents sensing a massive wave approaching—unable to tell if it is a harmless high tide or the precursor to a tsunami.

When Ray Dalio, the founder of the world’s largest hedge fund, Bridgewater Associates, warns that “the U.S. has three years left,” it isn’t merely the hyperbole of a billionaire looking for headlines. It is a reaction to a growing pile of data suggesting that the engine of the current global financial system is redlining. For those of us watching from the outside, the question is whether the American economic engine is simply due for a tune-up or if it is nearing a catastrophic mechanical failure.

The Warning Signs Written in the Budget

The most infuriating aspect of the current U.S. economic situation is that a staggering portion of the government's revenue is being siphoned off just to pay the interest on its own ballooning debt. As recently as 2000, the U.S. government spent about 11% of its tax revenue on net interest payments. Today, that figure has surged to 19%. For every $100 the government collects, nearly $20 is handed straight to creditors before a single dollar is spent on defense, infrastructure, or social programs.

This isn't just a dry statistic; it is a flashing red light. If the yield on U.S. Treasury bonds—the debt issued by the federal government—continues to climb, the federal budget will be entirely consumed by interest payments. According to reports from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), the non-partisan agency that provides budget and economic information to Congress, the annual fiscal deficit is nearing $2 trillion. The U.S. is inching toward a state of “fiscal dominance,” where the government is so shackled by debt that it loses the ability to manage the economy effectively.

📊 By the Numbers
Over the past year, the U.S. government’s net interest payments reached $882 billion, or 3.1% of GDP. This is the highest level in 28 years. As debt traps the government in a cycle where it must borrow more to pay interest on existing loans, the Federal Reserve (the U.S. central bank) finds its hands tied, unable to raise interest rates without risking a total fiscal collapse.

The Fed’s Impossible Balancing Act

Market analysts are increasingly convinced that the Federal Reserve is losing its grip on long-term interest rates. As the U.S. Treasury floods the market with new bonds to cover its deficits, the pool of willing buyers is shrinking. When supply outstrips demand, bond prices fall and yields spike. If the Fed steps in to buy these bonds—effectively acting as the buyer of last resort—it risks triggering “debasement,” a decline in the value of the currency caused by an oversupply of money.

Think back to the stagflation of the 1970s—a period where inflation ran wild while economic growth stalled. During that era, financiers and legends like Warren Buffett had to fundamentally rethink their survival strategies. Today, we are seeing signs of a similar malaise. Governments often resort to desperate measures in these times, such as capital controls or capping interest rates by decree. We may be witnessing the beginning of the Fed resorting to “Yield Curve Control” or similar emergency measures to keep the system from seizing up.

🔥 The Hidden Cost
While policymakers frame these interventions as “solutions,” the reality is that they often rely on debasing the currency, which erodes the wealth of the average citizen. Whether through forced bond purchases or artificially suppressed interest rates, the government is essentially trading the future purchasing power of the next generation to pay for today’s debts.

The Shift from Paper to Hard Assets

In this environment, the classic investment advice—"keep a balanced portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds"—is beginning to look like a relic of a bygone era. When the currency itself is being debased and debt is the source of the rot, bonds are no longer the "safe haven" they once were. They are increasingly viewed as a liability.

This is why we see a rush toward assets like gold and Bitcoin. Regardless of where one stands on the speculative nature of these assets, the underlying trend is undeniable: there is an exploding demand for “non-sovereign assets.” As long as the government maintains the power to print money at will, people will continue to seek out assets with guaranteed scarcity as a lifeboat.

What to Watch Next

The era of fueling growth through cheap, abundant debt is clearly drawing to a close. If Dalio’s “three-year” timeline is to be taken as a warning rather than a prediction, the focus for investors and observers should shift away from chasing short-term gains and toward defensive positioning.

Moving forward, keep a close watch on these three indicators:

  • The Term Premium: Watch whether investors demand significantly higher interest rates to hold long-term U.S. debt. If this spikes, it signals a loss of confidence in the U.S. government’s fiscal trajectory.
  • Central Bank Rhetoric: Listen for any language from the Federal Reserve that hints at “emergency measures” or “coordination” with the Treasury, as this is the precursor to the monetisation of debt.
  • Real Asset Performance: Monitor how gold and other hard assets perform during periods of stock market volatility. If they begin to decouple and rise while traditional markets fall, it confirms the market is losing faith in the fiat system.

We are standing at a unique inflection point in the modern economic cycle. Whether this ends in a controlled adjustment or a messy restructuring remains to be seen. In the meantime, the most prudent course of action is to stop asking how to maximize returns in a broken system and start asking how to ensure your own financial foundation is built on something more permanent than government debt.
