The End of the Korea Discount? Inside SK Hynix’s Record Quarter and $72 Billion Shareholder Gamble

October 29, 2026

For months, the trading desks along Yeouido—Seoul’s financial district—had been caught in a suffocating freeze. Despite the ongoing global frenzy surrounding artificial intelligence, a dark cloud of skepticism had settled over South Korea’s memory chipmakers. Foreign investment banks, led by Morgan Stanley, issued dire warnings that the AI semiconductor boom had hit a wall. Local brokerages began trimming their target prices, feeding the persistent fear of a "peak-out"—a rapid downward turn in the notorious boom-and-bust memory cycle. Portfolios were trimmed, retail investors stood paralyzed on the sidelines, and trading volumes dried up.

Then came SK Hynix’s earnings report. In a single release, South Korea’s leading high-bandwidth memory (HBM) manufacturer shattered the consensus, posting quarterly revenue of 104 trillion won (around $75 billion) and an operating profit of 83 trillion won (roughly $60 billion). Accompanied by an unprecedented 100 trillion won ($72 billion) capital return framework and confirmation that its high-end capacity is spoken for through 2029, the announcement landed like a boulder dropped into a stagnant pond. For international observers tracking whether Korea can dismantle its historic market discount, SK Hynix is suddenly offering an entirely new blueprint.

Monopoly Profits in the Silicon Foundries

The headline numbers announced by SK Hynix were not merely an earnings beat; they marked an uncharted frontier for the global semiconductor hardware industry. Market consensus had already penciled in robust performance, anticipating between 96 and 97 trillion won in revenue and roughly 76 trillion won in operating profit. Instead, the chipmaker exceeded those forecasts by 7 to 8 percent.

Following Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix became only the second company in South Korean corporate history to cross the 100 trillion won quarterly revenue threshold. But the truly startling detail lies in the profitability: an operating margin hovering near 80 percent. In a traditional hardware manufacturing business subject to heavy depreciation and immense capital expenditure, an 80 percent operating margin is almost unheard of. It reflects an effective monopoly over the highest-margin nodes in the artificial intelligence supply chain: high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and enterprise solid-state drives (eSSDs).

These margins translate into staggering liquidity. By the end of the third quarter, SK Hynix’s cash reserves climbed to 114.5 trillion won (about $83 billion), leaving the company with a net cash position of 98.7 trillion won ($71 billion) after subtracting debt. In the past, Korean chipmakers were viewed with suspicion even during quarterly blowouts because market participants assumed the profits were cyclical windfalls destined to vanish into inventory writedowns during the next downturn. This time, however, the cash hoard has created an airtight fortress for future research and development while unlocking a shareholder payout program without precedent in Seoul.

Ditching Chaebol Habits: The $72 Billion Shareholder Return

Beyond the operational figures, the structural shift in corporate governance is what caught seasoned Korean market watchers off guard. For decades, foreign investors have slapped a valuation penalty—the so-called "Korea Discount"—on Seoul-listed shares. Corporate leadership at major chaebol (family-run conglomerates) routinely treated outside shareholders not as partners, but as nuisances at annual general meetings, hoarding cash to fund convoluted succession plans or obscure family-controlled subsidiaries.

SK Hynix’s response to its cash windfall is an explicit break from that tradition. The company announced a 100 trillion won ($72 billion) total shareholder return policy through fiscal year 2026. This comprehensive program combines:

  • More than 60 trillion won (around $43 billion) earmarked for share buybacks and immediate cancellations, building upon the 40 trillion won cancellation carried out earlier in August.
  • More than 30 trillion won (around $22 billion) dedicated to cash dividends.
  • A freshly approved 20 trillion won share purchase, of which 10 trillion won will be immediately cancelled and retired from circulation, with the remaining half allocated to employee performance incentives.

The dividend metrics reflect this aggressive stance. For the third quarter, the company set a dividend of 20,000 won (about $14.50) per share. This includes a baseline operational payout of 10,000 won alongside an additional 10,000 won special dividend. The special component stems from SK Hynix cashing in on its strategic investment in Japanese chipmaker Kioxia, yielding roughly 20 trillion won in pre-tax gains—most of which management chose to pass directly to shareholders.

"It has been our established principle that when strategic investments bear fruit, the majority of those gains must be shared with our shareholders," noted SK Hynix CEO Kwak Noh-jung during the earnings call.

With roughly 700 million shares outstanding, this single quarterly dividend disbursement totals approximately 14 trillion won ($10 billion), representing a 1.56 percent quarterly yield (annualized at over 6 percent). By adopting an American-style playbook—similar to Apple's sustained reduction of its float to permanently elevate earnings per share (EPS)—SK Hynix is establishing a modern capital allocation standard that puts immense pressure on peer firms, including Samsung, to follow suit.

Crushing the "Peak-out" Narrative with Long-Term Contracts

The most acute anxiety hanging over the semiconductor market was the argument that AI infrastructure investment by global tech giants had hit its ceiling. Brokerage downgrades had multiplied; some local shops slashed SK Hynix price targets down to 1.85 million won, while others maintained bullish targets as high as 4.3 million won. The bearish camp argued that once hyperscalers finished their initial server installations, memory inventory would pile up, driving chipmakers back into the red.

SK Hynix dismantled this cyclical thesis with hard contract data. Management revealed that the number of global hyperscalers signed to binding Long-Term Agreements (LTAs) for five years or more had jumped from 10 to 14. This roster encompasses the world's 14 largest enterprise buyers of computational hardware.

More than 50 percent of the capacity from fabrication facilities scheduled for expansion through 2028 is already locked down under these long-term commitments. When combining HBM and next-generation DRAM requirements, the company's entire production capacity is effectively sold out through 2029.

This dynamic challenges the very definition of a semiconductor "cycle." Historically, memory markets spun through rapid two-to-three-year swings between extreme shortages and glut. But when supply is contractually committed and guaranteed across a five-year horizon, the structural nature of the business fundamentally alters. As SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won remarked at a recent Nasdaq event, dismissing the idea of technological exhaustion: "AI is still only five years old." Projections from KB Securities suggest global AI spending will surge from $390 billion in 2025 to $1.1 trillion by 2027, with memory's share of that hardware bill jumping from 14 percent to 50 percent.

What to Watch Next

As the initial dust from this record-setting earnings release settles, the focus shifts to whether this momentum can permanently reshape the market landscape in Seoul. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Buyback Execution Pace: Investors will be watching whether the board promptly retires the newly announced 10 trillion won slice of repurchased shares, and how smoothly the remaining 10 trillion won in incentive shares is absorbed without diluting public float.
  • The Remainder of the Kioxia Windfall: SK Hynix explicitly stated that the residual balance from its Kioxia monetization will be reflected in the year-end closing dividend, making the upcoming fourth-quarter payout another vital indicator of management’s capital-allocation discipline.
  • Samsung’s Strategic Response: With SK Hynix maintaining a tight grip on HBM yields and locking up hyperscaler contracts through 2029, all eyes turn to whether South Korea’s tech flagship, Samsung Electronics, can match both this technical execution and the sheer scale of SK Hynix's shareholder distribution roadmap.
