The Price of a Swing: Golf, 'Giregi,' and the Erosion of Trust in Lee Jae-myung’s Korea

November 05, 2026

The morning mist still clings to the fairways of the Yangpyeong TPC Golf Club, an hour’s drive east of Seoul. On a typical weekday, you might expect to see retired executives or business owners enjoying a round. But between July and October, the guest list featured a different demographic: sports and business reporters from 22 of South Korea’s most prominent media outlets. They weren't there to cover a tournament. They were there to play for free, courtesy of the Ramid Group, a mid-sized conglomerate with interests in hotels and leisure.

In South Korea, this practice is so common it has its own vocabulary of shame. Yet, a recent exposé by investigative journalist Jang In-su has sent shockwaves through the industry. It isn't just about a few free rounds of golf; it is a window into a systemic rot where the "watchdogs" of society have become the pampered pets of capital. In a country currently led by President Lee Jae-myung and a dominant Democratic Party (DPK), where "fairness" and "labor rights" are the rhetorical pillars of the administration, the revelation of this "golf cartel" highlights a jarring disconnect between the elite's lifestyle and the public’s expectations.

The Anatomy of a Junket: 28 Reporters and the 'Experience' Excuse

According to logs and internal documents, the scale of the Ramid Group’s hospitality was breathtaking. Over a period of just ten weeks, the group hosted five large-scale golf outings. A total of 28 journalists were identified as participants. Each "gift" of a round, including green fees and dining, was valued at approximately 325,000 KRW (about $235) per person. In the context of South Korean law, specifically the Improper Solicitation and Graft Act (often called the Kim Young-ran Act), this is a minefield. The law strictly limits the value of gifts and hospitality journalists can receive to prevent exactly this kind of quid pro quo.

The defense offered by the Ramid Group was as creative as it was cynical. They claimed these weren't "bribes" or "junkets," but rather "course experience opportunities" provided to specialized reporters so they could better understand the leisure industry. If that were true, one might expect nuanced reporting on the facility's pros and cons. Instead, the "experience" resulted in a deluge of 24 glowing PR articles—18 of which were written by the very journalists who had just stepped off the free green. These articles were essentially advertisements disguised as news, praising the "premium service" and "world-class facilities" of the Ramid Group’s properties.

When Jang In-su began contacting the involved reporters for comment, the reaction was telling. There were no public apologies or resignations. Instead, many of the journalists reportedly called the Ramid Group’s PR team to complain—not about the ethical breach, but about the "leak." One reporter allegedly asked, "How did you manage the information so poorly that a fellow reporter is asking me about this?" This "entitlement of the elite" suggests that for many in the Seoul press corps, free golf isn't a transgression; it’s a perk of the office they feel they are owed.

Chonji and the Ghost of 'Make Your Own Salary'

To understand why a $235 golf game matters so much, one must understand the history of the Korean press. In the 1980s and 90s, it was an open secret that newsroom managers would tell new recruits, "The company provides the press pass; you earn your own salary." This gave birth to the chonji culture—small envelopes of cash (literally "a token of my heart") handed out by government officials or companies to reporters on their "beat."

While the envelopes of cash have largely disappeared due to stricter laws and the professionalization of newsrooms, the DNA of the practice remains. It has morphed into "golf and galbi" (expensive beef dinners). This is facilitated by the chul-ip-cheo system, where reporters are assigned to specific "beats" like the Blue House (the presidential office), the National Assembly, or specific conglomerates like Samsung or Hyundai. Reporters spend their entire day in the press rooms of these organizations, often becoming closer to the people they cover than to the public they serve.

This proximity creates a "cartel" of information. Senior reporters, known as il-jin (a term also used for school bullies), often act as the gatekeepers. It has been revealed that some senior reporters' primary job isn't writing stories, but "managing the relationship" with the company, which includes booking tee times for their own editors or company executives. When a young, idealistic reporter enters this system, they are often told that participating in these outings is "part of the job" or "essential for networking." By the time they reach mid-career, the shame has been replaced by a sense of belonging to the inner circle of power.

The 'Giregi' vs. the YouTuber: A Double Standard

In recent years, the Korean public has coined the term "Giregi"—a portmanteau of gija (reporter) and sseuregi (trash). The term reflects a deep-seated resentment toward legacy media outlets that are seen as biased, sensationalist, or corrupt. This resentment peaked during the "backdoor advertising" scandal that hit the Korean YouTube community a few years ago. Influencers were shamed and de-platformed for failing to disclose sponsored content.

The irony is that legacy media reporters are now performing a version of "backdoor advertising" that is far more damaging to the social fabric. When a YouTuber promotes a skincare cream without a "paid promotion" tag, it’s a consumer rights issue. When a national newspaper reporter writes a glowing profile of a conglomerate in exchange for a weekend at a luxury resort, it’s a corruption of the democratic process. These reporters are trading the credibility of their institutions for personal leisure, yet they continue to look down on "new media" as unprofessional.

"We are witnessing a collapse of the system, not just individual deviance," writes one critic. "When 28 reporters from 22 different companies are caught in the same net, it means the industry's moral compass has stopped spinning entirely."

The silence from the major media outlets involved—including some of the country’s biggest daily newspapers—is deafening. In an age where trust in traditional institutions is plummeting, the refusal to conduct internal audits or issue public corrections only reinforces the "Giregi" narrative. It suggests that the media considers itself above the laws and ethics it applies so ruthlessly to others.

Political Complacency in the Era of President Lee Jae-myung

This media rot is occurring against a backdrop of significant political tension. The ruling Democratic Party (DPK), led by President Lee Jae-myung, has often campaigned on "cleansing" the old guard and fighting for the "little guy." However, the party’s own recent actions suggest they are suffering from the same arrogance as the media they occasionally criticize.

A recent controversy involving DPK heavyweight Kim Min-seok illustrates the point. During the party’s internal elections, Kim made unsubstantiated claims about a mass infiltration of the party by Shincheonji (a controversial religious group often labeled a cult). When the claims proved baseless, Kim’s "apology" was conducted behind closed doors in a private meeting of lawmakers, rather than publicly to the voters he had misled. Critics argue that the DPK, now comfortably in power, has become a "party of secrets," more interested in protecting its own than in transparency.

Perhaps more concerning is the government's recent legislative push. The administration is quietly advancing the "Mega-Special Zone Act" (or the Super-Regional Special Zone Act). While framed as a way to boost regional economies and the semiconductor industry, the fine print contains significant labor deregulation. Specifically, it seeks to expand "flexible work hours" to up to six months. In practice, this means a company could legally force employees to work grueling hours for half a year without overtime pay, as long as they "balance" it with time off later.

For a president who campaigned on a 4.5-day work week, this is a startling reversal. It echoes the policies of the "Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi era"—the former president who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment. By adopting the very labor-squeezing tactics they once condemned, President Lee’s government risks alienating its core base of workers and young people. When the media—busy playing golf with the very companies that benefit from these laws—fails to scrutinize these policy shifts, the democratic feedback loop is broken.

What to Watch Next

The Ramid Group scandal and the government's policy contradictions are not separate events; they are symptoms of an elite class that has stopped listening. As we look forward, several key indicators will determine if South Korea can break this cycle of "collusive elite" behavior:

  • The 'Jang In-su Effect': Will other whistleblowers come forward? Jang In-su’s investigation was based on internal logs. If more employees at golf courses and luxury hotels begin leaking guest lists, the "golf cartel" may finally be forced into the light.
  • The Anti-Graft Act Enforcement: Will the National Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission (ACRC) actually penalize the 28 reporters? Under the Kim Young-ran Act, "work-related" golf is almost always a violation. A lack of prosecution will signal that the law is effectively dead for the media.
  • Labor Resistance: Watch for the reaction of the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU). If the Lee Jae-myung administration continues to push the Mega-Special Zone Act, we may see a significant rupture between the Democratic Party and organized labor.
  • The 'New Media' Shift: As legacy media loses credibility, more Koreans are turning to independent investigative outlets. Whether these outlets can maintain professional standards without falling into the same "beat" traps as their predecessors remains the big question for the 2020s.

South Korea’s rapid development was built on a "can-do" spirit, but its current stability relies on a "fair-play" spirit. If the people tasked with reporting the truth are too busy counting their strokes on a free golf course to notice the erosion of labor rights or political transparency, the "fairness" President Lee Jae-myung promised will remain nothing more than a campaign slogan.
