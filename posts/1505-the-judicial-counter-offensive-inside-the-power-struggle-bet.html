The Judicial Counter-Offensive: Inside the Power Struggle Between the Lee Jae-myung Administration and the Courts

October 26, 2026

A recent four-hour dinner at the presidential residence was not merely a social gathering for the ruling elite. When President Lee Jae-myung sat down for an extended discussion with Representative Jung Chung-rae—a heavyweight in the Democratic Party of Korea and the influential chair of the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee—the message to the nation’s legal establishment was clear. This was a war council. As the Lee administration moves to dismantle what it describes as a "judicial cartel" left over from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the South Korean judiciary has begun to fight back, using the only weapons it has: verdicts and procedural delays. What is currently unfolding in Seoul is less a series of legal disagreements and more a "judicial coup" aimed at checking the power of a reformist government.

To understand the current tension, one must look back at the immediate aftermath of the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The Lee Jae-myung administration entered office with a mandate to overhaul the prosecution and the courts, which many supporters viewed as having become overly politicized. At the center of this storm is Jung Chung-rae, whose aggressive handling of the first round of judicial reforms has earned him both fervent praise from the party base and deep-seated resentment from the bench. However, recent rulings—most notably involving Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon—and strategic delays in high-profile cases suggest that the judiciary is no longer willing to be a passive subject of reform.

The Architect of the 'Lightning' Reform

The first phase of the Lee administration’s judicial reform was characterized by a speed that caught the legal establishment off guard. Jung Chung-rae, leading the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, pushed through a series of bills designed to dilute the concentrated power of the Supreme Court and the upper echelons of the judiciary. This included measures to increase the number of Supreme Court justices, a move intended to break the ideological monopoly of the existing bench and ensure a more diverse range of legal interpretations.

The resistance within the courts was, and remains, immense. Judges and administrative officials within the judiciary viewed these moves as an infringement on their independence. They argued that the legislature was attempting to "tame" the courts through sheer political force. Yet, Jung Chung-rae remained undeterred. He reportedly approached the task with a level of resolve usually reserved for existential political battles, successfully navigating the committee process without dropping a single key reform provision. For the Democratic Party, this was a historic victory; for the judiciary, it was a declaration of war.

This "lightning-fast" approach was born out of a specific political philosophy: that the judiciary, when left to its own devices, will always protect its own vested interests. By moving quickly, the Lee administration sought to deny the "judicial cartel" the time necessary to organize a coherent political counter-strategy. However, as the focus shifts from legislation to the courtroom, the limits of this strategy are becoming apparent. The courts may not be able to stop laws from being passed, but they can certainly control how those laws—and the politicians who pass them—faring in practice.

The Oh Se-hoon Verdict and the Signal of Resistance

The most explosive evidence of this judicial pushback came in the recent appellate ruling for Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul and a prominent figure in the People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition. Mayor Oh was facing charges under the Public Official Election Act. In the South Korean political system, the stakes for such trials are incredibly high: if a generic public official is sentenced to a fine of 1 million won (roughly $720) or more, they are immediately stripped of their office and barred from running in elections for several years.

The court’s decision to grant what many reformists call "immunity" to Mayor Oh has sent shockwaves through the political landscape. By ensuring the penalty remained below the critical 1,000,000 won threshold, the court effectively allowed a key opposition leader to remain in power. Critics of the judiciary argue that this was not a neutral legal judgment but a calculated political move—a signal to the Lee administration that the courts still hold the power of life and death over political careers.

This verdict is being interpreted by the ruling party as a "judicial coup." The argument is that while the President and the National Assembly derive their power from the people, the judiciary is using its unelected authority to shield the "old guard" and sabotage the current government's momentum. The contrast is stark: while reformist figures and those associated with the Lee administration often face the full weight of the law, conservative figures are seen as receiving a "rubber band" standard of justice that stretches to accommodate their survival. This perceived double standard is fueling the fire for a second, even more aggressive round of judicial reform.

Article 84 and the Strategy of Strategic Delay

Beyond individual verdicts, the judiciary is also being accused of using procedural "tricks" to stall the government’s agenda. A central point of contention is Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution, which states that the President shall not be charged with a criminal offense during their tenure, except for insurrection or treason. While this is intended to ensure the stability of the executive branch, it has become a focal point for a complex legal battle regarding ongoing trials that began before President Lee took office.

In a notable move, the Seoul High Court’s Criminal Division 7, presided over by Judge Lee Jae-kwon, recently declared a "later designation" for a trial involving the Public Official Election Act. Effectively, the court has put the trial on indefinite hold, citing the need to interpret the constitutional implications of Article 84. To the administration's supporters, this is a transparent attempt to keep a legal "sword of Damocles" hanging over the President's head, while simultaneously waiting for a more favorable political climate to emerge.

The timing of these delays often coincides with personnel reshuffles within the courts, further complicating the proceedings. By stalling these cases, the judiciary can observe the shifting tides of public opinion and the President's approval ratings before making a move. This "wait-and-see" approach is viewed by the Democratic Party as a form of political interference. If the courts can choose when to hold a trial, they can effectively choose when to create a political crisis. The debate over whether to live-stream such trials has also become a weapon; the threat of a live-broadcast verdict is seen by some as a way for judges to exert pressure on the administration by turning a legal proceeding into a public spectacle.

The Cho Hee-dae Era and the Hardening of Positions

The conflict has only intensified under the leadership of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The current leadership of the Supreme Court appears to be on a collision course with the Blue House. There is a growing sense within the legal elite that they are the last line of defense against what they perceive as "populist" overreach by the Lee administration. This has led to a hardening of positions on both sides.

Chief Justice Cho’s administration is seen by the ruling party as a continuation of the "politicized" judiciary that flourished during the Yoon Suk-yeol years. The memory of the "judicial power abuse" scandal—where the courts were accused of bargaining with the executive branch—remains fresh. For the Lee administration, the goal is not just to change the law, but to "democratize" the bench so that it is no longer a self-perpetuating caste of elites. For the judiciary, the goal is "independence," which the administration translates as "accountability."

The friction is visible in the smallest details of governance. When the judiciary refuses certain requests from the presidential office or delays appointments, it is seen as an act of defiance. The judiciary, meanwhile, views the President's frequent consultations with lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae and Kim Min-seok as evidence of a plan to subvert the separation of powers. This mutual distrust means that every ruling is scrutinized not for its legal merit, but for its role in the broader power struggle.

What to Watch Next

The "second round" of judicial reform is no longer a matter of if, but when and how. The Lee administration and the Democratic Party are likely to move toward more systemic changes that limit the discretionary power of judges. As this battle moves forward, there are several key indicators to watch:

  • Legislative Oversight: Look for new bills aimed at establishing more rigorous oversight of the courts, potentially including changes to how judges are disciplined or how court administrative power is distributed.
  • The 'Article 84' Ruling: The Constitutional Court’s eventual interpretation—or the High Court's decision to resume the delayed trials—will be a watershed moment. If the trials are resumed with a perceived bias, expect a massive legislative retaliation.
  • Public Sentiment and Protests: The Democratic Party’s base is highly sensitive to judicial fairness. If public perception shifts toward the idea that the courts are actively trying to "topple" the President, we may see a return to large-scale street protests similar to those that led to the impeachment of previous leaders.
  • Supreme Court Appointments: As vacancies arise, the battle over who replaces the current justices will be fierce. President Lee will seek to appoint "reform-minded" judges, while the opposition and the current judicial leadership will fight to maintain the status quo.

The current state of affairs suggests that the era of a "quiet" judiciary in South Korea is over. The courts have entered the political arena, and the Lee Jae-myung government is prepared to meet them there. Whether this leads to a more transparent and accountable legal system or a constitutional crisis that paralyzes the nation remains the defining question of the current administration.

"The judiciary must understand that its authority is not a mandate for self-preservation, but a trust granted by the people. When that trust is used to shield political allies or stall the democratic will, reform is no longer an option—it is a necessity."
