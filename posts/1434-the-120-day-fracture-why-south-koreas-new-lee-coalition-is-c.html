The 120-Day Fracture: Why South Korea’s ‘New Lee’ Coalition Is Collapsing

October 24, 2026

Just four months after securing the presidency, South Korea’s President Lee Jae-myung finds his political foundation shifting beneath his feet. Backed by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea’s commanding majority of more than 170 seats in the National Assembly, the administration initially appeared set for a prolonged honeymoon. Instead, the administration is grappling with an abrupt and structural collapse in public support. What was initially dismissed by party insiders as a standard post-election poll correction has evolved into a full-scale rupture inside the coalition that brought Lee to power.

The fracture is not evenly distributed across his base. Rather, it reveals a profound divorce between two distinct groups: the ideological loyalists who stood with Lee through his toughest trials, and a fickle cohort of pragmatists who joined his tent only after victory was assured. As real estate frustrations resurface and divisive institutional reforms dominate headlines, the latter group is heading for the exits in droves, laying bare the deep vulnerabilities of a governing party that confused short-term opportunism with lasting loyalty.

Anatomy of a Defection: Inside the Panel Data

The extent of this erosion was exposed in a comprehensive panel survey conducted by the progressive daily Hankyoreh alongside the Korean Political Science Association and polling firm STI. In May, during the fourth wave of the survey, President Lee’s positive job evaluation stood at a comfortable 66.9 percent. Just four months later, in the fifth wave, that figure had plummeted by 17.6 percentage points to 49.3 percent. Negative evaluations surged to 46.5 percent, effectively bringing the administration into a dead heat between supporters and detractors.

The demographic mechanics of this drop tell an even more alarming story. The panel specifically tracked the behavior of so-called "New Lee" voters—moderate, swing, or pragmatic voters who backed the president only after his election victory. Among this cohort, an astonishing 54.8 percent withdrew their support within four months, either switching to a negative evaluation or stating they no longer knew where they stood. By contrast, the defection rate among "Old Lee" voters—the traditional, long-standing progressive base that backed him before the election—was limited to 19.8 percent. The newcomers deserted three times faster than the core.

As a result, the share of "New Lee" voters across the entire electorate shrank from 18.5 percent in May to 9.1 percent. Other polling firms have confirmed the broader trend. In a separate Gallup Korea survey, President Lee’s approval dropped to 38 percent, with disapproval outstripping approval across all age brackets, including voters in their 40s and 50s who have historically anchored the center-left. Geographically, approval in the capital city of Seoul sank to 29 percent—trailing even conservative strongholds like Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province (34 percent). Among voters in their 30s, who had rewarded Lee's pragmatic economic messaging with a 67.3 percent approval rating in early surveys, support dropped by 21.5 percentage points to 45.8 percent.

Prosecution Reform and Property: The Ideological Chasm

Why did these newer supporters leave so swiftly? The survey responses highlight a clash of priorities. When asked to identify the primary failure of the administration, 25.3 percent of disapproving respondents cited real estate and tax policy. Economic management, livelihoods, and welfare followed at 18.0 percent, while prosecution reform ranked third at 14.1 percent. Positive assessments of real estate policy hit rock bottom among all defectors: just 7.3 percent among departed traditional supporters and a dismal 5.0 percent among departed newcomers.

The real estate blowback was reignited by debates over tax revisions targeting owners of single homes who do not reside in them, unsettling middle-class homeowners and renters in the capital region. Compounding these policy missteps were controversial ministerial nominations. Public pushback flared over the appointment of Kim Seung-won as Minister of Justice and Yong Hye-in as Minister of Gender Equality and Family. Public disapproval for Yong's nomination reached 61 percent overall, and even among self-identified progressives, 49 percent deemed her unsuitable, undermining the administration's claims to meritocratic governance.

Yet the most profound divide between old and new supporters lies in their view of institutional overhauls, particularly prosecution reform. The Democratic Party has long sought to dismantle the monopoly of power held by the state prosecution service, pushing to strip prosecutors of their supplementary investigative authority. For core progressives, this reform is an article of faith. Following years of what they viewed as targeted, politicized investigations against Lee by prosecutors under the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is now imprisoned—the core sees reforming the judicial system as a moral necessity.

The data demonstrates this ideological gulf: among defected "Old Lee" supporters, 53.8 percent still approve of the overhaul of the criminal justice system. But among departed "New Lee" voters, an overwhelming 77.1 percent view the prosecution reform package negatively. Many of these newer supporters had previously cast ballots for the conservative People Power Party in local elections. To them, prosecutorial overhauls represent not a grand quest for justice, but unnecessary political warfare that risks destabilizing everyday law enforcement and public safety.

The Opportunist Dilemma and Frans de Waal’s Chimpanzees

To unpack this internal dissonance, prominent progressive commentator and author Rhyu Si-min has pointed to his "ABC Theory" of political actors. In this framework, Group A consists of value-driven core loyalists—the inheritors of South Korea’s democratic reform traditions stretching through former presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in. These voters prioritize justice, ideological coherence, and systemic reform over personal economic windfalls, serving as the administration's human shield when crises hit.

Group B, by contrast, is purely transactional. Composed of pragmatic voters and ambitious politicians, this group views politics as a vehicle for power, career advancement, and material return. Rhyu notes that when the Democratic Party secured an overwhelming legislative majority and the Blue House, Group B swelled out of control. Political aspirants rushed to don "pro-Lee" badges, claiming unyielding loyalty to the president in order to secure nominations for upcoming elections, while aggressively silencing dissent by labeling moderate critics "subak" (watermelons—a derogatory term for politicians perceived as green on the outside but conservative red on the inside).

Group C represents the pragmatic reformers caught in the middle, attempting to balance long-term values with the compromises required to govern stably. When an administration is popular, Group B dominates the microphone, masking the fact that its loyalty lasts only as long as its interests are served.

Rhyu drew on primatologist Frans de Waal’s classic text, Chimpanzee Politics, to illustrate how this power dynamic functions. In primate hierarchies, an alpha male like "Goblin" governs exclusively through physical menace, terror, and intimidation. Such leaders extract compliance, but breed deep resentment; when a credible challenger surfaces, the group turns on the tyrant, tearing him apart. Rhyu likened this dynamic to the fate of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose heavy-handed reliance on prosecutorial apparatus to intimidate political opponents ended in impeachment and imprisonment.

In contrast stands "Amos," an alpha leader who maintains order by intervening to defend the weak in 70 to 80 percent of disputes, while dedicating the remaining time to nurturing emotional bonds with close peers. A sustainable administration must walk the path of Amos: delivering tangible public protections for ordinary citizens while honoring the ideological bonds of its core coalition. Instead, Democratic Party politics has recently been tarnished by self-serving jockeying, including controversies over mass party registrations by groups like the Shincheonji religious movement seeking to hijack candidate nominations.

Historical Echoes and the Danger of a Cooling Core

South Korean political history offers blunt warnings about fair-weather loyalists. During the early days of President Roh Moo-hyun’s administration, politicians clamored to identify as "pro-Roh." Yet when the conservative opposition forced an independent counsel investigation into illicit financial transfers to North Korea, and prosecutors later tightened the vise, many of those same figures abandoned Roh and demanded his arrest. A similar pattern played out following Moon Jae-in’s term: allies who basked in his 70 percent approval ratings swiftly distanced themselves once the political climate cooled.

The danger for President Lee is not merely that self-styled loyalists will abandon him when trouble strikes. The deeper peril is that the core Group A voters—the bedrock that endures political storms—are growing disaffected. These voters do not cross the aisle to vote for the conservative People Power Party; when pollsters call, they often still register approval to protect their camp. But their enthusiasm is waning.

When an administration dilutes its core reform promises to appease transactional factions, the core base does not revolt loudly—it simply goes cold. When core supporters stop organizing, defending, and sacrificing for a government, the administration becomes a hollow shell, incapable of withstanding institutional shocks.

This dynamic makes recent attacks on internal critics especially counterproductive. When Rhyu Si-min warned that the party was refusing to remove "the pebble in its shoe" and pointed out the collective hysteria gripping its factional echo chambers, party partisans attacked him for firing at his own side. Silencing thoughtful internal criticism to preserve the appearance of unity is a well-worn path to systemic failure.

What to Watch Next

As the Lee administration enters the next phase of its mandate, several markers will indicate whether it can steady its coalition or slide further into factional paralysis:

  • Cabinet Recalibration: Watch whether the administration presses forward with disputed nominees like Kim Seung-won and Yong Hye-in, or pivots toward broader consensus figures like Planning and Budget Minister Park Yong-jin, who was brought in to balance party factions.
  • Legislative Trajectory of Prosecution Reform: How the Democratic Party leadership handles the bill to strip prosecutors of supplementary investigative authority will test whether the government prioritizes the demands of its base or retreats to appease moderate swing voters.
  • Capital-Region Real Estate Stabilizers: With approval ratings in Seoul plunging below 30 percent, the administration must resolve its tax posture regarding non-resident homeowners before housing dissatisfaction hardens into permanent electoral hostility.
  • Upcoming Local Election Nominations: The primary battlegrounds for municipal and provincial races will reveal whether internal party machinery is captured by factional opportunists or whether institutional discipline can be restored.

The lesson of Lee Jae-myung’s first four months is straightforward: a political majority built on rapid, transactional gains dissolves the moment governance demands hard trade-offs. If the administration continues to ignore the stones inside its shoe, the road ahead will only become more painful.
