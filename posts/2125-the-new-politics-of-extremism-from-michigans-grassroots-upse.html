The New Politics of Extremism: From Michigan’s Grassroots Upset to Europe’s Far-Right Echoes

November 09, 2026

In the quiet, cobblestone alleys of Kraków, Poland—a city whose medieval architecture famously survived the ravages of World War II to earn a place on UNESCO’s World Heritage list—a sudden burst of political violence recently shattered the peace. A far-right extremist opened fire in the city’s central square, leaving six people dead and fifteen wounded, many of them foreign nationals and immigrants. When investigators searched the perpetrator’s home, they discovered an even darker horror: the bodies of three family members—his parents and sister—murdered before he set out on his campaign of hate. It was a terrifying reminder of how deeply violent extremism has mutated across the Western world.

Yet thousands of miles away, in the industrial heartland of Michigan, a fundamentally different political story unfolded. Abdul El-Sayed, a progressive candidate and practicing Muslim, achieved an unprecedented victory to become the first Muslim elected to the United States Senate. His win came against overwhelming odds, defying astronomical lobby spending and high-profile attacks from the top echelon of American conservative leadership. Together, these contrasting events illuminate a global landscape caught between an escalating wave of far-right populism and a burgeoning counter-movement built on pragmatic, local advocacy.

The Michigan Upset: Defeating Big Money with Everyday Pragmatism

To understand the magnitude of El-Sayed’s victory in Michigan, one must look at the immense financial and political machinery arrayed against him. The campaign was widely regarded by political analysts as an impossibly tilted playing field. The American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC), a powerhouse pro-Israel lobbying group, poured roughly $22 million (30 billion KRW) into supporting his primary opponent, Haley Stevens. The effort was further reinforced by direct campaign appearances from high-profile conservative leaders, including Donald Trump and JD Vance, who sought to leverage xenophobic tropes to derail El-Sayed’s candidacy.

Despite this massive financial onslaught, El-Sayed pulled off what can only be described as a political miracle. Operating on a shoestring campaign budget that represented just 9 to 10 percent of the funds deployed by his opposition, El-Sayed dismantled a formidable coalition of entrenched donor interests and far-right rhetoric. His campaign demonstrated that heavy lobby spending and identity-based fearmongering are not entirely invincible when confronted with disciplined, ground-level mobilization.

El-Sayed’s strategic breakthrough lay in his refusal to engage in ideological mudslinging. When opponents attempted to weaponize his ethnicity and Muslim faith, triggering waves of Islamophobic attacks, El-Sayed responded with a sharp, pragmatic campaign query that resonated deeply with voters weary of political division:

"Does attacking my faith and ethnicity lower your grocery bills?"

This simple, incisive line encapsulated the core frustration of average citizens. While political elites remained fixated on demonizing opponents and stoking culture war anxieties, working-class households were struggling with inflation, daily expenses, and neglected public infrastructure. By steering the conversation back to tangible economic realities, El-Sayed mobilized a coalition of young voters, working-class families, and pragmatic independents who chose real-world solutions over weaponized prejudice.

The Gamification of Hate: From Christchurch to Kraków

While Michigan demonstrated the potential of pragmatic politics, events in Europe underscored the alarming spread of far-right violence. The attack in Kraków’s central square was not an isolated act of madness; it was part of a systemic trend that experts describe as the "gamification of violence." European law enforcement officials noted that the Kraków gunman explicitly modeled his attack on the manifesto and methods of the 2019 Christchurch mosque shooter in New Zealand. In this subculture, perpetrators record and livestream their atrocities, treating mass violence almost like an online game where body counts are tracked and celebrated in extremist digital forums.

This rise in violent radicalization is closely tied to the internationalization of American-style populist tactics. Across the European continent, far-right leaders have coalesced around slogans explicitly modeled on American political movements. At a recent summit of right-wing European figures in Spain, attendees chanted "Make Europe Great Again" (MEGA), adopting both the optics and the rhetoric of Donald Trump’s political machine.

By constantly attacking immigrants, religious minorities, and democratic institutions, far-right figures have effectively granted tacit permission for extremist actors to translate hateful rhetoric into physical attacks. When public discourse legitimizes xenophobia, peaceful historic centers like Kraków—long considered safe havens from ideological terror—become target zones for racially motivated violence.

Trump’s Second-Term Cabinet: Ideological Hardliners Take the Reins

The global surge of far-right populism is slated to gain renewed momentum with the incoming appointments for Donald Trump’s second presidential administration. Among the most controversial choices is the selection of Stephen Miller as White House Deputy Chief of Staff for Policy. Miller, well known as the architect of aggressive anti-immigration measures during Trump’s first term, represents an uncompromising hardline stance on border control and executive power.

Legal experts and civil rights advocates have raised immediate alarms over Miller’s return to power. US-based legal scholar Park Dong-gyu characterized the appointment in stark terms, describing Miller as a dangerous xenophobe whose policies pose a fundamental challenge to constitutional norms, calling him a "malignant tumor on American democracy." The appointment has rattled even establishment figures within the conservative ecosystem; reports from outlets like Fox News indicate growing private anxiety among mainstream Republican lawmakers who fear that Miller’s unrestrained influence will drag the party into extreme ideological warfare.

Adding to the sense of spectacle surrounding the transition are reports surrounding the White House Press Secretary role. Media outlets including CNN and the BBC reported that the administration considered offering the position to Dana White, the Chief Executive Officer of the Ultimate Fighting Championship (UFC). While major news agencies like AP and Reuters have worked to verify the exact nature of these discussions and subsequent declines, the mere fact that a combat-sports promoter was evaluated for the primary diplomatic liaison between the presidency and the press corps speaks volumes. It reflects an administration strategy that views public communication not as a forum for reasoned policy explanation, but as a confrontational arena designed to intimidate critics and media outlets.

Global Security and the Korean Perspective: The Risks of Isolationist Diplomacy

The consequences of Washington's shift toward transactional, far-right governance extend far beyond domestic policy, threatening delicate geopolitical balances worldwide. For international observers—particularly those in East Asia—the prospect of an unpredictable, "America First" foreign policy introduces deep uncertainty into regional security frameworks.

Maintaining peace on the Korean Peninsula requires sophisticated, multilateral diplomatic engagement and long-term strategic commitments. Under previous diplomatic efforts, such as those pursued during former President Moon Jae-in’s administration, inter-Korean dialogue and regional stabilization were built on carefully negotiated agreements. However, an administration that views international alliances strictly through a commercial lens threatens to unravel these historical foundations, leaving Northeast Asian security vulnerable to sudden shifts and transactional dealmaking.

For South Korean citizens, this global slide toward political polarization carries a familiar resonance. International observers often draw parallels to South Korea's recent political turbulence under former leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, the domestic political landscape was heavily defined by the aggressive use of state prosecutorial powers, sharp partisan divisions, and an increasingly confrontational political climate.

South Korea ultimately navigated that period of democratic stress through massive civic mobilization, leading to the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and the subsequent election of President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. While South Korea restored a degree of institutional stability through constitutional processes, the experience served as a stark lesson: hyper-partisan rhetoric and systemic exclusion quickly erode civic trust, regardless of the country in which they take root.

What to Watch Next

As the second Trump administration prepares to take office and far-right movements expand across Europe, several key indicators will determine whether democratic institutions can withstand the current wave of polarization:

  • Implementation of US Executive Orders: Watch how quickly hardliners like Stephen Miller move to execute radical border and immigration policies, and whether federal courts or moderate lawmakers in the US Congress offer any meaningful opposition.
  • European Legislative Security Responses: Monitor how European nations, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe, adjust their domestic security protocols and counter-terrorism measures in the wake of the Kraków attack to address copycat threats linked to online extremist networks.
  • East Asian Diplomatic Realignments: Pay close attention to how President Lee Jae-myung’s administration in Seoul navigates foreign policy challenges, balancing security coordination with Washington against the need to preserve stable regional relations amid potential US tariff threats and isolationist maneuvers.
  • The Durability of Grassroots Counter-Campaigns: Observe whether progressive and pragmatic candidates across Western democracies adopt Abdul El-Sayed's focus on kitchen-table economics to counter far-right populist narratives in upcoming legislative elections.

The contrasting events in Michigan and Kraków demonstrate that the global struggle between divisive extremism and pragmatic governance is far from settled. While financial capital and weaponized hate remain potent tools for radical movements, the success of disciplined, policy-focused campaigns proves that voters will still respond to leaders who address their daily lives rather than their fears.
