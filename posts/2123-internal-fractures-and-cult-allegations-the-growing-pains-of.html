Internal Fractures and Cult Allegations: The Growing Pains of South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

November 09, 2026

Imagine standing at the helm of a massive political vessel that has finally reached the shores of power, only to find the hull creaking under the weight of its own passengers. This is the current reality for the Democratic Party (DP), South Korea’s ruling party under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. While the party holds the presidency and a commanding influence in the legislature, it is currently grappling with a series of internal crises that threaten to undermine its mandate for reform. From allegations of a controversial religious sect infiltrating its ranks to a brewing "civil war" between top leaders, the DP is finding that governing from the top is often more treacherous than campaigning from the bottom.

For those outside the Korean Peninsula, the stakes are high. The Democratic Party’s ability to maintain internal discipline directly impacts its capacity to pass major economic reforms and overhaul the nation’s powerful prosecutorial system. If the party remains mired in scandal and factional infighting, the legislative gridlock could stall the "Lee Jae-myung era" before it truly hits its stride. At the center of this storm is Kim Min-seok, the party’s chairman, whose leadership is being tested by a trifecta of controversies: cult-related allegations, a rivalry with a firebrand deputy, and the constant pressure from a revitalized pro-reform base.

The Ghost of Shincheonji and the "Closed-Door" Controversy

The most explosive issue currently rocking the Democratic Party involves allegations of mass membership by followers of Shincheonji, a controversial religious group often described by critics as a cult. In South Korea, Shincheonji is a "third rail" in politics; the group gained international notoriety during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic for its secretive nature and has long been accused of attempting to infiltrate political organizations to gain influence and protection.

During the DP’s recent party convention, rumors began to circulate that a large number of Shincheonji members had joined the party en masse, particularly in the Honam region (Southwest Korea), which serves as the traditional heartland of liberal support. The fear within the party is that an outside group could skew internal elections and dictate the party’s platform. The situation worsened when it was revealed that certain members of the Supreme Council—the party’s top decision-making body—had allegedly viewed the membership lists in a "closed-door session" without making the findings public.

This lack of transparency has sparked a firestorm. The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, has seized the opportunity to go on the offensive. Representative Jang Dong-hyeok and other PPP leaders are now calling for a formal investigation by law enforcement, accusing the DP of trying to hide the truth. For the DP, this is a dangerous "double bind." If they ignore the allegations, they risk appearing compromised by a cult; if they open their books to investigators, they risk letting the opposition and the state’s investigative machinery poke holes in their internal security. The perception of "closed-door" politics is particularly damaging for a party that prides itself on being the champion of "member-centric" democracy.

The "Seok-Cheong Battle" and the Struggle for the Party’s Soul

While the Shincheonji allegations are an external threat, a more personal battle is unfolding within the DP’s leadership. The Korean media has dubbed this the "Seok-Cheong Battle," a portmanteau of the names of Chairman Kim Min-seok and Supreme Council member Jung Cheong-rae.

Kim Min-seok, a veteran politician known for his strategic mind, was elected to lead the party and stabilize the government’s legislative agenda. However, he is facing a significant challenge from Jung Cheong-rae, a high-profile firebrand with a massive following among the party’s most radical activists. Jung is known for his uncompromising stance on judicial reform and his willingness to use aggressive rhetoric against political opponents. The friction between the two centers on the speed and method of the party’s reform agenda. While Kim seeks a broader, more inclusive approach to "field politics" to win over moderates, Jung and his supporters push for a "scorched earth" policy against the remnants of the previous administration’s influence.

To assert his leadership, Kim has embarked on a tour of the provinces, holding Supreme Council meetings in cities like Gumi and Gwangju. This "field politics" is an attempt to reconnect with the grassroots and demonstrate that the party is focused on regional development and the people’s livelihoods rather than just internal bickering. However, political analysts suggest that visiting regional offices is only a temporary fix. Unless Kim can reconcile with Jung—or decisively outmaneuver him—the DP risks appearing like a two-headed monster, sending mixed signals to the public and the presidential office.

Consolidating the Base: The Rise of the Innovation Meeting

Amidst the leadership struggles, a powerful faction is consolidating its influence: "The Democratic National Innovation Meeting." This group represents the core "pro-Lee Jae-myung" faction within the party, consisting of both sitting lawmakers and influential outside activists. Recently, the group launched its fourth leadership cycle, with Representative Lee Kwang-hee serving as its standing chair.

The Innovation Meeting is the engine behind the DP’s most aggressive legislative goals. Their primary focus is the passage of a "Special Prosecutor Act on Fabricated Indictments." This bill is a direct response to the legal battles President Lee Jae-myung faced during the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment. The DP and its base argue that the prosecution service, under Mr. Yoon’s tenure, weaponized the law to target political rivals through "fabricated" evidence and selective investigations.

The Innovation Meeting’s growing power signals a shift in the DP’s internal dynamics. They are moving away from traditional "smoke-filled room" politics toward a model where the rank-and-file members have a direct say in party policy. While this energizes the base, it also creates a "purity test" for party members. Those who are seen as too willing to compromise with the opposition or who hesitate on judicial reform are increasingly marginalized. This factional consolidation is what gives the DP its strength to face the opposition, but it also makes the party less flexible in a divided legislature.

President Lee’s Strategic Outreach and Ethical Speed Bumps

Outside the party's internal machinery, President Lee Jae-myung is attempting a broader political maneuver: the "Pro-Roh Integration." By appointing figures associated with the late President Roh Moo-hyun—a beloved figure in Korean liberal history—to key positions, Lee is trying to unify the various factions of the liberal establishment. This is a strategic move to secure the Busan and Gyeongnam (PK) regions, which are historically difficult for the DP to win but were the political home of President Roh. If Lee can successfully merge his "reformist" brand with the "traditional liberal" legacy of the Roh era, he will have a much more stable foundation for the remainder of his term.

However, this high-level strategy is being hampered by "moral fatigue" at the local level. Recently, the DP had to launch an internal investigation into a Hwaseong City councilor accused of a drunken tirade and verbal abuse. While this might seem like a minor local issue, it becomes a liability for a ruling party that has promised to be "morally superior" to the previous administration. Furthermore, the DP is facing pressure from its ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party (led by Cho Kuk), which recently demanded an apology from the DP over alleged opinion poll manipulation during a previous primary.

These frictions show that being the ruling party is an exercise in constant damage control. The DP must manage its relationship with smaller allied parties while simultaneously policing the behavior of its own thousands of local officials. Any slip-up is immediately magnified by the opposition and the media, feeding the narrative that the "new" government is falling into the same traps as the old ones.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will be a defining period for Chairman Kim Min-seok and the Democratic Party. To understand where the DP is headed, there are several concrete developments to monitor:

  • The Shincheonji Investigation: Watch whether the DP conducts a transparent internal audit of its membership lists or if the issue is forced into the hands of state prosecutors. A failure to clear the air could lead to a permanent stain on the party’s legitimacy.
  • Legislative Speed: The "Special Prosecutor Act on Fabricated Indictments" is the litmus test for the party’s unity. If the DP can pass this bill without significant internal dissent, it will signal that the Innovation Meeting and the pro-Lee faction have successfully seized total control of the party’s direction.
  • The "Seok-Cheong" Dynamic: Keep an eye on the public interactions between Kim Min-seok and Jung Cheong-rae. If the two can find a "working peace," the DP will be a formidable legislative force. If their rivalry breaks out into open conflict, it could paralyze the party's decision-making.
  • By-election Results: As the party focuses on "field politics" in the provinces, upcoming local by-elections will serve as a referendum on whether the DP’s internal troubles are alienating the general public.

The Democratic Party is currently a giant in transition. It has the power of the presidency and the mandate of the people, but it is discovering that its greatest enemies might not be the opposition across the aisle, but the shadows and rivalries within its own house.
