Fractured Fronts: Internal Infighting and Judicial Wars Grip South Korean Politics

November 24, 2026

The doors to the Supreme Council chamber of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation’s ruling party, usually open to announcements of new policy or criticisms of the opposition. But on November 23, the sounds emerging from the room were far less orderly. High-ranking members were shouting. Tears were shed. Senior officials walked out in protest. At the center of the storm was Kim Min-seok, the party leader marking his 100th day in office—a milestone that should have been a victory lap but instead felt like a desperate attempt to keep his leadership from unraveling.

Across the aisle, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, fared no better. While the ruling party grappled with internal shouting matches, the opposition was rocked by a whistleblower’s claim of a "premeditated purge." Allegations surfaced that the party leadership had orchestrated a secret plan to expel Han Dong-hoon, a prominent and often polarizing figure within the party, to coincide with court rulings against Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence.

This is the current state of South Korean politics: a landscape where internal divisions are so deep that party leaders are spending more time fighting their own members than their political rivals. With the ruling party’s approval ratings cratering and the opposition embroiled in conspiracy theories, the battleground has shifted to the one place that is supposed to remain neutral: the judiciary. As the DPK moves to impeach the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court and the PPP retaliates by suing individual judges, the very foundations of the country’s legal system are being pulled into the partisan fray.

The Hundred-Day Blues of the Democratic Party

When Kim Min-seok took the helm of the Democratic Party 100 days ago, he promised a "New Era" built on what he called the "ABC Plan"—an acronym for "Aspiration, Bread, and Communication." The goal was to focus on the livelihoods of ordinary citizens and expand the party’s reach beyond its traditional base. However, three months later, the "ABC Plan" has been largely overshadowed by "Internal Conflict."

The numbers tell a grim story. According to a recent poll by Kuki News, a domestic news outlet, Kim’s disapproval rating has surged to 67.4%. In some metrics, his support has plummeted by over 12 percentage points since he took office. For a leader of a ruling party serving under President Lee Jae-myung, these figures are more than just a dip in popularity; they represent a fundamental crisis of authority. When a party leader cannot command the respect of his own base, the government’s ability to pass legislation and drive national policy is severely compromised.

The tension reached a boiling point during a recent Supreme Council meeting over allegations involving Shincheonji. To the uninitiated, Shincheonji is a controversial religious organization often described as a cult, which gained international notoriety during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. In Korean politics, being "linked" to Shincheonji is a toxic accusation, often used to suggest that a politician is being influenced by shadowy, undemocratic forces.

During the meeting, Park Sun-won, a member of the Supreme Council, reportedly clashed with Kim Min-seok over how the party was handling rumors of Shincheonji members infiltrating the party ranks. The argument became so heated that Kim issued a formal verbal warning to Park. Park, visibly emotional, accused the leadership of being "excessively harsh" and claimed that the party was being distracted by "distortions" rather than focusing on unity. The sight of senior leaders walking out of a meeting in tears or anger has signaled to the public that the ruling party is currently a house divided.

A "Premeditated Purge" Rocks the Opposition

While the ruling party is fighting over religious shadows, the People Power Party is dealing with a scandal that feels more like a political thriller. The PPP is currently led by Jang Dong-hyeok, but his leadership is being challenged by a series of explosive revelations from a current member of the party’s Ethics Committee.

The whistleblower alleged that there was a coordinated plan to expel Han Dong-hoon from the party. Han, a former Justice Minister who has a significant "fandom" following among conservatives, has often been at odds with the current party leadership. According to the allegations, the timing of this expulsion was no accident. It was reportedly designed to take place exactly when the courts were set to deliver a sentencing demand for Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The implication is that the party leadership wanted to "clean house" and remove a rival while the public's attention was diverted by the legal drama of the former president.

Han Dong-hoon has not taken these allegations lying down. He characterized the move as a "manufactured expulsion" and a "planned purge," calling for Jang Dong-hyeok to take responsibility. The conflict has split the PPP into warring factions. Supporters of Han, such as former Supreme Council member Kim Jong-hyeok, have gone as far as to say that if these allegations are proven true, the current party leadership should retire from politics entirely.

"If the party's disciplinary process is being used as a weapon for political assassination rather than a tool for ethical standards, the very foundation of our party democracy has collapsed," one critic noted during a party briefing.

This internal warfare comes at a time when the PPP should be consolidating its power to challenge President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. Instead, heavyweights like Hong Joon-pyo, the Mayor of Daegu and a veteran conservative voice, have criticized the party leadership for using "bizarre legal tactics" to settle internal scores. With the opposition's disapproval rating also sitting high at 61.5%, the Korean public is witnessing a political vacuum where neither side seems capable of leading.

The Judiciary as a Political Football

Perhaps the most concerning development in this week’s news is the escalation of hostilities against the judicial branch. In a move that has sent shockwaves through the legal community, the ruling Democratic Party announced it is finalizing a motion to impeach Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court.

The DPK’s justification for this drastic measure is rooted in what they describe as the Chief Justice’s "failure to protect judicial independence" and specific "errors" in handling cases related to the current administration. However, the opposition sees it differently. Jang Dong-hyeok of the PPP labeled the move "self-destructive impeachment," arguing that the ruling party is simply trying to intimidate the courts to protect President Lee Jae-myung from ongoing legal scrutiny. This is a high-stakes gamble; if the impeachment fails in the Constitutional Court, the political backlash against the DPK could be immense.

The PPP has responded with its own legal offensive. They have announced plans to file a criminal complaint against Judge Lee Jin-kwan, who presided over a recent high-profile case involving a former Prime Minister. The charge? "Law Distortion" (법왜곡죄). This is a relatively new and highly controversial concept in the Korean political lexicon. It suggests that a judge can be prosecuted if they "intentionally" misapply the law to reach a specific verdict.

Legal experts warn that this "tit-for-tat" warfare against the courts is dangerous. When politicians begin suing judges or impeaching the Chief Justice because they dislike a ruling, the "rule of law" begins to look more like the "rule of the mob."

  • Independence: Judges may become hesitant to rule against the party in power for fear of impeachment.
  • Public Trust: If the public believes that court rulings are merely the result of political pressure, the entire legal system loses its legitimacy.
  • Precedent: Using impeachment as a routine political tool lowers the bar for future constitutional crises.

The Rebranding of the Third Way

Amidst the chaos of the two major parties, a third force is attempting to reinvent itself. The Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP), led by the former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, has announced it will change its name. Currently, the party’s Korean name is "Cho Kuk Innovation Party," directly incorporating the leader’s name.

While this helped the party gain instant recognition and surge to over 10% in recent polls, it also branded them as a "personality cult" party focused solely on one man’s legal battles and political redemption. Cho Kuk himself suggested the change, proposing names that include broader values like "Democracy" or "Reform."

The RKP’s strategy is clear: they want to capture the "fatigued" voters who are tired of the DPK’s infighting and the PPP’s internal purges. By removing the leader's name from the masthead, they hope to transition from a temporary protest group into a sustainable, value-based political party. In the current climate, where the two main parties are perceived as failing, a "cleaner" and more "value-driven" RKP could pose a significant threat to the DPK’s dominance over the liberal wing of Korean politics.

However, the RKP is not staying out of the judicial fight. Even as they seek a more "moderate" name, they have been vocal in their criticism of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, aligning themselves with the ruling party’s hardline stance. This suggests that while the name may change, the "firebrand" nature of their politics likely will not.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves into the final month of the year, several key developments will determine whether this political gridlock eases or turns into a full-blown constitutional crisis.

  • The Impeachment Vote: Watch for the formal tabling of the impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The DPK’s internal unity will be tested here; if a significant number of moderate members balk at the move, it could lead to another leadership crisis for Kim Min-seok.
  • The "Law Distortion" Precedent: If the PPP follows through with its criminal complaint against Judge Lee Jin-kwan, it will set a radical new precedent. Watch for the reaction from the Korean Bar Association and other legal bodies, which have so far expressed deep concern over the politicization of the bench.
  • PPP’s Internal Disciplinary Results: The Ethics Committee’s decision regarding Han Dong-hoon will be a bellwether for the future of the opposition. If he is expelled, expect a splintering of the conservative base and potentially the formation of a new "pro-Han" political group.
  • The RKP Rebranding: The final choice for the Rebuilding Korea Party’s new name will signal how aggressively they plan to court centrist voters. A move toward more "universal" terminology could indicate they are preparing for a long-term role as a "kingmaker" in the National Assembly.

For the average voter in Korea, the events of November 23 have been a source of deep exhaustion. With the economy facing headwinds and global uncertainty rising, the sight of the country’s top leaders engaged in shouting matches and judicial vendettas suggests a political class that has lost its way. Whether they can find their way back to "the livelihoods of the people" remains the most important, and yet most unanswered, question of the season.
