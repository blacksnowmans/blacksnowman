Defying the Bear: Why Foreign Capital Is Quietly Accumulating Samsung Electronics

October 31, 2026

As the South Korean stock market has weathered a punishing streak of volatility, a striking pattern has emerged beneath the surface of the panic. While retail investors—often referred to in Korea as "ants"—have been rattled by headlines of an AI bubble and slumping U.S. tech stocks, a different force has been quietly at work. Foreign institutional investors are stepping into the fray, consistently scooping up Samsung Electronics shares at every dip. This deliberate buying suggests that global capital sees the current market turbulence not as the beginning of a structural collapse, but as a long-term entry point.

To understand why this is happening, we must look beyond the immediate gloom. While the rhetoric around AI skepticism has cooled sentiment, the underlying data—specifically the cloud infrastructure revenues of Big Tech—tells a story of a massive, ongoing industrial transformation that is far from over.

The Reality of Big Tech CAPEX and the AI "Bubble"

Recent volatility was sparked by earnings reports from heavyweights like Alphabet (the parent company of Google) and Amazon. When these companies reported massive capital expenditures (CAPEX) for AI, markets reacted with reflexive fear. The narrative became: "Are these companies spending too much on AI with too little return?"

However, this skepticism overlooks a fundamental shift in how businesses operate. Unlike the dot-com bubble of the early 2000s, where companies with no revenue models burned through speculative debt, today’s tech giants are funding their AI infrastructure through robust, existing cash flows and solid balance sheets. When companies like Microsoft, Amazon, and Alphabet increase spending, it isn’t a gamble; it is a calculated race to dominate the cloud infrastructure that will power the next decade of computing. While traditional search advertising revenue is facing pressure, it is being offset by explosive growth in cloud and AI services. The market's "miss" on short-term earnings expectations ignores the fact that this is a capital-intensive transition phase, not a structural failure.

Samsung Electronics: The Foreign Long-Position Signal

In the domestic market, the most telling indicator of sentiment isn't the daily index movement, but the behavior of foreign investors toward Samsung Electronics—the flagship of the Korea Exchange. Whenever the stock hits a floor, foreign capital moves in to absorb the selling pressure. This is not the work of "hot money" looking for a quick day-trade; it is the construction of a long-term "long position" by institutional players who recognize that Samsung's valuation is sitting at a cyclical bottom.

This trend is mirrored by SK Hynix, another pillar of the Korean semiconductor industry. Despite market concerns, SK Hynix has effectively "sold out" its supply of next-generation DDR5 and High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) for the foreseeable future. With global demand for AI data centers showing no signs of slowing, the massive orders placed by global IT giants ensure that the semiconductor super-cycle remains intact through at least 2027 or 2028. Foreign interest in Korean semiconductor ADRs (American Depositary Receipts) trading at a premium in New York further confirms that international investors are hungry for exposure to these specific, high-quality supply chain components.

Macro Indicators and the "Strong" Economy

The "bear market" narrative currently dominating social media and retail investor forums often lacks a grounding in reality. A true secular bear market requires a mass exodus from risk assets into safe havens like gold or government bonds. We are not seeing that. Instead, capital is sitting on the sidelines in cash-equivalent instruments, waiting for a clearer signal.

Furthermore, the U.S. Federal Reserve has recently characterized the American economy as "strong." When the economy is growing and unemployment is low, a temporary correction in tech valuations does not equate to a recession. In fact, we are seeing a decoupling: while tech stocks have faced downward pressure, consumer discretionary sectors—such as retail and apparel—have shown resilience, suggesting that the broader economic foundation remains firm. Furthermore, in the derivatives market, the heavy accumulation of "short" (downward) positions has been steadily clearing, suggesting that the institutional appetite for further downside risk is waning.

What to Watch Next

For investors navigating this environment, the focus must remain on the following:

  • Institutional Flows: Continue to track foreign net buying patterns in semiconductor leaders like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. As long as this "smart money" continues to defend price floors, the thesis for a cyclical recovery holds.
  • Derivatives Clearing: Watch for the continued unwinding of short positions in the futures and options markets. A reduction in these positions is a primary indicator that the market's "bottoming out" phase is nearing completion.
  • Macro Disconnect: Do not be misled by trailing indicators like the NBER’s official recession labels, which often confirm a downturn long after it has passed. Instead, look at the strength of U.S. consumer spending and the ongoing capital deployment of major cloud providers.

As the market moves toward the end of the year, political events in the U.S. and the stabilization of commodity prices will likely serve as the next catalysts. Investors who can ignore the immediate noise and focus on the structural, long-term demand for AI infrastructure are the ones most likely to benefit when the market eventually shifts back toward growth.
