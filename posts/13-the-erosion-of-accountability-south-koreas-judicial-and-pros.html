The Erosion of Accountability: South Korea’s Judicial and Prosecutorial Crisis

September 24, 2026

Watching the headlines coming out of South Korea’s legal sector lately feels, at times, like watching a slow-motion collision. The Chief Justice of the Supreme Court is locked in a direct confrontation with the Presidential Office, while the prosecution remains mired in endless infighting over reorganization plans. To the casual observer, the rhetoric is filled with high-minded talk of constitutional clauses and legal principles. But beneath the surface, it is a raw power struggle: a defensive wall of established interests fighting against those attempting to reform them. The current standoffs involving the prosecution and Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae are not merely petty bureaucratic squabbles; they are a mirror reflecting the uncertain future of democratic legitimacy in South Korea.

The Prosecution’s Culture of Convenience and Accountability Gaps

The controversy surrounding the indictment of former prosecutor Jung Jin-woong by the Seoul High Prosecutors’ Office highlights a troubling pattern of behavior. In late 2020, during a period of unprecedented turmoil that included the disciplinary standoff between the Justice Ministry and then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk-yeol, internal consensus was reached to charge Jung with physical assault during an investigation. According to reports, not a single prosecutor in the room voiced dissent at the time.

However, as time has passed, those involved have begun to rewrite history, claiming they were secretly opposed to the move. Former lawmaker Park Eun-jung recently provided evidence in the form of original "trial cards"—the formal documents prosecutors sign to authorize an indictment. In the Korean prosecutorial system, signing such a card is not a minor administrative chore; it is an act of personal and professional endorsement. To claim "I was against it all along" after having already stamped the document is not an act of conscience; it is an act of cowardice.

The structure of the prosecution is designed to be a top-down hierarchy. Indictments proceed only after signatures travel through the chain of command, from the deputy chief prosecutor to the Prosecutor General. When high-ranking officials—like former Supreme Prosecutors’ Office Criminal Department Chief Kim Ji-yong—approve an indictment only to later distance themselves from the political fallout, they reveal a deeply opportunistic mindset. This culture, where officials embrace the power of the office but disavow the consequences of their decisions, undermines the integrity of the entire justice system.

The Constitutional Clash: Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae vs. The Presidency

In late August, the tension reached a boiling point when the Presidential Office requested a re-nomination for a Supreme Court justice seat, effectively rejecting Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s initial candidate, Son Bong-ki. Cho, the head of the Supreme Court—South Korea's highest judicial body—remained silent for 25 days before issuing a public statement flatly refusing the request.

Cho’s refusal relied on three arguments: that the Presidential request lacked the mandatory countersignature of the Prime Minister, that there was no constitutional basis for a President to request a "re-nomination," and that the request lacked a specific justification. Critics, however, argue that this is a classic case of procedural hair-splitting designed to shield the judiciary from democratic oversight. The root of the conflict lies in a seven-month period of total silence between the Court and the President regarding appointments. After failing to communicate, the Chief Justice’s attempt to claim "judicial independence" as a shield against a formal request for re-evaluation strikes many as an abuse of his position.

The core issue here is "popular sovereignty." Under Article 1 of the South Korean Constitution, all power derives from the people. While judges are not directly elected, their appointments must function within a framework of mutual check and balance. By treating the Supreme Court as a personal fiefdom and acting as if his appointment power is superior to the constitutional will of the executive and legislative branches, Chief Justice Cho has invited calls for his resignation. The current attempt by the conservative People Power Party to have the Constitutional Court settle the dispute—rather than the Chief Justice doing so himself—is viewed by many as a calculated delay tactic, likely to ensure the case remains unresolved until his term ends next July.

Prosecutorial Overhaul: A Half-Measure for Reform?

The Ministry of Justice recently finalized a plan to reorganize the Prosecution Service, aiming to separate investigative and prosecutorial powers. The scale of the change is significant: of the nearly 3,000 investigative officers currently serving, roughly 70%—or about 2,048 positions—are slated for removal or reassignment. The traditional "investigative officer" role is effectively being erased, with the remainder transitioned into administrative legal roles.

However, the skepticism remains high because of who is not being cut. While the support staff is being gutted, the number of prosecutors remains steady at approximately 1,900. If the agency is moving toward a system where prosecutors strictly handle indictments rather than investigations, why is the bloated roster of prosecutors left untouched? This discrepancy suggests that the "reform" is less about streamlining justice and more about preserving the elite status of the prosecution itself.

True reform requires more than changing the names of departments or shuffling administrative duties. It requires the surrender of the excessive, centralized power that has allowed the prosecution to function as a law unto itself for decades. As long as those holding the "sword of justice" refuse to relinquish their institutional advantages, true democratic control will remain an unfinished project.

What to Watch Next

The landscape of Korean law is shifting, and the coming months will be decisive. Key areas to monitor include:

  • The Push for Impeachment: Within the opposition Democratic Party of Korea, the momentum to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is building. Even if such a motion fails to clear the Constitutional Court, the political signal it sends would be a historic rebuke of judicial overreach.
  • The Impact of the Reorganization: As the new structure of the prosecution takes shape, watch for whether the remaining prosecutors attempt to bypass the new restrictions on investigations by exerting influence over the newly formed legal administrative units.
  • Constitutional Court Rulings: With the Constitutional Court serving as the final arbiter in the standoff between the President and the Chief Justice, the court's composition—and its willingness to check judicial power—will dictate the boundaries of authority in the final year of the current administrative cycle.

The legal system is intended to be a wall that protects the public. When that wall is instead used to protect the people inside the institution from the law itself, the entire democratic project is at risk. For now, the "imperial" nature of these institutions is being challenged, but whether these challenges lead to genuine reform or simply more entrenched conflict remains to be seen.
