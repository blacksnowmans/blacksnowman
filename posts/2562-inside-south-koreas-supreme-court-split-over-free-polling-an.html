Inside South Korea’s Supreme Court Split Over Free Polling and Political Money

November 20, 2026

In the summer of 2021, South Korea’s political calendar was entering its most volatile phase. Inside the People Power Party—South Korea’s main conservative party, which now serves as the primary opposition to President Lee Jae-myung’s ruling Democratic Party—contenders were jockeying for position ahead of the party’s primary to choose a nominee for the national election. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who had only recently joined the party and is today imprisoned following his impeachment and removal from office, faced an urgent challenge: proving to party insiders and voters alike that he was the clear frontrunner.

At that critical juncture, a series of public opinion polls conducted by political consultant Myung Tae-gyun in partnership with the Korean financial daily Money Today abruptly stopped. On July 5, 2021, Mr. Yoon’s camp, including his wife, Ms. Kim Keon-hee, urged Mr. Myung to resolve the problem quickly. Mr. Myung moved fast, securing a new media partner in the conservative outlet New Daily. Between late July and late October 2021, he planned, funded, and carried out a dozen polls without charging a fee, delivering the results directly to Mr. Yoon and Ms. Kim.

Years later, those unpaid surveys have triggered a sharp legal debate in South Korean campaign finance. At the heart of the dispute is a question that reaches far beyond one politician or one election: when a consultant spends tens of thousands of dollars on polling and hands the data to a candidate for free, is that merely informal political advice—or an illegal political donation?

Why Unpaid Polls Became a Legal Flashpoint

To understand why South Korea’s courts have divided over this question, it helps to look at how tightly the country regulates political money. Under Article 45, Paragraph 1 of South Korea’s Political Funds Act, receiving political funds through any channel not explicitly authorized by law is a criminal offense. Rather than allowing wealthy individuals or outside groups to spend freely around a campaign, South Korean law routes donations through strict, transparent accounting structures designed to sever backroom ties between political power and moneyed interests.

Recognizing that illicit funding rarely arrives in simple envelopes of cash, South Korean lawmakers wrote a catch-all clause into Article 3, Subparagraph 2 of the act. Alongside money and physical goods, the statute defines political donations to include "other benefits," a deeming provision intended to cover intangible favors and services with real financial value.

The indictment against Mr. Yoon and Mr. Myung covered 58 polls in total. Among them, 12 surveys—conducted between roughly July 28 and October 21, 2021—were carried out specifically as Mr. Yoon’s camp sought to consolidate its lead in the People Power Party primary. In the first-instance trial, the court focused on 14 polls delivered directly to the couple, valued at roughly $20,000 (27.92 million won). The legal fight has never been about whether the polls took place, but about how the law should classify unpaid services when there is no written contract and when polling is intertwined with personal networking.

Justice Oh Kyung-mi’s Dissent Against the Majority Standard

In the Supreme Court’s examination of the dispute, the majority opinion adopted a strict threshold for criminal liability. Under the majority’s reasoning, unpaid polling counts as an illegal political donation only if there is a rigorous prior agreement between the parties covering the polling or its delivery, along with objective foreseeability that the third party’s spending directly substituted for expenses the politician’s camp would otherwise have had to pay.

Justices Oh Kyung-mi and Kim Seong-soo broke sharply with that view. In a detailed dissenting opinion, Justice Oh argued that the majority’s rigid test ignores how political alliances actually function in South Korea—and effectively leaves room for what critics call stealth political support.

"The majority opinion failed to consider the statutory definition of political funds and the distinct characteristics of different types of donations, imposing a uniformly strict standard that unjustifiably narrows the scope of illegal political contributions," Justice Oh wrote.

In reality, Justice Oh noted, major political figures and consultants rarely sign formal written contracts. Instead, polling is often planned and executed on the basis of tacit needs and aligned interests. Requiring prosecutors to prove an explicit upfront deal, she warned, creates a legal blind spot that shields covert transactions.

Central to her argument was the legislative intent behind the "other benefits" clause in Article 3 of the Political Funds Act. Justice Oh pointed out that earlier Supreme Court precedents applying strict interpretations mostly involved tangible items—such as cash, luxury watches, or massage chairs—and argued that those narrow rules should not be applied mechanically to intangible data and professional services.

"Lawmakers established the deeming provision of 'other benefits' in the latter part of Article 3, Subparagraph 2 of the Political Funds Act precisely to prevent the harms of money politics by comprehensively regulating the provision of pecuniary benefits that appear in highly diverse forms," Justice Oh explained.

She drew a clear boundary between personal camaraderie and paid professional output. A consultant who introduces a candidate to influential figures or offers verbal advice is engaging in permissible political assistance, she wrote. Running election polls, however, costs thousands to tens of thousands of dollars in real expenses. Whether those polls are released to the public or kept internal for campaign strategy, Justice Oh argued, they carry independent financial value that must be evaluated separately from general political advice.

Because Mr. Yoon and Ms. Kim knew Mr. Myung was spending his own money to run the 12 polls following their camp’s request—and actively used the data in their primary strategy without paying—Justice Oh concluded that receiving illegal political funds was established on at least those 12 counts. Even if Mr. Myung hoped to expand his own influence or business prospects by helping Mr. Yoon win, she added, that personal motive did not erase the financial benefit Mr. Yoon’s camp received.

How Lower Courts Reached Opposite Verdicts

The Supreme Court’s internal divide mirrors a dramatic reversal in the lower courts before the case reached Seoul’s highest bench.

In the first trial, Criminal Division 33 of the Seoul Central District Court found Mr. Yoon and Mr. Myung guilty in connection with 14 polls delivered directly to Mr. Yoon and Ms. Kim, valued at roughly $20,000 (27.92 million won). Even without a written contract, the district court inferred an implicit, sequential meeting of the minds between the two sides. By letting Mr. Myung cover costs that Mr. Yoon’s camp would otherwise have needed to pay itself, the court reasoned, the couple received a concrete economic gain. It sentenced Mr. Yoon to two years in prison and Mr. Myung to 18 months.

On appeal, however, Criminal Division 7 of the Seoul High Court—presided over by Judge Koo Hoe-geun—overturned the convictions and acquitted both men on all 58 counts. The appellate judges framed Mr. Myung’s role not as a vendor supplying paid data, but as a political ally offering broad strategic advice and introductions throughout the campaign. In that framing, the polls were merely supporting tools for political advice—a "political benefit" rather than a quantifiable financial asset—and there was no explicit agreement proving the surveys were meant to be provided free of charge.

Those conflicting verdicts highlight a recurring tension in South Korean criminal law. Judges bound by the principle of legality—requiring criminal statutes to be construed strictly in favor of defendants—are cautious about stretching campaign finance rules to cover informal networking. Yet legal observers pointing to earlier Supreme Court precedent, including a 2019 ruling (2019Do11892) that penalized candidates for accepting free corporate vehicles or having third parties pay rental fees outside official donor committees, note that South Korean law has historically treated unpaid services as unlawful financial benefits. Critics of the High Court’s acquittal argue that bundling expensive polling under the vague label of "advice" weakens that precedent and undermines the Political Funds Act.

What Is at Stake and What to Watch Next

Beyond the individuals involved, the debate sparked by Justice Oh’s dissent touches a core vulnerability in modern elections. In South Korea’s fast-moving party primaries, both public and private surveys shape media narratives, supporter momentum, and campaign strategy.

If courts treat unpaid polling—or consulting services worth hundreds of thousands of dollars—as legally indistinguishable from casual conversation unless a written contract exists, campaigns could face a strong incentive to bypass official spending rules. Rather than commissioning surveys through transparent campaign accounts, candidates could rely on informal brokers to supply costly data off the books, creating hidden debts of gratitude that risk turning into political favors after an election.

Whether South Korea’s judiciary closes or widens that door remains an open question. Following the Seoul High Court’s acquittal of Mr. Yoon, the special prosecutor’s team immediately appealed to the Supreme Court. Meanwhile, a parallel case involving Ms. Kim Keon-hee—who was acquitted in both the district and appellate courts—is currently pending before the Supreme Court’s en banc panel, where all justices sit together to resolve major legal disputes and potentially shift precedent.

For observers watching South Korea’s legal and political institutions, two developments will be decisive in the months ahead:

  • The en banc Supreme Court’s review of Ms. Kim Keon-hee’s case: Whether the full bench affirms the strict, formalistic standard requiring explicit prior agreements, or adopts the substantive approach championed by Justices Oh Kyung-mi and Kim Seong-soo.
  • Future boundaries for non-cash campaign services: How Korean courts and lawmakers distinguish protected political advice from regulated financial value as campaigns rely ever more heavily on expensive data, analytics, and outside consultants.

Justice Oh Kyung-mi’s dissent has placed a sharp legal challenge on the record. How South Korea’s highest court ultimately resolves that challenge will shape not only the final outcome of the polling cases tied to Mr. Yoon’s time in office, but also the rules governing money and influence in Korean politics for years to come.
