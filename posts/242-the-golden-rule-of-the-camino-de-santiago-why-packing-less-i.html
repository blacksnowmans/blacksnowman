The Golden Rule of the Camino de Santiago: Why Packing Less is the Only Way to Finish

September 07, 2026

Standing at the starting line of the Camino de Santiago, looking down the barrel of an 800-kilometer (approx. 500-mile) trek across unfamiliar terrain, a very specific kind of dread tends to settle into the bones. Can a single human body really sustain a month of continuous walking day after day? Almost universally, the very first obstacle every aspiring pilgrim faces is the backpack. Driven by a low-grade anxiety that whispers "what if you need this?", it is terribly easy to pack a bag that quickly transforms from a piece of luggage into a punishing instrument of torture. Veterans of the trail are unanimous in their advice to ruthlessly shed weight, and this is not merely an aesthetic nod to modern minimalism. It is a hard-earned, bone-deep realization that when you are walking 20 kilometers (approx. 12 miles) a day, every single 100 grams matters immensely to your physical stamina and your overall survival.

Indeed, drop into the trash bins at the *albergues*—the traditional pilgrim hostels—in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, the classic starting point in the French Pyrenees, and you will find towering piles of discarded heavy hardcover books and stiff denim jeans. Pilgrims quickly discover that a light backpack is the single greatest salvation for crossing the formidable Pyrenees. Keeping your pack to roughly 10 percent of your total body weight is not a casual suggestion found in a guidebook; it is an absolute law of survival if you want to make it to the finish line.

The Anatomy of Foot Care: Why Bliss is Found in a Lightweight Stride

When you are logging anywhere from 10 to 30 kilometers (6 to 18 miles) a day, your feet are your only mode of transportation. It is no exaggeration to say that your footwear dictates the success or failure of your entire journey. Rather than reaching for expensive brands or heavy, stiff mountaineering boots, the secret lies in choosing lightweight trekking shoes that offer reliable, stable ankle support. Heavy, rigid boots only accelerate foot fatigue, which is why discarded boots are a common sight along the trail.

Socks demand equal strategic planning. The "double-layering" method—pairing thin toe socks with a thicker pair of trekking socks—is exceptionally effective at preventing blisters. Functional toe socks reduce friction between the toes, drastically cutting down blister rates. Furthermore, building a strict habit of slipping off your shoes and socks to thoroughly air out your feet every single time you encounter a patch of shade is far more potent than any expensive medical remedy. If foot pain becomes unbearable, swapping your shoes for lightweight sandals can offer temporary relief. Ultimately, what protects your feet on the Camino is not the price tag of your gear, but the daily attentiveness you show to your own body.

Smart Gear Choices: Bunking Down and Weathering the Storm

Pilgrim hostels generally provide little more than a mattress and a pillow, making a sleeping bag non-negotiable. However, because these communal spaces are notorious breeding grounds for bedbugs, there is zero reason to invest in a high-end, luxury sleeping bag. A budget-friendly, lightweight synthetic bag (such as popular entry-level models running around 20,000 KRW, or about $15) is more than enough; expensive down bags only add unnecessary maintenance anxiety and weight.

The weather is another fickle variable. Autumn in Spain can start with blazing sunshine, only to dissolve into persistent, chilling rains the further west you travel into the Galicia region. A poncho large enough to cover both you and your backpack is an absolute must. While reliable, lightweight ponchos can be packed beforehand, there is no need to panic if you forget one: local sports shops and pilgrim outfitters along the route are fully stocked with inexpensive gear designed specifically to lighten your load.

Practical Tools for Endurance and Self-Care

Knees are usually the first part of the body to stage a revolt on the Camino. If you have any history of joint weakness, a solid knee brace is non-negotiable. Trekking poles are similarly misunderstood; they are not just for climbing uphill. On descents, they distribute your body weight across your arms, dramatically reducing the mechanical load on your knees and ankles. Using a pair of poles to maintain your center of gravity is infinitely more efficient than relying on just one.

When it comes to personal grooming, the grueling routine of waking up before dawn leaves zero room for elaborate beauty regimens. Multi-tasking, solid bar cleansers that double as shampoo and body wash can drastically strip down your pack weight, while basic, inexpensive moisturizers found in local convenience stores will easily keep your skin from drying out. More importantly, the fierce Spanish sun requires more than a standard baseball cap. A wide-brimmed hat is essential to block the unforgiving UV rays coming at you from all angles—proof that practicality will always beat vanity on the long road.

Finding Your Own Rhythm on the Road

Impatience is a poison on the Camino. If you exhaust yourself trying to match the rapid pace of younger, fitter travelers, your body will sound the alarm within a matter of days. As many seasoned older travelers have proven by successfully finishing the route, discarding a rigid itinerary in favor of listening to your physical limits is a crucial form of wisdom. If your body demands it, there is no shame in stopping after just 10 kilometers to rest.

Finding a village you love, stopping for the day, and cooking a simple meal from local market ingredients is where the true soul of travel lives. The Camino is not a race to be won; it is an exercise in meeting yourself. Resisting the pressure to conform to someone else's speed is the ultimate way to keep your pack light, both physically and mentally.

What to Watch Next

For those planning to tackle the journey, the weeks leading up to departure are a test of psychological discipline as much as physical training. Watch for how your mindset shifts when you consciously remove the word "what-if" from your packing checklist. Pay attention to the infrastructure of local pharmacies, the availability of lightweight gear swaps at regional hostels, and the practicalities of international travel insurance for unexpected medical needs in Europe. As you take your first steps out into the crisp morning air, remember that the lightest backpack begins with the simplest mindset. Clear out the excess, trust in the local resources, and step forward into a journey designed to strip away the unnecessary until only what truly matters remains.
