The Cannibal Politics Threatening South Korea’s Ruling Coalition

October 30, 2026

Turn on any prime-time broadcast on Minjoo TV, the official media arm of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party, or scroll through the dominant progressive political channels on YouTube, and an outsider might expect a relentless barrage aimed at the conservative opposition. Instead, what one finds is something far more bizarre: a bitter, inward-looking war of attrition. Pundits, lawmakers, and hardline influencers spend hours taking aim not at their ideological opponents, but at dissenters within their own ranks and fellow progressive allies.

For observers of South Korean politics, the spectacle is confounding. President Lee Jae-myung entered office backed by a broad coalition of progressive, reformist, and moderate voters who were eager to turn the page following the dramatic political upheaval that led to the impeachment, removal, and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Yet, only months into this governing mandate, that grand progressive tent is coming apart at the seams. Crucially, the damage is not being inflicted by the conservative People Power Party, which remains on the back foot as the main opposition. It is entirely self-inflicted.

This internal unraveling was laid bare by Rhyu Si-min, a veteran progressive commentator and former cabinet minister whose opinions carry immense weight among center-left voters. Rhyu warned that the ruling bloc’s behavior increasingly resembles a mysterious biological disaster, likening the party's relentless attacks on internal critics to a pod of dolphins beaching themselves on the sand for reasons no one can explain. By turning its fire inward, the ruling establishment is systematically alienating the very voters who put President Lee in the presidential office, shrinking its own coalition and isolating the administration.

The Echo Chamber and the Drive for Ideological Purity

To understand how the governing party arrived at this point, one must look at the dynamics of modern South Korean factional politics. In Seoul, party power relies heavily on highly mobilized activist fan bases. When party primaries conclude, democratic convention usually dictates an effort to build bridges and heal factional wounds. Instead, the current leadership surrounding Democratic Party head Kim Min-seok has doubled down on ideological orthodoxy.

During the recent party leadership election, Kim’s faction aggressively framed internal rivals and skeptics with venomous labels, going so far as to accuse internal competitors of being "schismatics" or linking them conspiratorially to "Shincheonji," an infamous fringe religious movement often weaponized in South Korean political rhetoric. While this rhetoric galvanized the party's militant activist base and helped secure the leadership, it came at an immense strategic cost: it deeply alienated nearly half of the Democratic Party's own rank-and-file supporters.

Worse, the triumphant leadership showed no inclination to make amends. Rather than offering an olive branch, the dominant camp treated its victory as a mandate to purge internal dissent. Party figures and prominent commentators began demanding unquestioning loyalty to the leadership, routinely branding constructive critics within the progressive tent as traitors or factional saboteurs. This aggressive posture extended to the press: rather than engaging with tough reporting, the ruling apparatus has leaned on heavy-handed legal complaints and prosecutorial investigations against critical reporters and commentators.

This combative stance directly echoes the fatal flaw seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, when insular factionalism, refusal to communicate, and relentless attacks on internal party dissenters ultimately doomed his presidency. For the Democratic Party to reproduce the exact same political pathology so soon after taking power has triggered profound disillusionment among moderate and reform-minded progressives.

The Polling Exodus: Why Progressive Voters Are Taking the "Stepping Stone"

The political price of this internal hostility is no longer theoretical; it is visible across the polling data. A close look at tracking surveys conducted between late August—shortly after Kim Min-seok assumed the party chairmanship—and late October reveals a steep and alarming slide in the ruling party's standing.

In automated response system (ARS) surveys, which typically capture the sentiments of politically engaged, highly motivated voters, the Democratic Party’s national support plummeted from 47.0 percent to 39.7 percent—a dramatic 7.3 percentage point drop in roughly two months. Live-interview telephone surveys (CATI), which tend to capture a broader swathe of swing voters and moderates, showed a similar downward slide from 44.8 percent to 41.9 percent.

The most telling metric, however, lies within the party's foundational core: self-identified progressive voters. Over that two-month span, the Democratic Party's support among core progressives in ARS polling crashed from 74.4 percent to 60.6 percent—a stunning collapse of 13.8 percentage points. This was accompanied by a direct rebuff of party leader Kim Min-seok himself. By mid-October, Kim's negative job rating stood at 63 percent against just 31 percent positive. Even among self-described progressives, 52.5 percent disapproved of Kim’s party management, far outpacing the 41.1 percent who approved.

Where are these disillusioned voters going? In polarized South Korean politics, crossing ideological lines to back the conservative People Power Party is an immense psychological leap—often compared by local political analysts to "swimming across the Han River." Disaffected center-left voters will not simply defect to the right.

Instead, they are crossing a much smaller "stepping stone" to the Rebuilding Korea Party, the smaller, unapologetically progressive party founded by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Over the same two-month period, the Rebuilding Korea Party saw its overall support jump from 5.7 percent to 8.8 percent in ARS polling (and from 3.5 percent to 6.2 percent in live interviews). Among core progressive voters, its support more than doubled, rocketing from 10.5 percent to 22.4 percent.

This voter mobility is deliberate. Polling reveals that between 80 and 90 percent of Rebuilding Korea Party supporters hold a negative view of how the current Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic leadership are running state and party affairs. These voters have not abandoned progressive governance, but they are deliberately parking their support with an allied party to issue a stern warning to the Democratic Party: compromise your values, silence internal debate, and you will govern alone.

The Peril of the "40 Percent Trap"

The tragedy of the ruling camp’s current course is that South Korean political history has already demonstrated how this story ends. Past administrations—including the progressive governments of Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in—accomplished significant policy achievements, yet both ultimately failed to retain power because they fell into the trap of governing exclusively for their passionate 40 percent base while alienating the remaining 60 percent of the electorate.

Power, by its nature, creates an illusion of invulnerability. Once seated in the presidential Blue House and holding a legislative majority, party leaders frequently confuse party discipline with political strength. They convince themselves that critics are merely disloyal and that a single dominant figure—in this case, President Lee—can govern through pure executive speed and personal political force.

However, governing a fractured democracy requires a radically different skill set than winning a partisan campaign. High-handed dismissals of opposition critique and the policing of internal party speech quickly drain a presidency of its moral authority. When an administration isolates itself, civil servants begin dragging their feet on key policy initiatives, prosecutorial organs look for openings, and ruling lawmakers quietly begin scheming for their own political survival as public fatigue sets in.

What to Watch Next

As the administration navigates this self-inflicted storm, several concrete developments will indicate whether the Democratic Party can reverse its slide or continue drifting toward structural paralysis:

  • The 100-Day Mark for Party Leadership: Kim Min-seok initially promised to boost the party's approval rating by 10 percentage points within his first 100 days. As that milestone approaches, watch whether the leadership publicly recalibrates its stance, abandons aggressive legal actions against critics, and formally apologizes to the internal factions bruised during the party convention.
  • Relations with the Rebuilding Korea Party: The Democratic leadership's posture toward Cho Kuk's party will be a critical barometer. If the ruling party treats the Rebuilding Korea Party as an adversary to be suppressed rather than a coalition partner to be respected, the progressive split will become institutionalized, severely hurting the bloc in future municipal and legislative contests.
  • President Lee’s Governance Rhetoric: Observers should watch whether President Lee shifts his public messaging away from one-sided declarations and defensive social-media engagement toward broad-tent political outreach. An explicit olive branch to moderate factions, combined with an effort to rein in aggressive pro-government broadcast influencers, would signal that the administration understands the danger of the beached-dolphin syndrome before it is too late.
