Navigating Rising Tides: South Korea’s Domestic Turmoil and Washington's Trade Pressures

September 24, 2026

South Korea’s political capital is gripped by a quiet, pervasive tension. While the administration's attention is pulled outward toward high-stakes diplomacy in North America, the home front is straining under the weight of fluctuating public approval, a sudden and high-profile resignation at the heart of the presidential office, and an increasingly aggressive trade posture from Washington. For foreign observers watching Seoul from afar, Korean politics can often look like a rapid succession of sudden crises. Yet beneath these headline-grabbing events lies a deeper story about a government trying to maintain its footing midway through its term while navigating a changing geopolitical landscape.

To understand the current moment, one has to look past the superficial numbers and examine the fractured nature of South Korean public opinion, the structural pressures facing the presidential staff, and the hardball economics playing out behind the scenes of the U.S.–South Korea alliance. This is a critical juncture where domestic political survival and economic statecraft intersect.

The Tale of Two Polls: Reading South Korean Public Opinion

One of the perennial confusions for anyone following South Korean politics is the sheer volatility and contradiction of public opinion polling. Depending on the methodology used, the political mood of the country can appear entirely different on the exact same day. This was precisely the case following a recent high-profile presidential press conference, where two distinct polling methods yielded sharply divergent results.

In telephone-intervied surveys, where live operators guide respondents through questions, the president's positive approval rating edged out negative marks, landing at 50.5% compared to 45.3%—putting it safely outside the margin of error. However, automated response system (ARS) surveys, where respondents punch numbers into their phones in response to a recorded voice, painted a much bleaker picture, with negative evaluations coming out on top. Analysts note that this divergence is not merely a matter of one method being right and the other wrong; rather, they capture entirely different layers of the public psyche.

Live interviews tend to elicit more considered, restrained responses, whereas ARS surveys act as an immediate emotional outlet for frustrated voters to register their discontent. Within progressive and reform-minded circles, lingering grievances from past policy battles and cabinet appointments still run deep. Real-time commentary across social media platforms reflects a persistent undercurrent of disappointment. Nevertheless, compared to the precipiacitous drops in support the administration faced during previous scandals, the fact that positive ratings have held their ground indicates a certain resilience.

Weekend polling trends confirmed that the press conference managed to avoid total disaster—often considered a victory in the high-stakes game of Korean spin. But dwelling on minor numerical upticks misses the broader erosion of support. A closer demographic breakdown reveals that the administration has shed roughly 30% of its base among progressive voters, 20% among moderates, and 10% among conservatives since the highs leading up to the local elections. While a brief bump following foreign tours and the press conference provided temporary relief, a clumsy social media misstep by the president over the weekend quickly reignited criticism, neutralizing potential momentum and reminding the public of the fragility of political trust.

The Departure of the Chief of Staff and the Burden of Personnel Failures

As the domestic political landscape sputtered, the administration absorbed a heavy blow with the abrupt announcement that Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik intended to resign, a mere 15개월 (15 months) into his tenure. Kang’s departure is framed as taking comprehensive, ultimate responsibility for a series of painful political setbacks, most notably the high-profile withdrawal of several controversial cabinet nominees and the steady slide in presidential approval ratings.

Unlike many traditional chiefs of staff who function primarily as administrative caretakers or ceremonial gatekeepers, Kang had carved out a reputation as an unusually visible and influential policy player—often described by political insiders as an indispensable "ace" within the administration. For the Blue House, losing such a central figure in the middle of a political crisis creates a profound institutional vacuum. Instead of a graceful exit at the height of an achievement, Kang’s departure resembles a classic political shield sacrifice, absorbing the fallout of personnel missteps to protect the broader administration.

This leaves the president in a classic double-bind. Cutting loose a top-tier strategist weakens the inner circle, yet attempting to shield him against a mounting wall of public criticism invites further populist backlash. The president’s own reaction during a senior staff meeting—remarking that Kang was finally looking to "graduate" after a grueling tenure, while withholding a definitive decision on whether to accept the resignation—reflected this heavy internal deliberation. Observers expect a sweeping personnel shakeup to follow the president’s return from abroad, setting the stage for what will likely be the most turbulent administrative reshuffle of the term so far.

Washington’s Heavy Hand: Trade Pressures and the US-Korea Alliance

While domestic politics simmered, the president embarked on a vital overseas tour to the United States and Mexico, where the primary testing ground centers on relations with the Trump administration. The prospect of a face-to-face meeting between the two leaders has dominated foreign policy discussions, carrying massive implications for everything from defense cost-sharing and regional security deployments to stalled inter-Korean diplomacy.

Yet, enthusiasm over alliance solidarity is heavily tempered by anxiety over aggressive American trade tactics. Negotiations over massive South Korean investments in the United States have run into severe friction as Washington attempts to rewrite previously agreed-upon terms. Last year, Seoul thought it had secured a vital safeguard known as the "commercial rationality" clause—a defensive mechanism ensuring that massive foreign capital outlays would at least make business sense and avoid outright losses.

Now, those safeguards are being actively challenged. For instance, investments in the Texas gas-fired power plant project have already ballooned well past initial targets to over $22 billion (about 30조원), despite lingering questions over who the core corporate customers will actually be. Simultaneously, Seoul is facing heavy-handed pressure to absorb equity stakes in struggling American firms like Westinghouse. Other nations, such as Japan, have quietly walked away from similarly dubious projects like the Alaska LNG initiative due to poor commercial viability. For South Korea, the challenge is resisting one-sided trade demands disguised as alliance obligations without triggering a rupture in bilateral ties.

Amid these tensions, former U.S. State Department official and North Korea envoy Stephen Biegun visited Seoul, offering soothing words in a media interview by declaring that economic policy and national security are separate tracks rather than a single bundled package. While the sentiment was clearly designed to calm growing anti-American sentiment and fears that Washington is weaponizing security issues like naval deployments to extract economic concessions, Korean analysts remain deeply skeptical. In practice, Washington has rarely hesitated to deploy the rhetoric of security alliances when it needs economic concessions, only to wall off security concerns when Seoul asks for reciprocal benefits. For South Korea's negotiators, charm offensive statements must be weighed strictly against the concrete pressures being applied at the bargaining table.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this volatile intersection of domestic fatigue and external pressure, several concrete milestones will dictate the political trajectory over the coming weeks:

  • The Blue House Reshuffle: Watch for the official acceptance or rejection of Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik’s resignation immediately following the president's return from the North American tour, which will signal the depth and severity of the upcoming cabinet overhaul.
  • Investment Treaty Outcomes: Monitor the final text of the U.S.–Korea investment agreements—specifically whether Seoul successfully preserves commercial viability safeguards or is forced to absorb distressed corporate assets under the banner of alliance duty.
  • Polling Trends in Key Demographics: Pay attention to whether the administration can claw back support among disillusioned progressive and moderate voters, or if social media missteps and economic anxieties continue to depress core approval numbers.
