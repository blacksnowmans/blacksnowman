Why South Korea's Progressive Party Is Bleeding Support Despite Holding Power

October 09, 2026

For many progressive South Koreans, the feeling of winning the presidency has given way to a quiet, bitter exhaustion. On paper, the democratic camp holds the executive branch, and the conservative opposition is reeling from the historic impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Suk Yeol. Yet, rather than celebrating a golden age of reform, core supporters are weeping out of frustration.

This paradox was at the center of a recent episode of the political commentary show hosted by Hong Sa-hun, featuring prominent party figures. Instead of projecting triumphalism, the discussion laid bare deep internal fractures, falling approval numbers, and a growing sense of betrayal among the grassroots. To understand why South Korea's ruling liberal bloc is floundering while in power, one has to look past the legislative victories and examine a political machine at war with itself.

The Eternal Shadow of the Prosecution Service

A primary source of anguish for the progressive base is the stubborn, unyielding culture of South Korea’s prosecution service. Under Yoon Suk Yeol's past tenure as prosecutor general, the agency wielded its investigative powers with near-absolute impunity, targeting political rivals and consolidating institutional muscle. Now, even with a progressive administration in power under President Lee Jae-myung, reform-minded critics argue the prosecution's institutional DNA has barely mutated.

Panelists on the show pointed out that the prosecution's primary institutional reflex remains unchanged: protecting its own turf and corporate prestige by leveraging investigative authority as a political weapon. Despite legislative efforts to curb their powers—such as moves toward establishing a dedicated serious crimes investigation agency—individual prosecutors continue to operate within a rigid, hierarchical system of obedience. In this culture, loyalty to senior figures supersedes democratic accountability, effectively creating a self-preserving cartel shielded from outside control.

This dynamic reared its head again around personnel debates and confirmation hearings, such as the controversy surrounding candidates like Kim Ji-yong. Critics argue that recycling figures tied to the old prosecutorial establishment amounts to institutional backscratching. When high-ranking officials manage disciplinary processes only to bow out abruptly or protect institutional interests behind closed doors, it shatters the trust of party members who staked their hopes on radical judicial reform. France and Germany limit direct prosecutorial investigations to avoid such overreach, but South Korea's combined monopoly on investigation, indictment, and warrant requests remains a legacy of a less mature judicial system.

Falling Approval Ratings and Leadership Silence

Perhaps the most alarming data point discussed was a recent Gallup poll showing the ruling Democratic Party's approval rating sinking to a concerning 35 percent. More shocking than the numbers themselves was the alleged reaction—or lack thereof—from the party leadership. According to senior party officials on the program, serious discussions regarding this downward trend have rarely surfaced in official leadership meetings.

This institutional silence points to a widening gulf between party elites and their traditional base. The core supporters of the liberal camp—many of whom carry deep political scars stretching back decades—poured their hopes into the current administration, only to feel alienated by internal backstabbing and ideological purity tests. Instead of addressing policy concerns or bridging gaps, parts of the party apparatus have resorted to aggressive factionalism, branding dissenting voices as traitors or internal subversives.

President Lee Jae-myung himself has taken to social media to voice his frustration, warning against radical factions that demand unrealistic concessions, attack colleagues from within, and weaponize ideological purity when things do not go their way. Yet, the fact that the president is forced to air these grievances directly on social platforms speaks to a wider failure of the administration's public communication lines and a hostile mainstream media environment that routinely magnifies internal friction over substantive policy achievements.

The Three Conditions for Retaining Power

History suggests that retaining power requires a strict adherence to political fundamentals. The debate outlined three non-negotiable conditions for the progressive camp to survive and win future elections.

  • Economic Competitiveness and Livelihoods: The Lee Jae-myung administration must deliver tangible results in the real economy—managing trade, bolstering corporate competitiveness for giants like Samsung and SK hynix, while ensuring the economic fruits trickle down to small business owners and laborers. Without a solid economic floor, political support will inevitably drift.
  • Genuine, Compelling Leadership: Leaders must embody authenticity and a willingness to confront structural contradictions head-on, rather than sliding into comfortable institutional complacency.
  • Zero Internal Fractures: History shows that ruling parties rarely lose to the opposition; they lose to themselves. Factional infighting, vicious labeling, and public squabbling drain a party's vitality faster than any external attack.

The party's salvation, according to the show's participants, lies in "party member sovereignty"—ensuring that ordinary rank-and-file members who sustain the movement on the ground retain a real voice in its direction. They urge supporters not to abandon the party in despair, but to stay and use their collective weight to force accountability.

What to Watch Next

As South Korean politics moves forward, several concrete developments will indicate whether the ruling bloc can course-correct before its base slips away entirely.

  • Prosecutorial Reform Bills: Watch for how fiercely the party pushes structural judicial reforms through the National Assembly, and whether compromises dilute the original intent of separating investigative and indicting powers.
  • Personnel Confirmations: Pay close attention to upcoming parliamentary confirmation hearings for key judicial and administrative posts. If the leadership steamrolls through controversial nominees with prosecutorial-establishment ties, expect a sharp backlash from the party's grassroots.
  • Leadership Response to Polling: Monitor whether the ruling party leadership shifts from defensive silence to open town halls and transparent internal debates regarding its sliding public approval numbers.

The democratic space hard-won by citizens in the streets cannot be sustained by complacency or elite consensus. If the ruling party fails to heal its internal wounds and respect the grassroots supporters who built it, the tears of its base will turn from sorrow into a force of political retribution.
