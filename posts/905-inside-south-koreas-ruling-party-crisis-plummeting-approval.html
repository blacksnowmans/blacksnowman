Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Crisis: Plummeting Approval and the Trap of Transactional Politics

October 10, 2026

A closed-door meeting that stretched for four hours between President Lee Jae-myung and former party leader Chung Cheong-rae was supposed to signal a unified front for South Korea’s ruling party. Instead, it seems to have laid bare a deepening fracture within the progressive bloc. Behind the scenes, quiet frustrations are boiling over into public view, aimed squarely at the current leadership and a presidency struggling to read the room as public goodwill evaporates.

For outside observers trying to understand South Korea's fast-moving political landscape, this moment captures a familiar tragedy of modern governance: a ruling party winning the ultimate prize of executive power, only to find itself increasingly isolated from the very voters who put it there. With approval ratings sliding to alarming lows and internal dissent growing louder, the administration's grip on momentum is slipping.

The 35% Shock: When Core Voters Cross Their Arms

The numbers coming out of recent opinion polls are difficult to spin. The Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s main liberal party currently holding the presidency, has seen its nationwide approval rating plummet to 35%. More alarming for party strategists are the regional and demographic breakdowns: in the crucial capital region of Seoul and among voters in their 30s—traditionally the backbone of the progressive coalition—support has cratered to a dismal 29%.

This is not merely a temporary dip; it is a structural warning sign. Yet the blame game inside the party has quickly turned toxic. Critics within the progressive camp point the finger at Party Leader Kim Min-seok, whose fondness for hardline rhetoric and a seemingly endless stream of showy, performative task forces has alienated moderates. When a party leader rolls out dozens of high-sounding committees that produce no tangible legislative output, voters notice. The perception is one of top-down, insular management that ignores democratic deliberation in favor of political theater.

Compounding the problem is President Lee Jae-myung’s own communication style. Recent social media messages from the president appeared to directly target lawmakers within his own party who dared to voice concerns over contentious policies. Rather than building a broad coalition, this combative approach risks freezing out wavering supporters. As political commentators have acutely observed, the voters abandoning the administration right now haven't necessarily flipped to the conservative opposition—they have simply crossed their arms, stepped back, and decided to watch with deep skepticism.

Prosecution Reform or a Dangerous Double-Edged Sword?

Nowhere is this disconnect more visible than in the ongoing debate over prosecution reform. The original intent behind restructuring South Korea's powerful investigative agencies was to check overreaching state power and protect citizens' rights. However, critics argue that the current iteration of the reform has devolved into a distorted, partisan weapon.

In a political system scarred by the downfall and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Seok-youl—who was impeached and removed from office for abusing state power—institutions were supposed to be reinforced to prevent future abuses. Instead, critics argue that the ruling party is pushing through tools that could easily become dangerous in the hands of a future administration. If the conservative People Power Party wins back the presidency in the future, a heavily politicized investigative apparatus could easily be turned against today's progressive lawmakers.

By prioritizing short-term political gains, current reformers risk creating a political boomerang. History suggests that power changes hands in South Korea with cyclical regularity. Ignoring the long-term institutional risks of rushed, partisan legislation leaves the entire party vulnerable to the very mechanisms they are currently constructing.

The Trap of Reducing Everything to "Interest"

Perhaps the most revealing aspect of the current political crisis lies in how the presidential office interprets dissent. According to multiple reports and internal critiques, President Lee views the political world almost exclusively through the lens of transactional self-interest.

Under this analytical framework, any criticism is quickly dismissed as a bid for personal advancement or a grievance over a missed appointment. When a lawmaker expresses caution about prosecution reform, the administration's internal calculus often assumes the critic is simply chasing headlines or popularity. When commentators offer tough advice, they are accused of chasing views and financial incentives.

This worldview leaves little room for genuine conviction, ideological debate, or principled concern for the nation's direction. When a leader believes that everyone is simply calculating their own bottom line, true listening stops. It creates an echo chamber where cautionary voices are silenced and sycophants thrive, blinding the administration to the genuine economic fatigue and disillusionment felt by ordinary citizens.

What to Watch Next

As the administration navigates this turbulent phase, several key milestones will determine whether it can reverse its fortunes or slide deeper into crisis:

  • Cabinet Reshuffle Follow-Through: Following recent personnel controversies and subsequent apologies from the presidential office, watch closely to see whether substantive administrative appointments actually materialize to refresh the government's image.
  • Internal Dissent in Parliament: Pay attention to whether backbench and moderate lawmakers in the Democratic Party continue to speak out against hardline party leadership, or if they are successfully silenced by party discipline.
  • Metropolitan Polling Trends: Keep an eye on approval ratings specifically in Seoul and among voters in their 30s; if these numbers fail to rebound, panic among lawmakers facing upcoming local or legislative dynamics will intensify dramatically.

Power has a way of convincing those who hold it that public sentiment can be managed, calculated, and controlled. But as South Korea's current leaders are discovering, public trust cannot be engineered—and ignoring the warning signs of a disillusioned electorate is a gamble no political party can afford to win.
