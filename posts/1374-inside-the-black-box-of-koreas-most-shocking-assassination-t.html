Inside the Black Box of Korea's Most Shocking Assassination: The Untold Secrets of the Mun Se-gwang Investigation

October 21, 2026

The assassination of First Lady Yuk Young-su on August 15, 1974, remains one of the most haunting and transformative moments in modern South Korean history. On paper, the case was straightforward: a young ethnic Korean resident of Japan, Mun Se-gwang, slipped across the border, infiltrated a packed National Liberation Day ceremony at the National Theater of Korea, and fired a revolver at then-President Park Chung-hee, fatally striking the First Lady instead. But beneath the neat narrative delivered to the public lies a maze of institutional blunders, fabricated clues, and dark intelligence agency cover-ups that have never been fully explained.

For decades, the inner workings of the special investigation have remained locked behind the doors of South Korea's military dictatorship era. Now, fresh testimony from Choi Jong-sun, a young agent who served on the special investigation headquarters of the Korean Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA), is pulling back the curtain. His accounts of the physical evidence, the chaotic investigation rooms, and a mysterious figure known only as "Y" paint a chilling picture of an investigation designed not to find the truth, but to manage a pre-written script.

Examining these historical cover-ups is not merely an academic exercise. As South Korea navigates the political landscape under President Lee Jae-myung—whose administration has prioritized reckoning with past state abuses—uncovering the institutional rot of the country’s past intelligence apparatus is vital. Much like the public reckoning that followed the corruption and information-monopolizing abuses of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, dragging these suppressed histories into the daylight is a necessary continuation of deep-rooted democratic reforms.

The Crime Scene Evidence: A Cheap Suitcase and a Weaponized Radio

When Choi Jong-sun first arrived at the KCIA investigation headquarters, the atmosphere was thick with frantic energy and intense global pressure. Sitting on his desk was a battered brown vinyl-over-veneer suitcase recovered from the Niagara Hotel, where the assassin had stayed. It was so awkwardly large that it disrupted the office layout, but its contents were even stranger. Inside lay an ordinary, almost pathetic collection of personal items: a worn Dunhill cigarette case, personal photographs, and a cheap leather utility belt.

To an investigator, the amateurish nature of the luggage felt entirely out of place for a planned presidential assassination. But that sloppiness may have been an intentional illusion. The investigation team was sharply divided. Across the hall sat older KCIA officials—many of them former Japanese colonial police officers who had built careers torturing independence activists—who were eager to seize control of the probe simply because they spoke Japanese. The clash between their outdated methods and the younger agents' analytical mindset turned Mun’s flimsy suitcase into the gateway of a massive state conspiracy.

The most crucial piece of evidence, however, was a transistor radio tucked inside a worn leather case. Stripped of all its internal electronic components, the radio had been hollowed out to snugly house a Smith & Wesson .38-caliber revolver loaded with five rounds, secured with precisely fitted screws. It was a clever evasion tactic designed to bypass airport metal detectors. Accompanying it was the utility belt filled with hammers and tools—a built-in excuse for any metallic clinking detected by security checkpoints, meant to trick guards into thinking the noise came from repair gear.

The Smith & Wesson inside that radio ultimately took First Lady Yuk Young-su’s life. The transformation of an everyday household item into a lethal weapon mocked the era's security systems. Yet, the presence of the decoy tool belt hinted at something larger: this was not the work of a lone wolf acting on a whim, but the product of professional tradecraft.

Inside the KCIA Basement: A Gagged Giant and a Cold Realization

Driven by curiosity, Choi descended into the heavily guarded KCIA basement interrogation cells to see the suspect face-to-face. Flashing his inspection badge past military police, he entered a grim, pressurized environment. On an iron cot lay a massive man, his arms and legs stretched diagonally and secured with handcuffs at all four corners in a sprawling "大" shape.

The suspect was enormous, his heavy frame filling the cot entirely. Stripped of his civilian clothes, he had already been forced into a military uniform—a standard KCIA psychological tactic meant to strip detainees of their civilian identity and civil rights, a playbook later mirrored during the arrest of General Kim Jae-gyu years later. The man's face was hidden behind a mask, and a gag was stuffed tightly into his mouth to prevent him from biting his tongue and committing suicide.

When Choi demanded the guards remove the mask to compare the prisoner against the passport photo, he was met with hesitance. Once uncovered, Choi looked at the passport bearing the name Yukio Yoshii—a stolen identity belonging to a real Japanese citizen—and then down at the heavy, breathing man before him. Hit by a wave of cynical exhaustion, Choi muttered aloud: "Dammit, if you're going to shoot, at least shoot right. Why'd you shoot a heavy woman?"

The man on the bed was Mun Se-gwang. He had intended to kill President Park Chung-suu, but missed, striking the First Lady. Up close, Mun looked less like a hardened revolutionary or master assassin and more like a massive, unlucky pawn. The gag in his mouth did more than prevent suicide; it ensured he could not speak out of turn, protecting the KCIA from any inconvenient truths slipping into the record.

The Investigation Bureaucracy: Why the KCIA Chased Ghosts

Until the evening of the shooting, the KCIA’s 6th Bureau genuinely believed the assassin was a Japanese national named Yukio Yoshii. The initial investigation report drafted by Choi reflected this exact false identity. KCIA Director Shin Ji-soo and other top brass kept domestic agents completely in the dark, citing the agency’s strict principle of "compartmentalization." In practice, however, this information silo functioned as an excuse for deliberate manipulation.

The agency was caught in a public relations and operational farce. While international press was being fed the narrative of a Japanese lone-actor assassin, other factions within the state apparatus already knew Mun was an ethnic Korean resident of Japan. Domestic investigators were left writing fiction based on scraps of information thrown down from above.

The illusion shattered completely the next morning. On August 16, the Chosun Ilbo newspaper published a front-page scoop detailing Mun Se-gwang’s exact background, family history, and personal profile—just hours after the KCIA had formally announced the suspect was a Japanese citizen named Yoshii. How did a newspaper secure detailed biographical data, down to the posters on Mun’s bedroom wall in Osaka, faster than South Korea's premier intelligence agency?

The answer exposed a deep rift within the KCIA. While the domestic investigation team stumbled blindly in the dark, the agency's overseas operations bureau had long been monitoring Mun. They had already gathered his file, yet they withheld it from their domestic colleagues. When the domestic team's initial public announcement collapsed into an embarrassing failure, the overseas wing stepped in to quietly "harmonize the scenario," feeding real details piece by piece to patch up the state's narrative.

The Mystery of "Y" and the Shadow of the Osaka Operations Unit

Perhaps the strangest element in Choi’s memory is the sudden appearance of an agent known only by his resident code "E" or "Y" inside the 6th Bureau office right after the attack. While the entire country was under a state of emergency, this operative—who had been stationed as an intelligence officer in Osaka, Mun’s home base—strolled into the headquarters looking completely relaxed.

When asked why he was there during a national crisis, the agent casually replied that he was just "stopping by during vacation." For an intelligence officer assigned to the exact region where the assassin was radicalized to be casually "on vacation" in Seoul during a presidential assassination is an absurdity.

Choi and other researchers suspect that this agent "Y" was the invisible handler who facilitated Mun’s movements. Mun could not speak Korean fluently, yet he had received phone calls and logistical assistance from mysterious helpers upon arriving in Seoul. Following Y’s casual office visit, the KCIA suddenly flooded the investigation with precise, advanced intelligence about Mun that they previously claimed not to have.

Agent Y represents the missing link in the Mun Se-gwang case. He was the rider behind the horse. Whether Y actively recruited Mun in Japan or simply monitored his descent into violence and allowed it to proceed as a catalyst for regime consolidation remains a haunting historical question.
