Record Profits, Plummeting Stocks, and the Memory Crunch Reshaping Global Tech

October 30, 2026

On the trading floor in Seoul, the numbers flashing across the terminals looked like a typographical error. Samsung Electronics had just reported an operating profit of 107.4 trillion won (around $80 billion) for the third quarter, setting an all-time record and crossing the mythical 100-trillion-won quarterly threshold for the first time in corporate history. The company’s Device Solutions division, which handles semiconductor manufacturing, generated 106 trillion won of that total by itself—accounting for roughly 99 percent of the entire conglomerate's operational haul. Yet within hours of the announcement, Samsung’s share price tumbled more than 4 percent, dragging down the benchmark KOSPI index and leaving foreign observers puzzled.

The contradiction captured the strange, hyper-compressed reality of the current semiconductor cycle. Driven by an insatiable global race to build artificial intelligence infrastructure, high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and enterprise data storage have turned into geopolitical gold. But beneath the staggering headline figures lies a more complicated landscape: uneven internal balance sheets, shifting bargaining power that has caught even Apple off guard, aggressive moves by Chinese competitors, and growing growing pains across South Korea's domestic manufacturing backbone.

The Great Divergence: Astronomical Balance Sheets and Investor Skepticism

To understand why a historic earnings report triggered an immediate selloff, one has to look at the gap between short-term numbers and market expectations. Samsung’s memory division surged on massive shipments of HBM3E and high-capacity server DRAM. The company disclosed that its next-generation HBM4 sales jumped more than threefold and that it had locked in long-term supply agreements with roughly ten global tech giants. In doing so, Samsung executives declared that its HBM production capacity was effectively sold out through 2027, with supply remaining extremely tight into 2028—a forceful rebuttal to Wall Street rumors that the memory cycle had already peaked.

However, the company’s internal dynamics told a conflicting story. While the chip division was minting money, the Device eXperience (DX) division—the arm responsible for consumer electronics, including smartphones and home appliances—fell into the red, recording an operating loss of roughly 800 billion won (about $600 million). For institutional investors, watching the chip business subsidize bleeding consumer divisions revived old worries about structural drag inside South Korea's largest conglomerate.

Compounding the problem was shareholder remuneration. While Samsung announced a substantial 30 trillion won (approximately $22.5 billion) cash dividend, institutional and foreign investors had priced in aggressive share buybacks and immediate capital returns. Instead, Samsung deferred the detailed blueprint for additional shareholder distributions until January, while simultaneously signaling that capital expenditures could climb from 76.8 trillion won to 84 trillion won (around $63 billion) by 2026. In the eyes of the market, management chose heavy long-term capital outlays over immediate payouts during a period of macroeconomic jitters.

The reception stood in stark contrast to that of SK Hynix, South Korea’s other memory powerhouse. SK Hynix delivered record quarterly results underpinned by an extraordinary operating profit margin of 80 percent—generating 80 won of profit for every 100 won of product sold. Bolstered by an expansive $75 billion (roughly 100 trillion won) shareholder return commitment, its shares gained nearly 2 percent. SK Hynix confirmed on its conference call that its DRAM and HBM lines are fully booked out through 2029, with over half of that production tied to firm long-term contracts. To keep commoditized competitors like China's ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) at bay, the firm is already accelerating development on next-generation architectures, including capacitorless 4F² memory cells.

When Customers Lose Leverage: Apple and the Memory Squeeze

The shockwaves from this supply crunch are reshaping boardrooms across Silicon Valley, most notably at Apple. For decades, the Cupertino tech giant sat at the pinnacle of the tech food chain. Armed with colossal procurement volumes, Apple routinely dictated terms and forced component suppliers—from SanDisk to Samsung—to accept aggressive price reductions.

That dynamic has collapsed. Driven by a wave of AI data center construction, demand for enterprise solid-state drives (eSSD) has stripped global NAND flash inventories bare. With prices multiplying over just a few months, investment banks like Citi warn that the shortage is unlikely to ease anytime soon. For Apple, which has long extracted estimated 80 percent gross margins on storage upgrades for iPhones and MacBooks, the spike in underlying component costs is directly eating into product profitability.

Apple recently received notices from memory suppliers demanding substantial price increases for NAND flash. Facing an acute supply deficit, the iPhone maker had little choice but to concede, marking a dramatic reversal in commercial leverage.

This dynamic extends far beyond Apple. The tech sector is confronting what analysts call "chipflation." Meta alone has earmarked $97.5 billion for AI infrastructure expansion, while Nvidia and AMD are vacuuming up every advanced memory wafer they can secure. Consequently, fabrication lines that once made consumer-grade memory for everyday laptops and mobile phones have been reassigned to hyperscale data centers.

The consumer fallout is already tangible. Certain high-performance DDR5 desktop memory kits from brands like Patriot have seen spot prices spike by over 1,200 percent, breaching the $1,500 mark—a phenomenon retail buyers are calling "RAMageddon." OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman recently cautioned that the traditional market mechanisms balancing memory supply have essentially broken down, underscoring the risk that ballooning enterprise AI spending will push hardware prices out of reach for regular consumers.

Geopolitics, Clustering, and the Antitrust Front

The global race to supply computing power has forced radical workarounds and drawn regulatory scrutiny. Under severe trade restrictions orchestrated by Washington, China’s Huawei is taking an unconventional engineering path to bypass export controls on advanced silicon. Because its proprietary processors trail Nvidia’s upcoming "Rubin" architecture by an estimated 14-fold in single-chip performance, Huawei is deploying clustering techniques, linking 1,024 chips together to brute-force comparable performance in enterprise AI workloads.

Meanwhile, the market leader is drawing fire on its home turf. The United States Department of Justice has launched an antitrust investigation into Nvidia regarding its acquisition of open-source AI platform Hugging Face. Regulators are examining whether Nvidia used software advantages and hardware bundling to penalize customers that buy chips from rival designers. The probe signals that the global AI arms race has entered a phase where antitrust enforcement and industrial policy will shape commercial outcomes just as much as transistor counts.

At the same time, leading foundry TSMC is entrenching its footprint in the United States, teaming up with Qualcomm and defense contractor Anduril to manufacture advanced chips for military-grade AI notebooks. Setting up operations at this scale is an immense physical undertaking: TSMC’s sprawling Phoenix facility is estimated to consume 4.75 million gallons of water every day. Over in the US memory sector, Micron is tying its executive compensation directly to capex discipline, seeking to avoid the boom-and-bust overproduction that plagued the industry in decades past.

The Domestic Supply Chain and the Cost of Growth

In South Korea, the memory supercycle is having an outsized impact on the domestic ecosystem of "so-bu-jang"—the Korean shorthand for materials, components, and equipment suppliers. These specialized firms have spent years attempting to move up the value chain from captive domestic suppliers to independent global players.

  • Wonik IPS, a leading semiconductor deposition equipment maker, reached a major milestone with cumulative shipments of its flagship GEMINI tool approaching 800 units over a ten-year span.
  • Korea Zinc engaged in high-level discussions with Micron, Intel, and TSMC at the Semicon West trade show in the US to supply ultra-pure semiconductor-grade sulfuric acid, signaling that Korean raw materials suppliers are integrating directly into Western chip foundries.
  • Hanwha Semitek posted a third-quarter operating profit of 37.3 billion won (roughly $28 million), a 115 percent year-over-year surge propelled by strong demand for thermal compression (TC) bonders and wafer-handling systems.

Yet this rapid expansion is exposing severe internal strains. Six labor unions representing workers at tier-one subcontractors for Samsung and SK Hynix recently formed a united front, organizing protests over compensation disparities. Their grievance centers on corporate bonuses: while primary chipmakers hand out six-figure performance incentives to their engineers, the rank-and-file workers at the supplier level see little of the windfall. Without equitable compensation, smaller equipment vendors risk losing specialized engineering talent just as demand peaks.

Physical infrastructure presents an even steeper barrier. Operating next-generation mega-fabs requires an immense supply of electricity and industrial water. The South Korean government and the Financial Services Commission recently approved roughly 1 trillion won ($750 million) via the National Growth Fund to finance solar energy projects in Haenam and build out regional supply-chain infrastructure. Concurrently, regional authorities are intervening directly: municipal leaders from Yongin traveled to the Netherlands to negotiate support with lithography giant ASML, while the city of Pyeongtaek inked an agreement with Samsung to proactively monitor and treat micro-pollutants in fab wastewater.

What to Watch Next

The semiconductor supercycle is no longer moving along predictable historical tracks. Rather than swinging through conventional supply-demand gluts, the industry has fractured into an ultra-premium tier driven by hyperscalers and a squeezed consumer tier. Over the coming months, several key milestones will show how long this dynamic can hold:

  • Samsung’s January 2025 Strategy Session: The market will look for the delayed details on share repurchases and cash returns, alongside progress reports on its HBM shipments to primary AI accelerator clients.
  • Consumer Electronics Price Adjustments: Watch quarterly filings from major consumer tech firms, particularly Apple, for margin compression or retail price increases designed to offset climbing NAND flash and DRAM procurement costs.
  • US Department of Justice Action on Nvidia: Any formal enforcement action or behavioral remedies placed on Nvidia’s software bundling could disrupt the proprietary ecosystem surrounding its CUDA platform and create openings for alternatives.
  • South Korean Infrastructure Deployment: The speed at which power and water grids are permitted and constructed in southern Gyeonggi Province will determine whether domestic foundries can actually bring new fabrication lines online without brownout risks.
