South Korea’s Foreign Policy Dilemma: Can Seoul’s Security Staff Keep Up with President Lee?

November 20, 2026

Imagine walking into a high-stakes summit where the world's traditional titans—the United States, China, and Russia—are visibly fraying at the edges. At the recent Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) leaders' gathering, the diplomatic shadow-boxing among global powers made one thing abundantly clear: the single-superpower era has yielded to a messy, multipolar struggle. For South Korea, sitting squarely at the geopolitical fault line of Northeast Asia, this shifting landscape presents both a perilous precipice and an unprecedented strategic opening.

Yet inside Seoul's foreign policy establishment, a dangerous gap has emerged. While South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has attempted to run as an energetic "pacemaker," pushing to re-engage Washington and reopen doors to peace on the Korean Peninsula, his diplomatic and national security staff appears stuck in neutral. Foreign policy experts in Seoul warn that unless South Korea’s security advisors abandon bureaucratic passivity and start executing aggressive, detailed working-level maneuvers, Seoul risks being passed over once again in global affairs.

The Fracturing Hegemonies of Washington, Beijing, and Moscow

For decades, South Korean foreign policy operated under a simple assumption: Washington set the agenda, and Seoul adjusted its sail accordingly. Today, that playbook is rapidly becoming obsolete. According to Professor Kim Hee-gyo of Kwangwoon University in Seoul, the recent summit dynamics around the APEC gathering highlighted a striking reality: the great powers are increasingly acting like "paper tigers," constrained by severe domestic political crises and regional entanglements.

In Washington, President Donald Trump faces significant domestic headwinds following unexpected midterm election losses and an unresolved, costly conflict with Iran that has drained American strategic energy. While Trump continues to trumpet "America First" rhetoric, his actual bargaining leverage against Beijing and Moscow has noticeably diminished. Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin are actively capitalizing on this strategic vacuum, working to construct a Eurasian order that operates independent of Washington’s dictates.

This erosion of American hegemony does not mean the great powers are harmless—their claws remain sharp and unpredictable. However, as Professor Kim points out, it creates unprecedented strategic space for South Korea. Rather than passively waiting for instructions from Washington or walking on eggshells around Beijing, Seoul has a window to carve out an independent diplomatic trajectory, using the rivalries and vulnerabilities of the great powers to protect its own national interest.

President Lee Jae-myung’s Pace vs. a Hesitant Security Staff

At the center of Seoul’s diplomatic push is President Lee Jae-myung, who has positioned himself as an active catalyst for regional diplomacy. Aiming to break the prolonged deadlock in U.S.-North Korea relations, President Lee has sought to engage President Trump directly, attempting to restart dialogue and rebuild trust on the Korean Peninsula.

However, analysts caution that presidential enthusiasm alone cannot rewrite regional security dynamics. Jeong Se-hyun, a former South Korean Minister of Unification—who historically managed Seoul’s inter-Korean strategy—has issued a sharp critique of the administration’s foreign policy apparatus. Jeong argues that while President Lee is sprinting ahead to clear a path, his national security advisors and diplomatic corps are lagging far behind, failing to fill in the crucial technical details.

"The president is running hard as a pacemaker, but the staff is merely standing by as spectators," Jeong noted, highlighting a persistent disconnect between executive intent and bureaucratic execution.

Jeong stresses that high-level summits are useless without rigorous, behind-the-scenes working-level maneuvers. Before a leader meets a foreign counterpart, security staffers must flood working-level channels—such as White House security aides—with fully drafted, actionable packages. During the presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached and removed from office, South Korea's diplomatic apparatus fell into a pattern of passive deference to Washington, surrendering strategic initiative. Jeong warns that if President Lee’s current security team maintains this overly cautious, risk-averse mindset, the administration’s overtures will end up as empty gestures.

Realpolitik on Pyongyang: Sanctions, Status, and Liaison Offices

To break the deadlock over North Korea’s nuclear program, foreign policy analysts argue that Seoul’s advisors must present Washington with realistic, step-by-step proposals rather than clinging to outdated demands for complete upfront denuclearization.

A key obstacle remains North Korean leader Kim Jong-un’s deep skepticism toward Washington, lingering from the failed 2019 Hanoi summit. Pyongyang has repeatedly stated it will not return to negotiations unless it is recognized as a de facto nuclear weapons state. Former Minister Jeong argues that while South Korea cannot endorse North Korea’s nuclear status, Seoul’s diplomatic team must convince Washington to pragmatically acknowledge current realities on the ground as a starting point for negotiations.

The core of a workable diplomatic package includes:

  • A step-by-step roadmap for sanctions relief: Implementing a principle of simultaneous action, where partial sanctions relief or temporary suspensions are exchanged for verified steps toward denuclearization.
  • Establishing liaison offices: Opening reciprocal diplomatic liaison offices in Washington and Pyongyang to establish permanent communication channels, lower the risk of accidental military conflict, and provide Kim Jong-un with a concrete political win.
  • Direct diplomatic normalization: Framing U.S.-North Korea diplomatic ties as a win-win deal that provides regime security for Pyongyang while handing Washington a historic foreign policy success.

Without such concrete, pre-packaged proposals fed directly to White House aides, any high-level encounter risks devolving into photogenic symbolism devoid of substance.

Beyond the Four Powers: Eurasia and the Middle East

South Korea’s strategic imperatives extend beyond the immediate Korean Peninsula. Hankuk University of Foreign Studies Professor Je Sung-hoon emphasizes the urgent need to repair relations with Moscow. Under the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, relations between Seoul and Moscow plummeted to historic lows after Mr. Yoon hinted at providing direct military aid to Ukraine. This alienated Russia and pushed Moscow into a closer strategic embrace with Pyongyang.

Professor Je argues that the Lee Jae-myung administration must restore functional communication channels with Russia. Maintaining a balanced, dual-track approach—preserving the strong U.S.-South Korea alliance while keeping economic and diplomatic pathways open with Moscow—is essential to prevent Russia from acting as a permanent spoiler in North Korean diplomacy.

Simultaneously, experts like Professor Lee Hee-soo of Sungkonghoe University and former Ambassador Lee Gyeong-ryeol advocate for broadening South Korea’s horizon toward the Middle East and Central Asia. As Washington’s focus is divided by Middle Eastern tensions and internal politics, regional heavyweights like Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar are seeking diverse partnerships.

South Korea’s advanced commercial capabilities in IT, infrastructure, and defense give Seoul tremendous leverage in these regions. By building independent economic and diplomatic partnerships across Eurasia, Central Asia, and the Middle East, South Korea can diminish its over-reliance on traditional great-power rivalries and enhance its overall geopolitical weight.

What to Watch Next

As the international order shifts into a more fragmented, multipolar structure, South Korea's diplomatic survival hinges on pragmatic self-reliance rather than ideological posture. To determine whether Seoul can capitalize on this window, foreign observers should monitor several key indicators in the coming months:

  • Personnel Changes in Seoul’s National Security Office: Watch whether President Lee Jae-myung overhauls his national security team to replace passive bureaucrats with proactive, strategy-driven operatives capable of driving working-level negotiations.
  • Working-Level Proposals to Washington: Look for whether Seoul submits concrete, actionable diplomacy packages—including partial sanctions relief mechanisms and liaison office proposals—to White House working groups ahead of upcoming bilateral summits.
  • Inter-Korean and U.S.-North Korea Signals: Track whether Pyongyang responds to revised diplomatic proposals or maintains its cold stance demanding official nuclear state recognition before returning to talks.
  • Rapprochement with Moscow: Observe whether Seoul takes tangible steps to reopen diplomatic dialogue and economic channels with Russia to counter the growing Moscow-Pyongyang axis.
