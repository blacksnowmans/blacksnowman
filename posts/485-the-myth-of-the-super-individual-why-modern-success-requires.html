The Myth of the Super Individual: Why Modern Success Requires More Than Just Talent

September 09, 2026

Picture a professional cycling race like the Tour de France. You see hundreds of riders bunched together, moving as a single, fluid organism. This formation is called a peloton—a French term for a "little ball" or "clump." To an outsider, it might look like a chaotic scramble, but there is a precise survival mechanism at work: the air pocket.

When a bike moves at high speed, most of the rider's energy is spent fighting wind resistance. By drafting behind the rider in front, cyclists can save up to 40% of their energy. They take turns leading, shielding one another from the wind so that the collective can reach a speed that no single rider could ever sustain alone. Modern organizational success follows the same logic, yet many companies are failing to grasp this, instead betting everything on the "super individual."

The Fallacy of the Solo Hero

We live in an era of AI, big data, and automation tools that allow a single person to handle tasks that once required a department. Because of this, it is tempting to ask: "Are teams and organizations even necessary anymore?"

The reality is that as the problems we face grow more complex, the need for collective intelligence has only intensified. Look at Nobel Prize winners in the sciences; once dominated by individual researchers, almost all modern awards are now shared. Human cognition has a limit—our "working memory" can only juggle about four pieces of information at once. In a business world defined by thousands of real-time, shifting variables, no single person has the vision to see the whole picture. A team isn't just a collection of people; it is a mechanism to expand that field of vision.

However, simply putting people in the same room does not create a team. Too many organizations are just "loose collections of individuals" occupying the same space. They speak about the same goals but visualize them differently. They work side-by-side but move in conflicting directions. To function, a team requires the "rope structure" rather than the "chain structure." A chain is only as strong as its weakest link; if one ring rusts or breaks, the whole thing fails. A rope, however, is composed of countless intertwined threads. If one thread frays, the others hold the structure together, distributing the tension and absorbing shocks.

The Trap of the "Super" Manager

Many high-performing employees eventually face the "Imposter Syndrome" when they are promoted to leadership. They fear their past success was a fluke and worry their every decision could derail the team. This anxiety is common because, in most cases, the transition from a star individual contributor to a leader is not a promotion—it is a total career change.

Consider the professional soccer player who becomes a coach. The coach's job is no longer to score goals; it is to create the environment where the team can score. When a leader sighs and says, "It’s faster if I just do it myself," they aren't being efficient—they are actively dismantling their team’s potential.

The late steel tycoon Andrew Carnegie once famously warned that anyone who tries to do everything themselves or take all the credit will never be a great leader. True leadership is about the courage to surround yourself with people who are smarter than you in specific areas. We saw this play out in the dramatic turnaround of Microsoft. When Satya Nadella took over, he didn't just push for numbers; he shifted the culture from a cutthroat, performance-obsessed environment to one of "coaching and learning." By asking his employees what they wanted to learn rather than just what they could deliver, he triggered a decade of exponential growth.

Psychological Safety: The Foundation of Innovation

Google once conducted an exhaustive internal study—the "Aristotle Project"—to determine why some teams consistently outperformed others. They analyzed everything from personality types to educational backgrounds, but the findings were surprising: the most important factor was not individual genius, but "psychological safety."

A team is not a place where mistakes never happen; it is a place where mistakes do not lead to shame or retaliation. When a junior staffer feels safe pointing out a flaw in a senior executive’s plan, innovation happens. Excellent leaders manage four key pillars: Trust (the foundation of safety), Empathy (valuing the person over the logic), Alignment (connecting individual growth to company vision), and Motivation (igniting the internal fire rather than just using carrots and sticks).

As the legendary football coach Vince Lombardi famously noted, leaders are not born; they are made through the fire of experience. The ultimate legacy of a great leader is not a spreadsheet or a quarterly report—it is the people they leave behind. If those who worked under you have grown into better versions of themselves, you have succeeded.

Building Your "Mileage" for a Changing World

Finally, we must rethink our relationship with time. We often view time as a river that just flows away, leaving us feeling empty at the end of the day. Instead, think of it as "mileage." Even if you aren't taking a major trip today, every small, disciplined act adds to your account.

The writer of the source material for this analysis is a practicing lawyer who spent over a decade writing for two hours every night after putting his children to sleep. That consistent accumulation led to the creation of the hit webtoon and drama Good Partner. We often complain about not having time, yet we squander our "alpha time"—the short, 5-10 minute gaps between meetings or during commutes—on mindless scrolling. If you treat those gaps like a savings account for your growth, you build an unstoppable momentum.

In the age of AI, where fears of job displacement are constant, the only way to stay grounded is to return to the basics. Kazuo Inamori, the founder of Kyocera, argued that the result of one's life is a product of Mindset × Enthusiasm × Ability. If your mindset is cynical or arrogant, even the highest ability will lead to a negative result.

The goal is not to force a massive, overnight change that leads to burnout. It is to find your "sensibility"—a term popularized by former Kakao CEO Jo Soo-yong—which isn't about trend-chasing, but the ability to make wise decisions in the mud of reality by deeply understanding your own values and showing empathy toward others.

What to Watch Next

  • Shift in Performance Metrics: Watch for companies moving away from individual-only KPIs toward team-based, collaborative incentives.
  • The "Learning" Culture: Observe how successful tech firms are prioritizing "psychological safety" over rigid, high-pressure hierarchies.
  • Personal Sustainability: Look for a growing trend in "recovery-focused" management, where employee downtime is treated as a strategic necessity rather than a weakness.
