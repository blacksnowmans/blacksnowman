South Korea’s Conservative Realignment: How Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s Acquittal Reshaped the Political Landscape

October 27, 2026

Inside the Seoul mayor’s official residence, a lighthearted dinner toast quietly signaled the start of a major political realignment. Lee Jun-seok, a prominent lawmaker from the reformist third-party Reform Party, raised his glass to Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, crafting a clever acrostic poem praising the mayor’s ability to weather political storms. Beyond the cordial atmosphere, however, the meeting was a calculated step toward redrawing the map of South Korea’s conservative opposition.

For months, South Korean politics had maintained a predictable baseline under the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and President Lee Jae-myung. The main opposition People Power Party (PPP) remained bogged down by factional infighting and the ongoing fallout from the impeachment and incarceration of Yoon Suk Yeol. But an appellate court’s decision to acquit Mayor Oh of key legal charges has suddenly energized the conservative camp. Cleared of his most pressing legal risks, the veteran mayor has stepped into the spotlight as the central figure around whom conservative forces are beginning to coalesce.

The Acquittal That Unlocked Oh Se-hoon’s Ambitions

In South Korean politics, few local offices carry as much weight as the Seoul mayoralty. Governing a capital city of nearly 10 million residents provides both immense policy visibility and a natural launching pad for the presidency. However, Oh Se-hoon’s political trajectory had long been weighted down by ongoing legal battles. His second-instance acquittal removed those legal uncertainties in one stroke, granting him the political freedom to maneuver beyond municipal governance.

Oh lost no time capitalizing on his legal victory. Moving quickly to expand his political footprint, he began holding a series of dinners with lawmakers across the conservative spectrum. His message has been consistent: while maintaining his focus on Seoul’s municipal administration, he intends to serve as a bridge builder to unite a fractured conservative movement.

Political observers in Seoul are already speculating about a potential triad alliance—dubbed the "Oh-Lee-Han" bloc—referencing Oh Se-hoon, Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party, and Han Dong-hoon, the former PPP leader. Such a coalition would unite traditional conservatives, younger reform-minded voters, and suburban moderates. However, building a lasting partnership among three distinct political brands with competing presidential ambitions will be far from straightforward. Without a shared policy vision, any informal alliance risks being seen as mere electoral arithmetic.

Furthermore, Oh’s rapid rise has stirred controversy within his own party. Rumors are circulating in National Assembly corridors that Oh may be forming a quiet alliance with "under-the-radar" pro-Yoon factions—figures who held influence during Yoon Suk Yeol’s term as president—to field a proxy candidate at the PPP’s upcoming national convention. If Oh relies on discredited party elites to secure organizational backing, he risks damaging his reputation as a pragmatic reformer.

A Polling Reversal in the Capital: Why the Opposition Surged

The political reverberations of Oh’s acquittal coincided with a notable shift in public opinion. Recent nationwide polling shows the opposition People Power Party overtaking the ruling Democratic Party outside the margin of error for the first time in four months. The surge was primarily driven by swing voters in Seoul and the surrounding metropolitan area.

While the PPP saw a noticeable bump in support within Seoul, the ruling DPK experienced a slight decline in neighboring Gyeonggi Province. This shift reflects growing fatigue among moderate voters regarding the ruling party’s legislative dominance, alongside renewed public interest in the opposition’s potential leadership reboot. In South Korean elections, the greater Seoul metropolitan area accounts for nearly half the national population, making swing voters in this region the ultimate bellwether for national political trends.

Key Takeaway: The PPP’s polling rebound reflects growing voter fatigue with the ruling party’s legislative push in the capital region, rather than a full endorsement of the opposition’s internal reforms.

Meanwhile, PPP Chairman Jang Dong-hyeok has managed to retain his post despite persistent pressure from internal rivals to step down, becoming one of the party's longest-serving recent leaders. Supported by core allies, Jang’s leadership team has called for a thorough purge of "legacy politicians" to modernize the party structure. Yet critics within the conservative camp argue that these calls for reform are primarily aimed at marginalizing party dissidents rather than enacting genuine structural change.

The Ruling Party’s Dilemma: Policy Friction and a Missing Counterweight

The opposition's sudden momentum has created new headaches for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Under President Lee Jae-myung, the DPK enjoys a firm legislative majority, but it currently lacks an obvious, high-profile presidential contender who can directly match Oh Se-hoon’s broad public recognition and administrative track record.

Compared to Oh, who commands significant media attention from City Hall, prospective DPK presidential contenders have struggled to build comparable national profiles. As Oh steps up his national messaging, concerns are quietly growing within the ruling party that they may be caught off guard if the opposition successfully unifies behind a single strong candidate.

At the same time, the Lee Jae-myung administration is facing policy pushback on the ground. The government’s recent decision to scrap the Super-Regional Special Zone Act—a legislative initiative originally designed to foster balanced regional growth—has drawn sharp criticism from labor organizations and local civic groups. Demonstrators recently held press conferences criticizing the ruling party for abandoning regional development commitments and ignoring local labor protections.

For a ruling party, managing governance friction while maintaining a strong legislative program is a delicate balancing act. If policy disputes continue to alienate core progressive constituencies while capital-area moderates drift toward the opposition, the DPK could find its political maneuverability increasingly constrained.

Echoes of the Past and Lessons for the Future

The current state of hyper-partisanship in Seoul was highlighted recently when politicians from across the political spectrum gathered at the funeral of the late Choi Hyung-woo, a former Home Affairs Minister and key political lieutenant to former President Kim Young-sam. Attending figures praised Choi as one of the last representatives of an era defined by personal political loyalty and cross-party dialogue.

That sense of nostalgia stands in stark contrast to today's political environment, where party rivalries remain highly polarized. Smaller parties are attempting to carve out distinct identities amid the clash between the two main blocs. For instance, the progressive Cho Guk Innovation Party recently issued a statement citing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s election victory in Brazil, framing it as a warning that voters will ultimately reject extreme right-wing politics.

Looking ahead, the realignment within South Korea's conservative opposition will depend on several key factors:

  • The PPP National Convention: Whether Mayor Oh Se-hoon chooses to back a proxy candidate for the party leadership, and how legacy factions aligned with the former administration respond.
  • The Viability of the "Oh-Lee-Han" Alliance: Whether Oh Se-hoon, Lee Jun-seok, and Han Dong-hoon can translate informal dinners into a workable policy platform that appeals to moderate urban voters.
  • The Ruling Party's Policy Adjustments: How President Lee Jae-myung and the DPK address public pushback over regional development policies and counter the opposition's momentum in the capital region.

Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s legal clearance has re-energized South Korea's conservative opposition, but holding a polling lead is very different from building a coherent political coalition. Whether this momentum leads to genuine institutional reform or devolves into another round of factional bargaining will determine the trajectory of South Korean politics in the months ahead.
