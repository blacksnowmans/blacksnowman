Impeachment Calls and Dissolution Threats: South Korea’s Politics Hits the Boiling Point

November 11, 2026

On a chilly November morning in central Seoul, the quiet promenade in front of Cheong Wa Dae—the presidential office compound nestled against the granite ridge of Mount Bugak—did not look like the seat of normal constitutional governance. Around eighty lawmakers from the conservative People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main opposition party, stood packed shoulder-to-shoulder on the pavement. Clutching protest placards, they shouted slogans into portable sound systems, transforming the street into a makeshift legislative chamber. Their target was a newly passed bill mandating a special counsel to probe alleged politically motivated indictments, an initiative pushed through parliament by the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea. The conservatives derided it as an illicit "prison break" shield designed to protect President Lee Jae-myung, demanding an immediate presidential veto.

Then, the rhetoric took a dangerous leap. Taking the microphone before the crowd, Jang Dong-hyeok, the PPP's senior supreme council member, looked past legislative grievances to hint openly at cutting the president's term short. Promising the conservative base that the country would see "a new president in a new spring," Jang effectively floated the prospect of presidential impeachment. Within hours, the ruling Democratic Party fired back with equal ferocity from inside the National Assembly. Denouncing the opposition’s rhetoric as nothing less than a "second insurrection conspiracy," Democratic Party floor leader Han Byung-do threatened to file a formal petition with the Constitutional Court to dissolve the People Power Party as an unconstitutional political organization. In a single day, the customary friction of South Korean politics collapsed into an existential clash between impeachment and party abolition.

From the Chamber to the Pavement: The Battle Over the Special Counsel

To grasp why eighty conservative lawmakers chose to abandon their desks inside the National Assembly and stage an on-site caucus meeting in the street, one has to examine the deep institutional stalemate gripping Seoul. The Democratic Party holds a dominant legislative majority, leaving the conservative People Power Party heavily outnumbered on the assembly floor. When the ruling party flexes its procedural muscle to pass contested legislation, the minority opposition finds itself virtually powerless inside the chamber.

The immediate spark for the confrontation was the passage of a controversial special counsel bill targeting alleged fabricated indictments. The Democratic Party argues the independent counsel is urgently needed to investigate abuses by prosecutors who pursued politically weaponized cases. The conservative opposition, however, vehemently rejects this rationale. Labeling the legislation the "Lee Jae-myung Prison Break Act," the PPP contends the measure is a brazen effort to nullify past legal scrutiny and grant immunity to the president and his allies.

Having exhausted their options inside the legislature, the conservatives resorted to a classic tactic of South Korean minority parties: taking the fight to the asphalt. By gathering outside Cheong Wa Dae for two consecutive days, the PPP sought to rally its conservative base and exert public pressure directly on the president to exercise his constitutional veto power. Furthermore, the party leadership signaled that the Seoul demonstration was merely the opening salvo of a nationwide tour, threatening to take anti-government rallies to major provincial cities across the peninsula. Yet by moving their party caucus to the pavement, conservative lawmakers also made a calculated gamble, abandoning committee rooms at the exact moment their constitutional duty required them to be inside.

"A New President in a New Spring": The Taboo of Impeachment

South Korean democracy has a visceral relationship with the word "impeachment." The removal of presidents from office—most recently the historic impeachment and removal of Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving a prison sentence—remains a searing memory in the national consciousness. Because presidential impeachment carries profound constitutional and civic trauma, invoking it is considered the ultimate nuclear option in political discourse.

That boundary crumbled when Jang Dong-hyeok stepped onto the stage outside Cheong Wa Dae. His public invocation of "a new president in a new spring" was immediately recognized across the political spectrum as a call for early presidential elections via impeachment. Coming from a senior leader of the main opposition rather than a fringe activist, the phrase ignited immediate fury across the ruling camp.

"Jang Dong-hyeok's talk of impeachment is nothing less than a second insurrection conspiracy. We are witnessing reckless, runaway behavior that seeks to upend the constitutional order."
— Han Byung-do, Floor Leader of the Democratic Party of Korea

The fallout from Jang’s speech was instantaneous. Rather than functioning as a pressure tactic to force concessions on the special counsel bill, the remark framed the opposition’s agenda as an illegitimate attempt to overturn the election outcome. For the conservative party, while the fiery slogan electrified hardcore partisans who yearn to regain power, it dealt a devastating blow to the party’s institutional standing, painting it into a corner as a faction unwilling to accept regular democratic tenure.

The Counterstrike: Threatening Party Dissolution

The Democratic Party’s reaction went far beyond typical parliamentary pushback. Convening an emergency general assembly of its own lawmakers at the National Assembly, the ruling party treated the conservative rhetoric as an attack on state authority. Floor leader Han Byung-do unleashed a blistering broadside, characterizing the opposition's actions as the conduct of an unhinged political force operating outside constitutional bounds.

More significantly, the Democratic Party raised the ultimate weapon available under South Korea's constitution: petitioning for the forced dissolution of an unconstitutional party. In South Korean legal history, the Constitutional Court has exercised this power only in extraordinary circumstances, most notably in 2014 when it banned the far-left Unified Progressive Party over allegations of pro-North Korean insurrectionary activity. By suggesting that the conservative People Power Party could face a similar fate, the ruling party signaled that it views rhetoric challenging the presidency as a subversive act.

Democratic leaders demanded that conservative leadership kneel and make a public apology to the nation, insisting that lawmakers on the parliamentary Legislation and Judiciary Committee immediately end their street protests and return to their duties. Yet by matching the opposition's impeachment talk with threats of formal party eradication, the ruling party also raised the stakes to an unsustainable level. When both sides treat the other not as political rivals to be negotiated with, but as criminal entities that must be removed from public life, democratic compromise becomes virtually impossible.

Governance Frozen: The Human Cost of Legislative Paralysis

The theatrical fury playing out between Cheong Wa Dae and Yeouido—Seoul’s legislative island—carries severe real-world consequences. This confrontation unfolded right in the middle of the regular parliamentary budget session, the single most critical period on the legislative calendar.

Under ordinary circumstances, November is when lawmakers pore over state spending line by line. The National Assembly’s Special Committee on Budget and Accounts, alongside standing committees like the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, is tasked with scrutinizing hundreds of billions of dollars in public expenditures for the coming fiscal year. Instead, committee chairs looked out at empty rows of desks as conservative members boycotted hearings to wave signs in the cold.

This shutdown comes at a perilous moment for ordinary South Koreans. Persistent inflation, high interest rates, and sluggish domestic consumption have placed immense pressure on small business owners, working families, and young people struggling to secure stable employment. Welfare allocations, small-business relief measures, and vital economic stimulus packages are all tied to the timely passage of the national budget. By turning the budget session into an ideological battlefield over presidential survival and legal immunity, both parties have effectively held the national economy hostage to their factional warfare.

What to Watch Next

With channels of communication between the two major parties completely severed, South Korea faces several pivotal milestones that will determine whether the political system can step back from the edge:

  • The Presidential Decision on the Special Counsel: The immediate catalyst remains the bill mandating a special counsel for alleged fabricated indictments. Observers will be watching whether President Lee Jae-myung signs the legislation into law or attempts to de-escalate tensions, and how the conservative opposition responds once that decision is finalized.
  • The Threat of Nationwide Rallies: The People Power Party has pledged to expand its Cheong Wa Dae protests into a traveling campaign across provincial metropolitan centers. If conservative lawmakers take to the streets nationwide, parliamentary committee work will remain hobbled indefinitely, likely provoking counter-mobilizations from progressive civic groups.
  • The Budget Deadline Crunch: South Korea faces a constitutional deadline in early December to finalize the annual government budget. If the deadlock persists through late November, the country risks unprecedented fiscal chaos, which would intensify public backlash against both the ruling party’s hardline stance and the opposition’s absenteeism.
  • Follow-Through on Party Dissolution: While the Democratic Party’s mention of dissolving the People Power Party may serve as political deterrence, any concrete administrative step toward requesting a review by the Ministry of Justice would trigger an unprecedented constitutional crisis, permanently altering the rules of engagement in South Korean politics.

When politics is reduced to an arena where one side threatens early presidential removal and the other threatens the legal annihilation of the opposition, parliamentary democracy ceases to function. For South Korea, the coming weeks will test whether institutional checks and public exhaustion can force its leaders off the streets and back into the rooms where the country's actual work gets done.
