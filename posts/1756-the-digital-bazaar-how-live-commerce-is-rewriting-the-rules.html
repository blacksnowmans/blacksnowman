The Digital Bazaar: How Live Commerce is Rewriting the Rules of Korean Retail

October 31, 2026

Imagine it is 8:00 PM on a Wednesday in Seoul. Millions of office workers are finishing their commutes, eyes glued to their smartphones. But they aren't just scrolling through social media or catching up on news; they are participating in a high-octane, interactive shopping experience that looks more like a K-pop concert than a retail transaction. On one screen, a rookie girl group performs their latest single while showcasing the sleek hinge of a new foldable phone. On another, an AI-generated host—indistinguishable from a human at first glance—answers technical questions about air purifiers in real-time. This is the current state of South Korean "Live Commerce," a sector that has evolved from a pandemic-era necessity into a sophisticated multi-billion dollar pillar of the national economy.

While the rest of the world often views live-stream shopping as a niche segment dominated by influencers, in South Korea, it has become the primary battlefield for the country’s largest tech giants, manufacturing conglomerates, and even traditional neighborhood markets. The latest data and corporate moves from late October 2024 reveal a market that is no longer just about selling products at a discount. It is about "commercetainment"—the seamless blending of entertainment, artificial intelligence, and global logistics into a single, frictionless mobile experience.

Where K-Pop Meets the Cutting Edge of Mobile Hardware

The most visible manifestation of this trend occurred recently on Naver Shopping Live, the live commerce arm of Naver (South Korea’s dominant search engine and web portal, often described as a hybrid of Google and Amazon). In a broadcast titled "Galaxy Night," Samsung Electronics partnered with Naver to promote the Galaxy Z Fold 8. To capture the attention of Gen Z and Millennial consumers, they didn't hire a traditional salesperson. Instead, they brought in RESCENE, a rising K-pop girl group.

The broadcast was a masterclass in modern marketing. It wasn't a static product demonstration; it was a mini-concert where the performers used the phone’s specific features—like its high-resolution cameras and foldable screen—as part of their choreography and fan interaction. This approach addresses a growing problem in the tech industry: "spec fatigue." Consumers are increasingly indifferent to incremental hardware upgrades. By wrapping the technology in the emotional "fandom" of K-pop, Samsung and Naver transformed a $1,500 smartphone into a piece of cultural memorabilia.

This "commercetainment" model is crucial because it keeps viewers on the platform. In the world of mobile retail, the most valuable currency is "dwell time." The longer a user watches, the more likely they are to engage with the chat, participate in polls, and eventually hit the "buy" button. When the line between a music show and a shopping channel disappears, the friction of the sales process vanishes with it.

The Rise of the Machine: AI Hosts and the Efficiency Race

While Naver is leaning into human stardom, 11th Street (one of South Korea’s major e-commerce platforms, owned by SK Square) is taking the opposite approach. As it kicked off its "Grand 11st" event—the Korean equivalent of Black Friday or Alibaba’s Singles' Day—the platform introduced its first AI-generated show host.

The introduction of AI hosts is a calculated move to solve the "scalability" problem of live commerce. Human hosts are expensive, they get tired, and they can only be in one place at a time. An AI host, powered by advanced large language models and realistic avatars, can broadcast 24 hours a day, answer thousands of customer queries simultaneously, and never miss a product detail. During the 11th Street event, the AI host managed the flow of a game-based shopping program, maintaining a high level of energy that would be difficult for a human to sustain over a multi-day festival.

However, this shift is not without controversy. There is a palpable tension in the industry regarding the "human touch." While AI is excellent at delivering facts and managing logistics, it lacks the spontaneous humor and emotional empathy that builds long-term trust with an audience. Critics argue that if live commerce becomes too automated, it risks losing the "live" element that made it successful in the first place, turning back into a glorified digital vending machine. For now, the Korean market seems to be betting on a hybrid future: humans for high-profile brand launches, and AI for high-volume, information-heavy sales.

K-Beauty’s Global Jump: From Seoul to the World

The success of live commerce is no longer confined to the Korean peninsula. The recent earnings report from APR, a leading Korean beauty and tech firm known for its Medicube and Age-R brands, offers a staggering look at the power of digital-first retail. APR reported that its cumulative revenue for the first three quarters of 2024 surpassed 2 trillion won (approximately $1.45 billion), with operating profits reaching about $116 million (160.6 billion won).

The most telling statistic in APR’s report is that 94% of its revenue now comes from overseas markets. This was achieved largely by bypassing traditional retail distributors and going straight to consumers via live platforms like TikTok Shop and Instagram Live in North America and Europe. By showing live, unedited demonstrations of their beauty devices—showing "real-time" results on skin texture—they have built a global brand in a fraction of the time it took traditional legacy brands.

Similarly, Pond Group and other fashion-beauty conglomerates are leveraging "Wanghongs" (Chinese influencers) to tap into the massive mainland market. A single broadcast with a top-tier Chinese influencer recently generated over $1.26 million (8.99 million CNY) in sales for Korean fashion items in a matter of hours. This demonstrates that live commerce is perhaps South Korea’s most effective export tool in the post-pandemic era, allowing even medium-sized companies to access global markets without the need for physical storefronts abroad.

The Invisible Hand: Logistics and Last-Mile Innovation

Behind the glitz of K-pop stars and AI avatars lies the cold reality of logistics. A sale made on a live stream is only successful if the product arrives at the customer's door. This is why Shinsegae Live Shopping, the digital arm of the Shinsegae Group (one of Korea’s oldest and largest retail giants, which also owns high-end department stores), recently signed a long-term strategic partnership with CJ Logistics.

CJ Logistics is the dominant player in the Korean delivery market, and this partnership is aimed at perfecting "last-mile delivery." In the Korean market, where "next-day" delivery is the standard and "same-day" delivery is the goal, the integration of live commerce platforms with smart fulfillment centers is a competitive necessity. By using data-driven AI systems to predict which items will sell during a live broadcast, these companies can pre-position stock in local warehouses, ensuring that a sweater purchased at 9:00 PM is on the customer’s doorstep by 7:00 AM the next day.

This logistical prowess also allows live commerce to expand into "high-involvement" goods—products that people usually want to see in person before buying. Hankook Tire, the country’s largest tire manufacturer, recently held a "Brand Day" on Naver, offering discounts of up to 51%. To overcome the hurdle of buying tires online, they bundled the sale with a "winter tire storage service" and expert consultations via chat. By solving the logistical headache of where to put your old tires, they used live commerce to digitize a segment of the auto industry that had remained stubbornly analog.

The Digital Lifeline for Traditional Markets

While the headlines are often dominated by billion-dollar tech firms, some of the most impactful live commerce news comes from the "grassroots" level. In Hongcheon, a rural county in Gangwon Province, the local Hongcheon Central Market recently held its own live commerce event. They sold five types of traditional local specialties, including handmade dumplings and buckwheat pancakes.

In a surprising turn, the event was a total sell-out. For traditional market vendors—many of whom are in their 60s and 70s—this technology is more than a novelty; it is a lifeline. As Korea’s population ages and rural areas face economic decline, live commerce allows these local artisans to reach a national audience. The "authenticity" of a grandmother making dumplings in a traditional market proved to be just as compelling to viewers as a K-pop performance. This suggests that the future of the market isn't just about high-tech AI, but also about "high-touch" human connection.

"The essence of live commerce is not the platform, but the trust. Whether it is a global tech brand or a local food stall, the camera doesn't lie. Customers are looking for a reason to believe in the product before they believe in the price."

What to Watch Next

As we move into the final months of 2024, the South Korean live commerce market is entering a new phase of maturity. Here are the three key developments to monitor:

  • The Convergence of Search and Shop: Watch how Naver and YouTube (which is aggressively expanding its "YouTube Shopping" feature in Korea) battle for dominance. The winner will be the one who can best integrate video content with a one-click checkout system.
  • Regulatory Scrutiny: As AI hosts become more common, expect the Fair Trade Commission (Korea's antitrust and consumer protection agency) to introduce new guidelines regarding "deepfake" transparency and AI-driven consumer manipulation.
  • The Global "K-Platform" Push: Keep an eye on whether Korean platforms themselves—not just the brands—start to expand into Southeast Asia and Japan to compete directly with TikTok Shop and Amazon.

Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, there has been a significant push toward "digital sovereignty" and supporting the digital transformation of small businesses. As the government continues to invest in the country's 5G and AI infrastructure, the barrier to entry for live commerce will continue to drop, making it the default mode of retail for the next generation of Korean consumers.
