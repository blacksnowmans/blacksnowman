South Korea’s Ruling Party Fast-Tracks Housing, Farmland, and Local Budget Reforms in Year-End Legislative Sprint

October 31, 2026

As the dust settles on this year’s National Audit—the annual parliamentary deep-dive into executive performance—the legislative halls of Yeouido in Seoul are shifting from confrontation to policy execution. With the regular session of the National Assembly drawing toward its end-of-year deadline, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP) is launching a coordinated legislative sprint. The party’s strategy focuses on transforming headline promises into binding statutory realities, targeting high-stakes policy bottlenecks in housing supply, social safety nets, agricultural land regulation, and local government finance.

For international observers watching South Korea's political landscape, this legislative push is a critical test of governance under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. Having moved beyond the political turbulence that led to the impeachment and imprisonment of former President Yoon Suk-yeol, the DP is seeking to cement its credential as a pragmatic governing party. By prioritizing practical bills over ideological rhetoric, the ruling party aims to address persistent structural problems that directly impact the daily lives of Korean citizens.

Pushing 76,700 New Homes to Tame the Seoul Housing Market

At the top of the DP’s legislative agenda is a fast-track plan to stabilize housing in the heavily congested Seoul Metropolitan Area (SMA), home to roughly half of South Korea’s 51 million residents. The core of the blueprint involves designating new residential sites capable of supplying 76,700 housing units. To ensure these plans do not become bogged down in years of administrative inertia, DP leadership—led by figures such as Representative Han Byung-do—is committing to clear the necessary legal pathways before the end of the year.

A primary bottleneck in South Korean urban development is the agonizing delay between initial site designation and actual groundbreaking. Regulatory hurdles, protracted environmental reviews, and inadequate transport planning have historically left homebuyers waiting years for promised housing stock. To bypass these delays, the DP is pushing amendments to the Act on the Maintenance and Improvement of Urban Areas. The goal is to streamline redevelopment procedures while simultaneously embedding liveability standards into high-density construction.

These housing bills are noteworthy for their focus on urban quality of life alongside sheer volume:

  • Density incentives for urban green space: Representative Lee Kwang-hee has proposed an amendment to urban renewal laws that grants developers a floor area ratio (FAR) bonus of up to 120% if they allocate at least 10% more land area to public parks and green spaces. This addresses the long-standing critique that Korean redevelopment yields concrete deserts.
  • Infrastructure-first urban planning: Addressing the chronic issue of "commuter hell" in suburban satellite cities, Representative Kim Joo-young (representing Gimpo Gap) is demanding that major transit projects precede housing completion. Specifically, the party is urging an exemption from long-form preliminary feasibility studies for the Seoul Subway Line 5 extension to support the newly designated Gimpo Ara residential development.

In response to the DP’s housing offensive, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, has proposed creating a joint "3+3 Real Estate Consultative Body." The PPP seeks to include discussions on scaling back the Reconstruction Excess Profit Recapture System—a tax designed to skim off windfall gains from property redevelopment—alongside mortgage deregulation and lease market fixes. While the ruling party has expressed willingness to talk, DP leaders remain wary that broad financial deregulation could re-ignite speculative buying, insisting that public policy must prioritize low-income, non-homeowning families.

A 10-Point Agenda to Patch Gaps in Daily Public Safety

Beyond macroeconomic housing goals, the Democratic Party has isolated a targeted slate of "10 Core Livelihood Bills" designed to address legal blind spots highlighted during the recent parliamentary audits. These measures focus on emerging social threats and daily quality-of-life concerns that have outpaced existing statutory frameworks.

Among these, strengthening legal protections against intimate partner violence (often referred to locally as "dating violence") and modernizing anti-fraud legislation to combat sophisticated voice-phishing rings have taken center stage. South Korean public outcry has grown sharply over instances where law enforcement lacked explicit statutory tools to issue restraining orders or protect victims prior to formal criminal indictments. The DP's proposed framework aims to lower the legal threshold for intervention and broaden emergency protection measures.

The 10-point package also extends to granular, community-level issues and public safety infrastructure:

  • Targeted transit and veteran support: Representative Lee Keon-tae has launched a task force to secure a stop for the KTX-Eum high-speed train at Sosa Station in Bucheon, a crucial transit link for commuters west of Seoul. Meanwhile, Representative Shin Jeong-hun introduced amendments to the Discharged Veterans Support Act to institutionalize job transition programs and social welfare benefits for retired military personnel.
  • Climate-resilient disaster prevention: In response to escalating super-wildfires and flash flooding brought on by global climate shifts, Representative Seo Sam-seok sponsored legislation mandating legally binding performance standards and dedicated budget allocations for advanced forestry protection equipment.

Overhauling Aging Farmland Laws to Protect Rural Economies

In one of its most radical regulatory shifts, the DP is aiming to pass the Special Act on Farmland before the year closes. This legislation seeks to legalize and formalize long-standing, informal agricultural leasing practices that exist across South Korea’s rural hinterlands.

The root of the issue lies in Article 121 of South Korea’s Constitution, which enshrines the principle of "land to the tiller"—stipulating that only active farmers should own agricultural land. However, decades of rapid industrialization and extreme demographic aging have created a stark mismatch between constitutional intent and rural reality. Elderly landowners who can no longer physically farm often lease their land informally to younger tenant farmers without registering the contracts, fearing administrative penalties or loss of land ownership status.

This informal system leaves tenant farmers in legal limbo. Without official lease agreements, actual cultivators are barred from accessing state agricultural subsidies, crop insurance, and modernization grants. The situation deteriorated further during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, when rigid administrative enforcement penalized actual farm operators while failing to resolve the underlying structural shifts in rural demographics.

The proposed Special Act provides a legal mechanism to register and normalize these customary leases. By bringing informal arrangements into the regulatory fold, the DP aims to ensure that actual working farmers receive legal protections and direct state income supports, establishing a pragmatic foundation for South Korea's agricultural supply chain.

A $1.5 Billion Local Budget Shortfall and the Battle for Regional Growth

The legislative debate is equally intense over local government funding. Representative Choo Mi-ae, a senior DP legislator, recently highlighted projections showing that municipal disposable budgets across South Korea are set to contract by roughly 2 trillion KRW (approximately $1.5 billion USD) next year. This fiscal contraction threatens local infrastructure projects, regional healthcare funding, and municipal welfare programs.

DP leadership places the blame for this shortfall squarely on the tax policy legacies of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, arguing that major corporate tax cuts combined with repeated national tax revenue miscalculations starved regional governments of their statutory transfers. When central tax receipts fell short, state grants to provinces and municipalities were slashed accordingly, forcing local mayors and governors to freeze local economic initiatives.

"Municipal budget collapses directly impair local safety nets and delay vital regional infrastructure," Representative Choo noted during legislative committee discussions, calling for a fundamental restructuring of the national-to-local tax distribution ratio.

To mitigate the crisis, the DP is intensifying direct coordination meetings between its legislative leaders and municipal governments. In Wonju, Gangwon Province, the party held its second joint consultation to realign provincial budget allocations. In Yongin, Gyeonggi Province, Representative Lee Un-ju is collaborating with the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport to expedite preliminary feasibility assessments for the Dongbaek-Sinbong and Eonnam-Dongcheon urban transit lines, aiming to complete economic reviews by mid-2027 to ensure funding continuity.

What to Watch Next in Yeouido

As the National Assembly enters the final stretch of its regular session, several key benchmarks will indicate whether the Democratic Party can deliver on its legislative agenda:

  • The December Urban Renewal Vote: Watch whether amendments to the Act on the Maintenance and Improvement of Urban Areas pass the floor without major dilution, particularly regarding floor area ratio (FAR) incentives and expedited review timelines.
  • Negotiation on the "3+3" Real Estate Framework: Pay attention to how the DP handles opposition demands from the PPP regarding the Reconstruction Excess Profit Recapture System and mortgage rule adjustments.
  • Passage of the Special Act on Farmland: The bill’s trajectory through the Agriculture Committee will demonstrate whether the ruling party can overcome conservative constitutional objections regarding land ownership to enact practical rural reforms.
  • Local Tax Allocation Debates: Track whether the DP successfully levers its parliamentary majority to alter tax transfer formulas, offsetting the projected 2 trillion KRW ($1.5 billion) drop in regional disposable budgets for the 2025 fiscal year.
