Why South Korea’s Ruling Progressives Are Stalling in the Polls

November 16, 2026

On paper, President Lee Jae-myung should be riding a wave of steady domestic tailwinds. South Korea’s semiconductor exports are surging, and the administration recently rolled out ambitious multi-billion-dollar investment packages aimed at revitalizing the central Chungcheong provinces. Yet when the latest political surveys landed this week, the numbers told a remarkably different story. Instead of a post-announcement bounce, Lee’s approval rating remained firmly anchored in the mid-30s, while his governing Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) fell behind the conservative opposition outside the statistical margin of error.

For observers looking at South Korea from the outside, the disconnect between macroeconomic indicators and political popularity can seem puzzling. But beneath the surface lies a compounding mix of voter fatigue, persistent cost-of-living anxieties, and sharp internal fractures across the progressive base. Rather than an erratic blip, the polling shift provides a window into how quickly public patience wears thin when structural reforms stall and everyday household budgets remain under pressure.

The Box-Pattern Dilemma: Headline Gains Swallowed by Kitchen-Table Strains

According to the latest tracking data from Realmeter, a prominent Seoul-based polling agency, positive assessments of President Lee’s performance ticked up by just 0.5 percentage points to 36.4%. In statistical terms, this movement is negligible. South Korean political analysts have described the administration's support as trapped in a tight "box pattern"—fluctuating within a narrow range from week to week without finding any genuine upward momentum.

The core problem facing the presidential office is that tangible successes are routinely drowned out by political friction. High-profile announcements regarding regional semiconductor clusters and tech investments have been overshadowed by controversies over real estate taxation adjustments, a high-stakes legislative standoff concerning a proposed special counsel investigation into alleged prosecutorial abuse, persistent volatility across domestic equity markets, and lingering internal party friction tied to the controversial Shincheonji religious group. Every positive headline is neutralized almost immediately by an unresolved dispute.

More importantly, ordinary citizens evaluate the administration through their grocery receipts and investment balances rather than export tables. Booming outbound shipments of microchips do little to ease the anxieties of salaried workers facing stubborn food prices and an unsteady stock market. As long as broader economic growth fails to trickle down into visible improvements for everyday livelihoods, public approval is unlikely to break out of its current slump.

The polling methodology itself sheds light on this growing unease. In interviewer-administered telephone surveys—where politically engaged respondents are more prone to answer systematically—Lee’s rating climbed 2.9 percentage points to 47.0%. However, in automated response system (ARS) robocalls, which often capture raw, unfiltered sentiment, approval fell 1.5 points to 41.9%. Driving that ARS drop was a startling 10.1 percentage point plunge in the southwestern Honam region. As the historic geographical heartland of South Korean liberalism, a double-digit decline in Honam is an urgent warning for the DPK. Local frustration has been mounting over delays in pledged regional mega-projects, demonstrating that even the administration's most loyal constituents are growing impatient with administrative inertia.

An 8.4-Point Reversal: Opposition Gains and the Progressive Fracture

The warning bells rang even louder in the party ratings. Realmeter recorded the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), climbing 3.3 percentage points to 43.0%, while the ruling DPK slid 1.9 points to 34.6%. In just three weeks, an 8.4 percentage point gap opened between the two rivals—placing the conservative opposition comfortably ahead beyond the margin of error.

This conservative rebound is not necessarily a reflection of surging public enthusiasm for the PPP's policy agenda. Instead, it reflects two distinct dynamics: anticipation within conservative circles over the potential return of lawmaker Han Dong-hoon to active party leadership, and widespread disappointment with how the DPK has managed its legislative supermajority. Han's prospective return has galvanized a fragmented conservative base eager for a formidable standard-bearer. At the same time, moderate swing voters have grown weary of the DPK’s confrontational posture on legislative priorities and special counsel probes, particularly when paired with a lack of decisive solutions on livelihood issues.

Crucially, voters abandoning the ruling party are not all defecting to the conservatives. Many are migrating further along the progressive spectrum. The Rebuilding Korea Party—a staunchly progressive outfit led by Shin Jang-sik and floor leader Kim Joon-hyung—surged to 8.7% in the same poll, its highest level in recent months. Disillusioned by what they view as the DPK’s hesitation to aggressively dismantle entrenched judicial cartels and advance prosecutorial reforms, hardline progressive voters are shifting their loyalty to an alternative that promises uncompromising resistance.

"When a governing party controls the legislative agenda but appears hesitant to execute its core promises, its base does not simply stay home—it searches for clearer, more assertive alternatives."

By attempting to moderate its tone to appeal to centrist voters while simultaneously failing to deliver decisive bread-and-butter economic relief, the DPK finds itself caught in an awkward middle ground: alienating the center through political gridlock while losing its committed progressive flank to bolder competitors.

Widespread Cynicism: Plummeting Leader Approval and Faux Favorability

The sense of political exhaustion is by no means confined to the ruling camp. A survey conducted by Kuki News reveals that voters are deeply disenchanted with the leadership of both major parties. Jang Dong-hyeok, head of the conservative PPP, registered a meager 31.5% job approval rating, against 61.5% negative reviews, as he struggles to contain internal factional infighting and manage thorny readmission questions.

The figures for DPK leader Kim Min-seok were even more severe. Kim garnered only 23.7% positive evaluations, while his disapproval rating soared to 67.4%. Leading a dominant parliamentary majority without setting a coherent national agenda has left Kim exposed to criticism from both opponents and supporters alike. When leaders on both sides of the aisle face negative ratings exceeding 60%, it illustrates a broader crisis of faith in the National Assembly, where partisan conflict has largely supplanted policy debate.

This climate of mutual distrust also distorts headline favorability measures. In recent polling by Gallup Korea, favorability toward the DPK dropped from 44% in July to 38%, while the PPP rose slightly from 22% to 26%. While conservative commentators might interpret this as a sign of renewal, the PPP's 26% rating still means that nearly three out of four South Koreans view the party unfavorably. Analysts note that much of the conservative uptick reflects sampling distortion: demoralized progressive voters are simply hanging up on pollsters or declining to identify with the ruling party, inadvertently inflating the relative weight of conservative responses. Both camps remain locked in a system of negative partisanship, surviving primarily on the missteps of the other.

The Neoliberal Reflex: Conflicted Public Views on Taxes and Market Regulation

Beneath the daily partisan squabbles lie deeply embedded, often paradoxical attitudes toward the state's role in the economy. When asked broadly about financial authorities regulating domestic stock markets, an overwhelming 65.7% of respondents stated that government intervention should be kept to an absolute minimum. This sentiment persists even as retail stock traders consistently demand fierce government crackdowns on unfair trading, stock manipulation, and predatory short-selling by institutional and foreign funds.

A similar ideological contradiction characterizes the debate surrounding the Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (known domestically as Jongbuse). In the latest survey, 48.5% of respondents supported easing the property tax, narrowly edging out the 47.0% who opposed cuts. What makes this split striking is that the wealth tax actually applies to only about 3% of the nation's wealthiest property owners.

Why would nearly half the country advocate for slashing a tax that 97% of citizens will never pay? Decades of persistent media campaigns framing property taxation as an aggressive "tax bomb," combined with widespread aspirations of upward real estate mobility, have cultivated an instinctive skepticism toward property-related levies. The prevailing sentiment treats public regulation not as a tool for wealth redistribution or social security funding, but as an infringement on private assets. For the Lee administration, which relies on state-led initiatives to finance regional investments and social safety nets, this structural public skepticism represents a formidable communications barrier.

What to Watch Next

The current polling slump leaves the government and the DPK facing critical choices heading into the coming legislative season. To reverse their slide, policymakers will have to navigate several brewing pressure points:

  • Judicial Showdowns and Trial Schedules: Public scrutiny of the judiciary is intensifying, marked by mounting controversy over the Supreme Court's delays in concluding the insurrection trial of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, alongside simmering public discussions regarding the potential impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. How the DPK navigates these judicial flashpoints without appearing entirely consumed by procedural battles will dictate whether it can win back progressive defectors.
  • Special Counsel Legislation: The protracted legislative conflict over establishing a special counsel to probe alleged prosecutorial fabrications has gridlocked parliament. The ruling coalition must decide whether to force the measure through or risk further alienating both moderates exhausted by conflict and hardliners demanding swift action.
  • Delivery on Regional Capital Outlays: With approval dropping by double digits in traditional strongholds like Honam, the administration must show concrete progress on its promised regional infrastructure and industrial projects. Blueprints and signing ceremonies will no longer suffice; voters expect visible construction and local job creation.
  • Conservative Consolidation: Observers will be closely watching whether the PPP can translate its poll lead into an enduring coalition, particularly if lawmaker Han Dong-hoon formally rejoins the fold to challenge Jang Dong-hyeok's fragile leadership structure.

The latest polling is less a mandate for the conservative opposition than an urgent wake-up call for the Lee administration. When abstract macro growth fails to reach the dinner table and judicial reforms languish in procedural wrangling, voter patience evaporates quickly. Whether the ruling camp responds with defensive retrenchment or decisive policy execution will determine if its current polling box becomes a temporary trough or a permanent ceiling.
