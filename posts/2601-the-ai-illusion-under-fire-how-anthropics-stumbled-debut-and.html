The AI Illusion Under Fire: How Anthropic’s Stumbled Debut and Nvidia’s SEC Crisis Shattered Global Markets

November 22, 2026

For months, global equity markets had been searching for a definitive catalyst to reignite the multi-trillion-dollar artificial intelligence rally. That catalyst was supposed to be the long-awaited public debut of Anthropic. Entering the Nasdaq with a record-setting offering price of $83 per share, the AI research giant briefly surged 17% during its first trading session, vaulting into the trillion-dollar market capitalization tier and making instant multi-billionaires out of its seven co-founders. To many observers, Silicon Valley’s capital engine seemed as invincible as ever.

Yet within forty-eight hours, the triumphant atmosphere dissolved into a punishing market route. Anthropic’s stock reversed course on its second day, sinking 8.5% and erasing $109 billion in market value as underwriting support waned and investors confronted a stark reality: the vast liquidity raised was largely flowing straight into debt payments, data center lease agreements, and hardware purchases rather than sustainable corporate profits. Before Wall Street could digest the disappointment, a far larger shockwave hit the tape. Reports revealed that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) had initiated a formal accounting investigation into Nvidia, sending the graphics processing unit powerhouse into a massive single-day tailspin.

By the closing bell, the Nasdaq composite had plummeted 5.3%, wiping out roughly $4 trillion in valuation and completely erasing its year-to-date gains. Across the Pacific, the panic reverberated instantly through South Korean markets, dragging the benchmark Kospi index below the 5,000 threshold and trapping retail investors in a devastating wave of paper losses. What began as a celebration of AI capital raising has swiftly turned into a rigorous, global re-evaluation of tech valuations and circular financial engineering.

Inside Anthropic’s Debut: Giant Valuation Meets Financial Reality

On paper, Anthropic’s public launch had all the makings of a landmark financial event. By securing an offering price of $83 per share, the company raised between 110 trillion and 161 trillion won (approximately $80 billion to $118 billion), cementing the debut as the largest initial public offering in tech history, surpassing previous private-market benchmarks set by SpaceX. The initial surge created astronomical paper wealth, elevating the firm's seven co-founders into a combined asset bracket exceeding 240 trillion won (about $175 billion), while total employee stock compensation rights reached 130 billion won (about $95 million).

However, the euphoria was short-lived. By the second day of trading, Anthropic’s shares experienced intense downward pressure, swinging wildly by more than 11% intraday and nearing its initial $83 subscription price before closing down 8.5%. The rapid retreat pushed the company down to twelfth place in global market capitalization rankings, shedding $109 billion in value in a matter of hours.

Underneath the short-term trading volatility lies a fundamental skepticism regarding Anthropic's long-term business model. Market analysts pointed out that a substantial portion of the record capital raised was earmarked not for breakthrough internal research and development, but rather to settle existing corporate debts, fund expensive data center leases, and purchase high-performance chips. This structural loop—where investor capital immediately leaves the balance sheet to pay infrastructure providers—highlighted the financial strain of operating frontier AI models. Compounding the anxiety was Apple’s ongoing push to build its own independent artificial intelligence ecosystem, fueling doubts over whether standalone AI model creators can preserve a defensible economic moat.

Circular Finance and the SEC Probe: Nvidia’s 14% Collapse

If Anthropic’s debut revealed the underlying margin pressures facing AI software developers, Nvidia’s sudden collapse exposed vulnerabilities at the hardware core of the entire sector. News that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission had opened an accounting investigation into Nvidia sent shockwaves through the tech ecosystem, triggering a single-day 14% crash in the company's stock.

According to reports, the SEC inquiry focuses on potential accounting misstatements stemming from "AI circular financing." Regulators are scrutinizing whether Nvidia engaged in arrangements where it invested capital into early-stage AI startups, which then turned around and used those funds to purchase Nvidia's specialized graphics processing units (GPUs). Critics argue this mechanism creates an illusion of artificial top-line revenue growth, effectively recycling corporate cash to inflate hardware demand figures.

The investigation comes at an exceptionally fragile moment for Nvidia. The company was already navigating leadership instability following the high-profile departure of longtime Chief Executive Officer Jensen Huang. While interim CEO Colette Kress publicly pledged that the company would institute significantly stricter internal audit procedures for future startup investments and corporate debt guarantees, her statements failed to calm institutional investors. Nvidia’s stock slid to $136 per share—its lowest level in 17 months and down nearly 50% from its all-time high—knocking the firm down to fourth place in market cap rankings amidst warnings from Wall Street that shares could slide below $100.

A $4 Trillion Wipeout and the 58% Bubble Verdict

The simultaneous blow of Anthropic’s volatility and Nvidia’s legal troubles quickly metastasized into a broad market sell-off. The 5.3% drop in the Nasdaq composite wiped out $4 trillion in market value in a single trading session, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index experienced an intraday decline ranging from 5.3% up to 10%. The flight from risk spilled over into alternative asset classes, with digital assets like XRP dropping 8% as institutional money sought safety.

Quantitative risk models monitoring tech sector valuations responded rapidly. AI predictive analytics models bumped the probability of a full-scale "AI bubble burst" to 58%, up dramatically from previous estimates. The dramatic shift underscores a deepening divide among Wall Street institutions.

One side of the debate argues that the market is finally confronting a structural flaw: the chronic absence of net profitability across the AI landscape relative to the hundreds of billions being spent on capital expenditures. The opposing view holds that the current crash is an overreaction—a momentary liquidity spasm triggered by temporary regulatory headlines rather than an end to the tech expansion cycle. Nevertheless, with revenue proof increasingly demanded by asset managers, narrative-driven valuations are facing their harshest test to date.

The Seoul Fallout: Retail "Ants" and South Korea’s Memory Titans

The fallout from Wall Street reached South Korea's financial center almost immediately, delivering a sharp blow to domestic markets and individual investors. South Korea’s retail equity investors—popularly referred to as "Seohak Ants" for their industrious buying of Western, primarily U.S., equities—had aggressively positioned themselves for an AI recovery.

Data shows that South Korean retail traders purchased over 800 billion won (approximately $580 million) worth of Nvidia stock in this month alone, aiming to capture a bottom. Instead, Nvidia’s 14% single-day collapse locked these retail accounts into aggregate paper losses estimated in the trillions of won. Panic selling ensued as investors sought to mitigate further downside risk.

The domestic stock market suffered severe collateral damage. South Korea's benchmark Kospi index broke below the key psychological threshold of 5,000 points under a heavy wave of coordinated selling by foreign institutions and domestic financial firms. The sell-off dealt a direct blow to South Korea's flagship semiconductor giants, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

As dominant global suppliers of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—the specialized memory chips essential for running Nvidia’s AI accelerators—Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are intricately bound to Nvidia’s operational health. Any reduction in GPU sales or delay in data center expansion directly threatens forward revenue forecasts for South Korea's crucial export sector. Meanwhile, domestic telecom carrier SK Telecom faced market headwinds despite holding an Anthropic equity stake valued at 5 trillion won ($3.6 billion) according to Yuanta Securities, proving that even substantial balance sheet assets could not shield companies from the broader risk-off sentiment sweeping global tech.

What to Watch Next: Regulatory Audits, Real Earnings, and HBM Orders

As the initial panic settles, global markets are transitioning from speculative euphoria to a rigorous scrutinization of corporate balance sheets. Over the coming quarters, three key developments will determine whether the tech sector can stabilize or if further structural corrections lie ahead:

  • The Scope of the SEC’s Investigation: Investors will closely monitor formal statements and filings from the SEC regarding Nvidia’s circular vendor investments. If regulatory scrutiny extends to other major tech hyperscalers or hardware vendors, systemic valuation adjustments could follow.
  • Anthropic’s Post-IPO Financial Reporting: With the hype of its public debut fading, Anthropic's upcoming quarterly financial results will be evaluated on cash burn rates and customer retention rather than total addressable market projections. Investors will look for proof that revenue can outpace infrastructure maintenance costs.
  • HBM Order Book Health in South Korea: Financial analysts will watch whether memory chip giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix report any capital expenditure cuts, order deferrals, or contract adjustments from major Western tech clients amidst the broader market cooling.

The era of buying AI stocks purely on promise appears to have met its limit. In the fallout of this week's market collapse, capital will likely flow only toward companies that can clearly demonstrate organic profitability and unassailable balance sheets.
