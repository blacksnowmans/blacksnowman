South Korea’s Brutal October: Inside the National Assembly Audits, Rising Property Anger, and Media Spin

October 06, 2026

As the calendar turns to October, the South Korean political landscape braces for what is reliably the most exhausting month of the year. For foreign observers watching Seoul, autumn brings a sharp escalation in political trench warfare. At the center of this storm is the annual National Assembly audit—a grueling constitutional check on government agencies that regularly devolves into partisan bloodletting.

This year, however, the stakes feel uniquely pressurized. The administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's primary liberal political force, find themselves caught in a tightly woven net of converging crises. From soaring housing costs and volatile retail financial products to aggressive media scrutiny and deep internal fractures among core supporters, October is shaping up to be an unremitting test of survival for the progressive government.

The National Assembly Audit: A Month-Long Gauntlet for the Ruling Party

The annual government audit is designed to hold the executive branch accountable, uncover administrative failures, and craft better policy. In practice, however, it has increasingly become a theater of systemic political attrition. For the entire month of October, the ruling Democratic Party will find itself on the defensive, fending off coordinated attacks from the conservative opposition, chiefly the People Power Party.

The schedule is relentless. The audit calendar is front-loaded with policy flashpoints, most notably a multi-day focus on real estate and housing inflation starting mid-month. For ordinary South Koreans, housing prices are an open nerve. By placing property at the center of the audit, the opposition is targeting the administration's most politically vulnerable flank—attempting to prove that the current liberal government is repeating the real estate missteps of its progressive predecessor.

The tension peaks in late October with the presidential secretariat audit by the parliamentary steering committee. This session promises to drag the administration's most sensitive personnel choices into the public spotlight. Conservative media and opposition lawmakers have spent months building a narrative around alleged cronyism, focusing specifically on figures linked to the "Seongnam line"—officials who rose through local government alongside President Lee during his tenure as mayor of Seongnam, a satellite city south of Seoul. For critics, the "Seongnam" label is shorthand for a closed-door power structure that bypasses traditional meritocratic vetting.

The Media Build-Up: Framing Social Media and Market Regrets

The opposition's onslaught inside the National Assembly does not happen in a vacuum; it is supported by a sophisticated, months-long media build-up designed to prime the public. Conservative newspapers and broadcasters have systematically stitched together disparate economic and social grievances into a single, cohesive narrative of government incompetence.

A prime example of this framing involves President Lee’s digital communication strategy. Recently, conservative outlets heavily publicized statistics showing that the president's social media output in September surged by over 200% compared to August. While increased communication is normally viewed as a sign of transparency, opposition figures quickly reframed the data. Senior conservative lawmakers mocked the volume of posts, coining derogatory phrases to paint the president's direct-to-public messaging as frivolous and unpresidential.

At the same time, the media has zeroed in on complex financial controversies, such as liability disputes surrounding leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs). As retail investors—often referred to in Korean media as "ants"—absorb heavy losses amid market volatility, opposition politicians and critical press are working hard to tie these financial casualties directly to former and current administration figures. By channeling public anger over lost savings into parliamentary attacks, critics are transforming technical financial policy into a referendum on the administration's economic competence.

Internal Exhaustion and the Alienation of Core Supporters

Perhaps the most telling aspect of October’s political storm is not the damage inflicted by the conservative opposition, but the quiet erosion of morale within the ruling party's own base. Governing coalitions are rarely defeated solely by their enemies; they fracture when they lose the faith of the people who put them in power.

In recent weeks, progressive activists and long-time loyalists have begun to voice a profound sense of alienation. In broadcast interviews and public forums, some core supporters have expressed a level of exhaustion and frustration they claim never felt under past authoritarian governments. The grievance is not merely external pressure, but a growing disconnect between the leadership and its grassroots—a feeling that the administration, consumed by internal factionalism and defensive bunker-mentality politics, is silencing dissenting views from within its own camp.

When a government begins treating its most dedicated followers as a nuisance rather than a partner in democratic reform, it risks losing its moral authority. History shows that political movements rarely collapse from external blows alone; they implode when they suffocate from internal isolation.

What to Watch Next

As October unfolds in Seoul, foreign observers should look beyond the daily theater of committee hearings and focus on a few key indicators:

  • The Real Estate Fallout: Monitor whether opposition attacks on housing policy translate into a permanent drop in public approval ratings, or if the government manages to present credible stabilization measures.
  • Personnel Confirmations: Watch how the Democratic Party handles high-stakes confirmation hearings and internal appointments. Concessions to internal factions could widen splits within the ruling bloc, while heavy-handed pushback will invite charges of institutional capture.
  • Grassroots Sentiment: Pay attention to how the administration responds to disaffected core supporters. Whether the leadership chooses dialogue or defensiveness will dictate the stability of the progressive base heading into future local and national elections.
