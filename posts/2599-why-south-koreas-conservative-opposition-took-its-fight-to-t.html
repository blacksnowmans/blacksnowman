Why South Korea’s Conservative Opposition Took Its Fight to the Streets of Daegu

November 22, 2026

On November 21, the center of Daegu—a southeastern city long regarded as the bedrock of South Korean conservatism—filled with banners, chants, and roughly 10,000 protesters, according to rally organizers cited in a Korean political briefing from the news series Black Snowman Daily Briefing. Lawmakers and party members from the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, had stepped out of the National Assembly and onto the street. Standing alongside them were 24 of the 25 National Assembly members representing constituencies in Daegu and the surrounding North Gyeongsang Province, a region known in domestic politics as "TK."

For international observers, an outdoor rally by a major opposition party might look like routine political theater. Yet as the Korean briefing notes, the November 21 demonstration marks a sharp escalation in a constitutional and institutional standoff between the PPP and the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). At the center of the confrontation are three explosive issues: a ruling-party bill to appoint a special prosecutor over alleged "fabricated indictments," a push by the ruling bloc to impeach the nation’s sitting Chief Justice, and opposition demands to impeach President Lee Jae-myung and restart his suspended criminal trials.

A Show of Force in South Korea’s Conservative Heartland

To understand why the PPP chose Daegu to launch its street offensive, it helps to understand the political geography of South Korea. Just as certain states or regions serve as ideological anchors in Western democracies, the Daegu–North Gyeongsang area has historically been the most reliable stronghold for conservative parties. Gathering 24 out of 25 regional lawmakers on one stage was a deliberate display of unity, designed to show both the ruling party and conservative voters that the opposition is closing ranks during a political crisis.

According to the Korean briefing, PPP leaders argued that parliamentary debate alone was no longer enough to check what they describe as the ruling party’s "legislative dominance." Because the Democratic Party holds the upper hand in the legislature and controls the presidency under Lee Jae-myung, the opposition has increasingly concluded that its institutional leverage inside the National Assembly is limited. Moving outdoors was a calculated decision to rally core supporters and build public pressure from outside parliament.

Even so, the atmosphere in Daegu was not entirely uniform. While the crowd cheered calls to confront the government, the briefing reports that scattered jeers and shouting also broke out among supporters at the venue. Those tensions reflected quiet disagreements within the conservative base over the party’s strategic direction—an early hint that hardline street politics carries risks as well as rewards.

The Special Prosecutor Bill and the Battle Over the Chief Justice

What pushed the opposition out of parliament is a pair of legislative moves by the Democratic Party that directly touch the balance of power between South Korea’s executive, legislative, and judicial branches. The source report outlines three core flashpoints driving the standoff:

  • The "fabricated indictment" special prosecutor bill: The Democratic Party is pushing a special counsel bill aimed at investigating allegations that prosecutors previously fabricated charges. The DPK maintains this legislation is essential to correct abuses of prosecutorial power. The PPP, however, condemns the bill as "100% unconstitutional" and derides it as a "Lee Jae-myung jailbreak bill," arguing it is designed to neutralize legitimate prosecutorial investigations and indictments.
  • The push to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae: The ruling party’s move to pursue impeachment against Chief Justice Jo has become a red line for the opposition. PPP leaders warn that targeting the head of the judiciary amounts to an act of "insurrection" and treason against the constitutional order, claiming the ruling bloc is trying to hold the Chief Justice hostage to prevent unfavorable court rulings.
  • President Lee Jae-myung’s suspended trials: The PPP is demanding the immediate resumption of court proceedings involving President Lee that have been put on hold, insisting that he must face judicial judgment. The two parties view the same legal reality through completely incompatible frames of "judicial justice."

In the PPP’s framing, defending Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae and blocking the special prosecutor bill are matters of safeguarding judicial independence and the rule of law. In the DPK’s framing, passing the special prosecutor bill and pursuing accountability in the judiciary are necessary steps toward judicial reform and curbing unchecked prosecutorial authority. Because neither side acknowledges the legitimacy of the other’s premise, normal legislative compromise has effectively ground to a halt.

Harsh Rhetoric, Ruling Party Pushback, and Quiet Doubts Inside the Opposition

The rhetoric on the Daegu stage underscored how personal and unforgiving South Korea’s political divide has become. According to the Korean briefing, the sharpest attack came from PPP lawmaker Jang Dong-hyuk, who drew loud cheers from the estimated 10,000 attendees when he declared that "impeachment is the only answer" regarding President Lee Jae-myung. Jang went further, asserting that the president is destroying the constitution and using graphic language to demand that Lee be "rolled up and forcibly removed" from Cheong Wa Dae, South Korea’s presidential compound, while calling for his trials to resume immediately.

Party leadership echoed that confrontational tone, warning that if the ruling party forces through an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, the Lee Jae-myung administration itself would collapse. By using terms like "insurrection" to describe the ruling party’s legislative agenda, the PPP appears to be aiming not only to fire up loyalists, but also to alarm moderate swing voters about potential overreach by the governing bloc.

The Democratic Party of Korea responded immediately and forcefully. DPK lawmaker Han Byung-do criticized the opposition for turning the country into a "stage for unrefined, vulgar remarks," accusing the PPP of ignoring citizens’ daily livelihoods in favor of provocative language meant solely to stoke supporter anger. Ruling party voices further questioned whether an opposition taking to the streets—allegedly to cover up criminal allegations—is fit to discuss state affairs, dismissing the Daegu rally as an emotional spectacle devoid of political justification. As the briefing notes, the ruling party’s counterattack is aimed at framing the opposition’s hardline speeches as reckless extremism in order to blunt the rally’s national impact.

Yet skepticism about the street strategy is not coming solely from the ruling party. Behind the scenes, the source report notes a subtle divergence inside the PPP itself. While 24 regional lawmakers stood together in Daegu, some party figures worry about how aggressive outdoor protests and extreme slogans play in the densely populated Seoul metropolitan area and among centrist voters. Emphasizing ideological purity in a conservative stronghold can consolidate the base, critics inside the party fear, while simultaneously alienating the moderate conservatives and undecided voters needed to win national elections.

"As both ruling and opposition parties look solely to their most ardent supporters and reach for extreme cards like impeachment and special prosecutors, citizens’ livelihoods and legal stability—the very things meant to be protected—are being pushed to the sidelines."

That assessment from the Black Snowman Daily Briefing captures the broader dilemma facing South Korean politics: when parliamentary disputes spill onto the streets, the space for institutional mediation shrinks rapidly.

From Daegu to Seoul: What to Watch Next

The Daegu rally was not meant to be an isolated event. Immediately after concluding the demonstration, PPP leaders and party members marched on foot toward Government Complex Seoul, the central administrative hub in the capital. By shifting its protest campaign from a regional stronghold to the heart of Seoul, the opposition signaled that it is preparing for a prolonged confrontation rather than a one-day show of force.

In the weeks ahead, several concrete developments will determine whether South Korea’s political stalemate deepens into a broader constitutional crisis:

  • The trajectory of the special prosecutor bill and Chief Justice impeachment: Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party moves ahead with floor votes on the "fabricated indictment" special prosecutor bill and the impeachment drive against Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, or whether public pushback forces a recalibration.
  • Expansion of street protests: Observe whether the PPP’s march toward Government Complex Seoul evolves into a sustained series of nationwide rallies, and whether rival rallies from ruling-party supporters escalate street-level polarization.
  • Centrist backlash and internal party discipline: Monitor polling and internal reactions within the People Power Party to see if harsh rhetoric—such as calls to forcibly remove the president—helps mobilize conservatives or triggers a backlash among moderate voters in the Seoul capital region.
  • Judicial stability: Pay close attention to how South Korea’s courts respond to relentless political pressure from both sides over Chief Justice Jo’s status and the fate of President Lee Jae-myung’s suspended trials.

For now, both parties appear locked on parallel tracks with little room to retreat. With the legislature deadlocked and the streets heating up, the coming clash over South Korea’s courts and special prosecutors will test the resilience of the country’s democratic institutions.
