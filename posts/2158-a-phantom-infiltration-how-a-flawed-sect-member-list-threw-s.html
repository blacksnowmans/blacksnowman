A Phantom Infiltration: How a Flawed Sect Member List Threw South Korea’s Ruling Party into Chaos

November 10, 2026

Inside the National Assembly in Yeouido, the island district that serves as the nerve center of South Korean politics, the atmosphere within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea should theoretically be one of consolidation. Having recaptured the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung following the historic impeachment, removal, and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the party holds the reins of executive power and a formidable legislative presence. Yet instead of driving through its flagship policy agenda, the ruling party finds itself paralyzed by a bitter, self-inflicted civil war—one revolving around secret databases, fringe religious sects, and allegations of an internal coup.

At the heart of the storm is the Shincheonji Church of Jesus, a controversial Christian-derived religious movement long viewed with intense suspicion by the South Korean public. Accusations that Shincheonji operatives systematically infiltrated the Democratic Party’s internal elections have rapidly spiraled from fringe online gossip into a full-blown constitutional and legal crisis. As senior figures trade accusations and party members open their wallets to sue their own leadership, the controversy touches on fundamental questions of due process, privacy law, and the fragile norms of party democracy.

The Inflated Numbers Game Behind the 'Shincheonji Infiltration'

To understand the panic, one must understand Shincheonji's unique standing in South Korean society. Long criticized by mainstream Protestant churches as a cult, the group became a household name—and a public scapegoat—during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, when a massive outbreak centered on its Daegu branch accelerated the country's first major wave of infections. In South Korean politics, any association with Shincheonji is radioactive. An accusation that a politician is backed by the sect can prove lethal to their public career.

Rumors began circulating within the Democratic Party that the sect had mobilized tens of thousands of members to join as voting party members, purportedly to hijack internal party leadership elections or tilt candidate nominations in favor of particular figures, such as lawmaker Jung Chung-rae. Factional actors claimed that upwards of 30,000 Shincheonji adherents had quietly invaded the voter rolls.

A closer look at the verifiable data, however, exposes a vast chasm between sensational rhetoric and hard reality. According to figures first reported by the Kukmin Ilbo, a mainstream daily newspaper, cross-referencing revealed that the actual number of party members with voting rights tied to Shincheonji stood at approximately 3,300. Out of a total voting electorate of roughly 780,000 party members, these individuals represented a minuscule 0.4 percent.

Statistically, four-tenths of a single percent is nowhere near sufficient to alter the outcome of a nationwide party convention. While party insiders seeking to amplify the scandal pointed to a figure of roughly 8,800 dues-paying members on the rolls, analysis by party management consultants such as Park Gyu-hwan revealed that this larger pool included thousands without actual voting rights. By circulating an unverified figure of 30,000, factional operatives weaponized fear, attempting to delegitimize democratic election results and manufacture an existential external enemy for immediate factional leverage.

A Dangerous Precedent: Suspected Misuse of State Epidemic Data

If the narrative of a mass takeover falls apart under mathematical scrutiny, a far darker legal issue looms over how the party obtained the data to conduct its cross-referencing in the first place. Neither South Korean state investigative agencies nor Shincheonji’s leadership would legally hand over confidential membership rosters to a political party. Where, then, did the Democratic Party obtain a digital registry of Shincheonji adherents?

The prevailing suspicion points back to the spring of 2020. At the height of the coronavirus crisis, then-Gyeonggi Province Governor Lee Jae-myung made national headlines by leading an administrative raid on Shincheonji’s headquarters to extract its roster for epidemiological contact tracing. That operation yielded a nationwide registry of approximately 212,000 sect members, which was placed under the control of the Central Disaster and Safety Countermeasure Headquarters (CDSCH) and distributed to local governments purely for quarantine and disease prevention.

Circumstantial evidence strongly suggests that this very epidemic database—compiled by the state under emergency public health powers—was leaked or repurposed by internal party elements to screen its own membership rolls. If true, using sensitive personal data collected for public health to conduct partisan vetting represents a flagrant violation of South Korea’s Personal Information Protection Act.

The potential illegality was clear enough to deter conscientious figures within the party. National Assembly lawmaker Kim Young-hwan, who represents the Goyang constituency, alongside party official Park Gyu-hwan, openly refused to examine or authenticate the cross-referenced lists. Kim pointedly warned that reviewing unverified data of dubious origin amounted to participating in an illegal act that would invite external legal intervention. Critics have noted the cruel irony: while the Democratic Party frequently condemned weaponized, politically motivated investigations during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term in office, elements within the party appeared eager to deploy the exact same unaccountable tactics against their internal rivals.

Song Young-gil's Escalation and the Factional Battleground

Rather than quietly retiring the dubious dossier, senior party figures chose to escalate. Lawmaker Song Young-gil, who currently serves as a special presidential advisor to President Lee Jae-myung, called a press conference to claim that an "organized infiltration" of the party had indeed been confirmed, effectively declaring open political warfare against opposing factions.

Song’s aggressive rhetoric has sparked fierce internal pushback. Commentators from political consulting circles, including analysts at Park Si-young Consulting, argue that Song chose to throw fuel on a dying conspiracy theory to arrest his own sliding political relevance and bolster his faction ahead of parliamentary realignments. Instead of reflecting on shifting public sentiment, critics argue, Song chose to blame external boogeymen.

The response from rank-and-file members and fellow lawmakers was swift and unsparing. Accusing Song of regressing into the authoritarian factionalism of past decades, several voices within the party openly demanded that he cease inciting division or exit politics entirely. For an advisor to the president to needlessly destabilize the ruling party—handing an easy propaganda victory to the conservative opposition People Power Party—is seen by many loyalists as an unforgivable failure of political responsibility.

Grassroots Backlash and the Legal Threat to Party Leadership

While Song faces anger from the base, the ultimate buck stops with party leader Kim Min-seok. The central question dominating Yeouido corridors is simple: Did Kim authorize, oversee, or tacitly endorse the illicit cross-referencing of party voter registries with external databases?

Public opinion and party faithful alike appear largely united in demanding answers. A recent opinion survey conducted by the polling firm Poll Flower (Yeoronjosa Kkot) showed that 63.5 percent of respondents in telephone interviews and 61.3 percent in automated response (ARS) polling believed Kim Min-seok must issue a formal public apology and accept responsibility for the affair. Crucially, this sentiment was shared by the Democratic Party’s core base: 61.1 percent of self-identified Democratic Party supporters in interview polling concurred that the leadership must be held accountable. In Honam, the southwestern region considered the historical bedrock of the progressive movement, demand for accountability surged past 70 percent.

Discontent has now broken out of the realm of polling and entered the courtroom. Convinced that the leadership breached statutory procedures and constitutional privacy protections, ordinary dues-paying members have launched a grassroots legal campaign. Gathering individual contributions of 33,000 KRW (about $25) to cover litigation costs, the members are filing an injunction with the district court to suspend Kim Min-seok’s official authority as party chairman, alongside a formal lawsuit to nullify the leadership election results.

For party members to crowdfund litigation against their own seated party leader is an extraordinary and embarrassing spectacle in modern Korean politics. It signals that the base no longer trusts internal dispute mechanisms to resolve abuses of executive power within party headquarters.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether the Democratic Party can contain this crisis or allow it to permanently fracture its governing coalition. Observers should keep a close eye on several key developments:

  • The Court’s Injunction Ruling: The legal petition to suspend Party Leader Kim Min-seok's executive duties will test whether the judiciary views the alleged membership vetting as a grave procedural breach or an internal party matter beyond judicial intervention.
  • Personal Data Inquiries: If law enforcement or data privacy regulators launch a formal probe into whether the 2020 government COVID-19 database was illicitly leaked into party headquarters, criminal liability could reach deep into the central party apparatus.
  • The Fate of Song Young-gil: With vocal segments of the grassroots demanding Song step down from his role as special presidential advisor, President Lee Jae-myung's office will eventually have to decide whether protecting a senior loyalist is worth the mounting political cost.
  • Legislative Momentum: As the ruling party expends its energy on factional intrigue, the conservative People Power Party is already seizing on the debacle to portray the government as erratic and lawless, threatening the administration’s legislative agenda heading into the next election cycle.

In South Korea’s vibrant but hyper-combative democracy, procedural legitimacy is everything. By indulging unverified conspiracy theories and allegedly compromising public data, the ruling party’s leadership has walked into a trap of its own making—one from which only full transparency and accountability can extract it.
