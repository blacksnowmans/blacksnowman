South Korea’s $1.5 Billion Bet: Can the Lee Jae-myung Administration Create 20 AI Unicorns by 2030?

November 12, 2026

In the glass-walled office towers of Pangyo Techno Valley—South Korea’s equivalent to Silicon Valley—the mood over the last year has been uncharacteristically somber. Despite the global frenzy surrounding generative AI, many Korean startups have found themselves trapped in a "funding winter," struggling to secure the massive capital required to train Large Language Models (LLMs) or compete for the world’s dwindling supply of high-end GPUs. For a nation that built its modern identity on being a fast-moving tech leader, the fear of falling behind in the artificial intelligence race has become a matter of national anxiety.

The Lee Jae-myung administration has signaled that it is ready to move from anxiety to action. In a comprehensive strategy recently unveiled by the government and the Ministry of Startups and SMEs (MSS), South Korea has committed to a massive state-led push to reshape its AI landscape. The goal is audacious: to increase the number of "AI unicorns"—startups valued at over 1 trillion won (about $750 million)—from the current six to 20 by the year 2030. Furthermore, the government aims to produce at least one "decacorn," a startup with a valuation exceeding 10 trillion won (about $7.5 billion).

This is not just a collection of aspirational numbers. It is backed by a historic 2.07 trillion won (about $1.5 billion) R&D budget for AI and Information and Communication Technology (ICT), marking the first time such spending has crossed the 2 trillion won threshold. As global tech hegemony becomes the primary theater of geopolitical competition, this policy represents Korea’s attempt to carve out a "third way" between the dominance of American big tech and Chinese state-led behemoths.

The 2030 Vision: From Tech Potential to Market Dominance

South Korea currently ranks roughly 9th in the world for AI unicorn holdings. While six unicorns is a respectable figure for a country of 52 million, it pales in comparison to the triple-digit numbers seen in the United States or the massive ecosystems in China and Europe. The government’s new roadmap is an admission that the previous "shotgun" approach—distributing small amounts of aid to thousands of startups—is no longer sufficient in the AI era.

The strategy focuses on "selection and concentration." By setting a 2030 deadline, the Lee Jae-myung administration is targeting the "golden time" of the AI revolution—the window where foundational models and industry standards are being set. The plan is to identify "deep tech" companies that possess unique intellectual property in LLMs or specialized application services and provide them with a continuous support "ladder" that takes them from early-stage R&D to global scale-up without the typical funding gaps that kill many Korean ventures.

Why does this matter? Historically, Korean startups have been excellent at localized services—apps for food delivery, finance, or chat—but have struggled to achieve the $1 billion valuations that come from global scalability. By specifically targeting the "unicorn" and "decacorn" status, the government is forcing a shift in mindset: moving away from being "big in Korea" to being a "global standard." For this to work, however, the government must ensure these valuations are backed by actual revenue and user growth, rather than just speculative bubbles fueled by state subsidies.

Beyond Subsidies: The $7.5 Million Scale-Up Injection

One of the most concrete pillars of the new policy is the change in how the Ministry of Startups and SMEs (MSS) allocates funds. Instead of small grants, the ministry will now provide "scale-up" funding of more than 10 billion won (about $7.5 million) per selected company. This is a paradigm shift for Korean industrial policy, which has often been criticized for "nanny-state" tendencies—keeping weak companies alive with small subsidies rather than helping strong ones dominate.

In the world of AI, 10 billion won is often the bare minimum required just to cover the electricity bills and hardware costs for training a sophisticated model. By concentrating these funds, the government hopes to provide a "priming pump" for private venture capital. In Korea, private investors have recently been risk-averse; a government commitment of $7.5 million acts as a de facto seal of approval, signaling to private markets that a company is a viable candidate for a global IPO or major acquisition.

The MSS also plans to bridge the gap to international markets. Because the domestic Korean market is too small to support a $7.5 billion decacorn, the policy includes aggressive support for overseas "accelerating" programs and networking. This includes placing Korean AI solutions into the marketplaces of global big tech firms and hosting regular "demo days" with foreign venture capitalists in major tech hubs like San Francisco and London. For a deep-tech founder in Seoul who may have world-class coding skills but lacks the marketing infrastructure to sell to a Fortune 500 company in New York, this state-sponsored "salesmanship" is vital.

A Record-Breaking Budget for a Physical AI Future

The financial backbone of this ambition is the 2025 R&D budget. The Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) has confirmed a total of 2.0755 trillion won (about $1.53 billion) for AI and ICT research. This represents a significant political pivot. The Lee Jae-myung administration has moved to restore and expand R&D funding following the controversial and drastic budget cuts that occurred during Mr. Yun Seok-yeol’s time in office, which many in the scientific community argued crippled Korea’s long-term competitiveness.

A significant portion of this $1.5 billion is earmarked for what the government calls "Physical AI." While the world is currently obsessed with chatbots, the MSIT believes Korea’s competitive advantage lies in marrying AI with its world-leading manufacturing and robotics sectors. This involves:

  • AI Transformation (AX): Implementing AI into traditional industries like shipbuilding, steel, and automotive manufacturing to boost productivity.
  • Physical AI: Developing the software brains for robotics and autonomous machines that operate in the real world.
  • On-Device AI: Creating energy-efficient AI models that run directly on hardware (like smartphones or home appliances) rather than in the cloud, a sector where Korean firms like Samsung excel.

The goal here is to prove that AI can do more than generate text—it can reduce defect rates in factories and improve safety on construction sites. If Korea can prove the "AX productivity" boost through these R&D projects, it will create a massive domestic market for its own AI startups to sell into, creating a self-sustaining ecosystem before they even head abroad.

Navigating the Global Regulatory Minefield

As the European Union passes the AI Act and the United States debates various safety frameworks, "regulation" is often seen as a hurdle. However, the Korean government is framing this as an opportunity. Ryu Je-myung, the Second Vice Minister of the MSIT, has argued that as global regulations tighten, the demand for "trustworthy" and "explainable" AI will skyrocket.

South Korea is positioning its startups to be the leaders in RegTech (Regulatory Technology). By helping startups meet the most stringent global standards for data privacy, ethics, and deepfake detection early on, the government believes Korean firms can enter the European and North American markets as "pre-vetted" reliable partners. This "Reliability-as-a-Service" could be a significant niche for Korean firms that might lack the raw data scale of Google or Meta but can offer high-security, ethically-compliant models for B2B and government use.

"The tightening of global AI regulations is a unique opportunity for our companies that are prepared," Vice Minister Ryu noted. "It opens a new market for those who can prove their technology is both powerful and safe."

What to Watch Next

While the vision is grand, the implementation will be the true test. Here are the key indicators for whether this policy is succeeding:

  • Private Capital Participation: Watch if the 10 billion won government injections are matched or exceeded by private venture capital. If private investors remain on the sidelines, the "unicorns" will be artificial, state-supported entities rather than true market leaders.
  • Physical AI Milestones: Look for "success stories" in the manufacturing sector. If a Korean AI startup can significantly optimize a major shipyard or a semiconductor line, it will prove the "AX" thesis.
  • Global Regulatory Compliance: Watch how many Korean startups receive certification under the EU AI Act. This will be the litmus test for whether the "Reliability" strategy is working.
  • Political Continuity: Given the history of budget fluctuations—most notably the cuts during Mr. Yun Seok-yeol's 재임 시절 (time in office)—the tech industry will be watching to see if the Lee Jae-myung administration can maintain this high level of R&D spending through the end of the decade.

The Lee Jae-myung administration has laid out the chips. With $1.5 billion on the table and a target of 20 unicorns, South Korea is betting that it can turn its manufacturing muscle into AI brains. If successful, 2030 will see Korea not just as a consumer of AI, but as a primary architect of the world's autonomous and industrial future.
