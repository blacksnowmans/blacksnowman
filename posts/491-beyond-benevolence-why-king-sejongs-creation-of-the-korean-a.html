Beyond Benevolence: Why King Sejong’s Creation of the Korean Alphabet Was a Radical Revolution

September 09, 2026

Picture the scene in fifteenth-century Korea: a rigid, paranoid court obsessed with appeasing its sprawling neighbour, Ming Dynasty China, while a tiny elite class guards its monopoly on literacy like a dragon on a hoard of gold. For generations, Korean history textbooks have taught students to view the invention of Hangeul, the Korean alphabet, through a warm and comforting lens of paternal benevolence. King Sejong the Great, the narrative goes, simply felt sorry for his illiterate subjects and gifted them an easy way to read out of the goodness of his heart.

Yet, when stripping away the comforting gloss of official histories, a far more dangerous and subversive picture emerges. The creation of Hangeul in 1443 was not a benevolent charitable gesture from an enlightened monarch. It was a high-stakes, intellectual coup d'état that struck directly at the heart of Joseon Dynasty Korea's ruling structure, risking the wrath of both the domestic aristocracy and the Chinese empire.

The Ideological Cage of Early Joseon

To understand the magnitude of what Sejong did, one must first grasp the suffocating geopolitical and social constraints of early Joseon. The dynasty itself was born from an act of pragmatic submission. When General Yi Seong-gye founded the dynasty after turning his army back at Wihwado rather than attacking Ming territory, he justified his treason with a classic tributary doctrine: "It is not right for a small country to oppose a great one." From its inception, the regime was structurally bound to a worldview of subservience to Beijing.

Within the royal court, this manifested as an intellectual lockdown. The elite scholar-官员 (Yangban), who accounted for just 4 percent of the population, lived inside a mental fortress built from Chinese texts like the Four Books and Five Classics. The remaining 96 percent of the population were kept in a state of enforced illiteracy. Because Classical Chinese was notoriously difficult and complex, the ruling class used it as an exclusive tool to extract perpetual labor and compliance from the common people. Power did not circulate; it was permanently consolidated through the monopoly of the written word.

When Sejong began developing a native script, the backlash from his own officials was ferocious. Court scholars openly upbraided the king, warning him that introducing a vernacular script would anger the Ming emperor and disrupt the moral order. Realizing he could not complete the project under the watchful eyes of his ministers in the main palace, Sejong spent over a decade quietly refining the script in secret before finally promulgating the 28 letters of Hunminjeongeum in December 1443.

An Alphabet Engineered for the Cosmos

While ancient scripts like Egyptian hieroglyphs or Mayan glyphs required years of specialized training to master—effectively locking out the common populace—Sejong and his scholars engineered something entirely unprecedented: a phonetically transparent writing system built on the mechanics of human speech itself.

Consonants were designed by mimicking the shape of the vocal organs used to pronounce them, while vowels incorporated the philosophical elements of heaven, earth, and humanity. By applying a principle of adding strokes to basic shapes, the alphabet could visually map virtually any sound a human mouth could make.

Linguists often marvel at the mathematical potential of this system. When accounting for its combinatory mechanics, Hangeul boasts a staggering number of potential phonetic combinations—roughly 28 factorial, or about 300 googol combinations, vastly outstripping the number of grains of sand on Earth. It is a script built to capture not just the sounds of the Korean language, but any sound in existence.

Today, this sixteenth-century phonetic toolkit has proven to be an accidental cheat code for the digital age. In an era dominated by artificial intelligence, speech recognition algorithms, and digital processing, a script where "letters equal sounds" is uniquely optimized for computation. What the conservative elites of Joseon once condemned as a dangerous barbarian tongue has evolved into a foundational pillar of modern digital civilization.

The Crisis of Modern Literacy

Yet, as modern South Korea sits atop this monumental linguistic inheritance, a quiet cultural crisis is unfolding. Step onto any subway in Seoul, and nearly every pair of eyes is glued to a smartphone screen. While the country boasts blazing-fast digital infrastructure and world-class semiconductor manufacturing, its population's deep literacy and reading stamina are facing unprecedented erosion.

Snackable short-form videos, endless social media feeds, and instant digital content have largely crowded out the long-form reading habit. The danger here goes far beyond a mere preference for screens over paper. Reading is the primary cognitive gym where human beings build patience, deep empathy, and internal intellectual muscle. When a society stops reading complex texts, its ability to process nuanced arguments shatters.

This collapse of deep literacy creates fertile ground for misinformation, populist demagogues, and radical polarization. Sejong fought to hand commoners the ultimate tool of self-empowerment precisely so they could think for themselves and avoid being exploited. Ironically, modern citizens, drowning in algorithmic stimuli, are voluntarily blinding themselves to that intellectual heritage.

Navigating the AI Era with Human Agency

As artificial intelligence begins to automate cognitive labor and generate prose in milliseconds, humanity faces a profound identity crisis. The temptation to throw up one's hands and surrender intellectual agency to machines is growing stronger by the day.

Yet the solution to this technological tidal wave does not lie in retreating from progress, nor in succumbing to digital fatalism. True human agency has always rested on existential inquiry—the willingness to ask fundamental questions about meaning, ethics, and purpose. While AI can synthesize language with staggering speed, it cannot replicate the painful, authentic interiority of human thought, nor the disciplined stamina required to build an examined life.

King Sejong did not merely invent a convenient set of letters; he broke the back of an oppressive class monopoly and handed ordinary people the keys to their own minds. Six hundred years later, as convenience threatens to erode our capacity for deep thought, the true challenge is ensuring that the gates Sejong unlocked are never closed again.
