The Crack in the Coalition: Why South Korea's Progressive Base Is Pulling Away from President Lee Jae-myung

October 08, 2026

When you spend time watching the landscape of South Korean political commentary, a strange atmosphere hangs in the air. For an administration that was expected to enjoy a smooth ascent following Lee Jae-myung’s rise to the presidency, the reality on the ground looks jarringly different. Presidential approval ratings have slipped into the mid-30s, and a wave of painful, public self-reflection is rippling through the progressive camp. A recent poll by Media Tomato captured this shifting mood dramatically: Yu Si-min, a veteran progressive writer and intellectual, drew a trust rating of 29.8%—outranking prominent political figures like Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok, media personality Kim Eo-jun, and Cho Kuk, head of the minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party. While general public polls still show regional and generational divides, among core progressive voters, trust in Yu approaches a staggering 90%. How did the overwhelming enthusiasm that preceded the election deflate so quickly? Dismissing this as mere opposition whining or conservative consolidation misses the point. The cracking sounds coming from within the progressive movement are simply too loud to ignore.

The Paradox of Progressive Trust and the Slide in Core Support

The high trust rating recorded for Yu Si-min is not merely a measure of personal popularity. It is a symptom of how anxious and thirsty core progressives are regarding the current administration's governance. Before the election, President Lee Jae-myung enjoyed enthusiastic support from his base that regularly cleared the 90% mark. Today, analysts note that core support has plummeted toward levels usually seen among young voters in their 20s. Progressive digital media outlets, currently suffering from plunging subscriber counts and punishing algorithm shifts, reflect this mass defection of public sentiment. Critics can call it petulance if they wish, but the atmosphere on the ground feels less like a tantrum and more like a blaring warning siren. Why are the people meant to be the administration's staunchest shield suddenly folding their arms and keeping their distance?

Recent data from Realmeter underscores this painful reality, showing President Lee's positive marks resting in the mid-to-upper 30s. Most alarming for the administration is the defection within the 4050 demographic (those in their 40s and 50s), traditionally the bedrock of progressive and reformist politics in South Korea. Negative sentiment among voters in their 50s climbed toward 60% (57.5%), while those in their 40s also registered majority disapproval. In political circles, a sharp adage is making the rounds: "No ideology exists that can override the injury of personal economic loss." Measures in the August 3 tax reform proposals—such as tightening taxes on single-home non-residents and abolishing long-term holding deductions—directly touched the financial interests of these middle-aged homeowners. Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok rushed to damp down the backlash during high-level party-government consultations, conceding that plans for the comprehensive real estate holding tax required deep deliberation. That fire-fighting effort was an admission that public anger could no longer be ignored.

The Danger of Early Lame-Duck Dynamics

Comparing past administrations using Gallup Korea data highlights how steep this descent has been. Unlike former presidents Park Geun-hye or Moon Jae-in, who enjoyed high-flying 60% to 70% approval ratings at their 15-month marks thanks to diplomatic events or inter-Korean summits, President Lee has faced an accelerated slide amplified around the local elections. A string of missteps—including a ballot paper shortage during local voting, controversies over real estate policies, single-stock leverage exchange-traded funds (ETFs), and internal factional fighting during the party convention—has severely sapped governing momentum. While some argue that progressive-leaning voters still hold steady around 80%, meaning the base has not collapsed, a drop of over 30 percentage points among younger generations and moderates cannot be brushed aside. Fraying bonds at the start of a term are the most dangerous tinder for an early lame-duck presidency.

"Renovation" Versus "Remodeling": The Metaphor of Party Reform

At the center of this debate is Yu Si-min's critique comparing the administration's approach to housing construction: "remodeling" versus "reconstruction." The Lee Jae-myung administration constantly preaches inclusion and integration, aiming to corral moderates to build a structural majority. No one faults the ambition to be a president for all citizens—after all, winning an election does not mean governing solely for one's own side. But the method is the problem. Yu diagnosed that while the government promised to "remodel" by expanding outward while keeping the sturdy structure of the existing base intact, once at the helm, it began hacking away at the foundational pillars. His sharp question hit home: "Is this remodeling, or is it a reconstruction project carried out without the residents even knowing?" The moment an administration signals to its core base—the very foundation of its physical strength—that it must step aside to woo moderates, that expansion ceases to be growth and starts looking like self-harm.

Compounding the problem is internal friction. Yu criticized the influx of "demolition squads" into the progressive public discourse while the administration chanted unity. He likened it to an autoimmune disease: an immune system attacking healthy internal cells instead of external enemies, a state that has persisted for nearly a year. This was a thinly veiled jab at hardline factions within the Democratic Party that have hurled derogatory labels at the party's old mainstream—the Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in loyalists. The fact that months of open vilification of former presidents went unchecked within and around the party bore an uncomfortable resemblance to how the pro-Yoon factions sidelined challenger candidates within the conservative People Power Party during the era of the imprisoned former leader, Yoon Suk-yeol.

A growing divergence over prosecutorial reform has further alienated core supporters. Supporters expected a thorough structural overhaul of prosecutors' offices—far beyond the mere separation of investigative and indicting powers. Yet actual appointments and policy rolls fell short of those campaign pledges. Yu bluntly stated that the prosecutorial reform envisioned by the president differed entirely from what the Democratic Party and its voters had been promised, accusing the president of misleading his base. While the administration might point to legislative roadblocks and practical side-effects, what the base feels is not administrative nuance, but the betrayal of a rolling-back of reform. That is where the reservoir of trust is running dry.

🔥 An Administration That Shut Off Its Warning Lights

This is akin to a driver pulling out the dashboard bulb because the red warning light is unpleasant to look at. The moment an administration dismisses the defection of the 4050 demographic and its core base as mere petulance, it strips away the only buffer capable of protecting it.

Why the Core Base Remains the Ultimate Foundation

The greatest illusion in politics is treating a support base merely as a collection of votes to be tallied on election day. The reality of a core base is entirely different. While they are often invisible during quiet times, when a government faces crisis and aggressive onslaughts from the opposition and media, these supporters form the frontline shield. They explain misunderstood policies to their neighbors, push back against fake news, and act as a shock absorber at critical moments. Moderates, by contrast, are fluid, shifting according to economic performance and public mood. Moderates matter immensely, but treating them by trampling the core supporters who keep the government upright in times of crisis turns logic on its head. A restaurant that tells its loyal, quiet regulars to leave in order to chase new walk-in traffic rarely survives.

Yu used another apt metaphor: "If a pebble gets into your shoe, you have to take it off and shake it out. Instead, people are just walking around saying 'it's uncomfortable.'" Those aides and extremist factions whispering division and pouring cold water on reform aspirations *are* the pebble in the shoe. When supporters cry out in pain and get scolded for being "manipulated by propaganda," the roles are deeply inverted. The harsh feedback from supporters does not stem from hatred of the president; it arises from an urgent desire for him to succeed. Refusing to listen to that feedback while denying reality with declarations that "our approval ratings are solid" is simply another name for political deafness.

Breaking the Vicious Cycle

The trap facing the Lee Jae-myung administration is clear. The more it denies the flight of its core base and marches further rightward to court moderates, the deeper the disillusionment of its original supporters, driving further departures. Yet without tangible economic results, moderates will not linger forever. The result is a perfect vortex where an administration loses both its core and the middle. Breaking this vicious cycle requires painful clarity. The administration must rebuild its broken trust from the ground up. Expanding outward is necessary, but its premise must always be a solid alliance and open communication with the existing base—the very engine of reform.

Transparent explanations are overdue: Why were certain appointments made? Why is reform moving slowly? How far does the government intend to go? These questions require proof through policy results, not mere rhetoric. Getting the base to drop their arms and applaud again is the sole breakthrough available to revive governing momentum.

💭 Editor's Note: What Support Truly Means

Watching politics makes one realize how heavy and sorrowful the word "support" can be. The core base does not ask the president for blind praise.

They have endured simply on the hope of a shared dream—a country where common sense and principles stand tall. Yet once in power, administrations invariably hide those founding ideals behind the wall of "political reality."

Yu Si-min's sharp words sting the progressive camp because everyone understands the size of the regret behind them. An administration that fears criticism and drives away harsh voices will inevitably isolate itself.

Even now, it is hoped that the president and his aides will clear the pebble from their shoes and look squarely into the eyes of those walking away. When you think it is too late, that very moment may be your absolute last chance. 📉

At the crossroads where forward momentum has paused, South Korea is once again asking the deeper questions of democracy and reform. The drop in poll numbers is not just a political crisis; it is the painful sound of trust cracking among companions who walked the same road. If the administration ignores those warning lights and simply accelerates into the distance, what waits at the end of the road will not be a glorious triumph, but an empty parking lot. The courage to look back at one's initial motives and self-correct is the virtue this government needs most.
