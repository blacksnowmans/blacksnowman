The Crisis of Prosecution Reform: Is the Lee Jae-myung Administration Betraying Its Core Promise?

September 10, 2026

For years, the promise of "prosecution reform" has been the heartbeat of South Korean political discourse. It was the central demand of the Candlelight Revolution that brought the current administration to power: a mandate to dismantle the unchecked power of the Prosecutor’s Office, specifically by separating the authority to investigate from the authority to indict. Yet, as the administration moves forward with its latest restructuring plans, its supporters find themselves not cheering for victory, but gathering in the streets to protest what they call a "betrayal of the public’s mandate."

The Illusion of Reform: Rebranding Power

The government’s proposed restructuring of the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO)—an agency established to independently investigate high-level corruption—has sparked intense backlash. Critics argue that the reform is a superficial "rebranding" exercise that creates a monster more powerful than the original prosecution it was meant to check.

The restructuring plan introduces new internal divisions within the CIO, including a "judicial control unit" that would effectively elevate the office to a quasi-judicial status, and a "major crimes unit" that mandates cooperation with the traditional prosecution. Observers argue this is not reform, but a consolidation of power. Instead of independent oversight, the proposal creates a structure that integrates the CIO deeper into the existing prosecutorial system. Critics on the ground have bluntly called it a "prosecution organization with a new nameplate, only stronger."

Compounding this frustration is the revision of investigative guidelines that now allow prosecutors to summon detainees from police holding cells at will. This move effectively sidelines the police, asserting the prosecution's historical dominance over the investigative process. That these changes are being pushed through via presidential decree—bypassing the legislative debate that should naturally occur in the National Assembly—has left many questioning whether the government has any genuine intention of relinquishing prosecutorial control.

The Contradiction of Appointing a "Pro-Prosecution" Chief

If the structure of the reform is problematic, the personnel choices have been catastrophic for the administration’s credibility. The government’s nomination for the head of the newly proposed Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA)—a body intended to strip the prosecution of its direct investigation powers—is a career prosecutor who has historically opposed the very concept of separating investigation from indictment.

For the administration to appoint an individual who previously signed petitions against the removal of former Prosecutor General Yoon Suk-yeol is, in the eyes of supporters, an act of political suicide. It signals an intent to leave the "house keys" with the very people who have guarded the status quo for decades. Critics argue that this appointment effectively ensures the new agency will remain under the thumb of the traditional prosecution. As one party member noted, "Appointing a pro-prosecution hardliner to lead the reform agency is like asking the fox to guard the henhouse."

The history of the Korean prosecution is scarred by decades of selective justice and the infamous "cabinet politics"—the practice of shelving sensitive files on political allies while weaponizing investigations against rivals. By installing a "prosecution loyalist" at the helm of the new agency, the government risks continuing this cycle rather than breaking it.

🔥 Missing the Golden Time for Reform

South Korea stands at a critical juncture where the "legal cartel"—the incestuous network of former prosecutors, judges, and high-ranking officials—is becoming further entrenched. By delaying reforms until after local elections and party conventions, only to introduce these "pro-prosecution" strengthening measures, the administration is effectively draining its own political capital. If this opportunity is squandered, the damage to the democratic process may be irreversible.

The Financial Inequality of the Investigative Shift

The structural imbalance is further evidenced by the budget. As part of the ongoing "reform," approximately 170,000 pending cases are slated to be transferred from the prosecution to the police. However, the budget increase for police investigative costs is a meager 300 million won (about $225,000).

This lack of financial support for police infrastructure reveals a cynical reality: while the government talks about decentralizing power, it refuses to provide the resources necessary for the police to function independently. For nearly two years, the position of Commissioner General of the Korean National Police Agency remained vacant, a vacancy that speaks volumes about the government’s lack of commitment to building a viable alternative to the prosecution’s hegemony.

By creating a "reform" that leaves the police underfunded and the prosecution structurally dominant, the government is essentially setting the police up for failure. When the public inevitably criticizes the police for "incompetent investigations," the administration can use that as an excuse to return investigative powers to the prosecution. It is a dangerous game of political theater at the expense of justice.

A Call for Return to Core Values

President Lee Jae-myung’s administration was built on a promise of "fairness"—a vision where labor is rewarded, the legal cartel is dismantled, and the law applies equally to the powerful and the common citizen. Supporters who endured long protest vigils and hunger strikes for these ideals feel that these core visions are being sacrificed for a fragile political peace with the establishment.

On September 10, 2026, a coalition of grassroots Democratic Party organizations issued a desperate appeal to the president: "Remember the voices of the citizens who stood with you in the squares. It is not too late." This is not an attack from an enemy, but a final warning from the base that brought the president to power. The message is clear: true leadership does not involve negotiating with a cartel that has historically acted against the public interest. It involves the courage to dismantle it.

What to Watch Next

  • The MCIA Confirmation Hearings: Watch how the government handles the confirmation of the controversial nominee for the Major Crime Investigation Agency. If they push through this appointment despite fierce opposition, it will likely mark a permanent rift between the president and his core supporters.
  • National Assembly Maneuvers: Keep an eye on whether the government submits to pressure from the Democratic Party's grassroots to halt the restructuring of the CIO and the MCIA. Any pivot toward genuine, rather than cosmetic, reform will require immediate legislative transparency.
  • Police Resource Allocation: Monitor the next budget cycle. If the government continues to offload investigative burdens onto the police without significant increases in personnel and operational budgets, it will confirm the suspicion that the "reform" is designed to fail.

The time for "refining" the prosecution is over; the time for systemic separation has arrived. Whether the current administration chooses to be the architect of a fairer nation or a hostage to the legal establishment will be decided in the coming weeks. The citizens who powered the candlelight movement are still watching, and they are still waiting for the promise of a fair society to be kept.
