Apple’s $2,400 Foldable Experiment: Hands-On with the iPhone Duo’s Unfinished Ambition

October 26, 2026

Unboxing a smartphone that costs 3.29 million Korean won (approximately $2,400) brings a rare kind of anticipation. The iPhone Duo—Apple’s long-rumored entry into the foldable form factor—arrives wrapped in the minimalist design language tech enthusiasts have spent two decades admiring. In the hand, its matte nano-texture glass feels exceptionally sleek, and when the main display illuminates, the Under Display Camera (UDC) vanishes under active pixels with impressive visual polish. For a brief moment, it feels like the future of mobile hardware has finally arrived.

Then you actually start using it.

Within minutes of setting up the device, navigating basic system menus, and attempting light multitasking, the illusion of seamless innovation begins to fray. What emerges is not the polished, uncompromising experience consumers expect from Apple at this astronomical price point, but a classical "first-generation" product burdened by optical compromises, severe software optimization glitches, unsettling thermal spikes, and questionable durability. In South Korea—where Samsung’s Galaxy Z Fold series has had eight generations to mature into a refined daily driver—the iPhone Duo feels less like a finished flagship and more like an expensive invitation to be a beta tester.

Hardware Brilliance Meets Optical and Thermal Compromises

On paper, the display assembly of the iPhone Duo represents a significant engineering achievement. The UDC integration is remarkably clean; when displaying bright, uniform backgrounds, the punch-hole camera effectively disappears, seamless enough to draw genuine praise during initial inspection as visual elements fill the space.

However, Apple’s choice to equip the display with nano-texture glass introduces unexpected visual drawbacks. Etched at the nanometer level to scatter ambient light and reduce glare, nano-texture coating works brilliantly on stationary monitors like the Pro Display XDR or the iPad Pro. On a handheld phone that is constantly tilted and viewed from varying angles under mixed ambient lighting, the trade-offs become glaring.

Instead of deep, ink-like blacks, the screen produces a washed-out, milky gray in low-light environments as light scatters across the etched surface. White backgrounds exhibit a subtle rainbow-like "sparkle" or graininess that softens crisp text edges—an immediate annoyance for developers, writers, or professionals who rely on razor-sharp typography. Off-axis viewing angles reveal noticeable color shifts, sacrificing contrast ratio and color purity purely for anti-glare properties.

Thermals tell a similarly troubling story. Inside, Apple has included a broad vapor chamber designed to dissipate heat across the chassis. Yet, even during basic non-gaming tasks—such as initial phone setup, web browsing, or switching between system menus—the rear casing around the hinge rapidly heats up to over 37.0°C (98.6°F). While the stereo sound system delivers astonishing tactile haptics, transmitting rich speaker vibrations directly into your palms, that sensory delight is undercut by the uncomfortable warmth radiating from the back of the device.

Software Missteps and Domestic Friction

The rough edges extend deep into the software setup. During the initial migration process from an older iPhone, visual alignment errors appear in unexpected places. Text boxes for language and region selection sit awkwardly off-center rather than in the middle of the screen. Migration approval pop-ups skew to the edge or fail to rotate properly, accompanied by delays that freeze the transfer process entirely if not acknowledged immediately on the secondary device.

For users in South Korea, these software headaches are compounded by a controversial hardware decision: the total elimination of the physical SIM card tray. While carrier ecosystems in some Western markets have embraced eSIM technology, many power users and tech-savvy consumers in Korea frequently swap physical SIM cards between devices to navigate data plans across local carriers like SK Telecom, KT, and LG Uplus, or when using secondary handsets. Forcing an eSIM-only framework imposes extra re-issuance fees and administrative hassle on buyers, removing a practical flexibility many rely on daily.

Once past setup, daily iOS navigation on the new form factor feels uncharacteristically unpolished. Rotating the phone between portrait and landscape modes causes frequent display hangs, dropped animation frames, and visual stuttering. Even the lock screen's folding clock animation—designed to slide fluidly behind background elements as the display closes—regularly skips frames or snaps abruptly across the screen, breaking the cohesive visual language iOS is famous for.

The Multitasking Illusion and Unergonomic Layouts

Foldable smartphones live or die by their productivity capabilities. Users pay a massive premium expecting an expanded canvas that makes split-screen research, document editing, and side-by-side app usage effortless. On the iPhone Duo, however, software interface choices severely restrict that extra screen real estate.

Popular third-party applications remain largely unoptimized for the expanded screen ratio. Running YouTube leaves huge letterboxes and wasted empty space on either side of the video window. High-end mobile titles like Genshin Impact suffer from awkward cropping, cutting off UI elements rather than expanding the field of view.

More frustrating still is Apple's approach to multitasking. The system enforces a rigid 50:50 vertical split-screen ratio with thick, rounded window borders and generous margin paddings. When opening two applications side-by-side—such as comparing notes or running two AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude simultaneously—the actual readable content area becomes squeezed into narrow strips. Rotating the device into portrait mode breaks the active split-screen session entirely.

Typing in landscape mode highlights another bizarre design choice. Rather than placing the virtual keyboard comfortably along the bottom edge where a user's thumbs naturally rest, the keyboard shifts high toward the middle-upper section of the display. To type, users must stretch their thumbs awkwardly upward while supporting the heavy lower half of the device, creating an unbalanced grip that feels dangerously prone to accidental drops. When the keyboard does appear, it occupies more than half the display, squishing active split-screen applications into tiny, unreadable boxes.

Heavy in the Hand, Fragile at the Hinge

When compared to the broader foldable landscape, the physical compromises of the iPhone Duo become impossible to ignore. In South Korea, Samsung's Galaxy Z Fold 8 represents the benchmark of a mature design evolution. Holding a cased Galaxy Z Fold 8 in one hand and an un-cased iPhone Duo in the other reveals a stark contrast: Samsung’s foldable feels noticeably lighter, better balanced, and far more comfortable for extended one-handed holding. Apple’s entry feels unnecessarily heavy and dense, imposing physical wrist fatigue over prolonged use.

Durability remains an even darker shadow hanging over the device. Reports from early adopters across global community platforms like Reddit, as well as buyers in the US, Australia, and China, point to alarming hardware failures. Users have documented structural screen failures occurring within hours of unboxing—where opening the device produces an audible crack at the hinge, followed by persistent vertical lines or broken internal glass layers. In one reported case from the US, a unit purchased through Verizon failed after just three hours and roughly 20 folds.

These failures align with reports from supply chain observers indicating that early production yield rates at Foxconn hovered around 60%. A 60% manufacturing yield rate signals significant immaturity in assembly line tolerances, suggesting the internal hinge mechanism remains vulnerable to dust ingress, stress fractures, or alignment shifts. For a device priced at $2,400, buying without an expensive AppleCare+ coverage plan represents an extraordinary financial risk.

What to Watch Next

The iPhone Duo demonstrates that even a tech giant with unmatched supply chain leverage and silicon design capabilities cannot shortcut the hard physical realities of foldable hardware. While its Under Display Camera technology and dynamic audio haptics offer a tantalizing glimpse of engineering potential, the current product is severely weighed down by unoptimized software, thermal inefficiencies, unergonomic layout choices, and fragile first-generation mechanics.

For consumers seeking a reliable, high-end foldable experience right now, Samsung's Galaxy Z Fold 8 remains the vastly more practical, polished, and durable choice on the market.

As Apple works to address these shortcomings, several key developments will indicate whether the iPhone Duo platform can overcome its troubled launch:

  • iOS Software Patches: Watch for upcoming iOS updates to see if Apple delivers customizable split-screen ratios, fixes landscape keyboard positioning, and resolves frame-rate stutters during display rotation.
  • Supply Chain Yields: Monitor whether Foxconn can stabilize production yield rates beyond the reported 60% threshold, which will directly impact hinge reliability and long-term durability in retail units.
  • Hardware Revision Cycles: Keep an eye on whether Apple addresses thermal heat distribution and chassis weight in a second-generation hardware release, or if eSIM-only restrictions will continue to alienate international buyers in markets like South Korea.
