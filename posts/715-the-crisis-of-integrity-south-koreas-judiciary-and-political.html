The Crisis of Integrity: South Korea’s Judiciary and Political Class Under Fire

October 05, 2026

To watch the news in South Korea today is to witness a political landscape in a state of near-constant turbulence. From the formation of a special investigation unit to probe an alleged insurrection, to an escalating standoff with the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, and the endless cycle of inflammatory rhetoric from politicians, it is difficult to find a moment of stability. For those outside the country, these headlines might seem like disjointed scandals, but they are symptoms of a profound erosion of public trust in the institutions meant to uphold the rule of law.

When the mechanisms of justice and democratic oversight appear to be failing, it is necessary to look past the noise. We must examine the facts, the power structures at play, and why the Korean public—typically divided by deep-seated partisan loyalties—is finding rare common ground in its frustration.

The Push for an Unrestricted Special Prosecutor

A recent flashpoint has been the proposal to form a "Special Investigation Unit" to look into the insurrection allegations. Crucially, the public mandate for this probe is not limited by a timeframe. Despite the heavy, unconventional nature of a "limitless" investigation, recent polling suggests the public is overwhelmingly in favor. In one poll (CATI), 56.1% supported the move, while more aggressive polling (ARS) showed support as high as 69.6%.

What is most striking is that this support crosses party lines to a significant degree. While the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (the main opposition party) unsurprisingly supports a thorough investigation, the intensity of the desire for a "root-and-branch" inquiry reflects a shared national exhaustion. South Koreans are tired of high-profile cases being "wrapped up" prematurely by authorities to shield the powerful. This demand for an open-ended investigation is not just about a specific incident; it is an expression of anxiety that if judicial justice is not absolute, the entire foundation of the country's social contract is at risk.

The Standoff at the Supreme Court

Simultaneously, the judiciary—led by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—is facing its own crisis of legitimacy. A fierce conflict has emerged regarding the appointment of new Supreme Court justices. The Chief Justice has been accused of acting unilaterally, ignoring the necessity for coordination with the presidential office.

Data from major polling firms indicates that 55% of the public believes the Chief Justice should withdraw his current candidates and restart the process in consultation with the President. Even among supporters of the People Power Party (the conservative ruling party), a majority (54%) agrees that the current, unilateral approach is unacceptable. This signifies that the public no longer views the judiciary as a neutral arbiter, but rather as an institution that has become insular and potentially protective of its own interests.

The authority of a court does not come from the prestige of the robes its judges wear; it comes from public trust. When the leadership of the judiciary operates as if it is above the law, it invites public cynicism. The call for reform is no longer a fringe opinion; it has become a central demand for the health of the state.

Political Theater and the "Han Dong-hoon" Factor

Amidst these institutional crises, individual politicians are attempting to reshape the narrative to their advantage. Han Dong-hoon, a prominent figure within the ruling party and a former Justice Minister, has recently invoked the name of the late President Roh Moo-hyun, framing the current debate over the abolition of the prosecution's investigative powers as a result of "fake narratives" surrounding the former president.

Many observers view this as a strategic maneuver—an attempt to shore up the political influence of the prosecution while delegitimizing the reform movements that began under the Roh administration. By stirring up ideological conflict, politicians like Han are often accused of trying to distract from their own legal vulnerabilities. The strategy is familiar: create a "sideshow," invite a vicious response from the opposition, and then claim the mantle of a martyr under attack. When major legacy media outlets amplify these provocative soundbites, the political theater effectively masks the underlying issues of governance and potential corruption.

The "Meal Receipt" Scandal: A Symptom of Institutional Rot

Perhaps most damaging to the public's perception of the judiciary is the recent revelation concerning Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s office, often referred to in the press as the "meal receipt scam." Reports from outlets like MBC and Newstapa revealed that officials at the National Court Administration—the body that manages the judiciary's finances—allegedly inflated the number of participants in expensive meals to circumvent spending limits. For instance, a luxury dinner costing approximately $120 (160,000 KRW) per person was purportedly billed as multiple meals of $22 (30,000 KRW) to stay under internal audit thresholds.

This occurred over 25 times in a single year. While the amounts might seem small in the context of a national budget, the symbolism is devastating. When the highest legal authority in the land is caught engaging in what essentially amounts to petty fraud, it shatters the image of integrity the judiciary is supposed to project. The refusal of leadership to take responsibility—instead pointing fingers at subordinates—has only deepened the public’s anger.

What to Watch Next

As these stories unfold, the focus must remain on two specific areas:

  • Legislative Action: Keep a close watch on whether the National Assembly successfully advances the bill to abolish or reform the National Court Administration. Without a structural change to how the judiciary manages its own budget and administration, transparency will remain elusive.
  • The Investigation Timeline: The "limitless" special investigation will face immense political pressure. Whether it maintains its momentum or is slowly stifled by political maneuvering will be the ultimate test of South Korea’s current judicial independence.

The core issue facing South Korea is not just a series of isolated scandals, but a persistent disconnect between the political and judicial elite and the citizens they serve. In a society where the basic expectation that "wrongdoing leads to consequences" is being tested, the upcoming months will be a critical indicator of whether the country can steer back toward institutional accountability or continue to slide into a culture of performative, polarized politics.
