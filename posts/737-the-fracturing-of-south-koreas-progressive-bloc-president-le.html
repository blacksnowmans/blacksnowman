The Fracturing of South Korea’s Progressive Bloc: President Lee Jae-myung’s Digital War

September 13, 2026

If you have been following South Korean politics lately, the digital landscape feels like a powder keg. President Lee Jae-myung—the former provincial governor and current leader of South Korea, known for his populist roots—has recently taken to social media in a series of outbursts that have left even his strongest supporters uneasy. What began as a platform for direct communication has transformed into a theater of political combat, as the President clashes openly with former allies and internal critics.

For a foreign observer, the current chaos within the administration may seem like a sudden rupture. However, it is the culmination of long-simmering tensions between the President’s inner circle and the broader progressive coalition that helped carry him to power. As the administration’s approval ratings fluctuate, the question remains: is this merely a rough patch in the reform process, or is the administration losing its grip on the very public sentiment that put it in office?

The Social Media Blitz and the War Within

The latest escalation began on September 12, when President Lee issued a scathing message on his social media accounts. He declared, “The biggest obstacle to reform is those who disrupt it in the name of reform,” and warned against “reactionary forces” that undermine his agenda. Most pointedly, he criticized the habit of appointing unqualified allies to public office, stating that doing so is a “shortcut to failure” and a sign of a “conqueror, not a public servant.”

The reaction was immediate. Park Tae-hoon, a former spokesperson for the Progressive Party (a minor left-wing party often aligned with broader progressive goals), took to Facebook to defend the President, attacking internal critics like Rhyu Si-min—a legendary figure in the South Korean liberal movement and a former Minister of Health and Welfare. Park accused Rhyu and others of “shaking the President from within” and engaging in “curse-like rhetoric.” President Lee signaled his approval by reposting Park’s statement.

When a government begins to label internal dissent as "sabotage," it risks losing the essential democratic capacity for compromise. History in South Korea, from the Joseon Dynasty’s factional feuds to the internal conflicts of the participatory government under former President Roh Moo-hyun, shows that ideological struggle is part of the territory. However, treating healthy internal critique as a personal "curse" is a classic hallmark of political isolationism.

The Rhyu Si-min Conflict and the "Daejang-dong" Appointments

The most prominent critic of this new, abrasive tone is Rhyu Si-min himself. During appearances on popular YouTube channels like Maebul Show, Rhyu lamented the shift in the "atmosphere" at the Blue House (the South Korean presidential office). He claimed that the President has become openly hostile toward him and that those who “shred” his reputation are the ones currently favored by the administration. Rhyu warned that the President’s tendency to hand-pick party leaders is reminiscent of a bygone era of “royal or aristocratic politics,” predicting that approval ratings could soon slide into the 30% range.

Adding fuel to this fire is the controversy surrounding the President’s inner circle. Despite his public declarations that he would not hire based on cronyism, the administration has faced intense backlash for appointing a large number of lawyers associated with the "Daejang-dong scandal"—a massive, years-long real estate development controversy that dogged Lee during his time as a mayor and presidential candidate.

The list of appointees, which includes figures now serving as presidential secretaries and members of the National Assembly, has sparked accusations of naeronambul—a uniquely Korean term describing the "if I do it, it’s romance; if you do it, it’s an affair" hypocrisy. For many voters, this has undermined the President’s message of "fairness and meritocracy." While the administration claims its institutional reforms—such as the creation of a new investigative agency—are on track for an October 2026 launch, critics argue these are bureaucratic distractions that lack real, tangible deadlines for justice.

Why Approval Ratings are Sinking

The numbers tell a sobering story. According to Gallup Korea, President Lee started his term with a robust 58% approval rating, peaking at 67% in early 2026. However, that figure has steadily trended downward to 61% and continues to struggle. The consensus among political analysts is that the decline is driven by the administration’s obsession with political infighting over the tangible, day-to-day economic struggles of the people.

In the beginning, the President won public favor by focusing on practical, granular issues: safety, labor conditions, and economic stability. Now, the discourse is dominated by talk of constitutional amendments, party leadership elections, and high-stakes prosecutor reforms. While these issues are important, they do not pay the bills. When the cost of living rises and jobs remain scarce, voters tend to punish leaders who prioritize political maneuvering over "min-saeng" (the livelihood of the people).

Reforming the powerful and often unchecked prosecution service is a vital goal for South Korea, but the current administration’s approach feels more like a rhetorical battle than a structural fix. To succeed, the President must demonstrate the courage to relinquish some of his own power rather than simply consolidating control under the guise of "reform."

What to Watch Next

  • Approval Trends: Keep a close eye on the next round of Gallup Korea polls. If the numbers slip into the 40s, the pressure on the President to reshuffle his inner circle of "Daejang-dong lawyers" will become overwhelming.
  • The "Progressive" Schism: Watch for whether figures like Rhyu Si-min continue to be sidelined or if there is a tactical reconciliation. The progressive bloc cannot afford a permanent divorce between its intellectual base and its executive power.
  • Economic Benchmarks: The administration’s focus on long-term institutional reform will likely remain secondary to inflation and job growth in the eyes of the public. Any further dip in these economic indicators will force the President to either pivot to the economy or face potential electoral consequences in the future.

Ultimately, the President’s success depends on his ability to remember that power is not a prize to be defended against internal critics, but a tool to serve the public. If he continues to view the world through the narrow lens of factional warfare, he risks losing the trust of the very voters who once saw him as the champion of the common person.
