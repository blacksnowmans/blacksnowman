Black Snowman

The Memo That Exposed South Korea's Ruling Party Fractures

September 10, 2026

Picture the grand, vaulted hall of the National Assembly in Seoul. The television cameras sweep across the rows of lawmakers, capturing the usual theater of parliamentary debate. But on one particular day, a single, carelessly held notepad laid bare the quiet, high-stakes civil war consuming South Korea's political establishment. For a governing party that swept into power promising reform and unity, the mood inside and outside the tent has turned remarkably bitter.

When the leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea sat at the back of the chamber and deliberately held up an open notebook for the press to capture, it was no accident. In South Korean politics, symbols and leaked intentions are a language of their own. While the public eye was immediately drawn to provocative scribbles about corporate giants and cabinet reshuffles, a far more revealing section of the page listed potential candidates for the upcoming Seoul mayoral race—complete with names, political calculations, and an unmistakable undercurrent of internal maneuvering.

The Seoul Mayoral Memo as a Political Weapon

The names scrawled in the notebook—veteran lawmakers and party figures—map out the raw, unvarnished power dynamics of the Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party. Party leader Kim Min-seok insisted that none of the individuals named had been consulted beforehand. Rather than acting as a defense, that admission confirmed just how unilateral and calculated the memo truly was. Listing high-profile figures like former party leader Chung Cheong-rae as potential mayoral candidates raised immediate alarm bells.

Chung has repeatedly and publicly stated he has no interest in running for Mayor of Seoul. By drafting him into the race anyway, critics argue the leadership was playing a cold game of preemptive political elimination. The Seoul mayoral post is widely viewed as a poisoned chalice in the upcoming local elections—a grueling race with a high probability of defeat. By pre-assigning potential rivals to a high-risk battleground, the dominant faction within the party could neatly pin the blame for an inevitable loss onto its internal critics.

Chung did not let the slight pass in silence, taking to social media to lash out at what he called cruel psychological games and public humiliation. When victors in internal power struggles begin turning their weapons on their own allies, the rhetoric of party unity starts to ring hollow. It is a calculated display of political engineering that leaves observers wondering who, exactly, benefits from such casual cruelty.

Alliances, Ambitions, and the New Power Axis

Another name featured prominently in the leaked notes was National Assembly Speaker Woo Won-shik. Once known as a reform-minded champion of small merchants and labor rights, Speaker Woo now appears to be aligning himself firmly with the ascendant faction of the party. Having reached the pinnacle of the legislative branch, a politician's thoughts naturally turn to what comes next. Much like previous parliamentary leaders who transitioned into prime ministerial roles during past administrations, Woo's positioning strongly suggests he has his eye on the next prime minister's office.

This steady concentration of power tells a familiar story: a shifting landscape of survival and ambition. As the leadership and its inner circle consolidate control, the diversity of opinion that once defined the party is being systematically ironed out. Comrades who once stood shoulder-to-shoulder against conservative opposition are now being managed out of the picture, replaced by a cold arithmetic of political survival.

Alienating the Grassroots and the Erosion of Trust

The fallout from this inward-facing factionalism extends far beyond elite circles, deeply alienating the party's traditional base. Across the country, long-standing supporters who formed the backbone of the liberal movement no longer feel like proud members of a democratic coalition. Instead, many feel branded as internal dissidents and systematically sidelined.

The psychological contract that bound the party together—the idea of a shared destiny against entrenched conservative power—is fraying. The current leadership demands uncritical applause, viewing any divergence in tone or policy as an act of betrayal. But loyalty cannot be successfully commanded when communication breaks down. A leadership divorced from its grassroots is building a castle on sand.

This institutional rot is also bleeding into basic administrative competence. A recent scandal involving official party promotional videos revealed that the content creator hired by the party had a history of hurling vicious abuse at former liberal presidents and opposition figures. When the controversy broke, mid-level staffers paralyzed by fear of the upper echelons simply looked the other way, paralyzed by bureaucratic self-preservation. When even basic vetting fails, public anger is an inevitable consequence.

Presidential Approval Ratings and the Cost of Arrogance

The political price of this insular culture is already showing up clearly in the data. According to a Hangil Research poll conducted in September 2026, the president's positive job performance rating dropped to 36.4%, while negative evaluations climbed to 62.1%. Most damningly, 40.1% of respondents stated there was "no policy at all" that the administration had handled well.

In the honeymoon period of a new administration, soaring approval ratings grant a government immense political capital; even controversial cabinet appointments are forgiven by a hopeful public. But when approval ratings bottom out, every policy initiative faces immediate, fierce resistance. The administration's focus on protecting its inner circle and neutralizing internal rivals has blinded it to the basic art of governing for the wider public.

The public did not hand this administration power so it could indulge in petty score-settling and internal purges. They wanted stability, competence, and a leadership capable of uniting a divided country.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward upcoming local elections and battles persistent economic anxiety, several concrete indicators will determine whether the ruling party can pull itself out of its current tailspin:

  • Cabinet and Staff Reshuffles: Watch for whether the administration replaces figures tied to recent administrative blunders and factional infighting with broader, neutral technocrats.
  • Legislative Tone on Key Reforms: Pay attention to whether the leadership doubles down on partisan messaging or opens genuine dialogue with moderate voters on pressing livelihood issues like real estate and inflation.
  • Internal Dissident Movements: Monitor how sidelined factions within the Democratic Party organize ahead of candidate nominations for the local elections, which will test the leadership's grip on local machinery.

Politics remains an art built entirely on trust. When leaders begin treating their own supporters as adversaries and prioritize internal maneuvering over public welfare, the damage goes deep. Without a sharp pivot back to genuine, inclusive governance, the power currently held by the ruling camp may prove as fragile as ink running off a stolen notebook.
