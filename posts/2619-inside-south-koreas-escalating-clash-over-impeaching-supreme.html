Inside South Korea’s Escalating Clash Over Impeaching Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae

November 22, 2026

On November 21, South Korean politics spent nearly twelve straight hours locked in a high-stakes standoff between the country’s legislature and its highest court. Starting just after noon and continuing until 11:21 p.m., no fewer than 18 major news headlines tracked a rapid escalation between South Korea’s two dominant political camps. According to reports from Korean outlets including Kukje News, YTN, JTBC, and the Seoul Economic Daily, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea formally adopted the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae as its official party platform, while the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, launched street protests and accused the government’s allies of trying to dismantle judicial independence.

For readers outside South Korea, a move to impeach the sitting chief justice of the Supreme Court may sound extraordinary—and in Seoul, it is. The confrontation is not merely about one judge’s tenure. As coverage throughout the day demonstrated, it sits at the intersection of separation-of-powers disputes, unfinished legal reckonings over insurrection allegations, and fierce partisan battles surrounding sitting President Lee Jae-myung, who hails from the Democratic Party.

Understanding why a single day of announcements and counterattacks dominated the national conversation requires looking closely at how South Korea’s political system operates. The Democratic Party holds a legislative majority in the National Assembly, giving it the numbers to advance an impeachment motion on its own if it chooses to act as a bloc. Under South Korea’s constitutional framework, once the National Assembly passes an impeachment bill, the targeted official is suspended from duty until the Constitutional Court rules on whether to confirm or dismiss the removal. When a ruling majority signals it is ready to trigger that mechanism against the head of the judiciary, every word, date, and countermove carries institutional weight.

The Democratic Party’s December 3 Target and Its Insurrection Rationale

The flashpoint began at 12:14 p.m. on November 21, when Kukje News—followed soon after by YTN and iNews24—reported that the Democratic Party had elevated the impeachment of Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae to an official party platform and begun mobilizing to push it forward. Less than half an hour later, M Economy News reported growing calls inside political circles for "immediate impeachment," and by 4:04 p.m., iNews24 noted that the ruling party had made clear its goal of pursuing the motion within the calendar year.

In South Korean parliamentary politics, moving an issue from individual lawmakers’ criticism to an official party platform is a decisive shift. It signals that party leadership is organizing legislative schedules and whipping votes for an actual floor decision rather than merely making a rhetorical point.

By mid-afternoon, that broad intent had hardened into a specific calendar target. In three separate bulletins at 2:01 p.m., 4:01 p.m., and 6:16 p.m., broadcaster YTN reported that a consensus had formed within the Democratic Party to file the impeachment motion around December 3. At 6:56 p.m., JTBC similarly reported an internal view within the ruling party that "December 3 is just right." Broadcaster Channel A added at 6:49 p.m. that some voices inside the Democratic Party were even discussing pairing the impeachment push with a criminal investigation.

Setting a target date shifted the conflict from abstract warnings to an imminent legislative timetable. For the ruling party, aligning around December 3 appeared aimed at maximizing political symbolism while syncing with the year-end National Assembly calendar. For opponents, however, putting both a firm date and the prospect of an investigation on the table looked like direct pressure on the bench.

Throughout the afternoon, multiple outlets reported the core justification the Democratic Party put forward for taking such a sweeping step. At 1:47 p.m., Gwangju Daily News, followed by the Seoul Economic Daily at 4:08 p.m. and Yonhap News TV at 4:52 p.m., reported that the ruling party anchored its case on a single principle: there must be "no impunity for insurrection." Democratic Party figures framed the proposed impeachment as a necessary measure to uphold constitutional order and enforce accountability, arguing that judicial attitudes or decisions must not result in forgiving insurrection-related matters.

The Opposition’s Counteroffensive: Street Protests and Presidential Trial Claims

The People Power Party’s pushback was immediate and unsparing. Within minutes of the initial party-platform report, Kukje News quoted the conservative opposition dismissing the Democratic Party’s move as "100% pure nonsense." At 12:44 p.m., M Economy News reported that the opposition had branded the effort "political retaliation." Across the afternoon—in Gwangju Daily News, iNews24, the Seoul Economic Daily, and Yonhap News TV—opposition lawmakers repeatedly characterized the campaign as "retaliatory impeachment" and "retaliatory group lynching."

Beyond attacking the legal merit of the impeachment drive, the People Power Party moved quickly to tie the effort directly to President Lee Jae-myung. At 1:36 p.m., Sisa Focus reported that the opposition was rallying around the claim that the ruling party’s true goal was "to erase Lee’s trials." Later in the afternoon, iNews24 highlighted opposition descriptions of the move as "retaliatory group lynching" connected to the president. By linking the chief justice’s fate to Lee’s legal exposure, the opposition sought to reframe the clash not as a matter of constitutional accountability, but as an effort by the governing camp to shield its own leader from judicial scrutiny.

By early evening, some opposition rhetoric escalated into an explicit counter-threat. At 6:56 p.m., JTBC reported that certain figures within the People Power Party had responded to the ruling party’s December 3 timetable by arguing that "the president should be impeached first."

Recognizing that its minority status in the National Assembly limits its ability to block a majority vote on the floor, the People Power Party also took its campaign outside parliament. In reports at 12:19 p.m., 2:01 p.m., and 4:01 p.m., YTN paired updates on the ruling party’s impeachment timetable with coverage of the opposition launching out-of-parliament street protests to rally public opinion. Meanwhile, Internet Times reported at 3:50 p.m. on an opposition warning that even if the Constitutional Court were to dismiss an impeachment against Chief Justice Jo, the Democratic Party would respond by moving to impeach Constitutional Court justices as well—a claim framed to warn voters of cascading institutional instability.

Shaking the Judiciary or Enforcing Accountability? How Harsh Rhetoric Crowded the Debate

As the story moved into the evening news cycle, coverage centered on the broader constitutional stakes. At 6:16 p.m., YTN reported that the People Power Party condemned the December 3 plan as "destruction of constitutional order." Channel A contrasted the ruling party’s talk of an investigation with the opposition’s accusation of an "impeachment-addicted Democratic Party," while the Energy Economic News reported at 6:50 p.m. that the opposition defined the entire push as "shaking the judiciary."

At its core, this framing battle reflects a deep disagreement over where democratic accountability ends and judicial independence begins. The ruling party argues that an elected legislative majority has a duty to pursue constitutional remedies when it believes the judiciary is failing to uphold accountability on grave matters. The opposition counters that a majority party using impeachment to punish or pressure the head of the courts violates the separation of powers and threatens to bend the judiciary to partisan will.

Yet substantive legal debate was frequently overshadowed on November 21 by personal invective—most notably from People Power Party lawmaker Chung Jeom-sik. At 2:47 p.m., OhmyNews reported that Chung denounced the Democratic Party’s initiative as "the worst low-grade impeachment in constitutional history" and mocked the ruling party with the Korean slang term tanhaeng-nyangi. Roughly translated as "impeachment-kitty," the nickname blends the word for impeachment with an internet-style diminutive for a cat, portraying the ruling party as compulsively meowing for impeachments.

The remark gained rapid traction. It was cited by Internet Times at 3:50 p.m. and featured prominently by Maeil Shinmun at 6:52 p.m. By 11:21 p.m., the final headline of the day came from Jeon Han-gil News, a conservative outlet that amplified Chung’s attack under a sharper framing of targeting the "tanhaeng-nyangi group."

As slogans like "retaliatory group lynching," "impeachment-kitty group," and "impeach the president first" collided with calls for immediate impeachment and parallel investigations, the concrete constitutional criteria required to remove a Supreme Court chief justice risked disappearing from public view.

That rapid migration of provocative insults—from parliamentary remarks to mainstream evening broadcasts and late-night partisan media—illustrates a recurring dynamic in South Korean politics. Sharp nicknames and sweeping accusations energize core supporters, but they make it far harder for voters to evaluate the actual legal merits of an unprecedented constitutional procedure.

What to Watch as December Approaches

With both camps dug in after November 21’s marathon confrontation, the coming weeks will determine whether the standoff turns into a full constitutional crisis or settles into protracted parliamentary warfare. Several concrete developments will serve as key indicators:

  • Whether a motion is filed around December 3: The immediate test is whether the Democratic Party follows through on its internal consensus and formally submits an impeachment bill against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae to the National Assembly.
  • The exact legal grounds in the draft bill: Until the text of the impeachment motion is made public, it remains unclear precisely what constitutional violations or judicial actions the ruling party will cite to back up its "no impunity for insurrection" argument.
  • Movement on a parallel investigation: Observers will be watching whether the mention of a criminal investigation reported by Channel A remains informal talk among individual lawmakers or becomes an official ruling-party initiative.
  • Committee clashes and plenary scheduling: Any formal motion will trigger fierce legal and procedural battles inside the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee and ahead of any floor vote, which would automatically suspend Jo’s duties if passed and send the case to the Constitutional Court.
  • The scale of opposition street protests: Whether the People Power Party’s out-of-parliament rallies remain symbolic demonstrations or expand to disrupt December legislative business will shape both public opinion and the broader political climate.

If an impeachment bill does reach the floor and pass, South Korea will enter rare constitutional territory: a suspended Supreme Court chief justice awaiting judgment from the Constitutional Court while the ruling party and opposition remain locked in conflict over President Lee Jae-myung’s leadership and the boundaries of judicial independence. How carefully both sides handle the legal substance—rather than the slogans—will matter long after December 3 passes.
