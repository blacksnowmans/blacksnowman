A Multi-Front Political War: Inside South Korea’s High-Stakes Assembly Clash

November 17, 2026

Outside the gates of Cheong Wa Dae—the presidential office housing the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—lawmakers from South Korea’s main conservative opposition party gathered on November 16 for an impromptu rally, their second consecutive week taking their grievances directly to the executive doorstep. Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, committee rooms dissolved into shouting matches over top-secret expense budgets, while makeshift protest tents sprouted on the legislative lawn. Across every major front of South Korean politics—the judiciary, the national budget, and internal party management—the legislative engine has ground to a tense, volatile standstill.

With a critical plenary session set for November 19, the South Korean political landscape has entered a state of maximum friction. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is attempting to ram through key legislative items and budget adjustments to protect the political momentum of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Meanwhile, the main opposition People Power Party (PPP), flanked by minor opposition groups, has launched a full-court press to block executive-backed legislation, cut off unvouched presidential spending, and turn the ruling party’s internal ethics scandals into a crippling vulnerability. Understanding how these distinct battles interlock is essential for anyone tracking South Korean political stability in the months ahead.

Judicial Warfare: Special Counsel Bills, Budget Slashing, and Impeachment Tents

The immediate catalyst for the crisis is a contentious piece of legislation known as the "Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel Bill." Designed by the ruling DPK to appoint an independent prosecutor to investigate alleged misconduct and politically motivated indictments by state prosecutors under previous administrations, the bill has become a flashpoint for systemic warfare between the legislative and judicial branches. DPK lawmakers argue the measure is vital to dismantle lingering prosecutorial overreach and shield President Lee Jae-myung’s reform agenda. The bill is slated for crucial committee and plenary votes on November 17 and 19.

The opposition PPP has mobilized aggressively against the bill, directing its sharpest attacks toward Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. Conservative leadership warned that if the Prime Minister fails to recommend a presidential veto against the special counsel legislation, he will be regarded by the public as an accomplice to a "legislative jailbreak." PPP strategists insist the bill is an unconstitutional attempt by the majority party to subvert court proceedings and intimidate judges handling sensitive political trials.

The conflict has already spilled directly into judicial funding and independence:

  • Courts hit in the wallet: In the Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee, DPK lawmakers unilaterally voted to slash 324 million won (about $230,000) earmarked for Supreme Court justices' discretionary encouragement allowances, citing a lack of transparency in how the high court disburses internal bonuses.
  • Impeachment tents on the lawn: The minor progressive Rebuilding Korea Party, led by figures aligned against the judicial establishment, established a protest encampment inside National Assembly grounds. Their demands include the immediate impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the swift passage of a revised Democratic Veterans Act.
  • Opposition backlash: The PPP condemned the budget cut and the impeachment campaign as a coordinated, heavy-handed campaign to threaten the judiciary and compromise trial impartiality ahead of pending court verdicts.

Battling Over the Executive Purse: Off-the-Books Funds and Committee Chaos

While the judicial front burned, the House Steering Committee descended into chaos over the 2027 fiscal budget for the Presidential Office at Cheong Wa Dae. At the center of the clash is the enduringly controversial category of state spending known as "Special Activity Funds" (teuk-hwal-bi)—discretionary, off-the-books expense accounts historically used by executive bodies, intelligence agencies, and presidential offices with minimal itemized documentation.

Opposition PPP committee members demanded an absolute, 100 percent reduction of the presidential office’s special activity budget as well as its diplomatic travel allocations. Conservative lawmakers argued that administration officials had submitted wildly inadequate accounting records, making it impossible to verify how taxpayer funds were being utilized behind closed doors. The PPP also raised questions regarding unverified reports surrounding executive use of private residence golf facilities.

In response, presidential aides and DPK legislators insisted that all necessary compliance documentation had been delivered within legal parameters. They warned that gutting executive discretionary funds and diplomatic travel budgets would paralyze routine state operations and undermine South Korea's international standing. The sessions were repeatedly suspended as shouting erupted over the appearance and testimony of key agency heads, including Kim Tae-gyu, the acting chairman of the Korea Communications Commission, whose eligibility and past remarks were fiercely challenged by ruling party lawmakers.

Cracks in the Majority: The Choi Min-hee Ethics Scandal and Internal Feuds

Compounding the legislative paralysis is a severe internal crisis shaking the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. A brewing ethics scandal centered on senior DPK lawmaker Choi Min-hee has exploded into open factional warfare, threatening party cohesion just as the leadership needs unified voting power in the Assembly.

The controversy deepened after Representative Choi performed a dramatic, formal 90-degree bow during a DPK Supreme Council meeting, publicly apologizing for "causing trouble for the President." However, immediately following her apology, Choi resumed provocative public statements, including demands for the release of membership lists related to the controversial Shincheonji religious organization. The mixed signaling ignited outrage among key internal factions.

The apology failed to calm the waters. Instead, it triggered a rare, organized revolt from within the ruling party’s freshman ranks, exposing deep ideological and strategic fractures between party loyalists and reformist backbenchers.

A caucus of first-term DPK lawmakers known as "The Mincho" (Deo-min-cho) launched a formal petition drive demanding that the party’s Ethics Tribunal impose severe sanctions on Choi, advocating for at least a suspension of her party membership rights. The backbenchers argued that soft-pedaling ethical lapses degrades public trust and drags down approval ratings. However, pro-Lee loyalists within the party leadership—such as lawmaker Han Min-soo—swiftly struck back, accusing the freshman caucus of executing an internal "coup" and improperly pressuring an independent ethics body.

This internal civil war has placed DPK Senior Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok and PPP Floor Leader Jang Dong-hyeok in complex positions. Both leaders must manage internal dissent within their respective ranks while attempting to project strength across the aisle. Recent tracking polls from major agencies such as Realmeter and Gallup Korea indicate that swing voters are growing disillusioned with the parliamentary deadlock, contributing to a slide in support for both the ruling DPK and President Lee Jae-myung.

Secondary Fronts: Constitutional Overhaul and Military Deployment Debates

As if budget battles and ethics scandals were not enough, two peripheral controversies emerged on November 16 to further complicate potential compromises ahead of the upcoming plenary session.

First, National Assembly Speaker Jo Jeong-sik formally launched a Constitutional Reform Advisory Committee, declaring that next year represents the ideal window to amend South Korea's 1987 Constitution. Proposed topics include restructuring executive power dynamics and ending the single-term presidential constraint. However, the opposition PPP flatly rejected the initiative on arrival, dismissing the constitutional talk as a calculated smoke screen designed to distract the public from the DPK’s legal vulnerabilities and special counsel push. Even elements within the ruling party expressed skepticism regarding the sudden timing of the proposal.

Second, foreign policy friction erupted over international security commitments. Civic groups and minor party figures staged rallies outside the National Assembly demanding the explicit rejection of any parliamentary consent motions concerning indirect overseas military deployments. Kim Jun-hyung, a prominent lawmaker from the Rebuilding Korea Party, publicly criticized the administration’s communication surrounding foreign military engagements, insisting that any indirect involvement in foreign conflicts requires strict, transparent legislative authorization. Though the administration dismissed the allegations as political grandstanding, security debates have now been woven into the November 19 legislative matrix.

What to Watch Next: Flashpoints for the November 19 Plenary Vote

As South Korea approaches the November 19 plenary session, the political landscape remains stretched to its absolute limit. With no middle ground established during committee markups, the coming days will test the structural resilience of the Assembly. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Plenary Vote: Watch whether the ruling DPK uses its legislative majority to pass the Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel Bill alongside the contested presidential budget allocations on November 19.
  • Executive Veto Posture: Track whether Prime Minister Han Duck-soo signals an intent to advise President Lee Jae-myung to exercise veto power should the special counsel legislation pass without opposition consent.
  • DPK Ethics Tribunal Ruling: Monitor the internal discipline proceedings against Lawmaker Choi Min-hee. A lenient sanction could trigger further public backlash from the freshman "Mincho" caucus, while severe punishment could alienate hardline party loyalists.
  • Voter Sentiment and Polling: Watch for upcoming polling data to see whether middle-of-the-road voters penalize the ruling party for aggressive legislative tactics or punish the opposition for total non-cooperation on the budget.
