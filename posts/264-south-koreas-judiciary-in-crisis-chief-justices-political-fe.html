South Korea’s Judiciary in Crisis: Chief Justice’s Political Feud and Expense Account Scandals

September 29, 2026

Picture walking into a quiet, wood-paneled courtroom where the arbiters of the law are supposed to sit above the fray of day-to-day politics. Now imagine pulling back the curtain to find those same judges playing high-stakes political poker, inflating restaurant bills with public money, and treating the country's constitutional checks and balances as optional guidelines. This is not a legal thriller script; it is the current reality unfolding within South Korea's highest courts.

South Korea’s judicial branch—long viewed by the public as a solemn temple of legal independence—is facing an unprecedented constitutional crisis. At the center of the storm is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, whose recent standoffs with the executive branch and revelations of petty, systemic expense-account padding have ignited a nationwide debate. For foreign observers trying to understand South Korean politics, this moment goes far beyond bureaucratic squabbling. It exposes the deep, unresolved tensions between a conservative-leaning legal establishment and progressive political power, raising urgent questions about who actually controls the levers of justice in Seoul.

A Chief Justice Playing Politics: The Standoff Over Supreme Court Appointments

The immediate catalyst for the crisis is a bitter dispute between the judiciary and the presidential office over high-level judicial appointments. Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae recently blocked the appointment of Song Bong-gi, a nominee backed by the presidential administration, to the Supreme Court. The Chief Justice's stated justification—arguing that the administration's request for re-nomination lacked clear administrative paperwork—has been widely criticized as a flimsy procedural pretext.

Critics argue that Cho is using legal technicalities as a smokescreen to directly challenge the president’s constitutional appointment powers. During parliamentary audits, the National Court Administration floundered when pressed by lawmakers, giving evasive answers that highlighted the absurdity of the standoff. While acknowledging that the president holds the ultimate authority to appoint Supreme Court justices, judicial officials simultaneously claimed that official communications regarding the appointment were too ambiguous to act upon.

This is legal maneuvering at its most cynical. By weaponizing administrative rules, Chief Justice Cho appears to be signaling a political defiance reminiscent of past conservative figures who leveraged state institutions to clash with progressive governments. Commentators have been quick to compare Cho’s trajectory to that of Chae Jae-hyung, a former head of the Board of Audit and Inspection who resigned to dive directly into conservative partisan politics under the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party). When the men who wear the black robes of impartiality begin acting like opposition politicians, the boundary between the rule of law and partisan warfare dissolves completely.

Judges should be the final guardians of constitutional rights, not political combatants looking to score points against the executive branch. Using judicial authority to wage turf wars erodes public trust in the entire legal system.

Lobster Dinners and Inflated Headcounts: The Expense Account Scandal

If the high-level constitutional showdown shows the judiciary playing politics, recent investigative reporting by MBC has exposed a much more mundane, yet deeply damaging, form of institutional rot: expense account fraud.

Audits of the judiciary’s official operational expenses revealed that Chief Justice Cho and other top judges engaged in systematic padding of meal headcounts. At upscale restaurants in wealthy neighborhoods like Bangbae-dong, dinners officially recorded as hosting dozens of attendees—sometimes soaring from an actual 9 people up to a falsified 33—were routinely submitted to evade strict per-person spending caps. In short, public money meant for official government work was being manipulated so judges could enjoy high-end meals on the taxpayer dime.

The scandal is laced with embarrassing details. In one instance, an expensive *omakase* meal was officially logged as costing a modest 28,000 won (about $20) per person, when investigative checks revealed that the restaurant's cheapest menu item was 35,000 won—and that item was strictly a child's portion. This casual disregard for transparency sits uneasily alongside previous controversies, such as the judiciary spending over 100 million won (about $72,000) of public funds to renovate the Chief Justice's official residence.

When the people tasked with interpreting the law treat state budgets like a personal slush fund, it betrays a toxic sense of entitlement. It suggests a legal elite that views itself as entirely above public scrutiny.

The Structural Problem: Dismantling the Imperial Chief Justice

These scandals are not merely individual moral failures; they are the predictable product of a deeply centralized system. For years, critics have called for the abolition or radical restructuring of the National Court Administration—the powerful bureaucratic arm that gives the Chief Justice near-total control over the assignments, promotions, and evaluations of lower-court judges.

So long as a single individual holds unchecked administrative power over the career trajectories of every judge in the country, a chilling effect permeates the courts. Lower-court judges report an atmosphere where dissent is stifled and speaking truth to power can ruin a career. The "imperial Chief Justice" model turns the judiciary into a top-down hierarchy rather than an independent network of impartial arbiters.

Proposals to dismantle the administration and replace it with a more independent judicial administrative committee have repeatedly stalled due to conservative resistance within the legal establishment. However, the current compounding crises make it increasingly clear that the judiciary cannot be trusted to reform itself.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this multi-layered crisis, several key developments will determine whether real accountability takes hold:

  • Parliamentary Audits and Investigations: Watch how opposition and ruling lawmakers handle the upcoming budget and audit sessions. If expense-account fraud is quietly swept under the rug as a " bureaucratic custom," public backlash will likely intensify.
  • The Fate of Judicial Reforms: Keep an eye on legislative moves to dismantle or reform the National Court Administration. Any genuine push to strip the Chief Justice of absolute personnel control will signal a structural turning point.
  • Broader Prosecutorial and Investigative Shifts: As new investigative bodies—such as the newly launching Major Crimes Investigation Office—take shape, the public will be watching closely to see whether institutional watchdogs can maintain true independence from both executive and judicial overreach.

South Korea’s legal system stands at a crossroads. Unless the culture of unchecked privilege and political posturing is confronted head-on, the public's faith in the fundamental promise that the law applies equally to all will remain severely fractured.
