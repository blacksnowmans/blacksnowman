South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Faces a Crisis as Approval Ratings Crater

September 24, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is shifting beneath the feet of the ruling administration. Just over a year into his term, President Lee Jae-myung—who took office with strong promises of reform and economic renewal—is watching his approval ratings plummet into dangerous territory. According to a late-September poll by Research Well, Lee's positive rating has slumped to 30.6%, while negative sentiment has surged to 66.1%. For an administration that enjoyed comfortable support above 60% early on, this sudden, steep drop is more than a mid-term slump; it represents a deep and widening fracture with the very voters who brought the progressive bloc back to power.

The alarm bells are ringing loudest in demographics that were once considered the bedrock of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party. Support among voters in their 30s has cratered to a shocking 20.1%, and outside of the traditional progressive stronghold of the Honam region in the country's southwest, approval among voters under 40 has slipped below the critical 30% mark. Even more troubling for the presidential office is the widespread public consensus on Lee's ongoing legal risks: nearly 78% of respondents believe his suspended trials should resume immediately or without waiting until the end of his term. As political analysts try to make sense of this sudden freefall, the question is no longer just about public relations, but whether the administration can salvage its reform agenda before it is completely paralyzed by a looming lame-duck dynamic.

The Anatomy of a Collapse: Alienating Core Supporters and the Honam Base

To understand the depth of the current crisis, one must look closely at how the disapproval is structured. Over 56% of respondents in the Research Well survey stated that the president is doing "very badly"—a harsh indictment that goes beyond mere policy disagreements and points to a fundamental loss of trust in the administration's direction. While dismal numbers in conservative-leaning regions like Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province are hardly surprising, negative ratings have also crossed the majority threshold in Seoul (nearly 60%) and even in the historically loyal Honam region (54.5%). Unlike the Moon Jae-in administration, which maintained stable 60% support during a comparable phase of its term, Lee’s administration is seeing its political foundation erode at an alarming pace.

The defection of voters in their 30s and 40s is particularly damaging. These demographics were crucial in electing Lee, propelled by aspirations for social justice, fairness, and economic relief. However, as high living costs, stubborn inflation, and housing market anxieties persist without decisive government solutions, fatigue has set in. When a progressive administration fails to deliver on its core promises of economic equity, its base does not merely grumble—it walks away. Because the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has failed to capitalize effectively on these missteps, the drop in Lee's numbers reflects a broader wave of independent and moderate voters abandoning the ship rather than a sudden consolidation of the conservative electorate.

The Pro-Prosecution Paradox: How Reform Hopes Turned Into Betrayal

One of the most potent drivers of public disillusionment is a profound sense of betrayal regarding judicial and prosecutorial reform. During the campaign, Lee and his allies heavily criticized the unchecked power of South Korea's prosecutors and vowed to restructure the system. Yet, the reality of governance has produced a stark contradiction: the administration has quietly packed key Ministry of Justice positions and external assignments with former prosecutors, leaving critics to complain that the ministry has effectively become a branch office of the prosecution service.

Public interest groups, such as People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy, have pointed out that the number of active prosecutors seconded to government posts remains above 100—virtually unchanged from the final days of the preceding conservative administration led by Yoon Suk Yeol. For voters who cast ballots expecting a thorough dismantling of prosecutorial overreach, watching the administration cozy up to former prosecutors has induced acute cynicism. When a government abandons its reform principles in search of administrative convenience and stability, it sacrifices the moral high ground that justified its rise to power.

Silencing Critics and Building an Echo Chamber

As the administration's political fortunes sour, its internal dynamics have grown increasingly insular. This tension burst into the open recently when civic intellectual and prominent liberal commentator Rhyu Si-min offered a sharp public critique, comparing the administration's minor unresolved problems to "small stones in a shoe" that are ultimately crippling the president because leadership refuses to stop and shake them out. Rhyu also took direct aim at the administration's opaque approach to prosecutorial reform, arguing that failing to transparently outline plans for separating investigative and indicting powers amounted to misleading the public.

Instead of treating these warnings as constructive medicine, the presidential office and key pro-Lee loyalists reportedly chose to cut ties with Rhyu entirely, opting instead to lean exclusively on friendly media figures and enthusiastic online commentators. This defensive reflex—purging dissenting voices within the wider progressive camp in favor of an echo chamber—risks blinding the administration to harsh external realities. By treating internal critics as adversaries, the ruling camp has narrowed its field of vision precisely when it needs broad-based counsel the most.

A High-Stakes Showdown with the Judiciary

Compounding its political woes, the administration is now locked in a bitter institutional war with the judiciary. Tensions between the presidential office and Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae have escalated into a full-blown constitutional standoff. At the heart of the clash is a dispute over authority: the Supreme Court maintains that the chief justice’s constitutional prerogative to nominate Supreme Court justices must be respected by the executive, while the presidential office argues that blindly rubber-stamping these picks would neuter the democratic mandate of an elected president.

With relations completely frozen, the ruling bloc has moved past verbal condemnation and taken aggressive legislative action. Lawmakers from the progressive camp have introduced a motion backed by over 100 signatures to impeach Chief Justice Cho. Proponents of the impeachment argue that the judiciary has crossed a political line, accusing the court's leadership of weaponizing legal proceedings and scheduling decisions to undermine progressive political figures ahead of future elections. While critics view this as a dangerous escalation that threatens the separation of powers, supporters argue that ordinary parliamentary protests are useless against a judiciary accused of political bias, leaving impeachment as the only legitimate constitutional check available.

What to Watch Next

    Whether the administration shifts personnel: Watch for potential cabinet and staff reshuffles to see if the president brings in reform-minded outsiders or doubles down on loyalists. The trajectory of the impeachment motion: Follow how the National Assembly handles the push to impeach the Chief Justice, which will test party discipline and deepen the executive-judiciary rift. Upcoming legislative battles over institutional reform: Keep an eye on how the Democratic Party handles bills regarding investigative agencies and court oversight amidst sliding public trust.
