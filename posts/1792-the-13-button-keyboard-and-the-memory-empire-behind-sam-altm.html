The 13-Button Keyboard and the Memory Empire: Behind Sam Altman’s Closed-Door Summit with Samsung and SK

November 01, 2026

On the afternoon of October 31, a palpable tension settled over the glass-and-steel boardroom of Samsung Electronics’ Seocho headquarters in southern Seoul. Assembled in the room was an extraordinary congregation of corporate power: roughly fifty division presidents and top executive directors representing the sprawling electronics conglomerate. They were not meeting to review quarterly earnings or unveil a flagship smartphone. Instead, they had gathered for a private masterclass and strategic reckoning with Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI.

Altman’s whirlwind trip to Seoul—his return after a pivotal initial visit twelve months earlier—was far from a courtesy call. Over roughly thirty-six hours, the architect of ChatGPT held intensive strategy sessions with Samsung’s executive cadre and sat down for a high-stakes luncheon with Chey Tae-won, chairman of SK Group, South Korea’s second-largest family-owned business conglomerate (or chaebol). Between the closed-door workshops and private negotiations, one reality became unmistakable: Silicon Valley’s race toward artificial general intelligence is now inextricably tethered to the assembly lines and silicon cleanrooms of South Korea.

A 13-Button Controller for a New Corporate Operating Model

Among the senior leaders inside Samsung, the conversation quickly moved past abstract discussions of synthetic intelligence into the nuts and bolts of corporate survival. Delivering a address titled "The Next Ten Years Changed by AI," Altman urged the assembled executives to rethink the core structure of how global enterprises function. For a conglomerate whose traditional identity was built on hyper-disciplined manufacturing and iterative execution, the core takeaway was unsettling: technological superiority no longer belongs solely to who designs an algorithm, but to whoever embeds generative tools deepest into their everyday operational workflow.

Nothing crystallized that operational shift more vividly than the gift Altman brought for Samsung’s leadership: a specialized "Codex" keyboard, priced at around $225 (roughly 300,000 won). Stripped of standard QWERTY letter arrangements, the minimalist peripheral featured just thirteen buttons. It was conceived not as an instrument for typing sentences or populating spreadsheets, but as a dedicated physical console to direct, prompt, and orchestrate AI systems.

The symbolic weight of the device was not lost on the room. For decades, the personal computer and the office workstation have demanded that humans adapt their thoughts to keyboards, forms, and administrative paperwork. The thirteen-key console pointed to an inverted dynamic where natural language commands run entire software pipelines and automate code creation. Altman’s message to Samsung’s leadership was blunt: in an era of conversational agents and multimodal systems, legacy input methods and bureaucratic reporting layers represent costly drag.

Samsung’s response went beyond polite executive nod-and-smile formalities. During the session, the fifty presidents underwent a hands-on practical exercise using Samsung’s proprietary internal knowledge databases, directly querying and testing internal systems through AI interfaces. The electronics giant subsequently announced an accelerated, company-wide "AI Transformation" (AX) mandate spanning research and development, manufacturing line diagnostics, marketing, and general administrative services. Internal executive seminars will culminate in a massive re-education program for all employees, slated to roll out across the workforce this coming February.

Historically, South Korean industry conquered consumer electronics and microchips by operating as the quintessential "fast follower"—rapidly commercializing and perfecting technologies pioneered in the West. But generative AI breaks the traditional follower playbook because models and development cycles evolve weekly, not annually. For Samsung’s brass to submit themselves to direct tutoring from a foreign software founder reflects an institutional anxiety that incrementalism is no longer a viable defense.

SK, HBM, and the Race to Build the Physical AI Grid

If Samsung represented the internal software and operational side of Altman’s visit, his subsequent meeting across town with SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won addressed the physical constraints threatening to stall OpenAI’s grandest ambitions. SK Group controls SK Hynix, the South Korean semiconductor manufacturer that has emerged as the dominant supplier of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—the hyper-dense, vertically stacked DRAM chips essential for running OpenAI’s largest models and Nvidia’s flagship graphics processing units (GPUs).

Over a working lunch, Altman and Chey drilled into the arithmetic of infrastructure bottlenecks. OpenAI is currently navigating colossal infrastructure blueprints, including ambitious data center buildouts and its much-discussed "Stargate" computing initiative. Training and serving frontier models requires astronomical amounts of energy, computational processing, and, above all, specialized memory chips that can feed billions of parameters to processors without latency chokepoints.

The contrast between Altman’s two visits tells the story of the current boom. During his stop in Seoul a year earlier, memory chipmakers were suffering through one of the most punishing chip downturns in modern history, weighed down by post-pandemic inventory gluts. In the twelve months since, the generative AI boom triggered an unprecedented supply crunch for high-end server DRAM and HBM, causing Samsung’s semiconductor operating profits to surge fifteen-fold off cyclical lows.

For big tech firms like OpenAI, securing guaranteed allocations of high-end memory—often discussed in batches as large as 900,000 HBM stacks—is now a prerequisite for keeping data centers online. Simultaneously, American hyperscalers are desperately seeking ways to loosen Nvidia’s near-monopoly pricing on AI silicon. Whether OpenAI develops its own neural processing units (NPUs) or partners with alternative silicon vendors, those chips still require Korean HBM and advanced packaging to operate. Altman’s sequential summits with Samsung and SK confirmed that while software architectures may be authored in California, the computing bottleneck can only be resolved in South Korea.

The Risk of Remaining Silicon Subcontractors

Yet for all the eye-popping financial figures, Altman’s trip served as a reminder of an enduring structural risk for South Korea’s industrial leaders. Amid rising debate on Wall Street about an "AI bubble" and the uncertain timeline for enterprise software monetization, Korean chipmakers risk being treated as cyclical capital equipment providers rather than architectural owners of the AI platform era.

Selling record volumes of HBM at premium margins provides immediate balance sheet windfalls, but it leaves hardware manufacturers exposed to the capex spending whims of a tiny circle of American tech giants.

If Samsung and SK remain confined to the role of component foundries, they remain vulnerable to down-cycles the moment major tech players pull back infrastructure spending. The real strategic imperative facing Seoul’s conglomerates is whether they can translate their near-monopolistic leverage over advanced memory into broader equity, cloud partnerships, and proprietary AI software ecosystems.

Furthermore, building out domestic AI infrastructure poses formidable domestic challenges. Large-scale AI data centers (AIDCs) and advanced semiconductor fabrication lines demand massive, uninterrupted baseload electric power. In South Korea, upgrading regional power grids and securing municipal permits for high-voltage transmission lines remain contentious public and regulatory battles. Without aggressive infrastructure support from the government, the cutting-edge hardware produced by domestic factories will simply be shipped abroad to power data centers in North America and the Middle East, rather than seeding an independent domestic technology base.

What to Watch Next

As the immediate buzz of Altman’s visit recedes, several concrete milestones will indicate whether South Korea’s corporate AI reset yields structural dividends:

  • The February Workforce Rollout: Watch whether Samsung’s scheduled company-wide AI training across its tens of thousands of employees produces genuine operational restructuring—particularly in automated defect detection on semiconductor fabrication lines and R&D simulations—or degrades into perfunctory corporate box-checking.
  • Dedicated Foundry and HBM Long-Term Contracts: Look for concrete supply and co-development announcements linking OpenAI's custom hardware initiatives directly to SK Hynix’s next-generation HBM lines and Samsung’s contract chip-manufacturing (foundry) division.
  • Grid Modernization and Domestic Data Center Policies: Keep a close eye on administrative approvals and regulatory clearances for specialized AI power transmission corridors, an essential test of whether policymakers can match the deployment speed demanded by tech leaders.

Altman departed Seoul with the memory commitments his computing clusters require to survive the next generation of model development. Samsung and SK, meanwhile, were left with a stark visualization of their future: a tiny, thirteen-button console that represents an economy where raw industrial scale matters far less than the algorithmic intelligence directing it.
