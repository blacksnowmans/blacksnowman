South Korea’s Legal Civil War: Inside the Fiery Clash Over the 'Fabricated Prosecution' Bill

November 08, 2026

On a chilly November afternoon in central Seoul, the stone walls outside Gyeongbokgung Station were swallowed by a sea of red placards. Conservative protesters, organized by the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition, shouted denunciations of the government through blaring megaphones. Across town in the National Assembly on Yeouido, lawmakers were just emerging from an exhausting round of round-the-clock filibusters. The chamber had passed a contentious piece of legislation known as the "Fabricated Prosecution Special Counsel Act"—and with that vote, South Korea's bitter political divide crossed another point of no return.

For observers looking in from the outside, the scene might resemble standard partisan brinkmanship. But what played out on November 7 is part of a sweeping, existential power struggle over the country’s criminal justice system. The clash pits the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, which is determined to dismantle and hold accountable what it calls the "political prosecution" of the past, against a cornered conservative opposition that accuses the current administration of using legislative muscle to shield the head of state. Between street demonstrations invoking presidential impeachment and heated battles over a newly nominated justice minister, the crisis cuts to the very foundations of institutional power in South Korea.

A Reckoning with the Past: The 'Fabricated Prosecution' Special Counsel Bill

The core catalyst of the current eruption is the passage of a special counsel bill targeting alleged prosecutorial abuses. According to the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, state prosecutors during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was removed from office following impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence—routinely abused their authority. The ruling camp asserts that prosecutors launched targeted investigations, fabricated evidence, and engaged in forced indictments against political opponents.

To investigate these charges, the legislation authorizes a mammoth special counsel team exceeding 300 personnel, endowed with sweeping powers of compulsory search and seizure. For the Democratic Party, the measure is a necessary reckoning. In their view, justice cannot be restored until the bureaucratic apparatus that wielded state power against democratic norms is thoroughly scrutinized and reined in.

The People Power Party, however, views the special counsel as an unconstitutional weapon. Conservative lawmakers and legal critics argue that granting expansive coercive powers to an ad-hoc investigative body—at a time when broader reforms are stripping the standard prosecution service of direct investigative jurisdiction—upends the nation's criminal justice system. Former conservative party leader Han Dong-hoon immediately attacked the bill as a tailor-made shield, labeling it an unconstitutional "one-man privilege act" and a "jailbreak bill" intended to paralyze ongoing judicial proceedings involving President Lee Jae-myung. Heavyweights across the conservative aisle, including former lawmaker Yoo Seung-min and Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, joined the chorus, demanding the bill’s complete scrapping.

The stakes could hardly be higher. If the special counsel uncovers verifiable evidence that prosecutors systematically fabricated indictments under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, it will permanently discredit years of high-profile state investigations. If the probe falters or appears overtly biased, the Democratic Party will face severe accusations of weaponizing legislative authority for partisan vendettas.

Taking It to the Pavement: Opposition Rallies and Impeachment Rhetoric

Having exhausted their legislative defenses—including an around-the-clock filibuster that was ultimately broken by the ruling majority—the People Power Party abandoned parliamentary negotiations and took their battle directly to the streets. The massive rally near Gyeongbokgung Station brought party leadership, conservative civic groups, and rank-and-file members together in an open revolt against the legislative branch.

The tone of the rally quickly moved past procedural complaints into open challenges to the administration's legitimacy. Jang Dong-hyeok, the leader of the People Power Party, took the stage to issue blistering warnings, going so far as to invoke the specter of presidential removal:

"President Lee Jae-myung has betrayed the people. A president who lacks the will to defend the Constitution deserves only impeachment."

By openly calling for the president to be rolled out on an impeachment mat, the opposition leadership signaled an all-or-nothing strategy. The ruling Democratic Party hit back immediately, dismissing the street rally as nothing more than a desperate "bulletproof shield" designed to protect rogue prosecutors from accountability. Other minor opposition factions, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party, also took aim at the conservative rally, criticizing the People Power Party for abandoning substantive policy duties during the critical regular parliamentary session to engage in divisive street populism.

There are serious questions about whether this scorched-earth strategy will resonate beyond the conservative base. With the South Korean public grappling with domestic economic headwinds, many view the return of megaphone politics with open fatigue. Recent survey data assessing the latest parliamentary audit cycle revealed that 46 percent of surveyed citizens viewed the political spectacle and viral "short-form" grandstanding as having produced no meaningful results for everyday life.

The Battle Over the Justice Ministry: Enter Lee Yong-cheol

Against this turbulent backdrop, President Lee Jae-myung made a decisive move to consolidate his legal agenda by nominating Lee Yong-cheol as the new Minister of Justice. Lee Yong-cheol currently heads the Defense Acquisition Program Administration (DAPA), South Korea’s central defense procurement agency, and formerly served as a presidential secretary under the progressive administration of the late Roh Moo-hyun.

The selection of a non-prosecutor signals the administration's intent to push through sweeping structural reforms. The government is working to replace the traditional prosecutorial monopoly with a bifurcated framework comprising a dedicated Prosecution Agency for indictments and a separate Major Crimes Investigation Office. The incoming justice minister will be tasked with calming deep internal unease within the prosecution service while executing these reforms and managing the fallout from the special counsel's upcoming inquiries.

Predictably, the nomination instantly ignited another partisan flashpoint. The People Power Party accused the nominee of belonging to the president’s personal political network, labeling him part of the so-called "Seongnam Line"—a reference to the city of Seongnam where President Lee once served as mayor. Conservative critics claimed the nominee was being rewarded for past advisory roles tied to the long-disputed Daejang-dong land development initiative, demanding that his nomination be withdrawn immediately.

The Democratic Party dismissed the allegation as baseless smear campaigning. Ruling party spokespersons countered that the nominee's municipal advisory work concluded long before any plans for the Daejang-dong development were even conceived, warning of direct legal action against what they termed reckless slander. Yet as confirmation hearings approach, the debate risks degenerating into another round of recycled grievances rather than an examination of the nominee's fitness to manage legal reform.

Shifting Global Currents and the Forgotten Domestic Reality

While the capital’s political class engaged in ferocious point-scoring, major international and domestic realities continued to move independently of the partisan brawl. Abroad, international dynamics saw subtle recalibrations following the United States midterm elections, where gains by the Democratic Party placed institutional limits on Trump-style unilateralism. In South Korea, high-profile figures like Daegu Mayor Hong Joon-pyo noted the global shift, which has introduced new uncertainties into bilateral defense discussions regarding wartime operational control (OPCON) transfer and domestic nuclear submarine procurement.

Meanwhile, the presidential office pursued practical economic initiatives overseas. President Lee Jae-myung attended the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in China, securing notable commercial breakthroughs, including the first official export clearance for South Korean sweet persimmons into the Chinese market—a concrete gain for struggling rural agricultural producers.

Domestically, regional leaders from both sides of the aisle converged on the National Assembly to fight for basic survival. Local government officials, including North Jeolla Province Governor Lee Won-taek and Buan County Mayor Kwon Ik-hyun, lobbied fiercely for expanded subsidies in the 2027 state budget to offset shrinking local tax allocations. Simultaneously, the Ministry of Employment and Labor convened emergency national meetings to order sweeping, punitive workplace safety inspections following a series of fatal industrial accidents at manufacturing hubs like the Daebul Industrial Complex in Yeongam.

These parallel realities offer a stark contrast to the spectacles at Yeouido and Gyeongbokgung Station. While workers face hazardous conditions and provincial towns scramble to maintain municipal infrastructure, the central political stage remains almost entirely consumed by retrospective investigations and constitutional showdowns.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters the winter legislative session, the showdown between the government and the conservative opposition will test the durability of both camps. Key indicators will determine how this standoff unfolds in the weeks ahead:

  • The Confirmation Fight for Lee Yong-cheol: The upcoming National Assembly confirmation hearings for the justice minister nominee will serve as a referendum on the administration's prosecutorial restructuring. Watch whether the proceedings focus on the separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers or devolve entirely into disputes over past municipal appointments.
  • The Formation of the 300-Person Special Counsel: As the "Fabricated Prosecution" special team is formally empaneled, its initial subpoena list and investigative scope will signal how deep into past state decisions the probe will reach. Any legal challenges to the team's constitutionality will quickly rise to the judiciary.
  • The Viability of Conservative Street Politics: The People Power Party’s embrace of hardline impeachment rhetoric carries substantial political risk. Whether their weekend rallies gather broader momentum among moderate voters or provoke a backlash against legislative obstruction will determine the opposition's strategy heading into the next election cycle.

South Korea’s democracy has demonstrated remarkable resilience across decades of volatile political transitions. But as the fight over judicial authority shifts from debate chambers into the open squares, the line between institutional accountability and permanent political retribution has never looked thinner.
