Two Shock Waves in Seoul: Kim Keon-hee’s New Warrant and Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s Controversial Acquittal

October 28, 2026

On the afternoon of October 27, 2026, a video screen inside Courtroom No. 1 at South Korea’s Supreme Court in Seocho-dong, Seoul, flickered to life. Appearing on the remote connection was Kim Keon-hee, testifying directly from the Seoul Southern Detention Center where she remains behind bars. Dressed in detention attire, Kim argued that her deteriorating health required immediate release for medical treatment. She even invoked her husband, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the impeached former president now serving a prison sentence himself—claiming he was far too clean and principled to ever let her interfere in state appointments.

The hearing lasted just 44 minutes. Unpersuaded by her health claims and citing clear risks of evidence destruction and flight, the bench issued an additional arrest warrant. Outside the detention center gates, die-hard loyalists hung banners reading messages of encouragement. Yet just four days earlier, another blockbuster judicial decision sent entirely different shockwaves through South Korea’s political establishment: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon was acquitted on appeal in a high-profile election law case, dramatically rescuing his political career from the brink of collapse.

To understand why these twin legal developments have consumed South Korean headlines, one must look at the broader political landscape following the fall of the previous administration. Under the current government of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the justice system has become the primary battleground where the legacies of former power holders and the ambitions of future presidential hopefuls collide. On one side stands Kim Keon-hee, whose alleged influence peddling continues to draw severe legal scrutiny. On the other stands Oh Se-hoon, a heavyweight figure in the People Power Party—South Korea's main conservative opposition party—who was fighting to stay in office. Together, these cases highlight a deep-seated public anxiety over whether South Korea’s legal system operates with equal vigor against all political figures.

An Additional Arrest Warrant for Kim Keon-hee

The Supreme Court’s decision to issue a fresh arrest warrant for Kim Keon-hee reinforces her continued confinement. During the October 27 hearing, Kim’s legal team argued that the additional warrant constituted an improper double jeopardy maneuver on separate charges. Kim herself pleaded for leniency, claiming severe physical weakness.

Critics, however, quickly pointed out the flaw in her health argument. In South Korean correctional facilities, inmates follow strictly regulated daily schedules and meal plans. This structured environment often leaves high-profile detainees in better physical shape than during their frantic public lives—a logic previously cited during the incarceration of past political figures. Kim’s attempt to use poor health as an exit ticket appeared transparent to legal observers.

Furthermore, Kim’s defense during the hearing raised eyebrows when she brought up her husband. "Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is such a clean and principled person," Kim insisted to the court, "why would he ever leave personnel appointments to me?" That statement stands in sharp contrast to evidence surfaced during investigations into political broker Myung Tae-kyun, where phone recordings revealed Kim actively discussing state nominations and government appointments.

Outside the Seoul Southern Detention Center, a dedicated group of supporters continues to stage rallies, stringing banners along the perimeter fences with messages promising to await her return. However, with the court confirming both the flight risk and the danger of evidence tampering, Kim remains firmly behind bars as investigators prepare further legal proceedings.

Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s Shock Acquittal and the Polling Scandal

If Kim Keon-hee’s warrant confirmed her bleak legal reality, the appellate ruling for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon provided a dramatic plot twist for South Korea’s conservative opposition. On October 23, 2026, the Seoul High Court completely overturned a lower court verdict, clearing Oh of all charges related to the Political Fund Act.

The case stemmed from the April 2021 Seoul mayoral by-election. Prosecutors alleged that Oh, whose political standing was struggling during early primary races against intra-party rivals, received ten private opinion polls managed by political broker Myung Tae-kyun. According to the prosecution, a wealthy business supporter named Kim Han-jeong paid 33 million won (about $25,000) on Oh's behalf to cover these polling costs.

In the first-instance trial, Judge Jo Jeong-ung found Oh guilty of accepting five of those polls and benefiting from 21 million won (about $15,700) in off-the-books payments. The court sentenced Oh to a fine of 10 million won (about $7,500) alongside a 21 million won surcharge—a penalty that, under South Korean election law, automatically voids an elected official's victory and strips them of office.

However, Senior Judge Gu Hoe-keun of the Seoul High Court rejected the primary witness testimony. The appellate bench ruled that broker Myung Tae-kyun’s statements lacked credibility, noting that Myung had changed his story after Oh threatened legal action. The court also highlighted newly submitted audio recordings where Myung told an associate that Oh "didn't know" about the financial arrangements.

The sudden acquittal triggered immediate celebration among People Power Party lawmakers, who flocked to the courthouse to congratulate Oh. His legal team hailed the verdict as a vindication. Yet the Special Counsel team led by Min Joong-ki announced an immediate appeal to the Supreme Court, arguing that the appellate court ignored the clear operational logic of political campaigns, where candidates routinely rely on proxy payments arranged by close aides to bypass strict political finance limits.

Public Disillusionment and the Conservative Judicial Elite

The sharp divergence between these rulings has reignited a fierce debate over judicial neutrality in South Korea. A central target of public criticism is the structure of the high court bench itself. Senior high court judges and Supreme Court justices predominantly reside in the affluent southern districts of Seoul, such as Seocho and Gangnam, forming what critics describe as an entrenched conservative legal elite.

Under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the judiciary faces growing accusations from the left of displaying political bias and delaying key cases involving conservative figures. In response, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea approved a resolution during a general caucus to push for a formal parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho and the broader judicial administration. Democratic Party spokesperson Jeon Eun-soo emphasized that the National Assembly must intervene to audit alleged political interference within the court system, with formal legislative motions scheduled to begin on November 5.

The rising public skepticism towards the court system has brought back a famous historical parallel. In 1909, legendary Korean independence activist Ahn Jung-geun authored a famous calligraphy piece shortly before his execution, writing: "International law is not as good as a cannon" (만국공법 불여대포). The phrase reflected his bitter realization that legal frameworks become meaningless when they are manipulated by the powerful. Today, critics of the judicial system echo that sentiment, warning that if the courts are perceived as protecting political elites while applying the full weight of the law only to their opponents, public faith in the rule of law will completely erode.

What to Watch Next

Several crucial milestones in the coming weeks will determine whether South Korea's judicial controversies deepen or find resolution:

  • The Special Counsel’s Supreme Court Appeal: Special Counsel Min Joong-ki is preparing his final appeal to the Supreme Court regarding Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s acquittal. The high court's evaluation of Myung Tae-kyun's witness credibility will undergo intense legal scrutiny.
  • Parliamentary Investigation into the Judiciary: On November 5, the Democratic Party-led National Assembly will move to table a motion for a parliamentary audit into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the Supreme Court administration, setting up a direct constitutional clash between the legislature and the judiciary.
  • Kim Keon-hee’s Expanding Trials: With the additional arrest warrant secured, prosecutors and special investigators will continue pushing forward with multiple indictments against Kim Keon-hee, testing whether her defense strategy can withstand trial proceedings while she remains in detention.
