Power Struggles in Seoul: The Silencing of Voices Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 04, 2026

Behind closed doors inside the headquarters of the Democratic Party of Korea—South Korea’s ruling liberal party—tensions are reaching a boiling point. At a time when the administration of President Lee Jae-myung requires a unified legislative front to drive its national agenda, the party that holds the presidency is turning inward. Far from presenting a cohesive front, the party leadership under Representative Kim Min-seok is engulfed in intense factional infighting, baseless conspiracy accusations, and growing allegations of internal suppression.

For international observers watching South Korea, the headlines often focus on major economic shifts or diplomatic realignments following the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. However, the true barometer of South Korean political stability right now lies within the ruling party’s internal mechanics. A series of recent clashes—most notably the explosive silencing of a senior lawmaker during a top-level meeting—has exposed deep fractures over power, identity, and internal democracy.

Ungrounded Accusations and the Phantom "Three-Party Alliance"

The current crisis ignited when Democratic Party Leader Kim Min-seok publicly floated a sensational theory: that a minority faction within the party was forming a secret "three-party alliance" to destabilize the current leadership. The claim sent shockwaves through the party ranks. Yet, when pressed to produce concrete evidence to support the existence of this clandestine coalition, Kim provided none.

As backlash mounted, Kim attempted to quietly diffuse the situation with a emotional apology, framing his actions as an effort to preserve party unity. But the move backfired. Political commentators on major Korean political analysis broadcasts, such as Sisa Geongeon, sharply criticized the maneuver, describing it as a classic case of an attacker inflicting damage and then weeping as if they were the victim. Rather than soothing tempers, the unforced error severely damaged Kim’s credibility.

Political trust is hard-earned and easily squandered. Launching unfounded broadsides against fellow party members, only to retreat behind emotional appeals when cornered, reflects a vintage style of political maneuvering that clashes with modern expectations of governance. Senior party figures have refused to let the issue slide. Representative Jung Chung-rae, a prominent party voice, firmly rejected the secret alliance claims as outright fabrication and insisted that accountability must follow.

Key Takeaway: Factional unity cannot be built on quiet cover-ups. When party leaders manufacture internal threats without evidence, it signals weakness in leadership rather than strength.

The rift has split the party into clear camps. On one side, loyalists to Kim Min-seok—often grouped within the rising "pro-Kim" or "New Lee Jae-myung" factions, including lawmakers such as Kim Hyun-jung, Kim Dong-ah, and Park Beom-kye—have taken to social media to rally behind the party leader, urging critics to cease "shaking the leadership." Even local precinct leaders have been mobilized to issue public statements of support. On the other side, party stalwarts demand a full, transparent accounting of how such unfounded allegations were allowed to be weaponized in the first place.

Gag Order in the Supreme Council: The Choi Min-hee Incident

If the phantom alliance controversy demonstrated sloppy political strategy, a subsequent incident during a closed-door meeting of the Supreme Council—the party’s highest executive decision-making body—raised far more troubling questions about internal democracy.

According to revelations posted on social media by Representative Choi Min-hee, an elected member of the Supreme Council, Party Leader Kim Min-seok explicitly blocked her from speaking during a private meeting. The confrontation reportedly escalated into shouting when Choi attempted to raise questions regarding potential ties between certain political figures and Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious organization whose political entanglements have long been a lightning rod in South Korean public life.

The decision to forcibly silence an elected member of the supreme leadership team strikes at the core of party governance. The Democratic Party of Korea has historically built its identity on democratic deliberation and open debate. When a party leader unilaterally revokes a senior colleague's right to speak simply because the topic is uncomfortable, it creates a dangerous precedent.

In South Korea's political landscape, the topic of Shincheonji carries immense weight. The group’s alleged attempts to infiltrate political organizations have drawn fierce public scrutiny for years, and the Democratic Party has routinely demanded rigorous transparency regarding such connections. For the current party leadership to aggressively suppress internal discussion on the topic under the guise of maintaining order invites suspicions of a double standard. If the allegations are baseless, open debate is the proper mechanism to clear the air; silencing the question only fuels speculation that something is being hidden.

"New Lee Jae-myung" vs. the Establishment: Purging the Party's Roots

To understand why these seemingly isolated spats are occurring, one must look at the broader tectonic shift taking place within South Korean liberalism. Political analysts point out that the central battle inside the Democratic Party is a struggle for party dominance between the emerging "New Lee Jae-myung" faction and the traditional establishment built around loyalists to former liberal presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in.

This struggle manifests in several key dynamics:

  • The "New Lee Jae-myung" Push: A rising cadre of politicians seeking to reshape the party's ideological core, governance structure, and candidate selection process exclusively around the current president’s political brand.
  • The Legacy Factions: Pro-Roh and pro-Moon loyalists who view themselves as the traditional guardians of liberal political heritage, resisting what they perceive as an artificial purge of historical party pillars.
  • The Factional Dilemma: While the party leadership seeks total alignment with the executive branch, systematically side-lining established factions risks eroding the broad coalition that brought the party to power.

Politics ought to be an exercise in addition, not subtraction. While President Lee Jae-myung focuses on managing state affairs, economy, and foreign policy, the ruling party's leadership appears consumed by an internal clearing of the decks. Treating the party's historic factions as relics to be discarded is a risky gamble. A political movement that cuts away its own roots undermines its resilience during difficult legislative battles.

Furthermore, the outcomes of the party's recent national convention showed that the rank-and-file membership did not grant any single group an unchecked mandate. Party members expressed a clear desire for policy-driven leadership and checks and balances. Using shadowy conspiracy theories or public loyalty tests to enforce conformity misinterprets the electorate's demand for effective governance.

What to Watch Next in South Korean Ruling Party Politics

The health of the ruling party directly impacts President Lee Jae-myung’s ability to pass critical legislation through the National Assembly. If the Democratic Party remains bogged down in closed-door shouting matches and factional purges, its legislative momentum will inevitably stall, handing momentum to the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party.

In the coming weeks, political observers should monitor several key indicators:

  • Transparency of Leadership Meetings: Watch whether the Democratic Party reforms its Supreme Council procedures to prevent the arbitrary silencing of leadership members and whether detailed minutes or partial open sessions are introduced.
  • Formal Accountability Demands: Pay close attention to whether senior figures like Representative Jung Chung-rae press for formal party inquiries into the origin of the "three-party alliance" claims.
  • Factional Cohesion in Legislative Votes: Track whether pro-Roh and pro-Moon lawmakers vote as a unified block or begin expressing discontent through legislative delays on key government bills.
  • Public Opinion Trends: Keep an eye on voter support ratings for the Democratic Party; prolonged internal conflict historically leads to voter fatigue and drops in ruling party approval.

South Korea’s democracy thrives on robust debate. For the Democratic Party of Korea to successfully support the current administration, its leadership must move past authoritarian meeting tactics and embrace the diverse internal voices that give the party its strength.
