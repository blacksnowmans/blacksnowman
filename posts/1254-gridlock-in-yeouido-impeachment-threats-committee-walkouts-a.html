Gridlock in Yeouido: Impeachment Threats, Committee Walkouts, and a Presidential Reshuffle

October 18, 2026

Inside the committee rooms of South Korea’s National Assembly in Yeouido, the microphones rarely stay off for long, but on October 17, they captured more shouting than policy debate. Midday proceedings broke down into furious walkouts, point-blank accusations of constitutional subversion, and threats of impeaching the country’s top judicial official. The National Assembly is currently holding its annual audit of state affairs—a constitutionally mandated, month-long inspection intended to scrutinize government agencies, hold bureaucrats accountable, and examine state spending. Instead, this year's midpoint has laid bare a political landscape defined by bitter partisan warfare, lingering legal battles, and deep institutional distrust.

With the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) consolidating its agenda under President Lee Jae-myung and the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) fiercely resisting, the audit has turned into a high-stakes arena. Rather than focusing on inflation or faltering growth, legislative panels spent the day locked in trench warfare over media regulation, the criminal immunity of the presidency, and targeted prosecutions of political rivals.

Showdowns in the Assembly: Broadcasting Watchdogs and Supreme Court Threats

The day’s most chaotic scenes unfolded in the Science, ICT, Broadcasting, and Communications Committee. The session ground to an abrupt halt during a heated standoff over Lee Jin-sook, chair of the Korea Communications Commission (KCC), the country’s powerful broadcast regulator. Lawmakers engaged in furious verbal clashes over whether Lee should be formally recused and disqualified from questioning, while a procedural dispute over holding an in-camera, closed-door audit of public broadcaster MBC further inflamed tempers. As conservative PPP lawmakers accused the committee leadership of running biased, heavy-handed proceedings, the committee chair invoked order-maintenance authority, suspending the session entirely amid mutual shouting.

Meanwhile, in the Legislation and Judiciary Committee—traditionally the most contentious battleground in Korean politics—the confrontation reached a constitutional precipice. The Democratic Party formalized internal discussions regarding the potential impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the head of South Korea’s Supreme Court. DPK lawmakers intensified pressure on the Chief Justice to resign, framing the move as necessary judicial accountability. The People Power Party reacted with fury, branding any attempt to remove the nation's top judge as a naked assault on judicial independence and "the beginning of the collapse" of the governing order.

The clash over the judiciary quickly spilled into a high-level battle of constitutional law. Both sides traded arguments over Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution, which grants an incumbent president immunity from criminal indictment, debating bitterly over whether that protection halts ongoing criminal trials initiated prior to taking office. Lawmakers cited legal clauses to accuse one another of violating the republic's founding charter, turning what was supposed to be a review of 17 regional courts into an ideological proxy war over the separation of powers.

A Bureaucrat at the Blue House: President Lee Jae-myung’s New Chief of Staff

Outside parliament, the executive branch moved to steady its ranks. President Lee Jae-myung announced the appointment of Lim Gwang-hyun, a seasoned technocrat and former deputy commissioner of the National Tax Service, as his new Presidential Chief of Staff to lead the administration's second-phase lineup. By placing an economic and tax specialist in charge of presidential operations, the administration signaled an intent to sharpen its focus on livelihood issues, stabilize macroeconomic policy, and enforce tighter operational discipline between the presidential office and the ruling party.

Supporters welcomed the appointment as a pragmatic shift toward administrative competence. Opposition parties, however, dismissed the move. Figures from the conservative Reform Party described it as an uninspired, "revolving-door" shuffle that lacked genuine political vision, questioning whether a lifelong tax bureaucrat possessed the political instincts necessary to navigate a hostile assembly and bridge bitter divides with the opposition.

At the same time, authorities quietly closed a chapter on one of the most contentious political investigations of recent years. State prosecutors officially decided not to indict former President Moon Jae-in, former Presidential Chief of Staff Noh Young-min, and former Unification Minister Lee In-young regarding the 2020 killing of a South Korean fisheries official by North Korean forces near the maritime border. The sprawling inquiry had been launched with intense fanfare during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure as president, heavily utilizing the Board of Audit and Inspection alongside prosecutors to target officials of the preceding liberal government. The decision not to bring charges confirms that the wide-ranging probe ended with little legal substance, underscoring the recurring, damaging pattern in South Korean governance where major foreign policy decisions are criminalized when political power shifts.

Prosecutions, Scandals, and Bitter Infighting Across Party Lines

The courts and investigative agencies continue to exert an outsized gravity on party politics. Police announced on October 17 that they had forwarded former conservative lawmaker Yoo Seong-min to prosecutors on charges of obstruction of business. The case stems from allegations of preferential treatment in his daughter's hiring as a university professor. Yoo, long regarded as a moderate voice and potential presidential contender within conservative circles, furiously rejected the charges, denouncing the investigation as a politically motivated hit job designed to silence an independent critic.

Allegations surrounding family members have become standard ammunition in Seoul. In committee chambers, DPK lawmakers retaliated by resurrecting past questions regarding the academic credentials of the daughter of Han Dong-hoon, the former interim leader of the People Power Party, demanding equal investigative scrutiny. The opposition countered by amplifying the ongoing legal vulnerabilities of figures connected to the current administration.

The pressure is also exposing fractures within the conservative camp. Reports emerged that a recent closed-door meeting of the People Power Party degenerated into shouting and profanity between factions loyal to Han Dong-hoon and traditional loyalists of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. The altercation, sparked by disputes over the past electoral record of Supreme Council member Jang Dong-hyuk, highlighted the unresolved power struggle inside an opposition party still grappling with the fallout of Mr. Yoon's impeachment and subsequent imprisonment.

Rare Bipartisan Accord on Industrial Pollution and a Tragic Loss in Bucheon

Amid the relentless acrimony, lawmakers did manage brief moments of consensus on long-neglected public safety concerns. In the Environment and Labor Committee, legislators from both sides of the aisle united to issue a blistering critique of the Young Poong Seokpo Zinc Smelter. Located near the headwaters of the Nakdong River, the industrial facility has faced decades of accusations over releasing heavy metals into a waterway that supplies drinking water to millions in southeastern South Korea. Citing over forty years of regulatory wrist-slaps and persistent contamination, lawmakers demanded that the company seriously prepare for the plant's closure or relocation, castigating environmental regulators for decades of leniency.

A somber tone also fell over the capital following the tragic death of a firefighter battling a blaze at an industrial plant in Bucheon, Gyeonggi Province. President Lee Jae-myung and Prime Minister Han Seong-sook issued formal condolences honoring the sacrifice and pledged comprehensive upgrades to firefighter safety protocols and equipment.

Even grief, however, proved vulnerable to partisan friction. Conservative lawmakers quickly demanded apologies over past controversial comments regarding firefighters made by a Democratic Party member, while the DPK issued apologies in an effort to defuse the controversy. The episode offered a poignant reminder of how reflexively national tragedies in Korea are drawn into the cycle of partisan recrimination.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly heads into the final stretch of the audit, the political agenda will hinge on whether this institutional friction hardens into open crisis:

  • Judicial Impeachment Push: Watch closely whether the Democratic Party formalizes a floor motion to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Moving forward would trigger an unprecedented institutional standoff between the legislative majority and the judiciary, almost certainly landing before the Constitutional Court.
  • The Second-Phase Presidential Office: With Lim Gwang-hyun taking over as Chief of Staff, observers will watch whether the administration can pivot from legislative gridlock toward visible economic and tax policies ahead of upcoming national budget negotiations.
  • Prosecutorial Next Steps on Yoo Seong-min: Prosecutors must now decide whether to indict the former lawmaker or drop the obstruction of business charges. An indictment would sideline a leading moderate conservative voice, while a dismissal would bolster charges that the initial police referral was politically driven.
  • Legislative Follow-Through on Corporate Polluters: Monitor whether the Environment and Labor Committee's rare consensus against the Young Poong smelter translates into enforceable statutory penalties or punitive damages legislation, or fades once audit season concludes.
