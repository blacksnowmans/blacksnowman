The Primacy of the Primates: Rhyu Si-min’s Evolutionary Warning to South Korea’s President

October 29, 2026

On a recent broadcast of Park Si-young TV, a popular progressive political talk show on YouTube, writer and veteran commentator Rhyu Si-min sat before the microphone to diagnose a growing crisis at the heart of South Korean power. YouTube live-streams are not mere side shows in South Korean politics; they are the primary arenas where political narratives are forged, base supporters are rallied, and party doctrines are tested. Rhyu, a former Minister of Health and Welfare and one of the country's most respected progressive intellectuals, did not offer gentle advice. Instead, he delivered a scathing critique of President Lee Jae-myung, warning that the administration is systematically isolating itself and dismantling the very political coalition that brought it to power.

Rhyu framed the crisis around three core axes: personnel choices, political direction, and public policy. But what caught the attention of political junkies and casual viewers alike was not just the political diagnosis, but the unusual lens Rhyu used to deliver it. Drawing on evolutionary biology and primatology, Rhyu compared modern statecraft to the social hierarchy of chimpanzees, arguing that President Lee’s current trajectory violates fundamental laws of power survival that predate human civilization itself.

Primates, Power, and the Failure of 'Policing Behavior'

To understand why a commentary on presidential approval ratings turned into a lecture on animal behavior, one has to look at how political authority maintains its legitimacy. South Koreans often view their politics as a highly sophisticated arena of ideology and law. Yet, as Rhyu pointed out, human political behavior remains anchored in biological instincts that can be clearly observed in primate communities.

In chimpanzee troops, the top male—the alpha—does not maintain his dominance purely through brute strength or by rewarding his closest personal allies. Primatologists studying troop dynamics discovered a fascinating pattern: when disputes break out among group members, the alpha male intervenes on the side of the weaker party roughly 70 to 80 percent of the time. Only in the remaining 20 to 30 percent of cases does he use his intervention to favor his personal cronies or immediate inner circle.

This willingness to act as an impartial mediator for the vulnerable is known in evolutionary biology as "policing behavior." When an alpha male performs this role effectively, he builds broader social trust within the troop. Even when he grows old and physically weak, the other chimpanzees continue to share food with him and groom him. Conversely, an alpha male who devotes 100 percent of his power to protecting his personal cronies faces a brutal fate. It does not take long for the second-, third-, and fourth-ranked males to form an alliance, turn on the self-serving leader, and violently depose him.

Rhyu’s message to President Lee Jae-myung was unmistakable: power that serves only an inner circle invites its own destruction. By alienating broader political factions and relying primarily on loyalists, President Lee risks triggering the same biological law of rebellion that governs alpha chimpanzees.

Macroeconomic Data versus the Reality at the Grocery Store

According to Rhyu, President Lee has spent the 18 months since taking office on a path toward political isolation. The broader coalition of voters and political factions that supported his election victory is fracturing. Rhyu drew a sharp parallel to the trajectory of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president whose reign ended in impeachment and imprisonment after he dismantled his own conservative base through stubborn self-isolation and refusal to listen to internal criticism.

A primary driver of this political isolation is the growing disconnect between official political messaging and everyday economic reality. When a government becomes trapped in an echo chamber of curated data and social media feedback, it loses touch with public suffering. Rhyu cited reflections from former White House Press Secretary Karine Jean-Pierre during the Biden administration as an illustration of this global phenomenon: no matter how impressive a government's macroeconomic statistics look on paper, those numbers mean nothing to voters struggling with soaring grocery prices, rising rents, and stagnant wages.

In South Korea, where cost-of-living pressures hit urban voters with acute intensity, boasting about high-level economic metrics while ignoring kitchen-table struggles breeds deep resentment. This dynamic is compounded by the behavior of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Under the leadership of figures like Rep. Kim Min-seok and through the messaging of the party’s official YouTube apparatus, the ruling party has increasingly prioritized enforcing loyalty over healing divisions within its base.

Rather than addressing voters' lived experiences, partisan apparatuses often push disappointed voters away. As a result, many progressive leaning voters who once backed President Lee are withdrawing their support or looking toward alternative options, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller progressive party that emerged as a refuge for voters dissatisfied with the mainstream Democratic Party's internal politics.

Philosophical Materialism in a Hyper-Polarized Arena

Rhyu’s critique extended beyond immediate party tactics into a broader philosophical reflection on South Korean political culture. In recent years, Korean politics has increasingly taken on the character of a religious war, where party loyalty is treated as absolute faith and internal dissent is condemned as heresy. Against this backdrop, Rhyu advocated for a scientific, materialist perspective on life and politics.

Reflecting on mortality, Rhyu noted the simple physical truth that every human who has ever been born eventually dies. Accepting death as a natural physical boundary, he argued, frees an individual from the fear and grandiosity that fuel dogmatic thinking. Rhyu even offered a reinterpretation of Sakyamuni Buddha, viewing him not as the divine founder of an idolized religion, but as an early materialist who recognized that all things in the physical universe are impermanent and subject to constant change.

When politics devolves into idol worship—where leaders are treated as infallible deities and political parties function like religious sects—rational governance becomes impossible. Leaders stop listening to warnings, believing that faith alone will sustain their authority. Rhyu’s philosophical detour served as a blunt reality check for the ruling party: political power is subject to the same laws of physics and biology as everything else. It cannot survive on blind loyalty alone.

The Backlash from the Partisan Core

Predictably, Rhyu’s commentary stirred up a storm within the ruling party’s loyalist base. Hardline supporters accused the veteran commentator of betrayal, with some claiming he was "spitting into the well we all dug together" or effectively aiding opposition forces. Other partisans dismissed his insights entirely, accusing him of abandoning analytical commentary to play the role of an unhelpful fortune teller.

This emotional backlash highlights the exact problem Rhyu sought to diagnose. In a healthy democracy—and within a healthy ruling coalition—internal criticism serves as a critical early-warning system. Treating every warning sign as an act of treason forces a political movement to retreat further into its own bubble.

The historical warning is clear. When Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol was in power, his administration systematically purged internal critics within the conservative People Power Party, mistaking blind obedience for political strength. That strategy ended in total political collapse. If the current Democratic Party leadership under President Lee Jae-myung responds to valid critique by labeling critics as traitors, it risks repeating the exact missteps that brought down its predecessors.

What to Watch Next

Rhyu Si-min’s critique has drawn a clear line in the sand for South Korea’s ruling party. As the Lee Jae-myung administration moves deeper into its term, several key indicators will reveal whether the government takes this evolutionary warning to heart or continues down the path of isolation:

  • Presidential Outreach and Cabinet Adjustments: Watch whether President Lee makes effort to broaden his political base by bringing in advisers outside his core loyalist group, or whether upcoming personnel changes continue to favor trusted insiders.
  • Economic Policy Focus: Pay attention to whether the administration shifts its public messaging from high-level macroeconomic figures toward tangible, direct relief for middle- and lower-income households struggling with daily living costs.
  • Party Dynamics and Dissent: Monitor how the Democratic Party leadership under Rep. Kim Min-seok handles internal criticism. A shift toward accommodating diverse voices within the party will signal a effort to rebuild the coalition; further purges of critics will suggest deepening partisan isolation.
  • Support Trends for Third Parties: Track polling numbers for minor progressive factions like the Rebuilding Korea Party. Growth in these alternative outlets will serve as a direct measure of voter dissatisfaction with the ruling party's core strategy.
