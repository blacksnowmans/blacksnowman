South Korea’s Judicial Crisis: The Impeachment Threat Against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over Kim Keon-hee’s Release

October 19, 2026

A high-stakes political showdown is coming to a head in Seoul, centered on the marble halls of South Korea’s Supreme Court. With just under two weeks remaining before November 1, a strict procedural clock is ticking for the Korean judiciary, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), and the country’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP). At the center of the storm is Kim Keon-hee, wife of the impeached and currently imprisoned former president, Mr. Yun Suk-yeol.

Ms. Kim, who was handed a five-year prison sentence in her second-instance appellate trial for corruption and influence-peddling charges, faces the expiration of her legal detention mandate on October 31. If the Supreme Court does not deliver a final En Banc verdict—or if court authorities fail to issue a new arrest warrant before midnight on October 31—she will legally walk out of jail on November 1. For the ruling DPK under President Lee Jae-myung, such an outcome is seen not merely as a judicial oversight, but as deliberate nonfeasance by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. In response, senior DPK lawmakers are openly preparing to introduce an unprecedented motion to impeach the head of South Korea’s highest court.

The Ticking Clock: Why Kim Keon-hee Could Walk Free on November 1

To understand how a convicted figure could walk free despite a five-year appellate sentence, one must look at South Korea’s legal framework governing pre-final-verdict detention. Under Korean criminal procedure, criminal defendants can only be held in pre-trial or pre-final-sentence custody for strictly defined time blocks. Once an appellate court delivers its judgment, the Supreme Court has a limited window to either affirm the conviction, order a retrial, or extend detention through specific statutory channels.

The deadline for Ms. Kim’s current detention warrant is October 31. If the Supreme Court’s En Banc panel—a full bench assembly led by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—does not issue a ruling by that date, her custody status automatically lapses. Barring immediate intervention, prison doors will open for her on the morning of November 1.

To prevent this scenario, independent special prosecutors sent an urgent request to the Supreme Court’s petty benches (smaller four-justice divisions) to issue an additional arrest warrant based on secondary charges. However, the Supreme Court administrative office has delayed assigning the case to a specific bench. Because formal warrant hearings and documentary reviews require at least four to five business days, every day the court stalls brings Ms. Kim closer to freedom.

With the Supreme Court seemingly frozen, attention has turned to the Seoul Central District Court, led by Chief Judge Oh Min-seok. Two separate indictments involving Ms. Kim remain pending before the district court, including allegations of illegal political intervention involving the Unification Church. Progressive legal commentators and ruling party officials argue that Chief Judge Oh holds both the legal authority and the duty to issue an ex officio arrest warrant from the bench. Doing so would establish a new legal basis to keep Ms. Kim in custody while her broader appeals proceed.

A Fractured Judiciary: Allegations of Slow-Walking and Political Calculations

The push to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae goes beyond a single high-profile case. DPK lawmakers accuse the Chief Justice of engaging in a systematic strategy of procedural delay designed to destabilize the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung.

Critics highlight several key administrative decisions by Chief Justice Cho:

  • Stalling Bench Assignments: The Supreme Court has repeatedly delayed allocating critical warrant requests regarding Ms. Kim to petty benches, narrowing the window for prosecutors to secure an extension.
  • Neglecting Recusal Motions: A formal motion requesting the recusal of Senior Judge Lee Jin-gwan—who was assigned to cases involving President Lee Jae-myung—has sat unattended at the Supreme Court for days without a ruling, leaving procedural ambiguity unresolved.
  • Refusing Judicial Nominations: In an extraordinary move for a sitting Chief Justice, Cho has declined to exercise his constitutional mandate to formally recommend nominees for vacant Supreme Court justice seats, leaving the high court understaffed.

For the ruling DPK, these actions represent a pattern of political maneuver disguised as judicial discretion. By allowing Supreme Court vacancies to linger while selectively slowing down high-stakes rulings, Chief Justice Cho is accused of misusing his court administrative power to shield figures from the previous administration while keeping legal pressure alive against President Lee Jae-myung.

The Political Fallout: Conservative Re-alignment and the Opposition Strategy

The potential release of Ms. Kim on November 1 carries immense political significance. Following the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yun Suk-yeol, South Korea’s conservative bloc—represented in the National Assembly by the main opposition People Power Party—has struggled to mount a unified offensive against President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

For conservative organizers, including the hardline "Taegeukgi" rally factions that frequently gather in central Seoul, securing Ms. Kim’s release would be celebrated as a major political victory. It would serve as a powerful symbolic narrative: that the current ruling government is unable to hold its political opponents indefinitely and that the judiciary remains independent of DPK influence.

This strategy did not emerge overnight. Back in August, conservative media outlets published reports highlighting Ms. Kim’s deteriorating health in prison, which was swiftly followed by an organized campaign that submitted over 1,000 petitions to the court urging her release. Her legal defense team has simultaneously inundated the courts with extensive written briefs challenging every warrant extension requested by prosecutors, offering legal cover for sympathetic judges within the judiciary.

If Ms. Kim steps out of custody, conservative groups plan to leverage the moment to rally their demoralized base, frame President Lee Jae-myung’s administration as ineffective, and reshape the domestic political landscape ahead of upcoming electoral cycles.

The Impeachment Flashpoint and What Lies Ahead

As the October 31 deadline approaches, the ruling Democratic Party is moving closer to exercising its parliamentary majority to remove Chief Justice Cho. DPK Spokesperson Lee Yong-woo publicly warned that public patience has reached its limit, while Senior Supreme Council Member Kim Min-seok confirmed that an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice is officially on the table.

In response, the opposition People Power Party has launched a sharp counter-offensive, warning that impeaching a sitting Chief Justice would plunge the judicial branch into total paralysis. Conservative commentators have even claimed that freezing the Supreme Court could accidentally create legal loopholes resulting in the release of Mr. Yun Suk-yeol from prison.

Legal scholars and ruling party representatives dismiss these claims as fear-mongering. Under South Korea’s Constitution and the Court Organization Act, if a Chief Justice is impeached by the National Assembly, their duties are immediately suspended and an Acting Chief Justice automatically steps in from among the senior remaining Supreme Court justices. The court’s docket and operational capacity remain intact.

What to watch in the coming days:

  • Action by Seoul Central District Court: Watch whether Chief Judge Oh Min-seok exercises his power to issue an ex officio arrest warrant on pending charges before October 31.
  • Supreme Court Petty Bench Decisions: Watch whether the Supreme Court rapidly assigns and processes the special prosecutor's warrant request in the remaining days of October.
  • National Assembly Impeachment Motion: If the courts fail to act and Ms. Kim's release becomes imminent, monitor whether the DPK formally introduces an impeachment bill against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae on the National Assembly floor.

The unfolding standoff highlights the deep institutional tensions in post-impeachment South Korea. Whether through a sudden judicial warrant or a dramatic parliamentary vote to impeach the Chief Justice, the events of the coming week will test the boundaries between judicial authority, constitutional accountability, and political power.
