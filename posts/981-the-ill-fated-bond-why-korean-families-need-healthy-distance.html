The "Ill-Fated" Bond: Why Korean Families Need Healthy Distance

October 11, 2026

We are often told that the parent-child relationship is the most sacred, intimate, and affectionate bond a human can experience. Yet, in the quiet reality of many homes, this same proximity is the source of our deepest wounds and heaviest shackles. Recently, Boman, a Buddhist monk who appeared on the popular interview series Intellectuals’ Guest Seat, offered a provocative perspective: he argued that the parent-child relationship is, by its very nature, an "ill-fated" one—not because it is inherently evil, but because the emotions are so tightly intertwined that it becomes suffocating, making objective, healthy interaction nearly impossible.

The Emotional Violence Masked as Parental Love

Society often assumes that everything a parent says to a child stems from unconditional goodwill. However, beneath the surface lies a chilling desire for control. When a parent snaps, "I told you not to do that!" or sighs, "My life is ruined because of you," they are not merely expressing frustration; they are inflicting emotional trauma that can leave a lifelong mark. For a child, these outbursts feel like a negation of their very existence.

Perhaps the most insidious form of this violence is the parent who asserts, "You’ll thank me for this later." By forcing a child to conform to a pre-determined mold—often masquerading as a "model student"—parents effectively strip the child of their own voice. They stop asking, "What do you love? What kind of life do you want to live?" and instead turn the child into a functional component designed to satisfy their own unmet ambitions. This is the root of the modern unhappiness: when parents feel their own lives have turned into a hollow pursuit of societal standards, they project that emptiness onto their children, desperate to see them succeed where they failed.

Disciplining or Dominating? The Truth Behind the Lies

Children raised in rigid, controlling environments often become adept at lying. They do not lie because they are inherently deceitful; they lie to survive the looming threat of the parent’s anger and the fear of failure.

Monk Boman shared a personal anecdote from his childhood: when he was caught stealing candy from a local shop, he expected physical punishment. Instead, his mother sat on the floor and wept, telling him how deeply disappointed and heartbroken she was that her son would steal. That moment of emotional vulnerability was far more painful than any beating, and it was the last time he ever took something that wasn't his.

This highlights the distinction between true discipline and mere domination. When a parent lashes out with fury, they close the door to communication. When they open a space for a sincere conversation—asking why a child made a certain choice—they initiate a process of growth. Many parents today lack the emotional bandwidth for this; they are too busy trying to sculpt their children into perfect shapes to meet their own standards, failing to realize that they are simply creating a mask of obedience that will eventually shatter.

The Dangers of "Excessive Protection" and Emotional Enmeshment

In South Korea, it is common for parents to keep children in a state of "mental infancy" long after they have reached legal adulthood. There is a world of difference between saying, "Try it, and let’s talk about what went well and what didn’t," and saying, "I’ll do it for you." The latter does not protect the child; it dismantles the very scaffold they need to stand on their own.

When these "big babies" eventually lose their parental safety net, they often collapse into a state of panic. Without the tools to solve even minor life problems, they retreat into their rooms, shutting out the world. Parents often brush off a child’s failure as trivial, but to the child, that failure represents a total collapse of their world. A parent should not be an agent who lives life for the child, but a facilitator who waits patiently as the child carries their own burden.

The Wisdom of Distance: Why Proximity Kills

In the Buddhist tradition, there is a concept called Muyujeongbeop (無有定法), which teaches that there are no fixed, absolute truths. Defining education or parenting through a rigid, "this is the only way" lens—such as the relentless obsession with elite university entrance—is a recipe for disaster.

The most practical metaphor for the family dynamic is a pair of glass cups. If you press them tightly together, they are bound to crack or shatter at the slightest vibration. The same applies to families. When parents use "family" as an excuse to trample on privacy and enforce constant togetherness, they inevitably wound each other. By maintaining a "healthy distance"—treating one another with the same level of courtesy and respect one would show a stranger—they create a protective buffer that keeps the relationship intact.

The first step toward this healthy distance is language. Using polite, respectful speech (jondaetmal in Korean) between family members might feel like it creates a wall, but in reality, it is a shield. It prevents the kind of casual, cruel insults that destroy intimacy.

As we navigate a society currently undergoing significant shifts—from the political turbulence that led to the imprisonment of the former leader, the disgraced Yoon Suk-yeol, to the ongoing challenges under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—the home remains the most essential, yet often most volatile, sphere of influence. To break the cycles of generational trauma, we must stop viewing our children as trophies or extensions of ourselves. We must learn to let go of the "filial shackle" and accept our children as independent beings. Only in that cool, clear space of distance can the true warmth of a family finally begin to grow.

What to Watch Next

  • The Policy Shift: With the Lee Jae-myung administration prioritizing social welfare and emotional health, keep an eye on new state-sponsored parenting education programs that aim to move away from the traditional, rigid authoritarian model.
  • Societal Trends: Watch for the rise of "independent living" support initiatives for young adults, which may signal a gradual cultural move away from the extreme parental over-involvement that has historically defined the Korean family structure.
  • Personal Growth: Start by observing your own communication style. The next time a conflict arises, replace the urge to correct or command with a simple, "I am curious why you feel that way." The result may surprise you.
