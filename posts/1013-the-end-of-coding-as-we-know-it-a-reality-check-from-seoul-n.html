The End of Coding as We Know It: A Reality Check from Seoul National University

October 12, 2026

If you have spent any time online recently, you have likely seen the viral clips: a developer types a few sentences into an AI model, and seconds later, a perfectly functional, complex program spills onto the screen. For students eyeing computer science degrees and junior developers just starting their careers, this is not just a technological marvel—it is a terrifying prospect. The question looming over the industry is no longer "Will AI replace developers?" but rather "What is left for us to do?"

Lee Jae-wook, a professor of Computer Science and Engineering at Seoul National University (South Korea’s most prestigious university), recently weighed in on this exact anxiety. In a candid discussion, he breaks down why the traditional definition of "programming" is rapidly evaporating and why the current panic, while understandable, stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of what engineering actually entails.

The Shift from Writing to Judging

For decades, the life of a software engineer was defined by the physical act of "writing"—laboring over syntax, drafting code, and painstakingly debugging every line. Today, that manual labor is rapidly approaching 100% automation. When it comes to textbook problems or standard logic, AI outperforms humans in both speed and accuracy.

Professor Lee argues that the role of the developer has shifted from a "writer" to an "editor." If AI can generate the code, the human’s primary value is no longer the ability to type, but the ability to read and critique. The bottleneck is no longer implementation; it is verification. Does this code actually serve the business logic? Is it secure? Is it efficient? In the AI era, the developer is becoming a manager of intelligent agents, orchestrating output rather than crafting it from scratch.

However, Lee issues a stark warning: you cannot be a successful "engineering manager" if you do not understand engineering. Relying on AI without a foundation in computer science is like an architect who doesn't understand physics. Without deep, structural knowledge, you cannot distinguish between an AI’s "hallucination" and a brilliant, functional solution. The, "I’ll just tell the AI what to do" mindset is a trap for the unskilled.

The "Vibe Coding" Mirage

We are currently witnessing the rise of "vibe coding"—a trend where individuals with little to no technical background use conversational language to build functional prototypes. Stories abound of government officials creating complex legal comparison chatbots in an afternoon. While the speed of these prototypes is breathtaking, they often hide a dangerous reality.

In a production environment, 99% accuracy is often a failure. If you are running a platform with one billion users, a 1% error rate impacts 10 million people. In sectors like finance or cybersecurity, even a 0.1% margin of error can lead to catastrophic, multi-million dollar failures (KRW billions).

Research analyzing over 600 million commits suggests that as AI-generated code proliferates, technical debt accumulates in the shadows. Refactoring rates drop, and systems begin to rot from within. AI-generated code is not a finished asset; it is a rough draft that demands rigorous human verification. Ignoring this leads to the "9th commit" crisis—where the system seems fine initially, only to collapse under the weight of accumulated, unmanageable technical debt.

The "Capability Overhang" and the Education Crisis

The gap between the tools available to us and our ability to use them has created what experts call "Capability Overhang." A small group of power users is leveraging AI to achieve superhuman productivity, while the general workforce lags behind, failing to understand the limits and the blind spots of the technology.

This creates a massive challenge for higher education. In the past, the struggle of solving a problem was where the learning happened. Today, if a professor assigns a task, a student can have an AI solve it in 0.1 seconds. If students simply play the role of a "delivery person" between the AI and the classroom, they leave university with a degree but no deep knowledge. Education must move away from rote memorization and towards training students to define problems, set constraints, and think critically about the architecture of their solutions.

Economic Shifts: The New "Hybrid Developer"

Despite the anxiety, Professor Lee remains optimistic about the future of the field, drawing on the "Jevons Paradox"—the economic theory that as technology makes a resource more efficient and cheaper, consumption of that resource explodes. When ATMs were introduced, people thought bank tellers would disappear. Instead, the ease of banking grew the industry, and tellers shifted into higher-value roles like financial consulting and strategy.

We are seeing the birth of the "Hybrid Developer"—someone who understands both business planning and technical execution. The silos of the past, where a project manager would hand a spec to a developer, are collapsing. Today, those who can identify a real-world problem and bridge the gap with technology are the ones who will thrive.

As Madison Huang of Harvard University famously noted, AI is the "ultimate equalizer." It lowers the barrier to entry for non-majors, allowing anyone with a clear idea and a sharp eye for a problem to build software.

What to Watch Next

  • The Junior Crisis: Monitor how companies adjust their hiring practices. As entry-level coding tasks become automated, firms will need to find new ways to train the next generation of engineers.
  • Technical Debt Management: Watch for new software development lifecycle (SDLC) tools specifically designed to audit and "clean" AI-generated code, as companies struggle to maintain systems built by AI.
  • Educational Reform: Look for shifts in South Korean university curriculums. Universities that abandon "answer-based" testing in favor of "problem-definition" workshops will likely produce the most resilient graduates.

Ultimately, AI will not replace the need for critical thinking; it will make it the most valuable currency in the job market. The ability to ask the right question is, and will remain, the most human skill of all.
