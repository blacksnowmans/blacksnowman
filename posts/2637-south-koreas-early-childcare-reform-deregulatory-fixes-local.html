South Korea’s Early Childcare Reform: Deregulatory Fixes, Local Shortages, and the Reality on the Ground

November 23, 2026

Imagine being a young working parent moving into one of South Korea’s newly developed master-planned suburban towns, known locally as shindosi. You are surrounded by high-rise apartment towers, green parks, and walking paths filled with young couples pushing strollers. Headline writers worldwide routinely cite South Korea’s plummeting national birthrate, but in your specific zip code, young infants are everywhere. You need to return to your job, so you open the national enrollment system to register your seven-month-old child at a neighborhood daycare. The reality hits immediately: you are placed at the bottom of an agonizingly long waiting list, with dozens of local families competing for a handful of infant spots.

This paradox—a country facing an unprecedented demographic contraction while young parents in high-density suburbs cannot find infant care—captures the structural strain on South Korea's early childhood education and care system. On November 22, South Korea’s central government and local city councils unveiled a series of regulatory updates and legislative audits aimed at addressing these chronic bottlenecks. From loosening strict facility rules for infant classrooms to exposing local budgetary gaps, the latest announcements reflect both the urgency of government policy and the persistent friction on the ground.

Deregulating Infant Daycares: A Policy Pivot for High-Demand Suburbs

The headline regulatory change comes directly from the Ministry of Education, South Korea’s central government agency overseeing school policy and now leading the integration of early childhood care. On November 22, the ministry officially promulgated an amendment to the Enforcement Rules of the Infant Care Act. The central feature of this revision is a long-awaited rule change allowing daycare centers in high-demand areas—specifically rapidly growing suburban planned cities—to operate dedicated infant-only classes without being required to maintain mixed age groups or older toddler facilities.

Under previous administrative rules, daycare providers faced complex structural requirements that linked infant capacity (children under age two) with older toddler facilities (ages three to five). Because infants require significantly higher staff-to-child ratios and specialized physical facilities, private and community daycares often found it financially unfeasible to scale up infant capacity dynamically. As young families flooded into new housing developments in outer Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province, infant waiting lists surged while older toddler classrooms in older urban cores sat partially empty.

By removing the requirement to bundle infant care with older preschool tracks, the Ministry of Education is enabling facility operators to dedicate 100 percent of their licensed capacity to infants where neighborhood demand is concentrated. However, expanding physical access is only half of the equation. To address lingering parental anxieties regarding child safety and facility standards, the amended rules also institute a mandatory 40-hour retraining requirement for daycare teachers whose professional licenses were previously revoked and are seeking recertification. The ministry aims to ensure that as daycare doors open wider to infants, professional accountability measures are raised simultaneously.

These regulatory adjustments arrive amid a broader administrative overhaul known as Yubo Tonghap—South Korea’s ambitious policy project to unify its dual early childhood system. Traditionally, daycare centers (eorinjip) for infants and toddlers fell under the Ministry of Health and Welfare and municipal social care divisions, while kindergartens (yuchiwon) for children aged three to five were overseen by the Ministry of Education and regional school boards. The ongoing consolidation transfers full authority to the Ministry of Education. Demonstrating this shift, the Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education announced it would host its 2026 Consolidation Performance Sharing Event on November 23 to showcase pilot models, while local childcare organizations in regions like Danyang County held public pledges supporting the unified system.

Local Friction: Long Waiting Lists, Safety Deficits, and Demographic Mismatches

While central regulators in Seoul draft rule changes on paper, municipal council proceedings on November 22 highlighted how unevenly childcare challenges manifest across different cities and districts.

In Bupyeong District, located in the major western port city of Incheon, the struggle is not just inside physical daycare buildings but inside the home. Kim Jin-woong, a member of the Bupyeong District Council, revealed that 89 families in the district are currently stranded on waiting lists for the government-sponsored in-home childminding service (ai-dolbom). According to council data, the average waiting period for a family to receive an assigned home caregiver has stretched to 94 days—more than three full months. For dual-income parents attempting to return from parental leave, a three-month lag creates a severe care vacuum that state programs are failing to plug due to a shortage of qualified home caregivers.

Meanwhile, in Cheonan, South Chungcheong Province, local lawmakers raised alarms over basic physical safety in child facilities. City Council member Yoo Young-chae pointed out during a legislative session that Cheonan allocated precisely 0 won in its municipal budget for asbestos removal in local daycares and regional children's centers. Asbestos, a known carcinogen used in older building materials, remains unmitigated in aging neighborhood facilities where infants and young children spend up to ten hours a day, highlighting how fiscal oversight can lapse even as high-level national policy focuses on expansion.

The local reports also revealed stark demographic imbalances across different regions of the country. In Iksan, North Jeolla Province, local discussions highlighted an extreme operational anomaly: a local daycare facility operating with just 2 enrolled children but maintaining a payroll of 5 staff members and administrators. This contrast illustrates South Korea's dual childcare crisis. In booming suburban satellite cities, infants are turned away due to lack of space; in depopulating rural towns, state subsidies are drawn by under-enrolled facilities to maintain minimum legal staffing ratios, triggering calls for administrative restructuring and subsidy reallocation.

Administrative discipline has also emerged as a priority for local councils. In Suwon, Gyeonggi Province, Lee Chul-seong, chairman of the City Council’s Health and Welfare Committee, called for stringent follow-up audits on daycares that have received administrative penalties, warning that failure to enforce compliance allows compliance gaps and safety violations to recur unmonitored.

Meals, Workplace Daycares, and the Fragmented Support System

Beyond classroom enrollment and physical building safety, the quality of daily care—specifically nutritional support and workplace integration—remains a key battleground for parents and local assemblies.

In the Gyeonggi Provincial Assembly, representative Park Soon-hee called on the provincial government to immediately eliminate the funding gap for free meals between kindergartens and daycare centers. Because kindergartens historically operated under education board budgets while daycares relied on municipal welfare allocations, state-funded meal subsidy rates per child have differed based on institutional classification. For parents, sending a child to a daycare rather than a kindergarten has meant receiving lower per-capita food budget allocations—an inequity that policy advocates argue must be equalized as part of the broader Yubo Tonghap merger.

To fill remaining gaps, municipalities and public institutions are expanding alternative care structures:

  • Public-Type Daycares (Gonggong-hyeong): Yangsan City in South Gyeongsang Province announced new designations of "public-type daycares"—private facilities that receive public subsidies and operational oversight in exchange for maintaining government-level tuition caps and safety standards.
  • Hourly Care Services (Sigan-je Boyuk): The "Booyoung Love-As-It-Is Daycare" located in Busan New Port received a Minister of Education Award for its exemplary model of hourly childcare, allowing non-working or part-time working parents to drop off infants for short blocks of time without committing to full-day enrollment.
  • Workplace Daycares (Jikjang Boyuk): The Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service awarded 33 outstanding workplace daycares across the nation, recognizing corporate and public-sector facilities that allow working parents to commute with their children. Regional initiatives accompanied these announcements, including teacher professional development workshops in Mokpo and local daycare association forums in Namyangju.

What to Watch Next

For parents residing in South Korea and policy analysts monitoring Asia’s demographic trends, the developments of late November signal a critical transition period. As South Korea attempts to reverse its fertility declines by lowering the structural burdens of parenting, the focus shifts to how effectively local governments execute central mandates.

Key developments to track over the coming months include:

  • Local Authorization Pace for Infant-Only Classes: Watch whether municipal authorities in new suburban zones quickly approve applications from daycare providers seeking to convert facilities into infant-only centers under the revised Infant Care Act rules.
  • Caregiver Recruitment and Waitlist Reduction: Monitor whether local councils in high-density districts like Incheon Bupyeong succeed in budgeting higher compensation for home childminders to reduce average wait times below 90 days.
  • Budget Parity and Safety Audits in the 2026 Fiscal Year: Track municipal budget debates in cities like Cheonan and Gyeonggi Province to verify whether local assemblies allocate actual funds for asbestos abatement and equalized meal subsidies ahead of the full 2026 Yubo Tonghap integration.
