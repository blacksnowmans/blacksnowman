South Korea's Political Stalemate: President Lee Jae-myung's Sliding Approval and the Chief Justice's Clash with Parliament

October 07, 2026

Step into the current political atmosphere in South Korea, and you can almost feel the collective tension hanging over the country's timelines and news cycles. For an administration that came into office promising swift problem-solving and economic relief, the mood has shifted uncomfortably. Supporters who once anchored a comfortable majority are looking at the numbers with growing unease, wondering how momentum slipped away so quickly. To understand South Korea’s political landscape right now, one has to look at a convergence of sliding public approval, simmering economic fatigue, and a high-stakes institutional showdown between the judiciary and the legislature.

The Slipping Numbers: A Reality Check for the Lee Administration

Recent data from the pollster Jowon C&I, gathered from October 3 to 5 among 2,000 adults nationwide, painted a grim picture for President Lee Jae-myung. His approval rating sat at 37.1%, failing to break a persistent downward trend. More alarmingly, negative evaluations climbed to a high of 61.4%. When compared to earlier in the year—when approval hovered near 49.4% in July—the erosion of public backing has become impossible for the ruling camp to ignore.

The decline is not distributed evenly, revealing profound regional shifts. Outside of the traditional progressive stronghold of Jeolla (Gwangju, South Jeolla, and North Jeolla provinces), where approval remains above 50%, disapproval has surged past the majority mark nationwide. Particularly painful for the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's primary center-left party—is the shaky ground in the Busan-Ulsan-Gyeongnam (PK) region, where negative sentiment reached 64.9%. Urban centers like Seoul and Incheon/Gyeonggi are registering similar levels of discontent.

Political analysts attribute this fatigue not to a single catastrophic misstep, but to a compounding mix of economic strain, property market anxieties, and policy friction. Crucially, voters in their 30s—a demographic vital for navigating housing and asset-building—have shown distinct signs of alienation. When an administration's core base begins to fracture under the weight of everyday financial stress, the political fallout spreads rapidly.

Chief Justice Cho Hee-de and the Collision of Powers

While the executive branch grapples with public discontent, the judiciary has injected a volatile new element into South Korea’s politics. Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de sparked a major controversy by submitting a formal statement refusing to attend an upcoming parliamentary audit by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee.

Cho’s justification—that questioning the Chief Justice over judicial appointments infringes upon the separation of powers and judicial independence—drew immediate and fierce backlash. Critics from progressive factions did not mince words, accusing the top judge of crossing the line from judicial administration into partisan politics. Within the legal community around Seoul's Seocho-dong district, whispers of internal discontent have turned into open frustration, with low-level judicial polls indicating deep dissatisfaction with his leadership style.

In a constitutional democracy, legislative oversight is a foundational check on power. By attempting to bypass the National Assembly's audit, the head of the judiciary has transformed a routine institutional check into an open turf war. Critics argue that when the highest arbiter of law begins bending legal principles to shield executive power or personal prerogative, public trust in the rule of law suffers immeasurably.

Reclaiming Momentum Through Reform and Principle

For President Lee and the Democratic Party, the mounting crises present a stark strategic choice. Commentators recalling past political dynamics argue that retreating in the face of pressure rarely stops an offensive from the conservative opposition, led by the People Power Party. When poll numbers sag, preemptive appeasement often signals weakness rather than conciliation.

Instead, observers suggest that the administration's best path to recovering its footing lies in leaning into core reforms. The ongoing clash with Chief Justice Cho, paradoxical as it seems, offers the ruling camp an opening to draw a sharp contrast between its reformist agenda and entrenched institutional resistance. By addressing livelihood economic issues—such as stabilizing public utility costs and easing household financial burdens—alongside a firm defense of constitutional order, the government could revitalize its core base.

Public enthusiasm for reform is driven by tangible results and accountability. If the administration hesitates to confront institutional overreach, it risks losing the very sense of agency that voters entrusted to it.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward upcoming local elections and navigates an increasingly polarized political environment, several key developments will dictate the course of the administration:

  • The National Assembly's Next Steps: Watch how the Legislation and Judiciary Committee handles Chief Justice Cho's refusal to attend the audit, and whether the ruling party pushes for stronger legislative sanctions.
  • Economic Policy Rollouts: Pay attention to practical measures targeting household debt, inflation, and real estate, which will serve as the litmus test for the administration's competence.
  • Polling Trends in Swing Regions: Monitor whether the slide in key areas like the Seoul metropolitan region and the Gyeongsang provinces stabilizes or deepens in subsequent weekly surveys.

Ultimately, South Korea's current political friction is a test of resilience. Whether the administration can pivot from defensive management to decisive, principled governance will determine if it can weather the storm and regain the public trust.
