South Korea's Prosecution Reform or Illusion? Inside the Ministry of Justice's New Organizational Plan

September 10, 2026

For decades, South Korea’s prosecutors have wielded a near-monopoly on state power, combining the authority to investigate crimes with the sole discretion to indict suspects. It is a potent mix that has fueled countless political scandals, bringing down presidents and shaking corporate titans. For years, reformers have demanded a simple solution: split the powers. Take investigative duties away from prosecutors and hand them to police or specialized agencies, leaving prosecutors strictly to handle trials.

Now, the government is attempting to codify that exact division. But as the South Korean Ministry of Justice unveils its long-awaited organizational blueprint for the newly minted Prosecution Service—an agency stripped of its primary investigative powers—critics are taking a hard look at the fine print. Rather than a clean break from the past, what the Ministry has put on paper looks less like a revolution and more like an institutional magic trick. Beneath the progressive rhetoric of reform, the massive infrastructure, personnel, and subtle levers of influence belonging to South Korea's formidable prosecutors remain remarkably intact.

The Ghost of Investigations Past: Why Thousands of Staff Are Staying Put

To understand why watchdogs are sounding the alarm, one must look past the Ministry of Justice’s headline figures. The Ministry has touted its new organizational plan as a major downsizing, claiming a 21.4% reduction in overall staff. On paper, the new Prosecution Service is pegged at 8,412 positions, including 2,292 prosecutors and 6,120 general staff members.

However, basic math reveals a different reality. Once you subtract the 1,761 personnel being transferred to the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Office—South Korea's designated new primary investigative body—the actual net reduction in staff is a meager 238 people. Crucially, roughly 7,000 investigators who once drove the prosecutors' direct investigative machinery are moving over to the new Prosecution Service almost entirely en masse. Furthermore, the total cap on active prosecutors remains virtually unchanged at 2,292.

This creates a glaring contradiction. If an agency is legally barred from conducting direct investigations, why does it retain an army of 7,000 investigative personnel and a full complement of prosecutors? Critics, including lawmakers like opposition figures Kim Yong-min and Hwang Un-ha, argue that keeping this vast workforce intact serves a specific, unstated purpose: positioning the staff to continuously review case files from other agencies, effectively maintaining an indirect grip on investigations. In a country where political tides shift rapidly, leaving the physical and human foundation of the old prosecutorial system untouched ensures that the institution can revert to its former glory at a moment's notice.

Building a Bureaucratic Fortress: Oversight Boards and Backdoor Investigations

The structural maneuvering goes beyond merely preserving headcounts. The Ministry's blueprint introduces several new internal departments that critics argue are designed to circumvent the spirit of the reform entirely.

Chief among them is the proposed "Judicial Control Division," to be established within every regional prosecution office. Its official mandate is to review cases where police or other agencies have chosen not to file charges. On paper, supervising lower investigative bodies has historically fallen under the purview of prosecutors. But elevating this function into a grandly named, specialized division creates a strange institutional hierarchy—a bureaucratic layer that effectively places a non-investigative prosecution office back in a position to oversee and police the very agencies that took over its investigative powers.

Worse still are the newly minted "Major Crimes Departments" slated for both central and regional offices. The official rationale for these units is to "cooperate with and support" the Serious Crimes Investigation Office and other police bodies. But critics point out the obvious loophole: if investigation and prosecution are truly separated, a prosecution-only agency has no business maintaining massive, specialized crime units designed to interface with field investigators. By embedding these departments into the prosecution service, the blueprint creates a convenient backdoor, allowing prosecutors to inject themselves into ongoing investigations under the guise of administrative support and joint operations.

The Risk of a Prosecutor-Run Cartel and the Danger of Personnel Continuity

Institutions are ultimately defined by the people who run them, and this is where reform advocates see the greatest vulnerability. Under the current trajectory, both ends of South Korea's newly divided justice system—the primary investigative bodies and the prosecution service—risk being headed by former prosecutors.

When figures steeped for decades in the traditional "prosecutorial mindset" occupy the leadership chairs of both separated entities, the structural split becomes meaningless. Networking, informal communication channels, and institutional habits will effortlessly bridge the gap, effectively recreating the old power cartel under a new organizational name. Lawmakers have repeatedly warned that true structural separation requires aggressive "de-prosecutorialization" in top-tier appointments. Handing the keys of the new investigative agencies to figures molded by the old system risks spawning a two-headed monster rather than dismantling an overreaching bureaucracy.

For an outside observer watching South Korea's endless cycle of institutional reform, this latest battle offers a sobering lesson: declaring a system reformed on paper is far easier than stripping away the deep-seated muscle memory of a powerful state apparatus. Without rigorous oversight to trim bloated support staff, eliminate ambiguous "cooperation" clauses, and place outsiders rather than legacy prosecutors at the helm, the grand promise of separation may quietly dissolve.

What to Watch Next

As this organizational plan moves from proposal to implementation, several key developments will determine whether South Korea's latest reform has teeth or is merely cosmetic:

  • Legislative Review and Budget Fights: Watch for parliamentary committee debates—particularly within the Legislation and Judiciary Committee—where opposition lawmakers are expected to challenge the bloated personnel caps and push for legally binding cuts to prosecutor staffing levels.
  • Key Leadership Appointments: The eventual selection of the inaugural chiefs for the newly established investigative agencies will serve as a litmus test. Watch whether the administration appoints career prosecutors or independent outsiders with a mandate for true structural change.
  • Inter-Agency Turf Wars: Monitor how the newly formed regional Judicial Control Divisions and Major Crimes Departments interact with the National Police Agency and the Serious Crimes Investigation Office on high-profile cases, which will reveal whether prosecutors are actively attempting to reclaim de facto investigative command.
