The Quest for a Century-Long Political Vision in South Korea

September 10, 2026

The conversation at the "Century Committee"—a recurring forum centered on the future of South Korean politics—often begins with an unlikely ritual: stretching. As host Ryu Mil-hoe led the audience through a routine of shoulder rolls and posture adjustments, the message was clear: before we can fix a rigid political landscape or an ossified social system, we must first awaken our own bodies and senses. It sounds eccentric, perhaps even dismissive of the gravity of the issues at hand, but the underlying sentiment is profound. In a system that has grown stiff and unresponsive, the first act of defiance is simply to stop being numb.

Yet, the reality that follows this session is far more grueling than any physical tension. During the discussion, media critic Kim Ou-soo and his panel highlighted a recent "exclusive" report by the Chosun Ilbo, one of South Korea’s most influential conservative newspapers. The article claimed that a majority of police officers supported the prosecution retaining its investigative powers. On the surface, the headline suggested a consensus within the 130,000-strong police force. However, as the panel’s research team—dubbed "Makpick"—dug into the data, they discovered a staggering deception: the survey was based on the opinions of only eight people. When the media can transform the voices of five individuals into the mandate of an entire national organization, the collapse of basic public discourse becomes impossible to ignore. It is a reminder that in the current climate, facts are often treated as mere raw material for political engineering.

Novelist Jo Jung-rae and the Anatomy of a Broken Society

Into this chaotic atmosphere walked Jo Jung-rae, one of Korea’s most celebrated novelists, to discuss his latest work, Frost Flower. At 84, Jo is a literary giant known for epic historical novels like Taebaek Mountain Range. When asked if this would be his final book, he spoke with the bluntness of a man who has stared death in the face. Following a major surgery last year on his abdominal aorta—a procedure that left him with a 30-centimeter incision and a reliance on artificial vessels—he confessed that he writes not out of leisure, but out of a desperate, terminal responsibility. He rejects the doctors' warnings to rest, asserting that "no one but God can stop my will."

The spark for Frost Flower was a stinging critique from a reader who once asked why he never wrote about love. Taking that as a personal challenge, Jo has crafted a narrative that is far from a traditional romance. As described by the panel, the story follows a man who sacrifices his wealth for his lover, and a woman who attempts to immortalize their bond through art—only to have their lives dismantled by the colluding forces of corporate greed and a corrupt judicial system. While the panel joked about the grimness of the plot, the laughter masked a deep, collective ache: the recognition that in modern Korea, capitalism and legal power act as a pair of shears, cutting through the most intimate human connections.

For Jo, this novel is an indictment of a society where everything, even human affection, is commodified. By writing this in the twilight of his life, he is posing a sharp question to his readers: What is left to protect when the institutions meant to serve the public become the very mechanisms that destroy it?

The Challenge of Building a Sustainable Political Party

As the discussion shifted toward the possibility of a "century-long political party," Professor Park Koo-yong framed the problem in biological terms. He noted that while human biology has remained largely unchanged since our ancestors roamed the savanna, our technology—particularly the rise of artificial intelligence—has evolved at a pace that our democratic institutions are failing to match. We are living in a era of hyper-urbanization and digital acceleration, yet our political parties remain stuck in the cycle of short-term interest battles.

Park, who previously criticized the regionalism of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal party), argued that the political landscape has shifted. The old regional divisions are fading, replaced by a more intense clash over values and practical governance. He argued that for a party to survive for 100 years, it cannot merely function as a vehicle for winning the next election. It must evolve into a "philosophical umbrella"—a structure capable of protecting human life amidst the profound upheavals of the 21st century.

Adding to this, Professor Kim Hee-kyo argued that the current political turmoil stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of the electorate. The liberal movement has been sustained not by traditional party discipline, but by a "power of the powerless"—a self-organized, highly motivated citizenry. These are the people who have engaged in unprecedented forms of grassroots support, from crowdfunding to monitoring local elections. They are not mindless "fandoms" as critics often claim; they are politically awakened agents who are using figures like Lee Jae-myung (the leader of the Democratic Party) to push for the systemic reforms they demand. If the party fails to recognize this energy, they will find themselves irrelevant before the decade is out.

The Verdict: Political Altruism as Survival

In the closing moments of the discussion, Jo Jung-rae offered a cynical but sobering observation: "Politicians do not act for the people; they act for themselves." He pointed out that while Western democracies often have thousands of active civic organizations monitoring power, South Korea has very few. He suggested that if the country is to have a future, citizens must stop trusting politicians to regulate themselves and instead take on the role of constant, relentless overseers.

Journalists Hong Sa-hun and Joo Jin-woo echoed this by reframing the very definition of a successful party. They argued that the "century-long party" is a myth unless it adopts an altruistic stance. Parties that exist only to hoard seats and defend their own narrow interests eventually alienate the public. True political longevity, they argued, comes from a party that is willing to sacrifice its own immediate gains to address the long-term anxieties of the public—whether it be the climate crisis, technological displacement, or structural inequality.

Ultimately, the consensus among the panelists was both a warning and a call to action. The era of "calculating" politics is nearing its end. A party that survives the next century will not be the one that is most clever at deception, but the one that proves itself to be the most capable of genuine empathy. For the citizens watching these developments, the path forward is clear: the responsibility for the state’s future does not lie with the politicians in the National Assembly, but with the vigilance of the people who demand something better.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on the increasing influence of independent civic groups within the liberal bloc. As the gap between traditional party leadership and the "highly awakened" grassroots voters widens, the pressure for the Democratic Party to either adopt a more radical, reformist agenda or risk internal fracture will become the defining story of the coming election cycles.
