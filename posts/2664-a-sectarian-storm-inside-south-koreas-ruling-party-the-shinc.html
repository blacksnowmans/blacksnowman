A Sectarian Storm Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party: The Shincheonji Infiltration Feud Explained

November 24, 2026

The afternoon of November 23 was supposed to be business as usual for the leadership of South Korea’s governing Democratic Party of Korea. Instead, the party’s Supreme Council meeting collapsed into a shouting match marked by furious walkouts, traded insults, and reported tears. At the center of the uproar was not economic legislation or parliamentary strategy, but an incendiary accusation: that tens of thousands of members of Shincheonji, a secretive and deeply controversial fringe religious sect, had systematically infiltrated the ruling party’s grassroots membership.

For observers outside South Korea, the spectacle of a ruling party tearing itself apart over church rosters might seem bizarre. Yet in South Korean politics, few accusations carry the destructive toxicity of ties to Shincheonji. By the time the gavel fell on the truncated meeting, the controversy had exposed bitter factional fault lines inside the party, placed party leader Kim Min-seok under intense scrutiny, and handed the conservative opposition a potent weapon against the administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

The 43,000-Member Claim and a Council in Disarray

According to party proceedings, the confrontation erupted when Supreme Council member Park Sun-won took the microphone to unveil what he claimed were internal findings on religious infiltration. Park asserted that party channels had confirmed 14,388 Shincheonji members actively enrolled as party members, with the total potentially reaching as high as 43,000. Crucially, Park claimed that roughly 7,000 of these individuals were concentrated in the southwestern Honam region.

To an outside observer, the geography might seem incidental, but in Korean politics, Honam—comprising Gwangju and the Jeolla provinces—is the historic cradle and emotional heartland of the liberal movement. In primary races, securing organized blocs of voting party members in Honam can make or break legislative and presidential candidacies. Suggesting that a stigmatized religious movement had inserted thousands of disciplined votes into this primary engine was an existential challenge to the party’s legitimacy.

Rather than triggering a sober, closed-door review, Park’s sudden public announcement ignited instant chaos inside the leadership room. Fellow leaders accused Park of hurling unverified, explosive numbers into the media spotlight without sharing evidentiary backing or vetted rosters. Park countered by offering to reveal his whistleblower, but the confrontation escalated rapidly.

A loud argument broke out between Park and party spokesperson Han Min-soo, audible to reporters waiting outside the doors. Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee and other figures aligned with the "pro-Cheong" faction—loyalists of prominent lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae—angrily denounced Park’s tactics, asking whether this was how politics should be conducted. Choi and several colleagues abruptly stormed out of the session. As later reported by the domestic daily Hankyoreh, the emotional intensity inside the room was severe enough that some leadership members were seen in tears. The council was forced to suspend its proceedings, completely derailing scheduled discussions on pressing state affairs.

"Colonial Collaborators" and Anonymous Whistleblowers

The feud quickly deepened beyond membership rolls into vicious ideological name-calling. Pushing back against colleagues who questioned his claims or urged caution, Park Sun-won reached for one of the most radioactive metaphors in Korean political rhetoric: he compared his internal party critics to "Japanese colonial collaborators who sabotaged the Banmin Teukwi."

The reference carries profound historical weight. The Banmin Teukwi (the Special Investigation Committee on Anti-National Activities) was established in 1948 following Korea's liberation from Japanese colonial rule to investigate and prosecute pro-Japanese collaborators, only to be dismantled under conservative pressure. Within South Korea’s liberal camp, which prides itself on carrying the torch of nationalist resistance and historical rectitude, being branded a "collaborator" who undermines justice is the equivalent of a political excommunication. By likening his fellow council members to national traitors, Park effectively eliminated any space for collegial debate, triggering accusations of secondary victimization from party colleagues.

Meanwhile, the debate shifted to the credibility and security of the leaked intelligence. Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee struck back by publicly revealing details about the source behind internal leaks, highlighting that a recording of closed-door party member discussions had been provided by a whistleblower using the online handle "Jochuteollaeyouout." Choi argued that the provocative handle alone signaled malicious political bias, claiming that party leaders were being dragged around by tainted tip-offs designed to smear internal rivals.

The back-and-forth highlighted a broader dysfunction: instead of establishing whether a clandestine organization was manipulating its registration rolls, the Democratic Party’s top leadership spent hours debating offensive historical analogies and the online handles of anonymous informants.

Kim Min-seok’s Leadership Test and the Factional Backlash

The timing of the crisis could hardly be worse for Democratic Party Chairman Kim Min-seok. Approaching his 100th day at the helm of the ruling party, Kim had hoped to use the milestone to consolidate his authority and highlight legislative unity in support of President Lee Jae-myung’s policy platform. Instead, national television networks broadcast footage of his top council shouting and walking out.

Attempting to project authority, Kim intervened by issuing a formal verbal warning to Park Sun-won, rebuking him specifically for the "Japanese collaborator" slur and for jeopardizing internal party unity. Kim reportedly watched the meeting unravel with visible distress, conscious that unscripted internal warfare risked sabotaging the party’s major legislative initiatives, such as its ongoing push to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

Yet Kim's disciplinary action immediately backfired, creating a new round of factional grievance. Rather than accepting the censure, Park took to social media and broadcast interviews to complain that the chairman’s warning was unfairly one-sided. Park demanded that identical standards of discipline be applied to figures like lawmaker Lee Seong-yoon—who had previously sparked internal friction by invoking the "Chun Doo-hwan insurrection," referencing the brutal 1979 military coup—as well as spokesperson Han Min-soo.

Park’s refusal to back down triggered a cascading reaction on the party’s official online boards, where grassroots supporters began submitting dueling disciplinary petitions against various lawmakers, including Lee Seong-yoon, Park Hee-seung, and Moon Jin-seok. What began as a dispute over membership registries has evolved into a wider referendum on whether Kim Min-seok possesses the administrative muscle to discipline rival party barons.

Opposition Attacks and a Weary Public

The turmoil inside the governing party was swiftly seized upon by the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition. Conservative lawmakers took delight in turning the Democratic Party’s historical rhetoric against itself.

PPP lawmaker Jang Dong-hyeok and other conservative figures pointed out that if Park’s figure of 43,000 Shincheonji members is accurate, it points to a catastrophic collapse of internal oversight within the governing party. Jang sarcastically recalled past demonstrations where liberal critics protested outside conservative party offices over alleged cult ties, asking aloud whether citizens should now "march on the Democratic Party headquarters." For the opposition, the episode offers a valuable narrative counterweight, allowing them to frame the Lee Jae-myung administration and its party apparatus as compromised by the very fringe elements they once condemned.

Outside the partisan battleground, civic organizations responded with open disgust. Prominent watchdog group People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy (PSPD) issued statements rebuking the ruling party for indulging in factional turf wars and cult-hunting theater while working-class Koreans grapple with persistent economic stagnation and consumer inflation. The critique reflects a broader sentiment among moderate voters: that the governing party has become detached from kitchen-table realities, absorbed entirely by internal ideological purity tests.

What to Watch Next

As the Democratic Party attempts to move past the embarrassing spectacle, several key developments will indicate how deeply the damage runs:

  • Demands for an Official Registry Audit: With a specific figure of 14,388 verified members now on the public record, party leadership faces mounting pressure from both rivals and its own base to conduct a comprehensive audit of its membership rolls, particularly across the Honam district branches. Whether Kim Min-seok orders such an investigation—or buries it to prevent further friction—will test his authority.
  • Fallout on Legislative Momentum: The factional recriminations have diverted attention from the ruling party’s contentious legislative drives, including the drive to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Observers will be watching whether the internal mistrust spills over into parliamentary voting discipline.
  • Disciplinary Repercussions: The party’s ethics machinery may be forced to act on competing petitions targeting Park Sun-won, Lee Seong-yoon, and other factional surrogates. How the leadership resolves these complaints will reveal whether Chairman Kim can reassert control over the pro-Lee Jae-myung and pro-Jung Cheong-rae factions ahead of the administration's next political challenges.
