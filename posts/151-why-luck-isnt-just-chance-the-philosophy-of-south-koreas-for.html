Why Luck Isn’t Just Chance: The Philosophy of South Korea’s "Fortune Architects"

September 04, 2026

In South Korea, there is a common saying: un-chil-gi-sam. It translates to "seven parts luck, three parts skill," a phrase often used to resign oneself to the whims of fate. If 70% of life is predetermined by fortune, is the remaining 30% just a waiting room for the inevitable? According to Park Sung-jun, one of Korea’s most prominent practitioners of gwan-sang (the traditional art of face reading), this perspective is fundamentally flawed. To Park, luck is not a lottery ticket falling from the sky. It is a sensory engagement—the art of positioning your sails exactly where the wind is blowing.

Most of us spend our days tossing and turning in bed, blaming that elusive 70% for our stagnation. But as Park argues, the real tragedy isn't a lack of luck; it’s the fact that we haven't even prepared our sails to catch it.

Luck as a Wave, Not a Windfall

When life takes a sharp turn—a sudden resignation, the collapse of a long-term relationship, or the upheaval of a divorce—our instinct is to label it "bad luck." However, from the perspective of traditional Korean physiognomy, these crises are often the strongest signals that a new, grand cycle of fortune is beginning. If your social circle is shifting, your financial habits are evolving, or your interests are suddenly gravitating toward unfamiliar fields, the foundation of your life is being rebuilt.

The distinction between those who succumb to these changes and those who thrive lies in how they interpret the "waves" of their lives. You cannot control the wind, but you have total agency over how you trim your sails. Those who master their destiny do not fight the gale; they lean into it. They recognize that what appears to be a chaotic disruption is often the precise moment they should be hoisting their sails.

This is the "formula" for turning chance into destiny: you must endure the periods of "zero luck" with absolute composure. During lean times, people often arm themselves with resentment and self-pity, blaming the world for their circumstances. But building walls against the world is the most effective way to ensure that any incoming luck is turned away at the door. Surprisingly, the most potent "secret weapon" for attracting fortune is the mundane: making your bed, washing your face, and stepping out into the world to engage with people.

💡 The Takeaway

  • Luck is not something you wait for; it is the inevitable product of your attitude.
  • "Bad" changes are often just the teaser trailer for a new cycle of success.
  • The strongest methods for "reprogramming" your luck are maintaining daily routines and practicing selfless giving.

The "Class" of a Face: Why Energy Outweighs Aesthetics

In the West, we often view facial features through the lens of aesthetics or surgical perfection. In the Korean tradition of gwan-sang, the focus is on gi-se, or "spirit/momentum." It isn't about being conventionally beautiful; it’s about the density of one’s energy. Practitioners look at the nose—the jun-du—as a vessel for wealth, and the nostrils as the dam that prevents that wealth from leaking away. Yet, these are not fixed traits decided by genetics; they are reflections of how one manages their internal state.

We often critique our reflections in the mirror, searching for flaws to fix. However, your face is a sculpture you carve daily through your habits and expressions. If you spend your day with a furrowed brow (your "palace of destiny" between the eyebrows), you are effectively blocking the pathways of your own success. The ultimate winners are not those with the most symmetrical features, but those who have cultivated a sense of "class" or dignity in their own expression. It is a radical form of self-management.

The Aesthetic of Giving: Why Selflessness is Strategic

When a person hits rock bottom, Park Sung-jun often gives a counterintuitive prescription: Give more. But there is a catch—you must be able to "forget" the act immediately. The moment you give with the expectation of a return, it becomes a transaction. If the transaction doesn't go your way, resentment follows. Those who struggle with their luck almost always speak the language of "deficiency," feeling as though they are always being shortchanged.

By engaging in small, selfless acts—buying a coffee for someone, sharing a meal—you create a flow of energy. When you become the source of giving, your own energy expands rather than shrinks. In Korea, even visiting trendy hotspots or seeking intellectual stimulation at a bookstore are seen as signals to the universe that you are not "stagnant." Stagnant water rots; a life defined by the flow of generosity is much harder to break.

Consider the success of Korean public figures like Choo Sung-hoon or Kim Dong-hyun, who have amassed massive followings on YouTube. It isn't just their athletic prowess that draws people in; it is their "gi-se"—their magnanimity, their willingness to pick up the tab for juniors, and their relaxed, unbothered attitude. Their massive digital followings are not just metrics of skill; they are the result of the "dignified attitude" that people naturally gravitate toward.

What to Watch Next

The lesson here is clear: stop blaming your circumstances and start auditing your daily habits. If you want to change your fortune, you must change your daily "ritual."

What to watch: Look for how you respond to small, daily setbacks this week. Do you immediately pivot to self-blame, or do you maintain your "sails"? The next time you find yourself feeling unlucky, try an act of small, intentional generosity—not to win favor, but to regain your own sense of agency. The goal is to develop a "dignified atmosphere" that persists even when the wind is dead. Success is not a destination; it is the ability to keep your sails rigged and ready, even in the calmest of seas.
