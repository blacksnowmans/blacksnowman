The Eternal Work-in-Progress: Why the Sagrada Família Matters

September 07, 2026

Standing before the Sagrada Família, the towering, sky-piercing basilica in Barcelona, one is hit by a feeling that is difficult to translate into words. It is an overwhelming sense of witnessing a massive, living organism that has been breathing through hundreds of years of history. While some might dismiss it as a never-ending construction site, it is perhaps the most profound confession humanity has ever offered to a single architect.

The Comfort Found in Perpetual Incompleteness

To ask when the Sagrada Família will be finished is to misunderstand its very nature. Antoni Gaudí, the visionary architect behind the project, intuitively knew that this was a space that required the dimension of eternity. Most architects crave the satisfaction of seeing their final vision realized, but Gaudí accepted early on that he would not live to see the completion of his life’s work. That patience and sacrifice offer a peculiar kind of comfort to us today. In a modern society obsessed with speed and immediate results, the act of laying a single stone with an eye toward a future centuries away is a radical, powerful statement.

What is it like for those who carry on the work, tracing the geometric echoes Gaudí left behind? Despite the destruction of many original design documents during the Spanish Civil War, architects like Jordi Faulí continue to decode Gaudí’s vision. For them, mechanical completion is not the objective. They believe the true meaning of the project lies in the process—understanding the laws of nature that Gaudí observed and internalized. The lack of blueprints is not a deficiency; it is a creative freedom and a legacy passed down to the next generation.

The Bitter Intersection of Art and Bureaucracy

We must also address the pragmatic contradictions this great legacy faces within the city of Barcelona. For 136 years, the basilica proceeded without an official building permit. The resolution in 2018, which saw the project agree to pay about $34 million (466 billion KRW) in municipal fees, was a sobering reminder. Even the most sublime artistic achievements are not immune to the cold realities of capitalist urban planning and administrative oversight. As the basilica has become a massive tourist magnet, questions arise: Can art truly be separated from the reality that houses it? Is the "space dedicated to the soul" forever tainted by the commercialization required to keep it standing?

Architecture as a Conversation with Gravity

Gaudí’s genius was not merely aesthetic; it was rooted in rigorous science, specifically the use of hyperbolic paraboloids and catenary curves. He did not view gravity as an enemy to be conquered, but as a collaborator. By utilizing the inherent strength of hanging, weighted curves, he created interior columns that mimic the way branches in a forest reach upward to support a canopy. To Gaudí, the order he observed in nature as a child was the only manual he needed for architecture.

There is also a poignant dialogue between the different parts of the building. The "Nativity Façade," largely completed by Gaudí, is a collection of fluid, emotional curves. In contrast, the "Passion Façade," finished by sculptor Josep Maria Subirachs decades later, uses sharp, angular lines to tell a story of tragedy. Some critics find this shift jarring, but there is beauty in the dissonance. It proves that the building is not an exercise in forced uniformity, but a living record where the interpretations of different eras are layered upon one another.

The Analytics of Devotion

Today, the Sagrada Família welcomes millions of visitors annually. Since being designated a Spanish national monument in 1969, it has become a vital pillar supporting Barcelona’s economy. Yet, statistics fail to capture the silence inside the cathedral. They cannot quantify the solitude Gaudí felt during his final 12 years spent entirely at the construction site, nor the intensity with which modern researchers use computer simulations to decipher his geometric puzzles. What we consume as tourists is not merely a building; it is a record of one man’s lifelong hunger for perfection.

What to Watch Next

The Sagrada Família has transcended the work of an individual architect to become a document of collective human intelligence. It represents a desperate, beautiful attempt to translate the infinite realm of the divine into the language of stone and glass.

We are often trapped in an obsession with "completion," focusing only on the final product. But the Sagrada Família suggests otherwise. It reminds us that the process itself is beautiful, and that being a work-in-progress is the most honest evidence that we are still growing. I now understand why Gaudí chose to be buried in the crypt beneath the altar. He did not need to see the end. He only needed to know that his space provided refuge and prompted questions for those who followed.

As long as the cathedral's bells ring through the alleys of Barcelona, Gaudí’s architecture will never be "dead." In fact, when the final piece is eventually placed and the work is deemed "complete," we may find ourselves struggling with a profound sense of loss. Until then, we continue to walk through the fragments of time that one man dedicated his entire life to building.
