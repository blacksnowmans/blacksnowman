Beyond the Classroom: Why South Korea is Betting on Lifelong Learning to Save its Future

November 02, 2026

Imagine a bustling city square in Suwon, a major tech and industrial hub just south of Seoul. Instead of the usual commuters or shoppers, the space is filled with thousands of citizens—from toddlers to nonagenarians—crowding around 35 different "experience booths." In one corner, a retiree is getting a first-hand lesson in generative AI; in another, a group of housewives is demonstrating how their neighborhood study circle turned into a local social enterprise. This isn't a one-off street fair; it is the 20th anniversary of Suwon’s Lifelong Learning Festival, a cornerstone of a national movement that has taken on a new, urgent meaning in 2024.

To an outside observer, "lifelong learning" might sound like a pleasant, if somewhat peripheral, social hobby. But in South Korea, it has become a frontline strategy for national survival. As the country grapples with the world’s lowest birth rate and a rapidly aging population, the question of how to keep citizens engaged, employable, and socially connected is no longer academic—it is existential. On November 1, festivals and exhibitions erupted across the peninsula, from the high-tech corridors of Gyeonggi Province to the rural heartlands of Gyeongsangbuk-do, signaling a shift in how the Korean state views the "right to learn."

From Hobby Groups to Economic Resilience: The Evolution of the Learning City

South Korea’s "Lifelong Learning City" initiative is a government-designated program where local municipalities receive support to build educational infrastructure for adults. While the program has existed for decades, the recent festivals in Suwon and Hwaseong show a marked shift in sophistication. Suwon, a "Special Municipality" (a status given to cities with over one million residents, providing them with more administrative autonomy), has now spent two decades building what Mayor Lee Jae-jun calls a "platform for survival."

The Suwon festival, themed "Twenty Years of Learning, Meeting the Future Together," illustrates how these programs have moved beyond simple flower arranging or calligraphy classes. Today, they are deeply integrated with the local economy. This year’s event was linked with the "Owl Street Night Market," a project designed to revitalize alleyway businesses. By connecting education with local commerce, the city is attempting to create a "virtuous cycle" where learning leads to community engagement, which in turn fuels the local economy.

In nearby Hwaseong—home to major facilities for Samsung Electronics and one of the fastest-growing cities in the country—the focus has shifted almost entirely to the "AI Era." Hwaseong Mayor Jeong Myeong-geun has emphasized that in a period of rapid digital transformation, the "technology gap" quickly becomes a "quality of life gap." For a city that prides itself on being a global manufacturing leader, ensuring that its older workforce and stay-at-home parents are not left behind by artificial intelligence is a matter of maintaining the city’s competitive edge. The festival there served as a proof of concept for a new model of "vocational-humanities fusion," where technical skills are taught alongside the critical thinking needed to navigate a digital society.

Healing the Scars of History through Literacy

While the tech-heavy cities of Gyeonggi Province look toward the future, the festivals in Gyeongsangbuk-do (the southeastern province often considered the heart of traditional Korean culture) highlight the profound social welfare aspect of lifelong education. One of the most moving segments of the 12th Gyeongsangbuk-do Lifelong Learning Fair, held in Yecheon, was an exhibition of "Poetry and Drawings" by learners in their 90s.

For many in this generation, formal education was a luxury stolen by the poverty following the Korean War or by the patriarchal social structures of the mid-20th century. To these "belated students," literacy is not just about reading a bus schedule; it is about reclaiming a lost identity. The current administration under President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea who took office following the impeachment of the previous leader—has framed this as a "universal basic right."

"Learning to read at ninety is not about preparing for a job; it is about the dignity of being a human being who can finally tell their own story to the world."

This perspective marks a sharp departure from the previous era. During the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi (the former president who was subsequently impeached and is currently imprisoned), these types of "humanistic" and "welfare-oriented" education budgets were frequently targeted for cuts under the guise of "fiscal efficiency." The current government’s pivot back toward funding literacy and disability-inclusive education—such as the Saeumteo Lifelong Education Festival in Jeju, which focuses specifically on learners with disabilities—is a deliberate political statement. It argues that education is a form of "preventative welfare" that reduces social isolation and long-term healthcare costs by keeping the elderly and marginalized active and mentally sharp.

Educational Diplomacy and the Fight Against "Regional Extinction"

Outside the major metropolises, South Korea faces a "regional extinction" crisis—a terrifying demographic reality where rural counties are seeing their populations vanish as young people move to Seoul. In this context, lifelong learning has become a tool for "local regeneration."

Bonghwa-gun, a mountainous county in the northeast, recently declared a new vision: "Focusing on the change in people, rather than the number of courses." This is a critique of the old "performance-based" administration, where officials would simply brag about how many classes they opened. Instead, Bonghwa is trying to use education to turn residents into "local protagonists" who can solve community problems themselves. When a resident learns how to run a local cooperative or preserve local heritage through a digital archive project, they develop a "sense of place" that makes them less likely to leave for the city.

This "software-centric" approach to saving rural Korea is increasingly seen as more effective than the old "hardware-centric" approach of building expensive, empty museums or stadiums. The 12th Gyeongsangbuk-do Fair saw 22 different municipalities competing not just to show off their skills, but to share "best practices" on how to keep a dying town alive through communal learning. This "educational diplomacy" between local governments is creating a network of resilience that the central government is now looking to formalize into a national "Learning Account" system, similar to Singapore’s "SkillsFuture" program, where every citizen is given a credit to spend on their own development.

The Political Battleground: Populism vs. Human Capital

Despite the festive atmosphere on November 1, a fierce political battle is simmering beneath the surface regarding the funding of these programs. The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, has stepped up its criticism of the Lee Jae-myung government’s education spending. PPP lawmakers in various provincial assemblies have labeled the expansion of lifelong learning support as "populist budget-wasting" and "giveaway projects" intended to buy votes from the elderly and rural populations.

The Democratic Party, meanwhile, argues that these critics are stuck in a 20th-century mindset where education ends at age 22. The Lee administration’s philosophy of "Basic Rights" (including the controversial but popular discussions around Basic Income) extends to "Basic Education." From their view, in an era where the average lifespan is approaching 100, a one-time injection of education in one's youth is insufficient.

The shadow of the previous administration looms large here. Many educators at these festivals spoke of the "dark years" under Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi, when funding for community libraries and adult literacy centers was slashed in favor of large-scale infrastructure projects. The current revival of these festivals is seen by many in the progressive camp as a "restoration of common sense." However, the challenge for the Lee government will be to prove that these programs are producing measurable outcomes—such as re-employment rates for middle-aged workers or a decrease in depression among the elderly—to satisfy the fiscal hawks in the opposition.

What to Watch Next

The flurry of activity on November 1 is just the beginning of a larger shift in the Korean social contract. As we look toward 2025 and beyond, there are three key developments to monitor:

  • The "Learning Account" Legislation: Watch for moves in the National Assembly to create a permanent, legally mandated "National Learning Credit" for all citizens. This would move lifelong learning from a discretionary local budget item to a guaranteed national entitlement.
  • AI Integration: The success of Hwaseong’s AI-focused learning model will be a litmus test. If the city can successfully retrain its manufacturing workforce for the automation age, it will provide a blueprint for the rest of the "Rust Belt" in Korea.
  • The 2027 Fair Bidding: Several cities, including Gyeongsan, are already aggressively bidding to host the 2027 regional fairs. The competition for these events is becoming as intense as the bid for major sporting events, as they are seen as key to branding a city as "livable" and "forward-thinking."

Ultimately, these festivals reveal a South Korea that is trying to learn its way out of a demographic corner. Whether it is a 90-year-old grandmother finally writing her own name or a laid-off technician learning to prompt an AI, the "culture of learning" is being weaponized to fight off the stagnation that threatens the nation. In the words of one educator in Suwon: "We used to learn to get into a good company; now we learn so that we don't disappear."
