Civil War in South Korea's Main Opposition: How Internal Fractures and Alleged Fabrications Threaten the Conservative Revival

November 12, 2026

Imagine a major political party facing a high-stakes national election, where instead of building a unified campaign strategy, its top leaders are publicly trading personal insults and accusing each other of fabricating internal disciplinary records. That is precisely the scene unfolding inside the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition party. On November 11, long-simmering factional feuds erupted into open warfare, exposing deep structural vulnerabilities at the very moment the party needs to present a credible alternative to the ruling government.

With general elections fast approaching, South Korea's conservative camp finds itself entangled in a multi-front power struggle. The clashes involve high-profile figures including Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, PPP Secretary-General Jang Dong-hyeok, and former minister Han Dong-hoon. What began as a strategic disagreement over how to win over voters in the crucial Seoul metropolitan region has descended into accusations of political purges, rigged candidate nominations, and undue influence from far-right commentators. For international observers trying to understand South Korea's political landscape, the implosion of the PPP offers a vivid case study in how deep-seated factionalism can paralyze an opposition party.

1. Mayor Oh vs. Secretary-General Jang: The Open Battle for Party Control

The latest flare-up erupted when Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon dropped a political bombshell on November 11. Addressing the PPP’s precarious standing, Oh openly declared that the party cannot win the upcoming legislative elections under the current leadership of Secretary-General Jang Dong-hyeok. Pointing to abysmal approval ratings of less than 10 percent in the greater Seoul capital area, Mayor Oh argued that the central party leadership lacks both strategic foresight and electoral competitiveness. His proposed remedy was drastic: dissolve the existing leadership structure and transition immediately to an Emergency Response Committee.

For Mayor Oh, whose political fate relies heavily on the sentiment of moderate voters in the capital, the party’s current trajectory represents an existential threat. Capital region races in South Korea are notoriously tight, and a weak central party brand can easily sink local candidates. By pushing for an emergency committee, Oh sought to rally anxious lawmakers from the capital region and reassert his own status as a primary leader of the conservative movement.

However, the response from party headquarters was swift and bitterly personal. Secretary-General Jang Dong-hyeok fired back, questioning Oh’s motives and accusing him of destabilizing the party for personal political gain. In an outburst that underscored the hostility within party ranks, Jang mockingly suggested: "If you are so deeply concerned about party affairs, why don’t we just swap jobs? You take the party leadership, and I will take over as Mayor of Seoul."

This exchange highlighted a troubling shift in Korean political discourse. Rather than engaging in substantive debates over election strategy or policy platforms, key party leaders resorted to sarcastic retaliations. Political analysts quickly noted that an emerging alliance between Mayor Oh and Han Dong-hoon—another heavy-hitting conservative figure—is effectively isolating Secretary-General Jang. The pressure on Jang intensified further when Cho Gap-je, a veteran conservative commentator, publicly asserted that while Han Dong-hoon is indispensable for rebuilding the conservative movement, Jang is someone who must be removed from leadership.

2. Fabricated Disciplines and Rigged Primaries: A Leadership Crisis Deepens

As if the public feud between top leaders were not damaging enough, the PPP is now reeling from explosive allegations of administrative fraud within its own ranks. Claims have surfaced that past disciplinary measures taken against Han Dong-hoon were intentionally fabricated as part of a coordinated political purge by current party management. Han himself condemned the disciplinary actions, characterizing them as an illegal effort to eliminate political rivals under the guise of internal party justice.

The allegations target the party secretariat under Secretary-General Jang, suggesting that officials altered documents and bypassed internal procedures to enforce penalties against Han. If proven true, these allegations would represent not just a breach of party ethics, but potentially serious legal violations for a major public organization. In response, Jang Dong-hyeok denied all wrongdoing, taking a confrontational stance: "There was no fabrication. If anyone feels wronged, take legal action."

Rather than calming the waters, Jang’s push for a legal showdown signals that the party’s internal disputes are escalating toward court battles. Relying on the judiciary to resolve internal political disputes reflects a total breakdown of internal party governance. Furthermore, distrust in the party leadership is spreading beyond central headquarters into local chapters. In the Yongin-A district, renewed allegations of primary irregularities have fueled suspicion that leadership manipulated candidate selection procedures to favor specific allies. This combination of national scandals and local nomination disputes has severely eroded trust among rank-and-file party members.

3. Attacking Government Real Estate Policy While Divided Within

While consumed by internal strife, the PPP has attempted to maintain an external offensive against President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. On November 11, senior PPP figures, including lawmaker Jeong Jeom-sik, launched sharp attacks against the government’s housing policies. The opposition claimed that President Lee’s administration and the ruling party were exhibiting policy discord, labeling their recent housing measures as a fraudulent "pre-election performance" designed to trick voters ahead of the polls.

Attacking the ruling party on real estate is a standard tactic in South Korean politics, as housing affordability remains a paramount concern for voters. To amplify this narrative, the PPP has even sought tactical alignment with Lee Jun-seok's splinter Reform Party, echoing joint criticisms against the government's economic track record. However, this cooperation remains strictly limited to policy critiques. The PPP leadership remains unwilling or unable to forge a broader political alliance with Lee Jun-seok's party, leaving the broader conservative camp fractured.

This dynamic creates a major paradox for the opposition. While housing market discontent presents a prime political opportunity, the PPP's internal chaos prevents it from capitalizing on government missteps. Media coverage of the party’s fierce internal feuds routinely drowns out its economic policy messaging. When voters view an opposition party as incapable of managing its own affairs, attacks on the ruling party ring hollow.

4. Far-Right Influence and the Erosion of Democratic Norms

The ongoing breakdown within the PPP also highlights the growing influence of hardline conservative commentators and fringe groups over party decision-making. The prominent role played by commentators like Cho Gap-je in dictating who should lead the party reflects a broader trend where external ideological voices hold outsized sway over official party policy.

This reliance on radical voices has been accompanied by an increase in street-level mobilization and aggressive rhetoric. Since the period of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency, internal factional conflict within the conservative movement has steadily intensified, shifting the party's focus away from moderate parliamentary debate and toward divisive culture-war tactics. By aligning with controversial online commentators and far-right media channels, party leaders risk alienating the crucial moderate voters of the metropolitan areas.

The fundamental crisis facing South Korea’s main opposition is not merely a disagreement over strategy, but a breakdown of procedural democracy within the party apparatus itself. When nomination processes and disciplinary measures are suspected of being weaponized, the integrity of the political party as a democratic institution is severely compromised.

What to Watch Next in South Korean Conservative Politics

As the general election approaches, the People Power Party stands at a critical juncture. To assess whether South Korea's main opposition party can recover or whether it will sink deeper into crisis, keep an eye on the following developments:

  • Formation of an Emergency Response Committee: Watch whether Mayor Oh Se-hoon and his allies gather enough support among capital-region lawmakers to force Secretary-General Jang Dong-hyeok out and establish an emergency committee.
  • Legal Escalation over Disciplinary Records: Pay attention to whether Han Dong-hoon carries out legal challenges regarding the alleged fabrication of his disciplinary files, which could bring internal party records into open court.
  • Opposition Realignment and Strategy: Track whether the PPP can move past tactical real estate critiques and form a functioning political alliance with Lee Jun-seok’s Reform Party, or if conservative votes will remain divided at the ballot box against President Lee Jae-myung’s ruling Democratic Party.
