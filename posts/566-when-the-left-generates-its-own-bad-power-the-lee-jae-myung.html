When the Left Generates Its Own Bad Power: The Lee Jae-myung Crisis

October 02, 2026

For decades, progressive politics in South Korea had a clear compass. The narrative was simple, shaped by decades of fighting military dictatorships: there was a clear line between the forces of democracy and the forces of authoritarianism. But what happens when that moral clarity dissolves from within? When a movement that rode to power on the promise of reform starts exhibiting the exact same symptoms of imperial overreach it once vowed to dismantle?

This is the agonizing question facing South Korea's progressive camp under President Lee Jae-myung. Observers and critics are increasingly forced to confront an uncomfortable truth: the uncritical loyalty that once sustained the left is beginning to curdle into something resembling the impunity they spent years fighting.

The Leak and the Double Standard

The controversy began with what should have been a fireable offense for any ordinary bureaucrat. A confidential state document was inadvertently posted on the social media account of President Lee Jae-myung. Under South Korea's strict laws regarding official secrets, a civil servant who pulled such a stunt would face immediate suspension, public disgrace, and likely criminal prosecution. Yet, because the leak originated from the nation's highest office, the response from the administration's inner circle has been a mixture of shoulder-shrugs and frantic damage control.

The hypocrisy has not gone unnoticed. Critics have pointed out the glaring double standard. When figures from previous administrations—such as former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae—faced intense legal scrutiny simply for reposting a news article related to a controversial media scandal, the progressive camp demanded strict accountability. Now, faced with a breach of state confidentiality by their own sitting president, the same voices preach patience and forgiveness.

This "rules for thee, but not for me" attitude corrodes the moral authority of the administration. When a leader's digital output resembles impulsive emotional venting rather than measured statecraft, it invites public cynicism. Presidential words and social media posts are historical records; when used carelessly, they return as boomerangs, dragging down the dignity of the office. An unrespected presidency quickly decays into a brittle, defensive regime.

Syphoning Power and the Cult of Loyalty

As the president’s personal authority becomes decoupled from institutional norms, a predictable ecosystem of sycophants and opportunists fills the vacuum. Incidents like a prominent political figure abruptly walking out of a live broadcast simply because they were summoned for dinner with the president illustrate a troubling shift in priorities. The guiding star of governance is no longer the public interest, but what critics are calling "Lee Jae-myung-firstism."

Lawmakers and loyalists within the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's primary liberal party—often project an air of entitlement born from the belief that they alone made Lee president. This creates an internal dynamic where dissent is treated as treason, and anyone questioning the leadership is sidelined as an internal saboteur. Under the shadow of a monolithic, one-man leadership structure, genuine debate suffocates.

This insular culture bleeds directly into personnel decisions. Rather than relying on a transparent, merit-based civil service vetting system, executive appointments appear increasingly driven by personal taste and unswerving loyalty. When key posts are handed out based on who is least likely to challenge the leader's ego, governance suffers. Blaming staff incompetence is a coward’s defense; the ultimate responsibility rests squarely on the desk of the president.

Learning the Wrong Lessons from the Fall of Conservatism

The irony is heavy. South Korea witnessed firsthand how the conservative government of former President Yoon Suk Yeol imploded in less than three years, crushed under the weight of its own prosecutorial overreach and refusal to listen to the public. Power, as the saying goes, is like sand in a fist: the harder you squeeze, the faster it slips away.

Yet, the current progressive leadership seems to be reading the wrong manual. Instead of learning humility from their predecessors' downfall, the ruling camp has weaponized its mandate. Dissenting voices within the left are muted, and the democratic achievements won through decades of street protests and citizen sacrifice are being slowly eroded by a new kind of insular, clubby power.

The Democratic Party is not the personal property of any single individual. It was built by the sweat and sacrifice of millions of ordinary citizens who risked their livelihoods to defend constitutional order. Treating that collective achievement as a personal fiefdom risks triggering a public backlash just as severe as the one that felled the previous administration.

The Dilemma of the Conscientious Supporter

For voters and commentators who lean progressive, this dynamic creates a profound psychological trap. The fear of handing power back to the conservative opposition—the People Power Party—creates a powerful compulsion to look the other way. Even when broadcasting sharp critiques, analysts often feel compelled to tack on a hollow endorsement, whispering, "Nevertheless, we hope President Lee succeeds."

This reflexive cheerleading must end. If the progressive movement is to mature, it must find the courage to confront its own bad power. Unconditional support is a slow poison. When a ruling party becomes arrogant and deaf to criticism, it needs a sharp reminder of the cost of losing public trust.

True political strength does not come from enforcing blind obedience; it comes from the civic courage to look a leader in the eye and say, "You are wrong."

What to Watch Next

  • Personnel Shakeups: Watch whether upcoming cabinet and secretarial appointments break away from the "loyalty-first" mold or double down on inner-circle favorites.
  • Internal Party Dissent: Monitor whether sidelined reformist factions within the Democratic Party begin to form cohesive coalitions to challenge the party's current leadership structure.
  • Public Opinion Metrics: Keep a close eye on upcoming approval ratings in core progressive strongholds, which will serve as the ultimate barometer of whether voters are losing patience with the administration's current trajectory.
