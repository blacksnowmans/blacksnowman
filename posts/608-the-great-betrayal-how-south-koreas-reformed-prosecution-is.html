The Great Betrayal: How South Korea’s "Reformed" Prosecution is Becoming a Monster

September 10, 2026

On the morning of September 10, 2026, the atmosphere at the National Assembly’s communication building in Seoul was heavy with exhaustion and betrayal. For months, members of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country’s primary liberal opposition party—had held onto a sliver of hope. When news broke that prosecutors were eagerly applying for positions in the newly formed "Major Crimes Investigation Agency" (MCIA), many supporters felt a rare sense of relief, thinking that maybe, just maybe, the long-promised structural reforms were finally taking root.

That hope lasted less than a week. The facade crumbled when 617 applicants suddenly withdrew their names, followed by the release of the Ministry of Justice’s new organizational restructuring plan for the Corruption Investigation Office (CIO). When the details surfaced, the realization was chilling: instead of a lean, focused agency, the government had unveiled a bloated, mutated version of the old prosecution service. To the observers on the ground, this was not a step toward justice—it was the birth of a new, more powerful legal behemoth.

The Anatomy of a "Mutant" Prosecution

The core of the frustration lies in the Ministry of Justice’s new organizational blueprint and the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the MCIA. Kim, a career prosecutor with a resume spanning the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office and the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, is a "prosecution purist." Notably, in 2022, he was a vocal opponent of the very reforms that sought to limit the prosecution's investigative powers. By installing a man who once led the charge against reform to lead the new agency, the administration has effectively signaled that the "fox will be guarding the henhouse."

The structural plan itself is equally alarming. Under the guise of separating investigation from prosecution, the Ministry has created 29 "Major Crimes Divisions" spread across 18 regional offices. Most concerning is the creation of a "Judicial Control Division" within these offices—a top-heavy bureaucratic layer that grants the agency quasi-judicial authority. By ensuring these offices are permanently linked with the Corruption Investigation Office, the government has essentially hardwired a system where truly independent investigations become impossible. As one party member at the press conference remarked, "They didn’t dismantle the prosecution; they just gave it a new sign and a bigger budget."

Furthermore, the Ministry’s updated "Investigation Rules" now allow prosecutors to summon suspects directly from police detention for questioning at their own discretion. This bypasses the spirit of the ongoing separation of powers, effectively inviting the prosecution back into the heart of active police investigations. For a government that rose to power on a mandate of reform, this retreat into old habits feels like a slap in the face to the voters who expected a fundamental shift in how the law is applied.

History Repeating: The Failure of Half-Measures

South Korea’s modern history is a grim ledger of judicial institutions being weaponized for political ends—from the fabricated spy cases of the Rhee Syngman era to the suppression of pro-democracy figures under the Chun Doo-hwan dictatorship. The pattern is always the same: when enforcement agencies are not checked, they consume the democracy they are meant to protect.

During the Moon Jae-in administration, a lack of resolve and constant hesitation led to a failure in prosecutorial reform, which eventually cost the liberal bloc dearly in subsequent elections. Now, the current administration seems to be repeating those same mistakes. By filling key advisory roles with former prosecutors and pushing through these restructuring plans while the President was on a state visit to France, the government has adopted a "back-room" style of governance that stands in direct opposition to the transparency citizens were promised.

The public anger is further fueled by the prosecution’s track record. Past cases—such as the "Yoo Woo-seong spy fabrication case," where evidence was forged to frame an innocent civilian, and the "Kim Hak-ui case," involving allegations of sexual bribery and cover-ups—remain unatoned. When the same institution that presided over these scandals is granted more power under a new name, it is inevitable that the public will revolt.

A System of Selective Justice

The fundamental problem remains the concentration of both investigative and prosecutorial power in one hand. This allows for "selective justice," where the prosecution can aggressively target political opponents while looking the other way when it comes to their own ranks. Currently, there are roughly 170,000 "cold cases"—unresolved files abandoned by the prosecution and dumped onto the police. Yet, in a move described by critics as intentional sabotage, the government has only increased the police’s investigative budget by about $225,000 (300 million KRW), an insultingly small amount given the massive influx of work.

This is not an administrative oversight; it is a calculated attempt to ensure the police struggle under the weight of the new caseload, thereby proving the "need" for the prosecution’s continued intervention. If the goal were truly to improve the justice system, the focus would be on robust oversight and a clean separation of roles, not on creating "Judicial Control" bodies that preserve the status quo.

What to Watch Next

  • Legislative Pushback: Watch for whether DPK lawmakers, including figures like Park Joo-min, can successfully organize a floor fight against the MCIA appointment and the new organizational rules.
  • Public Protests: As the reality of the "mutant prosecution" sets in, expect increased activity from civic groups and party members who feel betrayed by the current administration’s shift.
  • Institutional Gridlock: With the National Assembly deeply divided, the government’s attempt to bypass legislative debate through "enforcement decrees" is likely to spark a constitutional crisis if the judiciary eventually intervenes.

The lesson of history is clear: reform that is compromised at the outset is not reform at all; it is a surrender. For the DPK and the current administration, the clock is ticking. If they continue to ignore the grassroots movement that brought them to power in favor of cozying up to the established legal elite, they risk losing not just their political legitimacy, but the very democracy they claim to defend.
