The Politicization of South Korea’s Judiciary: A Dangerous New Normal

September 29, 2026

The atmosphere between the halls of the South Korean judiciary and the presidential office in Yongsan has grown dangerously thin. While whispers of tension have been circulating for some time, few expected the situation to spiral into the overt, aggressive political standoff we are witnessing today. At the center of this storm is Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, whose recent actions have raised a fundamental question: When did the highest office in the judiciary become a venue for playing political chess?

From the ongoing deadlock over judicial appointments to a bizarre scandal involving inflated restaurant receipts in Seoul's posh Bangbae-dong neighborhood, the evidence suggests that the country’s top judge is less concerned with legal precedent and more engaged in political maneuvering. To understand why this matters, we must look past the headlines and examine the unsettling landscape of a judiciary that appears to be losing its way.

The Chief Justice’s Political Pivot

The current impasse began when the presidential office requested that Chief Justice Cho resubmit a nomination for a judicial vacancy after the National Assembly failed to ratify the initial candidate. Cho refused, labeling the request "unclear." To the average observer, this justification holds little water; the president's intent to reject the original candidate was explicit. By refusing to engage with the executive branch, Cho has effectively signaled an open confrontation, one that many analysts believe he is using to cast himself as a defiant, independent figure—a political move disguised as institutional integrity.

This is not a fleeting dispute. It is widely expected to drag on until Cho’s term ends in June of next year, creating a persistent layer of paralysis within the judicial system. Supporters in conservative circles, such as the People Power Party (the main conservative party in South Korea), have encouraged this friction, framing it as a necessary defense against what they perceive as the current administration’s "constitutional destruction."

For observers of Korean politics, this trajectory feels hauntingly familiar. It mirrors the transformation of Choe Jae-hyeong, the former head of the Board of Audit and Inspection (the government agency that oversees state accounts). Choe famously transitioned from a neutral technocrat to an outspoken presidential candidate, driven by his disillusionment with the previous liberal government. The optics of a high-ranking, conservative-leaning official stepping out of the courtroom and onto the political stage are nearly identical.

The "Bangbae-dong" Receipt Scandal

While the constitutional crisis unfolds, the judiciary’s reputation has been further damaged by a more mundane but equally damning controversy: the "Bangbae-dong restaurant" scandal. Ahead of the annual parliamentary audit, records of the Supreme Court's dining expenses surfaced, revealing a pattern of creative accounting that would make any ethics officer wince.

Documentation from April to December 2025 shows that for several official "consultation dinners," the court booked and paid for significantly more people than actually attended. In one instance, a reservation for 38 people was processed, even though only nine individuals were present. In another, the paperwork claimed 38 attendees for a group of 32.

The math behind these entries is highly suspect. The restaurant in question has a maximum capacity of 33 people, making the booking of 38 physically impossible. Observers suggest this was a "smart trick" to keep costs per person under the 150,000 KRW (about $110) limit imposed by the Anti-Graft Law—also known as the Kim Young-ran Act—or to burn through the department's budget without triggering an audit. While the National Court Administration has dismissed these instances as "clerical errors," the frequency and precision of the inflation suggest a systemic effort to bypass budgetary oversight.

For a public already frustrated by economic instability, seeing the nation’s top judicial officials engaging in such petty, taxpayer-funded corruption is a massive blow to public trust. It raises a simple but cutting question: If the leadership cannot manage a lunch budget honestly, how can they be trusted to interpret the law fairly?

The Illusion of Absolute Authority

The root of this crisis lies in a fundamental disagreement over the power of the Supreme Court Chief Justice. Supporters of the Chief Justice argue that his power to "recommend" (or nominate) candidates is an absolute, check-and-balance tool for judicial independence. However, this interpretation ignores the reality of South Korea's constitutional system. If a Chief Justice can effectively block all appointments by refusing to make recommendations, the entire judicial system becomes a hostage to a single person’s political agenda.

The judiciary was designed to be independent, but it was never intended to be an unaccountable autocracy. When the Chief Justice uses his authority to pick fights with the executive branch, he is not protecting the judiciary—he is eroding it. As history has shown, when the courts start acting as political actors, they inevitably lose the moral high ground required to pass judgment on others.

What to Watch Next

As we head into the next few months, the focus will remain on the National Assembly's audit of the judiciary. Here is what to watch for:

  • Budgetary Scrutiny: Expect intense pressure on the National Court Administration to explain the discrepancies in their dining expenses. Any attempt to pin this on "low-level staff" will likely face backlash.
  • The Appointment Deadlock: Watch for whether the President and the Chief Justice find a face-saving compromise or if the judicial vacancy remains unfilled, further crippling the court’s capacity.
  • Public Perception: The biggest risk for Cho Hee-dae is the erosion of public support. If the judiciary continues to be viewed as a tool for political posturing, it will lose its most important asset: the legitimacy granted by the people.

True judicial independence is not about maintaining the power to defy the state for political clout; it is about standing on a foundation of unimpeachable morality and common sense. When judges trade their robes for political armor, they are not serving the law—they are merely serving their own ambitions at the expense of the democratic system.
