Facing 67% Disapproval, South Korea’s Ruling Party Leader Turns to Youth Critics

November 22, 2026

On November 21, the exact date marking 100 days since he took the helm of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea, party leader Kim Min-seok stepped into a room full of teenagers, twenty-somethings, and thirty-somethings to hear what his party was getting wrong. The occasion was the “1030 Youth Speech Contest,” a party-hosted event designed to put speakers from their teens through their thirties on stage, and it doubled as a stark gut check for a leadership struggling to connect with younger voters.

According to a Korean political briefing from Blacksnowman, the outreach was driven less by celebration than by urgency. Even as the Democratic Party marked its 71st founding anniversary, internal friction and fierce gridlock with the conservative opposition had slowed the ruling camp’s governing momentum. Worse still, polling data from South Korean outlet Kukinews cited in the briefing showed negative evaluations of Kim’s leadership climbing to 67.4 percent, fueled largely by disillusionment among political moderates and young adults.

Standing before young speakers who offered blunt, sometimes self-deprecating assessments of both their daily struggles and the party’s distance from them, Kim offered an unusually candid admission. The party, he warned, risked becoming out of touch unless it learned directly from a generation drifting rapidly away from establishment politics.

A 100-Day Milestone Shadowed by Polling Alarms

In South Korean politics, the 100-day mark for a party leader serves as an unforgiving report card. Since assuming control of the Democratic Party—which holds ruling status alongside President Lee Jae-myung’s administration—Kim has pushed to advance livelihood bills and broader national policy agendas. Yet the Blacksnowman briefing notes that persistent internal discord and relentless confrontation with the opposition in the National Assembly have kept those efforts from producing clear, visible legislative wins.

That stagnation is now reflected in public opinion surveys. According to the Kukinews poll reported in the source material, voter fatigue extends across South Korea’s deeply polarized political divide, with disapproval exceeding 60 percent for the leaders of both major parties:

  • Kim Min-seok, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, recorded a 67.4 percent negative evaluation.
  • Jang Dong-hyuk, leader of the conservative main opposition People Power Party, recorded a 61.5 percent negative evaluation.

While both party chiefs face steep public skepticism, high disapproval carries sharper consequences for a ruling party leader. As a core pillar of day-to-day governance, Kim is expected not only to unify competing factions inside his own party, but also to coordinate policy smoothly with the Lee administration. The briefing observes that a 67.4 percent negative rating goes beyond personal likes or dislikes; it signals broader public alienation from the ruling party’s political trajectory, with moderates and the “2030 generation”—South Korea’s shorthand for voters in their twenties and thirties—driving much of the decline.

Confronting an Aging Political Culture

For international observers, the Democratic Party’s youth dilemma is rooted in both history and party culture. Tracing its roots back 71 years, the party carries the organizational habits of a long-established institution. In recent years, critics inside and outside the party have argued that its messaging remains anchored in rigid hierarchies and the outdated political grammar of South Korea’s former student-activist generation—a culture that often alienates younger citizens who came of age in a vastly different economic environment.

By anchoring the party’s 71st anniversary programming around the 1030 Youth Speech Contest, Kim’s leadership sought to signal a break from routine politics. It is rare for South Korea’s ruling party to stage an open speaking competition spanning citizens from their teens to their thirties specifically to solicit criticism. Throughout the event, Kim framed the effort as a survival issue for the party’s future.

“One of the Democratic Party’s biggest worries is the desperation over how to walk together with young people. We must acknowledge that the party may have grown stale on its own, and learn the Democratic Party’s tomorrow from youth.”

Kim added that what the Democratic Party must guard against most is “becoming stale itself,” urging party veterans to listen to young citizens in order to rediscover the party’s contemporary mission. The Blacksnowman analysis interprets those remarks not merely as polite encouragement for the contest participants, but as a pointed warning aimed at entrenched insiders: without reforming its rigid internal culture, the party cannot win back younger voters.

From Stage to Spokesperson: Testing Real Power-Sharing

To prevent the November 21 event from looking like another photo opportunity, the Democratic Party paired its rhetoric with an immediate personnel decision. Party leaders announced that all four winners of the speech contest would be appointed as official deputy spokespersons for the Democratic Party.

In the tightly controlled world of Korean party communications, that move carries real symbolic weight. For decades, South Korean political parties have faced criticism for treating young recruits as “folding screens”—decorative backdrops rolled out for campaign posters and televised events, only to be sidelined when actual decisions are made. By naming the four contest winners as deputy spokespersons, the leadership is handing them an official platform to issue commentary and represent the party’s stance on major national issues to the press.

Whether that gesture changes the party’s voice remains an open question. As the Korean commentary notes, appointing young spokespersons is an encouraging first step toward putting younger perspectives inside the party’s formal communications apparatus. At the same time, the reform will quickly lose credibility if the new deputy spokespersons are pressured behind the scenes to simply echo the leadership’s pre-approved talking points rather than articulate how their generation actually views events.

Why Jobs, Housing, and Fairness Outweigh Symbolic Titles

Even if the four new spokespersons speak freely, communications reform alone cannot resolve the structural reasons young South Koreans have drifted away from the Democratic Party. Historically, voters in their twenties and thirties formed a dependable pillar of support for the party. Over time, however, economic pressures and political disillusionment have pushed many in that demographic into apathy or outright skepticism.

The Blacksnowman briefing identifies three core pressures behind this shift:

  • Employment scarcity: Intense competition and tight job prospects have left many young adults deeply anxious about their economic future.
  • Housing insecurity: Rising living costs and difficulty securing stable housing have made long-term financial planning feel out of reach for many young households.
  • Debates over fairness: Perceived gaps in opportunity and fairness have eroded trust in political institutions that promise reform without delivering tangible results.

Because the Democratic Party currently holds governing power, young voters are judging it on outcomes rather than slogans. In an editorial reflection accompanying the briefing, a writer in their thirties argues that while Kim’s willingness to admit the party’s staleness and promote four young members deserves credit, young citizens are not asking parties for flashy microphones or official titles. They want credible, practical solutions on jobs, housing, education, and labor—issues tied directly to everyday survival and social mobility.

What to Watch After Day 100

With his 100-day milestone now behind him, Kim Min-seok’s pivot toward younger voters faces three immediate tests in the weeks ahead:

  • The voice of the new spokespersons: Watch the official press briefings issued by the four newly appointed youth deputy spokespersons. If their commentary reflects genuine generational critique and fresh language rather than standard partisan scripts, it will signal that party elders are truly loosening their grip.
  • Budget and legislative follow-through: Upcoming policy consultations between the Democratic Party and President Lee Jae-myung’s government will show whether youth concerns over labor, housing, and education translate into concrete bills and budget allocations.
  • Movement in 2030 polling: Future public opinion surveys will reveal whether this outreach begins to chip away at Kim’s 67.4 percent disapproval rating among moderates and younger voters—or whether deep-seated cynicism toward both major parties remains entrenched.

For South Korea’s ruling party, turning a 71-year-old political machine toward the concerns of a skeptical younger generation will require far more than a single speech contest. Whether November 21 marks a genuine turning point or just another passing headline will depend on what the party delivers when the cameras leave the stage.
