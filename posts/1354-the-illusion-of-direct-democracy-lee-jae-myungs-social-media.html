The Illusion of Direct Democracy: Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Presidency and the Shadow of Political Realignment

October 20, 2026

On the evening of October 19, over a casual dinner of warm beef soup at the presidential office, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung pushed back against whispers sweeping through Seoul's political district of Yeouido. Addressing leaders of his ruling Democratic Party of Korea, Lee flatly rejected the notion that he was preparing an artificial shake-up of the National Assembly. "Can a president engineer a political realignment just because he wants to? It is unimaginable," Lee remarked, insisting he had pledged never to poach rival lawmakers or engineer forced party mergers. He argued that governing strictly for the partisan base that elected him would make him an "occupier" rather than a president, declaring: "I have never said I would be a president for only one side."

To outside observers, such words sound like the hallmarks of a statesman rising above polarization. Yet for ordinary South Koreans struggling under soaring grocery bills, a frozen job market, and elevated housing costs, the gap between presidential rhetoric and backroom maneuverings tells a far more complicated story. While President Lee presents himself as an accessible, direct communicator who circumvents mainstream gatekeepers via social media, his administration faces growing scrutiny over whether this unfiltered outreach is being used as a rhetorical shield to obscure unacknowledged political engineering and stalled institutional reforms.

A Lifeline to the Grassroots and the Traps of Platform Politics

Direct communication has long been the foundational pillar of Lee Jae-myung's political career. As a political outsider rising from the mayoralty of Seongnam—a satellite city south of Seoul—to the governorship of Gyeonggi Province, and ultimately to the presidency, Lee survived relentless scrutiny from conservative legacy media and state prosecutors by maintaining an unmediated channel to the public on platforms like X (formerly Twitter). During an off-the-record press session that he deliberately threw open to the public, Lee candidly described direct contact with voters as his ultimate survival mechanism: "At the final moment, the only air hole that can save you is your air hole to the people."

Notably, Lee has often bypassed the polished, conventional term for the public—gukmin (citizens or nation)—in favor of minjung, a heavy, historically charged Korean term referring to the laboring, marginalized masses. In mainstream South Korean politics, conservative and moderate figures have long shunned the word due to its association with radical 1980s democratization and labor movements. For Lee, invoking the minjung has served as an authentic signal to ordinary people bearing the brunt of economic hardship, setting him apart from elite technocrats and traditional establishment lawmakers.

Yet wielding that direct connection from the Blue House carries vastly different consequences than doing so as a regional governor or an embattled opposition candidate. When direct lines of communication are confined to digital echo chambers dominated by fierce loyalists, an "air hole" risks turning into a prison of confirmation bias. As political scientists have observed with figures like Donald Trump and his torrential posting on Truth Social, unfiltered text communication can rapidly strip away nuanced context, hardening societal divides into a combat zone of friends versus enemies. When presidential communication focuses heavily on curated digital engagement rather than clear explanations of contentious national strategies, it risks devolving from genuine dialogue into political marketing.

The Phantom of 'Structural Majority' and Nameless Realignment

The central paradox of Lee’s administration lies in the tension between his public disavowal of political engineering and a three-month campaign by his closest allies to build what they term a "structural majority." In South Korean electoral history, the progressive-democratic bloc has frequently found itself hemmed inside a structural ceiling of around 40 percent of the electorate. Breaking out of that box has long been viewed by strategists as a prerequisite for passing major legislation and retaining power.

Beginning in July, President Lee himself signaled an ambition to expand both candidate pools and parliamentary majorities. Shortly thereafter, Kim Min-seok, the leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, began publicly floating an expansion model reminiscent of the late 1990s Millennium Democratic Party, openly discussing the absorption of centrist and moderate conservative politicians. When prominent progressive commentator Rhyu Si-min cautioned against artificial political restructuring, party loyalists dismissed his concerns. By late August, the presidential office had established an official "Integration Promotion Task Force," prompting widespread media coverage of an impending political realignment. In September, during an appearance in Pyeongtaek, ruling-party figures openly appealed for a broad coalition, even extending public overtures to figures like Kim Yong-nam, a former lawmaker from the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party.

After three months of allowing his party apparatus to actively lay the groundwork for absorbing rival elements, Lee's sudden assertion that political realignment is "unimaginable" strikes critics as contradictory. In democratic theory, party realignments and coalition building are neither illegal nor inherently improper; South Korea's modern history includes watershed moments like the 1990 "three-party merger" engineered under Roh Tae-woo to overcome a divided parliament, though that backroom deal remains a deeply controversial chapter. The hazard today is not that political factions are attempting to build a working majority, but that they are executing a realignment while refusing to call it by its name.

Stripping an initiative of its name causes three vital democratic safeguards to vanish:

  • Public debate disappears: Parliament and civil society cannot formally deliberate or scrutinize a project that the government claims does not exist.
  • Party members lose their voice: Grassroots members of the ruling party are denied a formal vote or say over whether their party's identity should be merged with conservative defectors.
  • Accountability evaporates: If the strategy triggers policy deadlock or voter backlash, leadership can dodge responsibility by insisting no realignment ever occurred, while internal critics who sound the alarm are branded as factional saboteurs.

This approach stands in sharp contrast to that of the late progressive President Roh Moo-hyun. In 2005, Roh openly proposed a "grand coalition" (daeyeonjeong) with the conservative Grand National Party to overcome deep regionalism. The proposal shocked Roh’s base and battered his approval ratings, ending in political defeat. Yet Roh gave his gambit an unmistakable name, defended it publicly, and absorbed the blowback directly. Pursuing the benefits of political realignment while publicly pretending to reject it is a far more fragile posture.

Stalled Reform Pledges and the Real Judicial Battlefield

For the voters who brought Lee Jae-myung to power, the core priority was not blurry bipartisan maneuvering, but the dismantling of institutional cartels. Following the turbulent abuses of power that characterized the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—citizens demanded root-and-branch overhauls of the prosecutorial apparatus and the judiciary. These mandates were explicitly codified as the Lee administration's National Agenda Tasks No. 3 and No. 6.

Yet as the administration expends political capital attempting to construct a broad centrist tent, those foundational reforms have stalled. Compromising with traditional elite interests under the banner of becoming a "president for all" risks alienating the core democratic electorate, which views backtracking on institutional accountability as a breach of trust. A president elected on a clear partisan and reformist platform cannot simply dissolve their governing agenda into non-committal centrism without eroding the constitutional role of political parties.

While the ruling bloc debates its legislative coalition, a far more alarming challenge to constitutional norms has quietly emerged within the judiciary. South Korea's Supreme Court recently assigned an extraordinary filing—an injunction seeking to suspend presidential duties and challenge the legitimacy of the presidential election, submitted on behalf of the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—to its Special Division 3, assigning it a formal case number instead of dismissing it out of hand. In a stable constitutional republic, an attempt by an ousted, incarcerated former leader to litigate an election result through an injunction would typically face immediate rejection. That the nation’s highest court is entertaining the petition suggests that judicial factions may be testing the boundaries of political authority.

Compounding the fragility, minor opposition elements, including the Rebuilding Korea Party led by Cho Kuk, have shown signs of exploiting shifts in presidential approval ratings, stoking an atmosphere that challenges the administration's political standing. More striking still is the muted response from the ruling Democratic Party's core pro-Lee factions. While quick to assert control over domestic party appointments, they have remained strangely passive in the face of this judicial overreach.

What to Watch Next

As the Lee administration enters a pivotal phase of its term, the viability of its governing project will depend on whether it resolves the tension between its populist rhetoric and its backroom strategies. Observers should track three concrete indicators in the coming months:

  • Action on National Tasks 3 and 6: Watch whether the Democratic Party introduces concrete legislative timelines for restructuring the state prosecution service and judicial oversight, or whether those bills continue to be deferred in search of centrist consensus.
  • Formalization of Conservative Defections: Pay close attention to whether the administration's Integration Promotion Task Force formally admits former People Power Party lawmakers into the ruling coalition, which would force the Blue House to publicly acknowledge its realignment project.
  • The Supreme Court’s Special Division 3 Ruling: Monitor the Supreme Court's disposition of the injunction suit related to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. A decisive, swift dismissal will reinforce constitutional stability, while any prolonged deliberation will signal deepening institutional friction between the judiciary and the executive branch.
