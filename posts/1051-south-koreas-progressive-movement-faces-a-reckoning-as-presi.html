South Korea's Progressive Movement Faces a Reckoning as President Lee Jae-myung's Approval Plummets

October 13, 2026

For anyone who spent years braving cold winds and pouring rain at street rallies, holding glowing LED candles and dreaming of a fairer South Korea, opening the morning news has lately become a deeply sobering exercise. Across internet forums and progressive political spaces, a heavy sense of fatigue hangs in the air. When the latest Realmeter tracking data flashed across screens showing the presidential approval rating of President Lee Jae-myung sliding down into the mid-30s—with positive marks at 35.2% and negatives surging to 61.4%—it sent a sharp jolt through the progressive camp. For a brief moment, it had felt as though support might stabilize, particularly among voters in their late 40s. Instead, the floor has given way once more, and the anxiety radiating from the highest levels of the Blue House and the ruling party is palpable.

The Realmeter Numbers and the Shocking Collapse in Seoul

To understand the gravity of the current moment, one must look beyond the topline figures. The drop in positive ratings by 2.2 percentage points breaks what little psychological floor the administration had left in the upper 30s. Mainstream media outlets increasingly tiptoe around these rolling surveys, viewing them as politically sensitive territory, but for political organizers and grassroots supporters, these numbers are a flashing warning light on the dashboard.

Methodology matters deeply in South Korean polling, and the data reveals a stark split. Live-telephone interviews (CATI) show a somewhat narrower gap, with approval at 46.8% and disapproval at 52.8%, heavily padded by more than 10% of respondents selecting undecided or neutral options. But automated response system (ARS) surveys—which offer no middle ground, forcing a blunt choice—tell a far more severe story: 39.6% approval against 59.6% disapproval. ARS polls traditionally act as a sharp seismograph of public mood during times of political friction, and right now, they are registering a deep-seated accumulation of public frustration and disappointment.

Most startling is the regional breakdown within the capital. According to the ARS data, President Lee's approval rating in Seoul has cratered to an alarming 32%—falling below the numbers recorded in Daegu and Gyeongsangbuk-do (33%), the traditional conservative heartland of the country. Among young men in Seoul in their 20s, approval sits at just 27.4%, while among men of all ages in the capital, it touches a microscopic 18%. For South Korea's liberal camp, the capital region has always been the ultimate political barometer. When a progressive president's numbers in Seoul dip below those in conservative rural strongholds, it serves as a five-alarm fire for upcoming by-elections and national races.

Shifting Party Lines and the Rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party

The damage is not confined to the executive branch. In party support metrics, the conservative main opposition People Power Party (PPP) has edged ahead of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), registering 41.3% to the DPK’s 35.5% in Realmeter's weekly aggregate. While live-interview polls still show the DPK holding a slight edge at 42.8%, ARS surveys place them in a statistical dead heat at 40.7% to 40.5%.

What makes this stagnation particularly painful for progressives is that the conservative opposition has offered little in the way of inspiring leadership or visionary policies. Rather, the DPK's slide stems from a quiet wave of alienation: disillusioned voters are not switching sides so much as they are checking out, adopting a wait-and-see attitude, or simply deciding to sit future elections out. The rise of the "shy voter" phenomenon among core progressive bases is a classic precursor to electoral disaster.

Compounding this headache is the rapid ascent of the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by figures aligned with former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Hovering between 5% and 8% in surveys and pushing aggressively toward the 10% threshold—with particularly strong backing among women and voters in their 40s and 50s—the party is no longer functioning as a minor satellite fringe. It has entrenched itself as an independent political force. For the DPK, this is not a rising tide lifting all boats; it is a direct siphon drawing away core progressive voters who feel the DPK has lost its reformist edge.

Late-Night Social Media and the Erosion of Trust

Beyond the numbers lies a deeper crisis of trust. Early in the Lee Jae-myung administration, progressive supporters often voiced frustration over policy compromises or rough-edged rhetoric from certain factions, rationalizing them as growing pains. Over time, however, that disappointment hardened into something far more difficult to reverse: a profound sense of betrayal.

On online party boards and progressive community forums, the narrative has shifted from "the government is making mistakes" to "we were deceived." Trust, once broken, renders even the most sophisticated policy packages ineffective. Voters did not elect the administration expecting perfection; they expected consistency, moral clarity, and reformist grit. Instead, they have watched reform slow to a crawl, replaced by an executive communication style that many find abrasive.

Critics across the political spectrum have taken aim at the president's penchant for late-night social media posting. High-profile political figures have pointed out that a head of state burning the midnight oil on personal smartphone apps, engaging in real-time digital feuds, does not project strength—it projects instability. When a president uses public channels to label internal critics or skeptical factions with derogatory tags, it alienates the very coalition that brought them to power. The dignity of the highest office in South Korea demands weighty, unifying public messages, not divisive online labeling that turns loyal base supporters into targets of mockery.

Controversies Over Bureaucratic Reform and Double Standards

Friction within the reform agenda has further frayed party loyalties. The newly launched Major Crime Investigation Office—a cornerstone institution designed to overhaul the nation's prosecutorial landscape—managed to record its very first major controversy not by pursuing corrupt elites, but by initiating an internal inspection of Lim Eun-jung, a prominent reform-minded prosecutor heading the Gwangju branch.

Lim, who gained a reputation as a whistleblowing reformer during the tumultuous years of the disgraced, imprisoned former president Yoon Seok-yeol, recently used a Blue House invitation event to voice principled criticisms regarding the vetting of a controversial nominee. Under past administrations, figures like Lim were hailed as conscience-driven public servants. Under the current DPK government, however, her willingness to speak truth to power has been framed by some ruling party heavyweights as a dangerous challenge to presidential authority warranting disciplinary action. This stark double standard—praising whistleblowers when they attack conservatives while investigating them when they question progressives—has exhausted the party base.

Similarly, the DPK's hesitant handling of major legislative battlegrounds, such as the stalled push for a special counsel investigation into figures like conservative politician Han Dong-hoon, has drawn fierce criticism. While allied minor parties press aggressively for accountability, the ruling party has often appeared paralyzed by tactical caution, fearful of provoking a political backlash. For voters who marched in the streets demanding structural justice, this timidity looks less like pragmatism and more like a surrender of core principles.

What to Watch Next

    Monitor upcoming by-election polling in the Seoul metropolitan area: If the DPK fails to recover ground in the capital, expect frantic internal leadership struggles and demands for a sweeping cabinet reshuffle before national legislative schedules tighten further.
  • Watch the trajectory of the Rebuilding Korea Party: If their support officially stabilizes above 10%, it will permanently alter coalition dynamics, forcing the DPK to either radically realign its progressive platform or risk splitting the center-left vote entirely.
  • Observe the Blue House's digital communications strategy: A quiet period in presidential social media output, paired with formal, institutional press conferences, would signal an awareness of the leadership style crisis. Continued late-night digital skirmishing, by contrast, will confirm that the administration remains blind to its own eroding base.
  • Track legislative momentum on key reform bills: Whether the ruling party moves decisively on stalled special counsel bills and institutional reforms—or continues to stall out of fear—will determine whether progressives head into the next major election cycle deeply demoralized or re-energized.

History has repeatedly proven that reformist governments do not fall because of conservative opposition alone; they collapse when they lose the faith of the people who carried them to power. Unless the ruling establishment steps away from defensive posturing and rediscovers the principles that brought them to office, the cold numbers in Seoul will write a definitive ending.
