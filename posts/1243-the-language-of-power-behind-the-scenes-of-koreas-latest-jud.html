The Language of Power: Behind the Scenes of Korea’s Latest Judicial Showdown

October 16, 2026

The air inside the National Assembly hearing room on October 14 was thick with the kind of tension that only occurs when the law is used as both a sword and a shield. At the center of the storm was Kim Ji-yong, the nominee to lead the Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO)—a new agency designed to take over the investigative powers once held exclusively by the state prosecution. To a casual observer, the debate over "professional assault" versus "negligence" might have seemed like pedantic legal hair-splitting. But for those following the arc of South Korean politics under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party (DP), it was a visceral reminder of a battle that has defined the country for years: the struggle to defang a prosecutorial elite that many believe has become a law unto itself.

This confirmation hearing was more than a routine check on a public official’s resume. It was a symbolic autopsy of a high-profile clash from 2020 involving Jung Jin-woong, a former prosecutor, and Han Dong-hoon, a prominent figure during the previous administration. The hearing laid bare how the legal language used by the powerful can be "polluted" to protect their own, and why the establishment of the MCIO is such a high-stakes gamble for the current government. For the international reader, this isn’t just a story about a bureaucratic appointment; it is a window into how the ruling Democratic Party is attempting to rewrite the rules of Korean justice while grappling with internal fractures and a base that is starting to feel the chill of political isolation.

The Semantic Trap: Assault vs. Negligence

The crux of the controversy surrounding Kim Ji-yong stems from an incident that occurred during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment and removal from office. In July 2020, during an investigation into alleged collusion between a prosecutor and a journalist, Prosecutor Jung Jin-woong attempted to seize the mobile phone of Han Dong-hoon. A physical scuffle ensued. Jung was later charged with "abuse of authority assault" (dokjik pokhaeng), a serious crime involving a public official using violence during the course of their duties.

During the hearing, it was revealed that Kim Ji-yong—who was in a position of authority within the prosecution at the time—had played a complicated double game. On the surface, Kim claimed he opposed the indictment of Jung for "abuse of authority assault." To the current administration, this looked like the stance of a reformer who recognized the investigation into Han Dong-hoon was being unfairly suppressed. However, under questioning from Representative Lee Hae-sik of the Democratic Party, a different truth emerged. While Kim opposed the specific charge of intentional assault, he had actually advocated for indicting Jung on "professional negligence resulting in injury."

"Are you playing word games with the National Assembly?" demanded Representative Chun Choon-saeng of the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive party. The distinction is critical. By suggesting a charge of negligence, Kim was still advocating for the criminal prosecution of a fellow prosecutor who was conducting a sensitive investigation into the then-powerful circle around Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. To critics, this was a classic "prosecutorial alibi"—a way for Kim to claim he followed the law while still delivering the head of a colleague on a silver platter to satisfy the political winds of the time. This "legal double-talk" is exactly what the Lee Jae-myung administration claims it wants to end by shifting power away from traditional prosecutors.

The MCIO and the Ghost of the Prosecution Service

To understand why Kim Ji-yong’s appointment is so contentious, one must understand the Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO). For decades, the Supreme Prosecutors' Office held the near-total power to both investigate and indict. The Democratic Party has long argued that this concentration of power leads to "political prosecutions" and corruption. The MCIO is the final piece of the puzzle to separate these powers, leaving prosecutors to only handle the legal aspects of a trial while the MCIO handles the actual detective work for big-ticket crimes.

The irony, which was not lost on the lawmakers during the hearing, is that the man chosen to lead this "reformist" agency is a product of the very system he is supposed to overhaul. Kim’s behavior during the Jung Jin-woong incident suggests a "prosecutorial elitism"—a belief that the law is a tool to be manipulated for organizational survival. Sung Ki-hong, the Presidential Secretary for Public Communication, defended Kim, stating there were "no flaws or reasons for disqualification" and that Kim had explained himself well. But this defense feels hollow to a public that watched Kim evade direct questions for hours.

The "flawless" label applied by the Blue House (the presidential office) ignores the fundamental question: Can a man who used legal loopholes to pressure a colleague be trusted to build a new, transparent investigative culture? If the MCIO is led by someone who thinks like a traditional prosecutor, it risks becoming "Prosecution 2.0"—a new agency with the same old habits. This is the "bitterness" that Representative Yoon Kun-young expressed after the hearing, noting that while the DP lawmakers worked hard to expose these contradictions, the sense of emptiness remained because the institutional momentum for the appointment seemed unstoppable.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The "Assault vs. Negligence" debate was a legal maneuver to ensure a prosecutor was punished while providing the decider with an "alibi."
  • Kim Ji-yong’s appointment is seen by some as a betrayal of the spirit of the Major Crimes Investigation Office.
  • The Presidential Office’s defense of "no flaws" is at odds with the evasive testimony provided during the hearing.

Internal Fractures: The Shincheonji Shadow

While the judicial battle raged in the hearing room, the Democratic Party found itself embroiled in a strange and distracting internal controversy: the "Shincheonji" infiltration rumors. Shincheonji is a controversial religious movement often described as a cult, which gained international notoriety for its role in the early spread of COVID-19 in Korea. Rumors have been circulating within the DP that 30,000 to 50,000 Shincheonji members have joined the party to influence its leadership elections.

This might seem like a bizarre footnote to a judicial story, but it reflects the deep paranoia and factionalism currently haunting the ruling party. Representative Jung Chung-rae, a high-profile DP member, even used terms like "Anti-Lee/Divisionist/Shincheonji Secret Alliance" on social media. However, other party members, such as Representative Lee Seong-yoon, have pointed out that there is zero concrete evidence of such a mass infiltration. The fear is that the party is "hunting ghosts" instead of focusing on governance.

The danger here is twofold. First, it distracts from the crucial work of prosecutorial reform. Second, it creates a "loyalty test" culture where any dissent against the party leadership is labeled as "divisionist" or "cult-led." When Kim Min-seok, a senior DP leader, suggested that the party might hand over its membership rolls to the prosecution for investigation to "clear the air," it sent shockwaves through the base. The idea of the ruling party voluntarily handing its most sensitive data to the state prosecutors—the very people they are trying to reform—was seen by many as a sign of strategic bankruptcy. As one observer noted, "Politics should be solved with politics, not by calling the police on your own house."

📊 The Human Element of Governance

Out of hundreds of thousands of civil servants in Korea, only 126 hold political appointments of this rank. These individuals set the moral compass for the entire bureaucracy.

When a nominee like Kim Ji-yong uses "legal wordplay," it signals to the 9,400 new civil servants entering the system every year that technical correctness is more important than fundamental honesty. This is how the "dignity of the state" erodes—not through a single scandal, but through the normalization of evasion at the highest levels.

The President’s Isolation and the Road Ahead

Ultimately, the controversy over Kim Ji-yong and the internal party squabbles point to a larger problem facing President Lee Jae-myung: the "isolation of the presidency." As a president moves past their first year, the sheer volume of information they receive often creates a gap between them and their advisors. This can lead to a "Red Team" problem, where no one in the room feels empowered to say "no" to the president or point out that a nominee like Kim might be more trouble than he is worth.

The "바닥 민심" (badak minsim)—the grassroots sentiment—is shifting. Democratic Party lawmakers report that in their home districts, the once-warm handshakes of supporters are turning cold. Supporters are beginning to ask if the power they gave the DP is being used effectively or if it is being squandered on internal bickering and the appointment of questionable "experts" from the old guard. The main opposition People Power Party (PPP) is watching these fractures closely, hoping that the DP’s focus on prosecutorial reform will eventually look like an obsession that ignores the daily economic struggles of the people.

As we look forward, the appointment of Kim Ji-yong will be a litmus test for the Major Crimes Investigation Office. If he is confirmed and the agency begins its work, the public will be watching to see if it targets "big fish" with impartiality or if it simply becomes a weapon for the current administration to settle scores with the remnants of the Yoon Suk-yeol era.

What to watch next:

  • The Official Appointment: Whether President Lee proceeds with the appointment despite the "unfilled" feeling left by the hearing.
  • The MCIO's First Case: The first major investigation launched by the new office will define its reputation for years.
  • The Shincheonji Inquiry: Whether the DP leadership continues to pursue the cult infiltration theory or pivots back to a unified legislative agenda.
  • Public Approval Ratings: Specifically among the core 40s and 50s demographics, who are the most sensitive to judicial fairness.

The struggle for judicial reform in South Korea is a marathon, not a sprint. But as the Kim Ji-yong hearing showed, the most dangerous obstacles aren't always the enemies outside—sometimes, they are the habits and the language of the people inside the room.
