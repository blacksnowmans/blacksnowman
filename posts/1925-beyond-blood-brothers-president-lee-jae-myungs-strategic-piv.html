Beyond "Blood Brothers": President Lee Jae-myung’s Strategic Pivot with Turkey

November 04, 2026

On November 3, the Blue House—the center of power for the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's current ruling party—was the stage for a diplomatic encounter eight years in the making. President Lee Jae-myung welcomed Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan for a state visit that aimed to transform a sentimental historical bond into a hard-nosed strategic partnership. While the rhetoric of "blood brotherhood" flourished, the real substance of the meeting was written in the language of nuclear reactors, defense contracts, and critical mineral supply chains.

For a foreign observer, the timing of this summit is as significant as its content. South Korea is currently navigating a period of intense domestic and international recalibration. Following the turbulent administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently incarcerated following his impeachment and removal from office—the Lee Jae-myung administration has sought to redefine Korea’s "middle-power diplomacy." The goal is to move away from the ideological alignments of the past and toward a pragmatism that prioritizes economic security and technological sovereignty. Turkey, a nation that sits at the literal crossroads of Europe, Asia, and the Middle East, is the perfect testing ground for this new doctrine.

Upgrading the "Blood Alliance" for the 21st Century

To understand why this summit matters, one must first understand the unique emotional weight of the Korea-Turkey relationship. During the Korean War (1950-1953), Turkey sent the fourth-largest contingent of troops to fight alongside the South. This sacrifice earned them the title of "Kardeş" (Brother) in Turkish and "Hyeol-maeng" (Blood Ally) in Korean. For decades, this bond was the bedrock of bilateral relations, often invoked during sporting events like the 2002 World Cup or in commemorative ceremonies.

However, sentimentality does not pay for nuclear power plants. President Lee Jae-myung, known for his focus on "practical diplomacy," used this visit to signal that the era of relying solely on historical gratitude is over. During the summit, he noted that while the two nations are indeed "blood brothers," the relationship must now be "upgraded" to a comprehensive strategic partnership. This isn't just diplomatic jargon; it represents a shift toward treating Turkey as a gateway for Korean industry into the European and Middle Eastern markets.

The fact that this was Erdoğan’s first visit in eight years underscores a period of relative stagnation in high-level engagement. During the previous years, Korea’s foreign policy was often preoccupied with the shifting whims of the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era. By hosting Erdoğan now, President Lee is attempting to stabilize Korea’s western flank, securing a partner that remains a member of NATO while maintaining its own unique, often independent, foreign policy voice in the Islamic world.

Nuclear Energy and the Defense Gateway

The most tangible "prizes" of the summit were found in the energy and defense sectors. Turkey is currently looking to expand its nuclear power capacity to reduce its reliance on energy imports, and South Korea is hungry to export its nuclear technology. Specifically, the two leaders discussed the Sinop nuclear power plant project. This is a multi-billion dollar endeavor on Turkey's Black Sea coast where Korea is a leading contender for construction.

"Energy security is no longer just about buying oil; it’s about building the infrastructure of the future," President Lee emphasized during the talks. By pushing for Korean participation in the Sinop project, the administration is not just seeking a contract for Korean construction firms; it is looking to establish a decades-long technological dependency that will tie the two economies together. The summit produced a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) specifically on nuclear project financing, a crucial step in ensuring that these massive projects don't stall due to capital constraints.

Defense cooperation followed a similar logic. The "K-Defense" brand has seen a global surge in popularity, and Turkey occupies a unique position in this market. Unlike many other buyers, Turkey possesses a sophisticated domestic defense industry and often demands technology transfers rather than simple off-the-shelf purchases. The summit focused on joint production and technological cooperation. This is a win-win for the Lee administration: it secures a massive market for Korean defense tech while bypassing some of the geopolitical sensitivities of selling directly to more volatile regions. By co-producing with Turkey, Korea gains a manufacturing hub that can potentially serve the entire Mediterranean and Middle Eastern regions.

Institutionalizing the Economic Engine

Beyond the "big ticket" items like tanks and reactors, the summit addressed the plumbing of bilateral trade. The South Korea-Turkey Free Trade Agreement (FTA) has been in place for years, but it is increasingly seen as a relic of an older trade era. The two leaders agreed to expedite the revision of the FTA and, perhaps more importantly, to upgrade the Joint FTA Committee to the ministerial level.

This ministerial upgrade is a clear signal to the markets. It means that trade disputes, regulatory barriers, and investment hurdles will be handled directly by top government officials rather than being buried in the bureaucracy. For Korean companies—ranging from automotive giants to electronics manufacturers—this reduces the "political risk" of doing business in Turkey, which has struggled with currency volatility and inflation in recent years.

The summit also saw the signing of four key MOUs covering:

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI): Collaborative research and ethical framework development.
  • Energy Transition: Cooperation on renewables and hydrogen technology.
  • Supply Chain Security: Specifically focusing on "critical minerals."
  • Nuclear Financing: Creating the fiscal pathways for large-scale infrastructure.

The inclusion of critical minerals is particularly noteworthy. As South Korea tries to reduce its overwhelming dependence on China for the materials needed for EV batteries and semiconductors, it is scouring the globe for alternative partners. Turkey’s rich mineral deposits make it a strategic piece of the puzzle for Korea's high-tech manufacturing sector. This is "supply chain diplomacy" in action—using state-to-level summits to secure the raw materials that private companies need to survive.

Navigating the Fires of the Middle East

While the focus was heavily economic, the geopolitical elephant in the room was the instability of the Middle East. Turkey is a regional heavyweight with significant influence in the conflicts currently roiling the region. For South Korea, a country that imports nearly all of its oil and gas, any disruption in the Middle East is an existential threat.

President Lee and President Erdoğan spent considerable time discussing regional stability. For Korea, this was an opportunity to act as a "Global Pivotal State"—a term the administration uses to describe Korea’s aspiration to contribute to international peace. By aligning with Turkey on calls for regional de-escalation, Korea isn't just being a "good international citizen"; it is protecting the shipping lanes and energy prices that its economy depends on.

Furthermore, the Turkish side reiterated its support for peace on the Korean Peninsula. While Turkey’s direct influence over North Korea is limited, its voice in international forums like the UN and its relationship with other global powers make it a valuable diplomatic ally. In an era where the North Korean threat is evolving—especially with the North's reported involvement in the Ukraine conflict—having a NATO member like Turkey firmly on Seoul's side is a significant diplomatic asset.

What to Watch Next

The success of this summit will not be measured by the photos of the handshake, but by the speed of implementation. The Lee Jae-myung administration has set a high bar for "results-oriented" foreign policy, and there are three specific milestones to watch in the coming months:

  • The Sinop Nuclear Contract: Watch for whether the MOU on financing translates into a formal commercial agreement. If Korea wins this bid, it will be a major validation of the administration's "Nuclear Export" strategy.
  • FTA Revision Milestones: The first meeting of the newly upgraded ministerial-level committee will be a litmus test for how serious both sides are about removing trade barriers.
  • Defense Joint Ventures: Look for announcements regarding the joint production of specific hardware. If Korean tech is integrated into Turkish platforms, it will signify a deep, long-term military-industrial integration.

For the foreign reader, this summit is a reminder that South Korea is moving past its period of domestic political upheaval. Under President Lee Jae-myung, the country is looking outward with a cold, calculated eye on its economic interests. The "Blood Brothers" are still brothers, but they are now, more than ever, business partners in an increasingly dangerous world.
