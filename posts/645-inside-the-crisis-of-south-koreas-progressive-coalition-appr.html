Inside the Crisis of South Korea's Progressive Coalition: Approval Slumps and the Search for Reform

September 10, 2026

Politicians in Seoul are spending long hours in neighborhood coffee shops debating more than just sliding poll numbers. Behind closed doors, strategists and lawmakers are diagnosing a structural crisis facing South Korea's progressive administration. With President Lee Jae-myung's approval rating dipping to 38.2 percent and negative reviews climbing to 58.8 percent, critics within the broader liberal movement are asking a blunt question: Where did the momentum for reform go?

For international observers watching South Korea's fast-moving political landscape, these numbers are more than routine turbulence. They reveal a familiar political trap — an administration trying so hard to court the center that it alienates its core base, all while failing to deliver tangible policy victories. To understand why this matters, we have to look past the daily headlines and examine how the ruling camp is losing its footing.

The Anatomy of a Slumping Approval Rating

Recent data from research firms like NewsTomato and the National Barometer Survey (NBS) paint a concerning picture for the administration. President Lee’s approval stands at 38.2 percent, while disapproval has surged to nearly 59 percent. The real structural worry, however, lies in voter churn. While the administration managed to attract a modest 9.6 percent of new voters who previously sat on the sidelines, it shed 28.4 percent of its former supporters. That is a net loss of nearly three to one.

The administration’s strategy of aggressive outreach — often championed by the progressive camp's newer factions — was designed to broaden the electoral coalition. But sacrificing a quarter of your base to chase an elusive center is a high-stakes gamble that has backfired. This bleeding is not confined to a single faction; it cuts across moderate and progressive voters alike. Critics argue that by chasing an impossible middle ground, the government has ended up abandoning the very people who carried it to power.

Compounding this is a distinct lack of long-term vision. Major structural tasks like real estate reform and prosecution overhaul cannot be solved overnight. Yet, a year and four months into the term, the administration has struggled to articulate a clear roadmap for the next phase of its agenda. When economic anxiety and policy confusion set in, voters are left wondering what happened to the promised reforms.

Real Estate, Appointments, and the Trap of Short-Term Fixes

Nowhere is this policy drift more evident than in the government's approach to the housing market. South Korea's real estate prices have long been a political powder keg. Rather than sticking to a coherent, long-term supply and demand strategy, the administration has fallen into the habit of reacting hastily to localized complaints from homeowners. While these quick fixes may quiet immediate grievances from middle-class property owners, they breed deep frustration among younger, working-class renters who feel locked out of the market entirely.

A similar disconnect plagues high-level government appointments. Recent ministerial nominations have triggered public pushback not merely from political opponents, but from the public at large. When the rationale behind choosing specific candidates lacks transparency, the public is left without a clear narrative to defend. Parliamentary confirmation hearings were designed for nominees to make their case directly to the public, but when the government fails to articulate its personnel logic, it turns routine appointments into avoidable political liabilities.

Meanwhile, the opposition People Power Party — South Korea's main conservative party — has managed to hold ground despite offering few compelling alternatives of its own. In head-to-head polling, the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition-turned-ruling party) sits at 37.5 percent, running neck-and-neck with the conservatives at 35.5 percent. At the same time, smaller progressive partners like the Rebuilding Korea Party have seen steady gains, signaling that disillusioned voters are not necessarily switching sides — they are shopping for alternative progressive options.

Foreign Policy Pressures and the Hormuz Dilemma

External pressures are compounding these domestic headaches. The ongoing debate over whether to deploy naval assets to the Strait of Hormuz has exposed deep fault lines within the progressive camp. Public opinion polls show that 45 percent of citizens oppose the deployment, outweighing the 36 percent in favor, with particularly stiff resistance coming from voters in their 40s and 50s.

Sending military personnel into a volatile Middle Eastern flashpoint amid rising tensions with Iran carries profound security risks. Critics within the progressive movement have questioned the logic of joining foreign military operations without clear national interest justifications. For a political base that traditionally favors an independent, peace-oriented foreign policy, blindly following foreign security commitments feels like a betrayal of core principles.

History offers a warning here. Much like the dynamics seen during past European reform movements, when a government stalls on its core promises or adopts erratic messaging, the moderate middle drifts away and a backlash inevitably follows. The current friction within South Korea's progressive movement stems fundamentally from this sense of stalled momentum.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward upcoming local elections, several critical milestones will determine whether the administration can right the ship:

  • The Progressive Coalition Talks: Watch how the Democratic Party handles unification overtures with smaller allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party. Formalistic merger talks driven by short-term electoral math will backfire; genuine trust-building is required.
  • Policy Consistency on the Economy: Look for tangible shifts in how economic policies are communicated. The administration must move away from reactive fixes and demonstrate competence in stabilizing everyday household costs.
  • Personnel and Communication Strategy: Pay attention to how the executive branch handles upcoming legislative sessions and public addresses. A pivot toward open listening and transparent explanations could help recover lost ground.

Politics ultimately runs on public trust and a sense of efficacy. If the administration wants to reverse its fortunes before the back half of its term, it must stop blaming external critics and return to the foundational principles that brought it to power. Without a sharp course correction, the window for meaningful reform risk closing entirely.
