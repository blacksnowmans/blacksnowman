The End of Trumpism? What the U.S. Election Results Mean for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

November 06, 2026

In the late-night cafes of Seoul’s financial district and the quiet corridors of the Blue House, the screens have been glowing with the red and blue maps of a distant continent. For South Korea, a U.S. election is never just "foreign news." It is a fundamental shift in the tectonic plates upon which Korean security and economic prosperity rest. As the final tallies from the U.S. midterm elections solidify, the result is not merely a change in legislative math; it is a seismic rejection of the "America First" isolationism that defined the last decade. For the government of President Lee Jae-myung, these results offer both a blueprint for progress and a sobering warning about the volatility of modern democracy.

The Democratic landslide in the United States—capturing a dominant 54 seats in the Senate and 248 in the House—marks what many analysts are calling the "end of Trumpism." But as Korea looks toward its own future, the implications stretch far beyond the halls of Congress. From the invisible battlefields of hybrid warfare in Europe to the delicate diplomatic dance with North Korea and China, the world is entering a new era. For a South Korean public still navigating the aftermath of the turbulent years under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the American shift toward inclusive, progressive values feels like a mirror of their own recent history.

A Historic Shift in the American Political Landscape

The scale of the Democratic victory in the U.S. is difficult to overstate. By securing 54 seats in the Senate (including two independents) against the Republicans' 44, and a commanding 248-to-156 majority in the House, the Democratic Party has achieved its most decisive win in half a century. They also reclaimed the maps of state capitals, taking 28 governorships. This is not just a "blue wave"; it is the dismantling of the "Red Wall," the supposedly impenetrable fortress of conservative support in the American heartland.

What makes this shift particularly resonant for international observers is the diversity of the new American leadership. The election of Keisha Lance Bottoms as the first Black woman governor of Georgia and Deb Haaland as the first Native American woman governor in New Mexico signals a profound change in who holds power in the U.S. Perhaps most striking was the victory of Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, who became the first Muslim U.S. Senator by winning over the "Rust Belt" working-class voters—a demographic previously thought to be the bedrock of the Republican base. Along with the election of Byron Donalds in Florida and Chris Pappas, an openly gay Senator from New Hampshire, the results suggest that the American electorate is moving toward a more pluralistic and inclusive definition of national identity.

For South Korea, this transition is significant. During the era of "Trumpism," Seoul often found itself caught between a transactional U.S. president and an increasingly assertive China. The return of a predictable, values-based Democratic majority suggests a return to traditional alliances. However, the Lee Jae-myung administration is viewing these results with a cautious eye. Political experts in Seoul, such as Professor Park Gu-yong, note that while the House reflects the immediate will of the people, the Senate—with its six-year terms and power over presidential appointments—provides the long-term stability needed for consistent foreign policy. The Democratic sweep of both houses gives President Lee a more stable partner in Washington, but it also means that Korea must align itself with a U.S. that is increasingly focused on progressive global standards.

The Specter of Hybrid Warfare and the Vulnerability of Modern States

While the U.S. election results dominate the headlines, a darker trend is emerging from Europe that has Seoul’s security experts on high alert: the rise of "hybrid warfare." This is not the traditional war of tanks and trenches, but a creeping, multi-dimensional assault on the fabric of daily life. It involves cyberattacks, sabotage, and disinformation designed to hollow out a nation’s stability without ever declaring open conflict.

The recent catalog of incidents in Europe is chilling. In August, drones were sighted over Leipzig/Halle Airport in Germany, and an arson attack struck Milrem Robotics, a defense firm in Estonia. By September, Starlink satellite facilities in Poland were targeted. Perhaps most concerning for a country as digitally integrated as South Korea were the attacks in October, where hackers breached the refrigeration systems of supermarkets across 13 European countries and compromised blood storage facilities in France. These are not random acts of vandalism; they are strategic strikes on the infrastructure that keeps a modern society functioning.

European authorities have pointed toward Russia and Iran as the architects of these disruptions. The goal is simple: to create a sense of pervasive insecurity among the civilian population. South Korea, often described as the most "wired" nation on earth, is uniquely vulnerable to this style of aggression. Our supermarkets, our power grids, and our healthcare systems are all managed by the same digital threads that were severed in Europe. The lesson for the Lee Jae-myung government is clear: national security in the 21st century is no longer just about the DMZ (the Demilitarized Zone separating North and South Korea). It is about the firewall protecting a refrigerator in a neighborhood grocery store. As we watch the "invisible war" unfold in Europe, the necessity for a total overhaul of Korea’s cyber-defense protocols has become an urgent domestic priority.

Navigating a Shifting Geopolitical Order

The fallout from the U.S. elections will be felt most acutely in the diplomatic maneuvers surrounding the Korean Peninsula. Under the previous "America First" approach, diplomacy was often reduced to a series of high-stakes summits with little institutional follow-through. With the Democrats firmly in control of the U.S. legislative agenda, we can expect a shift toward more traditional, multi-lateral diplomacy. This creates a new opening for President Lee Jae-myung to assert Korea’s role as a regional mediator.

The timing is critical. We are seeing significant movements in the North Korea-China relationship, highlighted by the recent visit of Kim Sung-nam, a high-ranking North Korean official, to Beijing. This suggests a consolidation of the traditional "northern" alliance. Simultaneously, the U.S. is refocusing its attention on the APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) forum and strengthening ties with ASEAN (the Association of Southeast Asian Nations) through key hubs like Manila and Shenzhen. The "values-based" diplomacy of a Democratic-led U.S. will likely demand that Korea take a firmer stance on human rights and regional security, even as Seoul seeks to maintain its vital economic ties with China.

The challenge for the Lee Jae-myung administration is to ensure that Korea’s voice isn't lost in the renewed "great power competition" between a revitalized U.S. and a consolidating China-North Korea bloc. The U.S. election results prove that the American public wants a government that looks forward, not backward. President Lee must convince Washington that South Korea is the indispensable partner in this new, progressive Pacific order—not just a junior ally, but a co-architect of regional stability.

Lessons for Seoul: Governance in an Age of Reaction

There is a profound domestic parallel between the U.S. election results and the current state of South Korean politics. The "Blue Wave" in America is being interpreted by many in the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) as a validation of the path chosen by President Lee Jae-myung. Much like the U.S. is attempting to move past the divisions of the Trump era, the Lee government is tasked with repairing the social and political fractures left behind by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was ultimately impeached and is currently imprisoned, is now viewed as a period of significant democratic backsliding. His administration was marked by what many critics called "political fragmentation" and a disregard for the collaborative norms of the National Assembly (Korea’s parliament). Today, the DPK holds the presidency and a strong position in the legislature, but the U.S. results serve as a reminder that political victory is fleeting. Just as the American "Red Wall" crumbled, the DPK’s current dominance in regions like Honam (the southwest) or the PPP’s (People Power Party, the main conservative opposition) grip on Daegu and Gyeongbuk (the southeast) can be shifted by a government that fails to deliver on its promises.

The Democratic victory in the U.S. was won by reaching out to unconventional voters—the "Progressive Evangelicals" and the working-class laborers of the Rust Belt. For President Lee, the lesson is that a mandate for change must be sustained through inclusive governance that transcends traditional regional and ideological divides. The "reaction" to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s policies brought the current government to power, but it is the proactive building of a more resilient, technologically secure, and socially inclusive Korea that will keep it there. As the U.S. tries to end the era of "Trumpism," Korea must continue its work to permanently move past the "Yoon-ism" that once paralyzed its institutions.

What to watch next

  • The APEC Summit: Look for how the Democratic-led U.S. delegation interacts with President Lee Jae-myung. This will be the first major test of whether "values-based diplomacy" will translate into concrete economic benefits for South Korea.
  • North Korea’s "Beijing Pivot": Monitor the outcome of Kim Sung-nam’s meetings in China. If North Korea feels sidelined by a more unified U.S.-South Korea alliance, it may accelerate its military cooperation with Russia and China, potentially leading to more "hybrid" provocations.
  • Cybersecurity Legislation: Watch for the Lee Jae-myung administration to introduce new "National Resilience" laws. Following the European examples of hybrid war, there will likely be a push to classify civilian infrastructure (like food supply chains and medical databases) as critical national security assets.
  • The PPP’s Rebranding: As the U.S. Republican Party faces an identity crisis after the midterm defeat, watch for how the People Power Party in Korea adjusts its rhetoric. Will they double down on the legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, or will they attempt to find a new, moderate path to challenge the DPK?

The U.S. election has proven that even the most entrenched political movements can be upended when the public demands a return to stability and progress. For the global audience watching from outside, South Korea’s response to this shift will be the ultimate test of its maturity as a leading democratic power in Asia.
