The 72-Hour Purge? Inside the Democratic Party’s Civil War Over Choi Min-hee

November 17, 2026

In mid-October, during a closed-door meeting in Namyangju, a city just east of Seoul, Choi Min-hee—a high-ranking member of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—made a comment that would ignite a political firestorm. Speaking to local party members, she reportedly claimed that President Lee Jae-myung had actually opposed the impeachment of the now-imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol during the national crisis that led to his removal. When the recording of this remark leaked to the press on November 12, the reaction was not just swift; it was surgical.

Within 90 minutes of the report, the Blue House (South Korea's presidential office) issued a stern denial, calling Choi’s claim "groundless." Only 40 minutes after that, Kim Min-seok, the leader of the DPK, ordered an immediate internal investigation. By November 15—just 72 hours later—the case was fast-tracked to the party’s Ethics Tribunal for formal disciplinary action. In the world of South Korean politics, where bureaucratic delays are the norm, this "speed-of-light" discipline has left many wondering whether the party is maintaining order or conducting a factional purge.

At the heart of this conflict is a struggle for the soul and the leadership of the DPK. While the party leadership frames the move as a necessary measure to uphold party dignity and correct misinformation, a vocal faction of supporters and rival politicians see something more cynical: a "frame shift" designed to bury the leadership’s own recent scandals and tighten its grip on power ahead of the next election cycle.

The Anatomy of a "Slip of the Tongue"

The controversy surrounding Choi Min-hee, a member of the DPK's Supreme Council (the party’s top decision-making body), rests on two main pillars. The first is her claim regarding President Lee Jae-myung’s stance on the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol. For the DPK, whose identity is deeply rooted in the "Candlelight Revolution" that ousted the previous administration, suggesting that the current President was anything less than fully supportive of that movement is ideological heresy.

Choi has since apologized, characterizing her remarks as a "mistake of memory." Her defenders argue that in the heat of a private meeting, it is easy to confuse complex timelines—specifically the period before and after the insurrection that led to Mr. Yoon’s imprisonment. They argue that confusing whether the President was discussing the impeachment of Mr. Yoon or a different official, like Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, is a human error, not a disciplinary offense. They compare it to common verbal slips, such as accidentally calling the current President by the name of a predecessor.

The second pillar involves Choi’s derogatory remarks toward the press. In a group chat with party members, she reportedly referred to journalists as a "pack of hyenas." While the DPK has often had a contentious relationship with the conservative-leaning media, the leadership has seized on this language as evidence of "damaging the party's dignity."

However, the speed of the investigation remains the most jarring element. Under the previous leadership of Chung Chung-rae, internal discipline was typically reserved for "grave" or "urgent" matters—actions that clearly broke the law or posed an immediate threat to an upcoming election. Choi’s defenders ask: Did a private comment in a closed-door meeting, which was immediately retracted and apologized for, truly constitute a national emergency for the party?

The Shincheonji Ghost and the "Frame Shift" Theory

To understand why this internal discipline has become so explosive, one must look at what was happening in the DPK just days before the Choi scandal broke. Kim Min-seok, the current party leader, has been under intense pressure regarding his own rhetoric during the party’s leadership primary in July.

At the time, Kim had alleged the existence of a "three-way secret alliance" between "Anti-Lee Jae-myung" forces, "divisionists," and Shincheonji—a controversial religious group often described as a cult that has frequently been used as a political boogeyman in South Korea. Kim claimed this alliance was working to undermine the party from within. However, on November 3, during a closed-door meeting of party lawmakers, Kim admitted that an internal investigation found "no evidence" of such a cult-linked alliance. He issued a public apology on November 6.

The timing is suspicious to many. Only four days after Kim’s apology, a formal petition was filed by a "rights-holding member" (a core, dues-paying DPK supporter) to discipline Kim himself for spreading false information about the cult alliance. Under South Korean political law, spreading false information to influence a primary can carry a prison sentence of up to five years.

Critics, including high-profile political analysts and rival Supreme Council members like Lee Sung-yoon and Han Min-soo, suggest that the "Choi Min-hee Purge" is a tactical distraction. By aggressively pursuing Choi for her "slip of the tongue," the leadership creates a "frame shift," moving the conversation away from Kim’s unsubstantiated cult allegations and onto the "indiscretion" of a Supreme Council member who is often seen as a rival or a voice for the party's grassroots faction.

Double Standards and the "Themincho" Controversy

The internal rift has exposed a perceived double standard within the party’s hierarchy. While Kim Min-seok’s false allegations against his own party members were handled with a simple apology and a "magnanimous" closing of the case, Choi’s retracted comments were met with an immediate ethics referral. This has led to accusations of "terror politics" and "line-up politics," where the leadership uses disciplinary powers to silence dissent and force loyalty.

This friction came to a head with the involvement of the "Themincho," a group of freshman (first-term) DPK lawmakers. Initially, some members of this group were reportedly discussing the need for party unity and healing. However, within 24 hours, the group pivoted to issuing a collective statement condemning Choi Min-hee, arguing that her remarks undermined the "legitimacy of the People’s Sovereignty Government" (the Lee Jae-myung administration).

This move has been heavily criticized by the party’s grassroots. Defenders of Choi argue that if the legitimacy of the government is so fragile that a single misremembered comment in a private meeting can topple it, then the party has bigger problems. Furthermore, the act of sitting lawmakers issuing a collective statement to pressure the Ethics Tribunal—which is supposed to be an independent, quasi-judicial body—is seen by many as a breach of democratic principles. In South Korea, where the independence of the judiciary is a sensitive topic, seeing "political prosecutors" within one’s own party is a bitter pill for many supporters to swallow.

The tension is also reflected in the digital sphere. Reports have emerged of private KakaoTalk group chats—the ubiquitous Korean messaging app—where supporters of the leadership have allegedly used violent and sexually degrading language against Choi Min-hee. While the authenticity of every participant's identity in these chats is difficult to verify, the existence of such vitriol highlights the toxic nature of the current factional split.

What to Watch Next

The DPK is now entering a period of extreme volatility. How this "72-hour discipline" concludes will signal the direction of the party as it prepares for the second half of President Lee Jae-myung’s term. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Ethics Tribunal’s Verdict: The tribunal has the power to dismiss the case, issue a warning, or suspend Choi’s party membership. A heavy-handed punishment (such as suspension) would likely be seen as a declaration of war against the party's grassroots and the rival "Pro-Chung" faction, potentially leading to a formal split or a leadership challenge.
  • The Petition Against Kim Min-seok: The formal request for discipline against the party leader regarding his false "Shincheonji alliance" claims is still looming. If the party ignores this petition while punishing Choi, the accusations of a double standard will reach a boiling point among the "rights-holding members."
  • The Grassroots Revolt: Local party members in Namyangju have already issued a statement defending Choi, arguing that her communication with her constituents should not be "weaponized" by the central leadership. Watch for whether this local resistance spreads to other regional chapters.
  • Leadership Unity: Three members of the Supreme Council—Choi, Lee Sung-yoon, and Han Min-soo—recently boycotted a leadership meeting in protest. If the "Pro-Chung" and "Pro-Kim" factions cannot find a way to share the table, the DPK’s ability to pass legislation and support the President's agenda will be severely compromised.

Ultimately, this isn't just about what Choi Min-hee said in a room in Namyangju. It is a test of whether the Democratic Party of Korea can govern itself with the same transparency and fairness it demands of the nation. For a party that prides itself on being the vanguard of democracy in the post-Yoon Suk Yeol era, the sight of "internal purges" and "frame shifts" is a worrying sign for many observers of Korean politics.
