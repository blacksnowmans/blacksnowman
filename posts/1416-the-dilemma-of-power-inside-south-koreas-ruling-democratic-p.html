The Dilemma of Power: Inside South Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party and the Battle over Judicial Reform and Crypto Taxes

October 23, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) finds itself caught in an escalating war of nerves with itself. Despite holding the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung alongside a commanding parliamentary majority, the party is not marching in lockstep. Instead, bitter friction over the proposed impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, indecision over a contentious cryptocurrency tax, and controversial legislative compromises have triggered a sharp slump in voter approval. What was supposed to be a confident governing agenda has instead devolved into public feuding that exposes deepening cracks within the progressive establishment.

At the center of the storm is party leader Kim Min-seok, whose leadership is facing rebellion from vocal hardliners. As the party hesitates on key reforms and wavers over major economic policies, its loyal base is growing anxious, while a rival progressive force waits in the wings to seize the mantle of uncompromising reform.

The Showdown Over Chief Justice Cho and the Rise of the 'Pro-Jung' Faction

The most explosive fault line within the Democratic Party concerns whether—and how fast—to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. To the party’s grassroots and core loyalists, judicial reform remains unfinished business. During the turbulent tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was ultimately impeached and removed from office, progressives watched with fury as state prosecutors and judicial authorities exercised sweeping power. For many supporters, reforming the bench and holding top judges accountable was a primary mandate given to the current government.

Party leader Kim Min-seok, however, has urged a policy of "speed control." Wary of a potential political backlash from overreaching with an aggressive impeachment drive, Kim has favored a calibrated, cautious path. That hesitation has drawn open, biting fire from party heavyweights. Lawmaker Jung Chung-rae publicly targeted the leadership's stance, warning that

"hesitating and looking this way and that will cause us to miss our window entirely."

Jung’s public dissent is not merely an isolated critique; it marks the consolidation of a distinct intra-party bloc known as the "pro-Jung" faction. Sensing that the leadership is losing its nerve, Jung and his allies are positioning themselves as true champions of uncompromising reform. The clash spilled into the open when Kim Min-seok pushed back, remarking pointedly that "governing isn't just about talk," underscoring his irritation with the rising factional challenge.

Further complicating this internal feud is an external competitor: the Rebuilding Korea Party, the hard-charging minor progressive outfit founded by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Unburdened by the institutional responsibilities of governance, the Rebuilding Korea Party has championed aggressive positions—including immediate judicial impeachment and swift policy action. With its approval ratings climbing toward double digits, the Democratic Party leadership faces a painful dilemma: move too slowly and forfeit the reform agenda to rival progressives, or move too aggressively and alienate moderate voters.

Red Lights in the Heartlands: Disapproval Surges Across Honam and Chungcheong

The party’s internal paralysis is exacting an immediate toll in the polls. According to recent survey data from Realmeter, voter assessment of Kim Min-seok's job performance as party leader has cratered to alarming levels. Positive evaluations of Kim fell to 25.6%, while negative assessments surged to 67.6%. In an embarrassing reversal for a ruling party leader, Kim’s disapproval rating surpassed the 59.5% negative rating logged by Jang Dong-hyeok, head of the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP).

Most concerning for Democratic strategists is the geographic breakdown of the numbers:

  • Chungcheong Province: Negative appraisals reached into the 70% range, reflecting sharp dissatisfaction in a traditional swing region that often decides national elections.
  • Honam (the Jeolla Provinces): Disapproval climbed to 53%. For a Democratic leader to face majority disapproval in Honam—the historic geographic bedrock and political fortress of South Korean liberalism—is a loud alarm bell.
  • Young Adults in Their 30s: Discontent is running high among thirty-somethings struggling with elevated living costs and sluggish employment prospects, demographic groups that feel political elites are neglecting day-to-day welfare.

When a party’s traditional base turns cool, it rarely signals ideological desertion to the conservative opposition; rather, it indicates profound disillusionment. Supporters do not want a passive governing party that uses its massive parliamentary mandate merely to calculate short-term electoral odds.

The Crypto Tax Stall and Policy Hesitation

Beyond ideological warfare over the judiciary, the Democratic Party is struggling to present a clear, unified stance on practical economic policy—none more emblematic than the taxation of cryptocurrency gains. South Korea's plan to tax digital asset profits has already been postponed three times. Now, the government and the National Assembly face a decision on whether to delay it for a fourth time.

The fundamental tax doctrine in South Korea has long been that "where there is income, there must be taxation." Salaried workers have their taxes deducted automatically from every paycheck, creating deep resentment when speculative asset markets are granted repeated exemptions. Yet with millions of young retail investors heavily invested in cryptocurrencies, lawmakers fear an immediate backlash at the ballot box if taxation goes ahead.

Rather than providing decisive leadership, Kim Min-seok stated vaguely that the party would "reach a conclusion within the year." This open-ended ambiguity has left the National Assembly's Finance Committee divided and market participants frustrated. The conservative People Power Party has seized on the hesitation, attacking the ruling party for failing to build adequate administrative and reporting infrastructure while keeping investors in limbo.

Amid overseas debates over political crypto corruption—including controversies surrounding token revenues tied to Donald Trump—South Korean lawmakers cannot even agree on a basic taxation timeline. Shifting tax principles to curry favor with swing voters risks eroding public confidence in the fairness of the broader tax system.

An Identity Crisis in Governance: From For-Profit Hospitals to Firefighter Pay

The friction inside the Democratic Party extends into legislative design, where several initiatives appear to collide with progressive foundational values:

Foreign Corporate Hospitals and Labor Exemptions

In drafting legislation for new "mega-region special development zones," party planners included provisions permitting foreign corporate-owned hospitals. Progressive civic groups and party purists immediately sounded the alarm, accusing the leadership of back-door privatization of the healthcare sector. Commercialized healthcare has long been anathema to South Korea’s progressive tradition, which was championed across the administrations of Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in. Compounding the controversy, the PPP slammed the Democrats' dual proposal on regional labor exemptions as an evasive legislative gimmick that risks weakening workplace protections under the guise of regional growth.

The Debate Over National Firefighter Funding

On welfare and public safety, lawmaker Jung Chung-rae made waves by demanding that the central government assume full financial responsibility for the payroll costs of the nation's firefighters. In response, the party and the administration began reviewing full state budget coverage to resolve lingering workplace disputes. Yet this policy shift creates an awkward policy tension: President Lee Jae-myung had previously argued on the record that firefighting services should fundamentally remain a responsibility of local municipal governments.

While floor leader Han Byung-do toured provincial communities under the banner of combating regional depopulation, the party also maintained its legal and regulatory battles. The DPK filed formal criminal defamation charges against PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok and insisted that a recent Constitutional Court decision concerning Korea Communications Commission (KCC) head Lee Jin-sook did not amount to an acquittal. Yet these legal skirmishes do little to mask the larger problem: a governing party divided between technocratic compromises and populist demands.

What to Watch Next

South Korea’s political calendar leaves the Democratic leadership little time to indulge in factional squabbles. Several decisive indicators will show whether Kim Min-seok can steady his party or whether the internal fracture will deepen:

  • The Cho Hee-dae Impeachment Decision: Watch whether the party leadership succumbs to pressure from Jung Chung-rae and the pro-Jung faction by formally introducing an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice, or whether Kim holds his ground on "speed control."
  • The Year-End Crypto Tax Bill: The Democratic leadership must deliver on its promise to settle the digital asset tax question before the legislative session concludes. A fourth delay will invite charges of political cowardice, while enforcing the tax without consensus risks alienating retail investors.
  • Regional Polling in Honam: Keep a close eye on weekly tracking polls in the Jeolla provinces. If Kim Min-seok's disapproval numbers in Honam remain above 50%, calls for structural leadership changes and factional realignment will become deafening.

Holding both the Blue House and the National Assembly provides immense governing leverage, but it eliminates any room for excuses. For South Korea's ruling party, the greatest danger does not come from the conservative opposition across the aisle, but from its own inability to decide what kind of governing party it wants to be.
