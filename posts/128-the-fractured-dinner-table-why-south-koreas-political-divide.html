The Fractured Dinner Table: Why South Korea’s Political Divide is Tearing Families Apart

September 29, 2026

For many across South Korea, the recent Chuseok (Korean Thanksgiving) holiday—a time traditionally reserved for ancestral rites and family reunions—was marked by an uncomfortable, pervasive silence. While the holiday is meant to be a time of warmth, it has increasingly become a battleground for the country’s deepening political polarization. Conversations that once flowed freely are now stifled by the fear of explosive arguments, leaving many families to retreat into a self-imposed silence. According to a 2026 survey by Korea Research, a leading public opinion polling firm, 83% of South Koreans believe intergenerational conflict has reached a critical stage. This rift is not merely a generational gap; it is a manifestation of structural economic inequality and a media landscape that thrives on amplifying division.

The Domestic Toll of Political Tribalism

The family, once the primary unit for social and political education, has become a front line where different "truths" collide. In households across the country, political affiliation has superseded kinship. A mother in Seongnam, a satellite city near Seoul, described the familiar scene: avoiding political news or changing the channel whenever a broadcast comes on to prevent an argument from ruining the holiday meal. This is not just about choosing sides; it is about the erosion of mutual trust.

Even among families aligned with the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country's main liberal opposition party—tensions run high. Some members argue that public criticism of political leaders by figures like writer Rhyu Si-min is counterproductive and damages the party's approval ratings. Others, however, demand a more "vivid" and aggressive form of oversight, accusing the party of being too passive in the face of perceived government failures. This cycle of finger-pointing, where the source of one’s news—often fragmented YouTube channels—determines their perceived moral standing, has turned political debate into a zero-sum game of personal judgment.

Media critic Professor Jung Joon-hee has aptly categorized the psychology of political supporters into four distinct types. He argues that political entities make a fatal error when they attempt to consolidate all supporters into a single, monolithic block. There are the unconditional loyalists, the optimistic believers in the President’s good intentions, the "conditional" supporters who constantly pressure for reform, and those who have withdrawn their support but refuse to join the opposition. By treating these diverse groups as a single voting machine to be harvested, the current administration ignores the nuanced reality of its base, risking deeper internal fractures.

The Paradox of Presidential Communication

The dangers of ignoring these nuances were laid bare when President Lee Jae-myung’s X (formerly Twitter) account sparked controversy. Shortly after a press conference, the President’s account retweeted content that inadvertently included posts from accounts notorious for mocking and spreading hate speech toward specific individuals. While the President’s office later deleted the posts and issued a disclaimer—stating that it is impossible to vet every past post of an author being quoted—the damage was done.

What unsettled many was the President’s subsequent attempt to frame his supporters as "warriors of light" fighting for a better future. This rhetoric creates a dangerous binary, painting political battles as a struggle between good and evil. When a head of state adopts the language of internet fan culture, it legitimizes the "mud-slinging" that characterizes online discourse. As the old saying goes: "Getting even might feel good, but it is neither persuasive nor dignified." By failing to distance his administration from vitriolic online behavior, the President risks undermining the very reformist agenda he claims to champion.

Reform Under Fire: The Risks of Hesitation

The administration’s struggle to find a coherent message is further complicated by its inconsistent approach to reform. During a recent visit to France, President Lee warned against "excessive reform," citing the French Revolution as a cautionary tale where radical shifts led to reactionary backlash. While the message was intended to sound like a call for prudent, centrist governance, it sounded like a retreat to many who voted for him specifically for structural change.

The frustration is compounded by the administration's arbitrary use of "reform" as a justification. For instance, the government is pushing for a total survey of farmland to prevent speculation, essentially telling citizens that they must endure the discomfort and potential constitutional resistance for the sake of the greater good. Yet, when it comes to long-promised reforms—such as structural changes to the prosecution—the government suddenly pivots to fears of "backlash" or "destabilizing the system." This perceived double standard, especially when coupled with the appointment of former prosecutors to key investigative roles, has alienated many of the President's core supporters.

Economic Despair and the "I Don’t Know" Defense

While the political class debates ideology, the average citizen is facing a harsh economic reality. The "Jeonse" system—a uniquely Korean housing rental arrangement requiring a massive lump-sum deposit—is effectively vanishing. In its place, monthly rents in Seoul have skyrocketed by 150% to 200% in recent months, leaving many young people in a state of financial precarity.

The despair is most visible in the 2030 generation, who account for nearly 40% to 50% of personal bankruptcy filings. Many have been lured into high-risk leveraged ETFs and speculative assets in a desperate attempt to keep up with the cost of living. When the head of state, upon being asked about the plight of these young people, appears out of touch—or worse, dismissive—it signals a dangerous level of administrative incompetence. The "I don't know" defense, whether born of ignorance or strategic silence, is an affront to those struggling to survive the current housing and debt crises.

What to Watch Next

  • Policy Transparency: Watch for whether the government provides a clear, consistent standard for which reforms they will push through despite "resistance" and which they will delay.
  • Youth Economic Relief: Look for concrete measures beyond minor tax adjustments to address the specific debt crisis facing the 2030 generation.
  • Communication Strategy: Observe whether the President’s social media usage pivots toward more statesmanlike engagement or remains tied to the polarizing language of "warriors" and "opponents."

The government must realize that a mandate for reform cannot be sustained by rhetoric alone. If the administration continues to hide behind the shield of "stability" while the economic floor falls out from under the public, the trust that brought them to power will continue to erode, leaving behind not a "better future," but a more divided and desperate nation.
