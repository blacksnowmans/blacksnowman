The Seoul Housing Mirage: Why Seoul Apartments Cost More Than New York’s

October 12, 2026

If you have spent any time following South Korean news, you have likely heard the common refrain: apartments in Seoul are now more expensive than those in New York City. This is not merely idle gossip or an urban legend; it is a crushing reality that defines the survival of an entire generation. The dream of buying a home through disciplined saving has long since evaporated. Today, even for those who “borrow to the absolute limit”—a common practice known locally as yeong-geul—the goal of owning a home in the prestigious Gangnam district or even a modest villa on the outskirts of Seoul feels increasingly like a fool’s errand. How did the Korean real estate market, once heralded as an unstoppable engine of wealth, turn into such a monstrous burden?

The Myth of Reconstruction and the Vertical Slum Trap

For decades, the Korean public has operated under a powerful mantra: "reconstruction equals a jackpot." In the past, tearing down five-story low-rise housing to build 15- or 20-story complexes was a guaranteed path to profit. Owners would receive a larger unit and often even get a cash refund from the project’s surplus. This worked when the population was exploding and the economy was in a period of high-speed growth. Those days, however, are long gone.

Today, South Korea is grappling with world-leading low birth rates and near-zero economic growth. In this climate, the drive to push skyscrapers to 40 or 50 stories carries a hidden danger. As buildings grow taller, construction costs rise exponentially. Coupled with surging raw material prices and volatile exchange rates, the "additional contribution" required from apartment owners—often hundreds of millions of won (the equivalent of several hundred thousand dollars)—has skyrocketed. Owners in iconic complexes like the Eunma Apartment in Gangnam or the luxury clusters in Apgujeong are facing, or have already faced, bills exceeding $750,000 (1 billion won) just to move into their "upgraded" homes.

Many are forced to forfeit their properties because they cannot afford these fees. Furthermore, the math of future reconstruction is grim. As land shares per unit are diluted in these massive high-rises, the cost of rebuilding them 30 or 40 years from now will be astronomical, likely exceeding the value of the units themselves. The cold, hard truth is that today’s glittering skyscrapers may well become tomorrow’s "vertical slums"—decaying, high-maintenance monsters that no future generation will be able to afford to renovate or tear down.

The Truth Behind the Price-to-Income Ratio

Debates over Seoul’s housing market are often polarized. Some argue that compared to global hubs like New York or London, Seoul is still "reasonable." However, the most objective metric for evaluating market health, the Price-to-Income Ratio (PIR), tells a different story. The PIR measures how many years of total household income it would take to buy a home without spending a single cent on living expenses.

According to recent data from KB Kookmin Bank, the average apartment price in Seoul exceeds $1 million (1.4 billion won). With the average annual household income hovering around $45,000 (60 million won), the PIR sits at a staggering 23. This means it would take 23 years of total income for a family to afford a typical Seoul apartment. In comparison, the PIR is roughly 11 in New York, 8 in Los Angeles, and 12 in Japan. Given the overall size of the Korean economy, analysts suggest that Seoul's housing prices are inflated by at least double their fundamental value. We are living in a bubble that many have mistaken for reality.

This bubble has been partially masked by exchange rate fluctuations. As the value of the Korean won has softened against the dollar, foreign investors have found Korean assets to be "discounted." While domestic residents struggle under the weight of their own meager incomes, global investors see a different, more attractive valuation. This artificial demand, combined with domestic desperation, has created a house of cards that is incredibly fragile.

Policy Myopia and the Need for Tax Reform

The "First Generation" new towns—such as Bundang, Ilsan, and Pyeongchon—which were built in the 1990s as suburban paradises, are now facing the same aging crisis. While politicians, including those in the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, frequently promise "Special Acts" to accelerate the reconstruction of these areas, experts remain skeptical. The financial burden to rebuild these massive, aging cities all at once is simply not supported by the current economic reality. It is a promise made for election cycles, not for fiscal reality.

To fix this, the government must move away from the ineffective "demand-suppression" policies that have defined past administrations—and which were a hallmark of the failed real estate policies seen during the time of the incarcerated former leader, the disgraced Yoon Seok-yeol. Instead of stop-gap measures and transaction restrictions, South Korea needs a fundamental shift in its tax structure. Specifically, the country should adopt a system similar to the United States: significantly increasing holding taxes while lowering transaction taxes (capital gains and acquisition taxes). If holding a home becomes a transparent and consistent financial burden, the speculative hoarding that plagues the market will naturally dissipate.

What to Watch Next

The path forward requires the government to stop trying to force a "soft landing." History suggests that bubbles in markets driven by extreme human greed cannot be deflated gently. If the administration continues to inject liquidity to prevent any price correction, they will only be delaying a much more catastrophic collapse. Instead of focusing on massive private-sector high-rise construction, the government should pivot toward "land-lease" public housing—where the state retains ownership of the land and only the structure is sold, significantly lowering the barrier for the youth.

As a reader, monitor these two key indicators in the coming months:

  • Policy Shifts on Holding Taxes: Look for any serious movement to replace the current, punitive, and often arbitrary Comprehensive Real Estate Tax with a more predictable, universal property tax system.
  • Public Housing Initiatives: Watch how the Lee Jae-myung administration balances the interests of powerful construction conglomerates against the desperate need for affordable, land-lease public housing for the younger generation.

The era of "real estate never fails" is over. The coming years will likely be defined by a painful, necessary realignment of asset prices. For those currently trapped in the "borrow-to-the-limit" cycle, the wisest course is to recognize that they are navigating a market held together by hope and high-interest debt, rather than genuine economic growth.
