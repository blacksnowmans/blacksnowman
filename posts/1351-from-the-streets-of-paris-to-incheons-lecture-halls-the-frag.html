From the Streets of Paris to Incheon’s Lecture Halls: The Fragile Myth of Fairness in an Age of Discontent

October 20, 2026

The acrid scent of tear gas drifting across the Boulevard Saint-Germain in Paris is not an isolated European phenomenon. When hundreds of thousands of demonstrators take to the streets across France, shuttering transit networks and leaving storefronts boarded up from Marseille to Lyon, the immediate catalyst appears mundane: two years added to the legal retirement age. Yet, as luxury boutiques along Milan’s Via Montenapoleone are vandalized and high schoolers clash with riot police across southern Europe, something far more existential is rattling the continent. The social contract of the post-war era—the basic promise that working a lifetime guarantees a dignified retirement—is coming apart at the seams.

In Seoul, however, this transatlantic upheaval is reflected through an entirely different looking glass. Rather than examining the systemic failure of post-neoliberal economies crushed by post-war energy shocks and sky-high inflation, South Korea’s legacy media has largely reduced Europe’s rage to a cautionary tale about "welfare dependency." At the exact same time, a domestic scandal involving academic nepotism and elite privilege has erupted in South Korea, offering a bitter local counterpoint to the fury unfolding abroad. When viewed side-by-side, Europe’s burning streets and Korea’s quiet academic corridors tell the same unsettling story: the rules are rigged, and ordinary citizens are being asked to foot the bill.

Beyond the Retirement Age: Why Europe’s Streets Are Burning

To understand why an estimated 800,000 French citizens flooded the avenues of major cities, one must look beyond the simple actuarial math offered by French President Emmanuel Macron. The French government justified raising the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 by pointing to an impending annual pension deficit of up to €5 billion (around $5.4 billion) by 2030, arguing that longer life expectancies make the status quo mathematically impossible. Yet to the vast majority of working-class and middle-class citizens, this was nothing short of an economic ambush.

The outrage was compounded by an institutional maneuver that bypassed democracy altogether. Rather than risk a defeat in the National Assembly, the administration invoked Article 49.3 of the French Constitution—a contentious mechanism allowing the executive branch to bypass parliamentary approval and enact legislation by prime ministerial decree. While a subsequent no-confidence motion failed by a razor-thin margin, the procedural gambit shattered the administration’s democratic legitimacy. More than 70 percent of the French public opposed the measure, viewing it as an imperial decree handed down by a leadership unwilling to tax the ultra-wealthy or reign in corporate windfalls.

The pension fight is merely the tipping point. The structural foundation of European prosperity—built for decades on cheap Russian fossil fuels and inexpensive imported manufactured goods—evaporated in the wake of the war in Ukraine. As inflation soared and basic household staples like bread and heating fuel became luxury items, the state began pulling away the very safety net meant to cushion the fall. This desperation quickly leaped across borders. In Milan, a recent demonstration devolved into clashes that saw flagship stores like Chanel and Tiffany barricaded behind plywood, ending with 271 detentions—an astonishing 220 of whom were minors. When the young see no viable future within the existing economic order, public frustration inevitably sheds its peaceful veneer.

The 'Welfare Poison' Trap: How South Korean Media Misreads the West

For South Korean observers relying on mainstream domestic outlets, the nuance of Europe’s crisis was almost entirely invisible. Prominent conservative broadsheets, most notably The Chosun Ilbo, framed the French unrest with familiar ideological tropes, running headlines suggesting that decades of unchecked "welfare populism" had finally brought France to its knees. In their rendering, the protests were not a reaction to plummeting real wages and executive overreach, but the temper tantrum of an over-pampered workforce that refuses to accept economic reality.

This framing serves a very specific purpose within South Korean domestic politics. By sensationalizing the destruction of public property—such as the fire that damaged the historic bell tower of the Limoges city hall—conservative commentators seek to delegitimize the concept of social spending altogether. Any expansion of the social safety net is depicted as a direct path toward fiscal ruin and urban anarchy.

Furthermore, South Korean public discourse often clings to a hyper-moralistic standard of civil disobedience. Having witnessed the monumental "Candlelight Demonstrations" that peacefully ousted a corrupt administration without a single smashed window, many Korean commentators reflexively dismiss foreign unrest as inherently barbaric. Yet equating procedural decorum with moral superiority ignores why European protesters are so desperate. When formal institutional channels are shut tight and executive decrees replace legislative debate, polite dissent is often the first casualty.

Elite Entitlements and Salami Slicing: The Incheon National University Scandal

While Korean media outlets lectured European workers on fiscal responsibility, a much quieter, distinctly Korean breakdown of fairness was surfacing in higher education. The controversy centers on Yoo Dam, the 31-year-old daughter of Yoo Seong-min, a prominent former lawmaker from the conservative People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main opposition party.

In a higher education landscape where thousands of post-doctoral researchers spend a decade or more scraping by on low-paying temporary lectureships, Yoo achieved the extraordinary: she was appointed as a full-time assistant professor of international trade at Incheon National University just 75 days after obtaining her doctoral degree. During a parliamentary audit, lawmaker Jeon Seon-mi of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) revealed hiring records that defied standard academic logic:

  • A stark qualitative deficit: In the blind qualitative assessment of her research capabilities, Yoo placed near the bottom of the applicant pool, scoring a dismal 18.6 points (ranking around 16th).
  • A miraculous quantitative recovery: Despite the low qualitative score, she secured the professorship by receiving perfect marks in quantitative evaluations covering educational pedigree, publication volume, and career history.
  • The 75-day executive career: Her professional work experience amounted to precisely 75 days in a corporate strategy office—yet this brief stint was credited with maximum career points.
  • "Salami-sliced" publications: Yoo faced mounting accusations of carving up her Korea University doctoral dissertation into several smaller papers to artificially inflate her publication count—a practice known colloquially as "salami publishing."

The recruitment process itself was fraught with irregularities. Yoo had previously applied to the university's business administration department but was eliminated due to missing documentation. Immediately following that disqualification, the department’s hiring process was abruptly canceled. Shortly thereafter, the trade department published a tailored hiring notice that seemed custom-built for her credentials. Following a year-long investigation, police referred four individuals, including former lawmaker Yoo Seong-min and the president of Incheon National University, to the prosecution under the Improper Solicitation and Graft Act. The episode laid bare how the veneer of "quantitative meritocracy" can be weaponized to shield elite patronage.

Selective Justice: When the Law Operates on Political Fealty

What makes the Incheon National University case particularly combustible is South Korea’s recent history of prosecutorial crusades. A few years ago, the family of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk was subjected to dozens of coordinated raids and criminal indictments over academic credentials and an internship certificate awarded to his daughter. That high-profile investigation shattered reputations and sent family members to prison, heralded by prosecutors as a necessary cleansing of systemic unfairness.

Yet that prosecutorial zeal appears remarkably absent when applied to the conservative establishment. Han Dong-hoon, a prominent PPP lawmaker and former justice minister, faced serious public allegations regarding his own daughter's high school credentials, including allegations of ghostwritten research papers, extensive plagiarism in published math workbooks, and a questionable claim of 20,000 volunteer hours. The official response stood in jarring contrast to past probes: zero search warrants were requested, and the investigation quietly fizzled out after foreign authorities declined mutual legal assistance requests.

"Fairness that bends depending on who holds power ceases to be justice; it becomes an instrument of state violence."

This stark double standard is a direct institutional hangover from the era when Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol held sway over the state apparatus. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment and removal from office, utilized the state's prosecutorial machinery as a political battering ram. The selective application of the law—treating political adversaries with surgical ruthlessness while granting conservative royalty complete impunity—has hollowed out public faith in South Korea’s legal institutions.

What to Watch Next

The unrest in France and the patronage scandals in South Korea are two sides of the same coin. Whether in the form of street clashes over a stolen retirement or quiet fury over stolen academic opportunities, citizens across advanced democracies are reaching their limit with systems that protect the elite while demanding sacrifices from everyone else.

As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung pushes forward with domestic policy priorities, several critical fault lines will determine whether these structural grievances can be resolved through institutional means:

  • Prosecutorial Reorganization: Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party successfully implements structural reforms to dismantle prosecutorial overreach by dividing investigative and indictment powers between a Major Crimes Investigation Office and a separate Public Indictment Office.
  • The Incheon University Indictments: Monitor whether state prosecutors actually pursue formal indictments against Yoo Seong-min and university leadership following the police referral, or whether the case is allowed to quietly wither away.
  • South Korea's Own Pension Squeeze: With Korea aging faster than any other OECD nation, domestic debates over raising the National Pension contribution rates and retirement age are imminent. Policymakers in Seoul will have to decide whether they attempt a Macron-style top-down reform, or pursue an equitable compromise that avoids driving its own working class into the streets.
