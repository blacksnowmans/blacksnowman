Street Battles in Busan: Why South Korea’s Budget Season Just Collapsed Over a ‘Jailbreak’ Bill

November 15, 2026

On the rain-soaked plaza outside Busan Station on November 14, several hundred politicians and party loyalists gathered in yellow plastic ponchos, holding placards against the grey drizzle. At the microphone stood Jang Dong-hyeok, leader of the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition party. His tone was uncompromising. Accusing the government of destroying the constitutional order, Jang warned that power would have to be reclaimed from the administration, sounding a battle cry that carried across the coastal transit hub.

Busan, the historic industrial backbone of the conservative heartland in the southeast, was deliberately chosen as the launchpad for a nationwide series of outdoor protest rallies. Having found itself outvoted at every turn inside the National Assembly by the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the opposition has decided to take its grievances directly to the pavement. But this move has shuttered cross-party negotiations in Seoul precisely when the country can least afford it: in the middle of mid-November's high-stakes review of the national budget.

The Road to Busan and the Return of Asphalt Politics

To understand why South Korea’s conservatives have abandoned parliament for the streets, one must look at the mathematical reality inside the National Assembly. Following consecutive election defeats, the People Power Party holds a distinct minority against the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Inside legislative committee rooms, the conservative minority possesses virtually no procedural leverage to stop bills backed by the ruling party’s disciplined voting bloc.

When parliamentary debate offers no veto power, Korean opposition parties historically turn to what local commentators call "asphalt politics"—taking the fight to the public square to rally core supporters and generate enough outside pressure to shift the political momentum. Busan was merely the first stop. The party leadership announced that it will carry its roadshow next to Daegu on November 21, before marching through other major metropolitan centers across the peninsula.

The danger of this playbook, however, is that fiery rhetoric delivered to drenched party faithful in a plaza rarely leaves an easy off-ramp back to civil negotiation. During the Busan rally, conservative speakers did not merely critique policy; they issued direct warnings to the presidential office and called for an all-out struggle to strip the administration of its authority. By escalating the fight to an existential confrontation, the opposition has burned the bridges it needs to re-enter regular parliamentary talks.

The War Over the 'Fabricated Indictment' Special Counsel Bill

At the center of this bitter feud is a single piece of legislation pushed by the ruling Democratic Party: the so-called "Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel Bill." The ruling party frames the bill as an essential corrective measure. Under this legislation, an independent special prosecutor would be appointed to investigate allegations of politically motivated prosecutions and investigative abuses carried out during Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, when the state prosecutorial apparatus aggressively targeted opposition figures.

The People Power Party sees the measure in an entirely different light. Conservative lawmakers have branded the proposal the "Lee Jae-myung Jailbreak Bill," insisting it is an unconstitutional maneuvers tailored specifically to shield President Lee Jae-myung from his remaining legal hurdles and stalled criminal trials. In their view, deploying a legislature-mandated special prosecutor to overturn prosecutorial decisions and disrupt courtroom proceedings violates the fundamental separation of powers.

At the Busan demonstration, conservative rhetoric reached a fever pitch. Opposition figures warned cabinet members that any minister or prime minister who votes to approve the bill at a State Council meeting will face future criminal prosecution and imprisonment once the political winds shift. Han Dong-hoon, the former leader of the People Power Party, took to social media to demand that President Lee face personal responsibility for the bill rather than shifting the burden onto the Prime Minister and cabinet secretaries. By framing the dispute not just as bad policy, but as an unconstitutional crime, the opposition is pushing the administration toward a high-stakes standoff over whether the president will exercise his constitutional veto.

Abandoned Budgets and Skirmishes Over the Economy

The Democratic Party has hit back fiercely, seeking to frame the Busan demonstration as irresponsible grandstanding. Mid-November is typically the most grueling and vital stretch of the legislative calendar in Yeouido, Seoul's political district. Both the standing committees and the Special Committee on Budget and Accounts must work around the clock to scrutinize the multi-hundred-billion-dollar national budget before statutory deadlines expire in early December.

Ruling party spokespeople accused the conservatives of ditching their constitutional duty to examine the livelihood budget simply to stage an outdoor political theater designed to mock the head of state. In their framing, the opposition has chosen to hold public finances hostage while the working class suffers under persistent inflation and sluggish growth.

Yet the confrontation has not stayed confined to procedural complaints; it has spilled over into economic anxiety. When President Lee Jae-myung remarked on recent volatility in the domestic stock market by noting metaphorically that "when the mountains are high, the valleys are deep," conservative leaders pounced. At the Busan rally, opposition figures accused the president of treating the suffering of retail stock market investors—known locally as "ants"—with airy indifference, offering philosophical platitudes while individual portfolios bleed.

The Democratic Party countered that the market slump reflects broad macroeconomic headwinds and structural problems inherited from previous governance, dismissing the opposition's attacks as cheap opportunism. Nonetheless, the clash over market instability reveals how quickly raw political animus latches onto pocketbook frustrations to score points outside parliament.

Tit-for-Tat Probes and Deepening Institutional Distrust

As the conflict widened, the combatants turned their fire toward courtroom fairness and personal integrity. On November 14, an online skirmish erupted between former conservative chief Han Dong-hoon and Democratic Party lawmaker Choi Min-hee. The two tangled over the contrasting treatment of current and former leaders in the justice system.

Han questioned why the legal trials of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ousted via impeachment and is currently serving time in prison—moved with rapid speed, while legal proceedings involving President Lee Jae-myung appear to have ground to a halt or faced prolonged delays. Choi defended the divergence, arguing that the cases against Lee were built on malicious, politically motivated overreach by state prosecutors, rendering any direct comparison invalid. Han dismissed the explanation as clumsy double standards, snapping that digging in on untenable claims simply looked ugly.

Simultaneously, the People Power Party attempted to expand the battlefield beyond the single special counsel bill by demanding counter-investigations. Seeking to dent the moral standing of the Democratic Party, conservative leadership called for a separate special counsel probe into alleged ties between prominent liberal figures and religious movements, specifically naming Shincheonji and the Unification Church. Conservative lawmakers also demanded inquiries into leaked investigative requests concerning the National Health Insurance Service’s special judicial police powers.

The ruling party dismissed these demands as desperate diversions with no factual foundation. Yet the strategy underscores a depressing pattern in contemporary Korean statecraft: whenever one side introduces an exceptional investigative mechanism to bypass ordinary law enforcement, the other side responds with demands for rival investigations of its own, turning the rule of law into a weapon of reciprocal destruction.

What to Watch Next

The sudden move from committee rooms to street rallies leaves the South Korean political landscape in an exceptionally brittle state. Over the coming weeks, several concrete flashpoints will determine whether this confrontation breaks the legislative calendar completely:

  • The Daegu Rally on November 21: Watch the scale and tone of the People Power Party’s second major regional stop. If turnout is strong and rhetoric hardens further, the conservative leadership will have little incentive to negotiate an orderly return to the National Assembly.
  • The Statutory Budget Deadline: The National Assembly faces an official deadline in early December to pass the next year's government budget. If budget committee reviews remain boycotted or paralyzed by floor fights, the government will face the real threat of an end-of-year fiscal logjam.
  • The Floor Vote and the Veto Question: With its solid legislative majority, the Democratic Party retains the power to force the Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel Bill through the plenary session unilaterally. If passed, all eyes will turn to the State Council and the presidency, where a decision to enact the law—or veto it—will provoke immediate legal and political warfare.

For international observers, the standoff in Busan is a stark reminder that South Korea's sharp partisan divide is no longer confined to parliamentary debate. When judicial decisions, criminal indictments, and the national budget all become subordinate to the battle lines of competing rallies, institutional compromise becomes almost impossible to sustain.
