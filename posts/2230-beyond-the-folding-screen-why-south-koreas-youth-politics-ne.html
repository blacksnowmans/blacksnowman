Beyond the Folding Screen: Why South Korea’s Youth Politics Needs a Budget and a Vote

November 11, 2026

Imagine a South Korean election campaign in full swing. The streets are filled with neon-colored windbreakers—the bright blue of the ruling Democratic Party and the deep red of the opposition People Power Party. On the campaign trucks, standing just behind the seasoned veterans of the National Assembly, you will almost always see a handful of twenty-somethings. They are the "Young Talent" recruits, brought in to signal that the party is modern, inclusive, and focused on the future. They smile, they bow, and they represent the "new blood" that every politician claims the country desperately needs.

But once the ballots are counted and the campaign trucks are packed away, these young faces often vanish. In the brutal world of Yeouido—Seoul’s political district—these young recruits are frequently described as byung-pung, or "folding screens." Just as a decorative screen provides a nice backdrop for a room without serving any structural purpose, youth politicians are used to decorate the stage for the party establishment. When the show is over, they are folded up and put into storage until the next election cycle.

For a country facing a demographic cliff and a growing disconnect between the aging political elite and a struggling younger generation, this "ornamental" approach to politics is no longer sustainable. If youth politics in South Korea is to survive, it must move beyond biological age as a credential. It requires a fundamental shift in the "operating system" of Korean parties: specifically, the creation of independent youth delegate systems, the granting of real budgetary authority, and a move toward grassroots, policy-driven training. Without these systemic changes, "youth politics" remains a hollow marketing slogan.

The Cycle of Recruitment and Disposal

The core problem lies in the "black box" of political entry. In the Democratic Party (the current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung) and the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition), the path for a young person to move from an activist or a volunteer to a decision-maker is entirely opaque. There is no standardized "ladder" of progression. Instead, there is a "parachuting" system where a few lucky individuals are hand-picked by party leaders to run for office, while thousands of others who have worked in local committees for years are ignored.

This lack of transparency breeds deep cynicism. When a young person enters a party, they are often relegated to a "Youth Committee" that has no real power. They might spend years organizing rallies or managing social media, only to see a celebrity or a high-profile activist "parachuted" into a plum nomination because they look good on a poster. This creates a culture of "line-standing," where young aspirants focus more on gaining the favor of a powerful faction leader than on developing policy expertise or serving their local constituents.

Moreover, when these hand-picked "youth icons" do make it into the National Assembly, they often find themselves isolated. Without a base of support within the party or a track record of legislative success, they are easily pressured to fall in line with the party's elderly leadership. They become the "young face" for old ideas, further alienating the very voters they were meant to attract. To break this cycle, the party must move from "recruiting" talent to "cultivating" it through a transparent, merit-based system.

From Ornament to Decision-Maker: The Youth Delegate System

The most significant proposal to fix this is the creation of an independent "Youth Delegate System." In the current Korean party structure, delegates—the people who vote on party rules and leadership—are largely selected based on their loyalty to local committee chairs. While there are quotas for youth, these young delegates are often just "additional votes" for whatever the party leadership has already decided.

A truly reformed system would see the creation of a separate Youth Delegate body with the power to set "Party Doctrine" on issues specifically affecting the younger generation. Instead of thousands of people gathering for a symbolic vote, this body would engage in intensive, policy-driven debate. Most importantly, decisions made by this youth body should be binding. If the Youth Delegates agree on a policy regarding housing or labor reform, the party’s leadership and its lawmakers in the National Assembly should be constitutionally required to pursue it as official party policy.

This would transform youth politicians from "petitioners" into "decision-makers." Currently, young members have to beg the party leadership to listen to their concerns. Under a delegate system with teeth, the leadership would have to negotiate with the youth wing. This is not a radical idea; several successful European parties operate with autonomous youth wings that serve as a check on the main party. By embedding this into the party rules (the dang-won-dang-gyu), the ruling Democratic Party could ensure that youth influence doesn't evaporate every time there is a change in leadership.

The Four-Billion-Dollar Mirage: The Need for Budgetary Autonomy

Power in politics is inseparable from the power of the purse. Currently, South Korea allocates a staggering amount of money to "youth policies." Between various government agencies and local municipalities, the total "youth budget" is estimated at over 6 trillion KRW (approximately $4.3 billion). However, much of this money is a mirage. It is often tied up in bureaucratic red tape or spent on "one-off" events and "awareness campaigns" that do little to change the material reality of young lives.

In fact, many youth organizations struggle to actually spend even 100 million KRW (about $72,000) on meaningful, self-directed projects because they lack the authority to execute the budget themselves. They must constantly seek approval from older bureaucrats or party officials who may not understand the nuances of the problems the money is meant to solve.

The solution is the establishment of a "Youth Party within the Party," modeled after experiments seen in smaller groups like the Reform Party (a centrist party often associated with younger voters). This would involve taking a fixed percentage of the party’s state subsidies and membership fees and handing it directly to the youth wing. This "Youth Party" would have the autonomy to design its own programs, hire its own staff, and—crucially—be audited on its performance.

Learning how to manage a budget, prioritize spending, and take responsibility for financial outcomes is the best possible training for a future lawmaker. A politician who has never managed a $100,000 budget is ill-equipped to oversee the trillion-dollar national budget. By giving young politicians real money to manage, the parties would be creating a class of "practical" leaders rather than just "vocal" ones.

Building Legitimacy in the Trenches

While systemic changes are necessary, young politicians must also prove their worth through "fieldwork." One of the most effective ways to do this is through initiatives like "Ordinance Competitions." In Seongnam, a major city in the Seoul Capital Area, local councilors like Lee Semi have advocated for programs where young residents and aspiring politicians identify specific local problems—like a lack of street lighting or inefficient waste disposal—and draft the actual legislative language (the ordinance) to fix them.

This does two things. First, it provides a "survival manual" for how government actually works. Second, it builds a track record of "political efficacy." When a young politician can point to a specific law they wrote that improved their neighbor's life, they earn a level of legitimacy that a "hand-picked" nomination can never provide.

Another proposal involves "Youth Field Teams" tasked with handling civil complaints. In Korea, "civil complaints" (min-won) are the lifeblood of local politics. However, there is a danger here: if a young person is sent to a market to listen to a shopkeeper’s complaints but has no power to fix them, they are seen as nothing more than a messenger for the "real" politician. For these field teams to work, they must be backed by the authority of sitting National Assembly members. The senior politician must use their power to execute the solutions identified by the youth team, creating a collaborative ecosystem where the young identify the problems and the elders provide the legislative muscle.

What to Watch Next

The test for South Korean youth politics will not be found in the speeches given at the next party convention, but in the fine print of the party rulebooks. As the Democratic Party settles into its role as the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, observers should watch whether the "Innovation Committees" (Hyeok-sin-wi) actually propose changes to the delegate system and budget allocation.

Specifically, look for the following indicators of real change:

  • Amendment of Party Rules: Watch for the formal institutionalization of a "Youth Party" with its own independent budget (a percentage of the party’s state subsidy).
  • Local Committee Reforms: See if the parties move away from "parachute nominations" in favor of a "step-by-step" track that requires service at the local and provincial council levels before running for the National Assembly.
  • The "Self-Correction" Test: Pay attention to how young politicians handle internal party criticism. True growth will be signaled by youth leaders who have the "courage to be disliked" by the party establishment, but also the humility to admit when their own policy proposals are flawed.

South Korea’s political system is currently designed to preserve the status quo, even as the country's demographic and economic foundations shift. The "folding screen" era of youth politics must end. If the parties are not willing to share real power—and real money—with the next generation, they may find that when they finally decide to look behind the screen, there is no one left to take their place.
