South Korea’s Sinking Approval Ratings Point to a Broken Political Class

September 01, 2026

Picture yourself checking your brokerage account on a crowded morning subway in Seoul, watching leveraged exchange-traded funds bleed value while inflation quietly eats away at your grocery budget. For many South Koreans in their thirties, the daily reality feels less like a modern developed economy and more like a low-intensity financial warzone. In this climate, politics is rarely discussed with intellectual detachment. It is felt directly in the pocketbook, and right now, the verdict coming from the public is overwhelmingly grim.

Recent polling numbers have laid bare a deep-seated frustration with the entire political establishment in Seoul. Rather than inspiring voters with visions of economic growth or social progress, both the conservative administration and the liberal opposition find themselves bogged down in historic levels of unpopularity. For outside observers trying to understand the pulse of East Asia’s third-largest economy, these numbers reveal more than just fleeting discontent—they signal a profound crisis of confidence in South Korea’s democratic institutions.

The Cruel Statistics of Public Discontent

To understand the current mood, one must look closely at the numbers behind the recent approval ratings. President Yoon Suk Yeol’s administration—led by the conservative People Power Party—has seen its positive job evaluation hover around a dismal 36.2%. More alarming for the ruling camp is the cold shoulder turned by voters in their thirties, where approval sits at just 27.3%. These are citizens grappling with real-world financial pressures, and their economic anxiety has translated directly into a harsh political judgment. When personal balance sheets are shrinking, praise for national leadership evaporates quickly.

Equally striking is the erosion of traditional voting blocs. Even in historically loyal regions and among the core demographic groups of the 40-50 age bracket, majority support has fractured. Political parties can no longer rely on entrenched regional loyalties or generational habits to secure ballots. Today’s electorate responds in real-time to individual policy failures and economic shocks, abandoning traditional alliances with a sharpness that leaves strategists scrambling.

The Rise of the Anti-Government Mood and Bipartisan Unpopularity

When pollsters ask voters how they view the upcoming 2028 legislative elections, nearly half—49.9%—frame their perspective around the need to punish the ruling government. This overwhelming sentiment for a "judgment frame" suggests that a significant portion of the electorate has already made up its mind: before debating policy efficiency or long-term national goals, many voters simply want to hit the brakes.

Yet, the most fascinating twist in contemporary South Korean politics is that the main liberal opposition, the Democratic Party of Korea, is hardly reaping the rewards of this anger. In fact, public disfavor toward the Democratic Party has climbed to 44.6%, even surpassing the ruling party’s negative rating of 40.1%. While liberal lawmakers likely view themselves as the rightful instruments of public oversight, ordinary citizens increasingly view both major political factions as equally deserving of rebuke.

This dynamic has turned national politics into a race to the bottom, where voters are no longer asking which party is better, but rather which is slightly less objectionable. The exhaustion among the electorate is palpable, fueling a dangerous sense of political alienation.

Legislative Machinations and the Battle Over Breach of Trust Laws

Nowhere is this self-serving political calculus more evident than in the recent wrangling over legal reform. The government and ruling party previously pushed for revisions surrounding the criminal charge of breach of trust—a corporate and legal concept central to ongoing high-profile corruption trials, including the sprawling Daejang-dong development scandal linked to opposition figures.

When the Democratic Party abruptly shifted gears and pumped the brakes on these proposed legal changes, it did little to convince the public of its legislative diligence. Instead, the about-face was widely interpreted as a panicked maneuver to protect party leadership from potential legal fallout. When fundamental legal principles are bent or defended solely based on partisan survival, it confirms the worst suspicions of a cynical public: that lawmakers care far more about court outcomes and political shielding than about the livelihoods of ordinary citizens.

This kind of strategic backtracking accelerates the perception of a government and parliament sliding into a premature lame-duck phase. When the political class appears entirely consumed by self-preservation, the gap between the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido and the daily struggles of the street widens into an unbridgeable chasm.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward future electoral showdowns, several concrete indicators will determine whether this political stalemate deepens or breaks:

  • The Center-Ground Shift: Watch the movement of independent and moderate voters, whose recent defection accounts for the sharpest drop in approval. Their willingness to swing away from both major parties is the ultimate barometer of government legitimacy.
  • Substantive Policy vs. Legal Drama: Pay close attention to whether lawmakers pivot back to bread-and-butter economic issues—such as household debt, retail inflation, and real estate—or remain bogged down in retaliatory legal battles.
  • Upcoming Electoral Framing: Monitor how third-party or emerging political movements attempt to capture the growing segment of the population that feels orphaned by both the conservative and liberal mainstays.

Ultimately, the simmering anger of the South Korean electorate is not tethered to a single party. As politicians of all stripes prioritize their own survival over public welfare, the reckoning will inevitably catch up to them. The question is no longer which faction holds the upper hand in the polls, but rather which political leader will finally wake up and speak the language of real life before the system reaches a breaking point.
