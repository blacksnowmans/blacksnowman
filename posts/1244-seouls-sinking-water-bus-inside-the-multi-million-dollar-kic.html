Seoul's Sinking Water Bus: Inside the Multi-Million Dollar Kickback Scandal on the Han River

October 16, 2026

For decades, the wide, grey ribbon of the Han River has defined the geography of Seoul, cutting South Korea's sprawling capital in two. It carries bridges choked with rush-hour traffic and banks lined with manicured parks, but its waters remain remarkably empty. Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a heavyweight in South Korea’s conservative People Power Party, vowed to change that with a flagship policy: the "Hangang Bus," a fleet of public commuter catamarans designed to ferry commuters across the water and transform Seoul into a riverfront metropolis. The plan was flashy, highly publicized, and driven by an uncompromising target date of October 2024.

Instead of a triumphant launch, the Han River water bus has collided with a severe political and legal storm. Behind the sleek promotional renderings lies a web of suspicious public procurement, allegations of a 1.5 billion won (roughly $1.1 million) kickback scheme involving a municipal housing authority, and the bewildering selection of a paper-thin manufacturing firm that had never built a single ship. With the project now delayed until next spring and opposition lawmakers demanding criminal probes, the Hangang Bus has turned from a showcase of municipal innovation into a case study in what happens when political deadlines overpower administrative due diligence.

The Mayor’s Rush and the Ousting of a Qualified Builder

The trouble began with an artificial deadline. When Mayor Oh announced the river transit initiative, City Hall circled October 2024 on the calendar. Seoul officials later justified the schedule with an almost whimsical rationale: the river gets too chilly by November, and the city wanted passengers boarding while the autumn weather was still pleasant. But shipbuilders operate on structural physics and supply chains, not seasonal marketing windows.

In February 2024, the Seoul Metropolitan Government initially contracted a seasoned regional shipbuilder based in Sacheon, South Gyeongsang Province. That firm, an established mid-sized builder with annual orders exceeding 8 billion won (around $6 million), had already spent months working on designs. The original production schedule required delivering two vessels by late September and the remaining six by late October. Yet City Hall’s Future Hangang Project Headquarters abruptly moved the goalposts, demanding that all eight 199-passenger vessels be fully completed and ready by early October.

The builder pushed back. Constructing passenger craft certified for public transit requires meticulous aluminum fabrication, specialized systems integration, and strict safety testing. Compressing the timeline by weeks risked catastrophic failures on the water. Rather than negotiate a realistic launch date, Seoul chose to terminate the arrangement and shop the contract elsewhere, prioritizing a rigid political calendar over manufacturing reality.

A Paper Company Wins a $12 Million Contract

To replace the experienced yard, the project’s executing agency—the Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation (SH Corporation), a municipal public enterprise best known for urban redevelopment and public housing—turned to an obscure newcomer called Gadok Heavy Industries. Incorporated on December 22, 2023, the firm was barely three months old when SH Corporation handed it a 17.16 billion won (approximately $12.5 million) deal in March 2024 to build six commuter catamarans.

The company had no track record whatsoever. It had registered zero shipbuilding orders in its brief existence. More astonishing still, state health insurance and pension records revealed that Gadok Heavy Industries did not employ a single worker when the contract was signed; its first five employees were officially enrolled only in early April, weeks after the deal was inked. When investigative journalists from South Korean broadcaster MBC visited the firm’s registered address in Busan, they found no dry docks, no gantry cranes, and no welders. Instead, they discovered a shed-like building adjacent to a single-story private residence, containing a solitary desk and a lone computer.

SH Corporation justified its choice by claiming that out of 45 shipbuilders surveyed, only two claimed they could meet the October deadline, and Gadok submitted the lower bid. But the firm possessed no actual manufacturing capacity. By June 2024, an internal inspection conducted by the vessel operating firm painted an alarming picture: piles of raw aluminum plates sat untouched, with the production progress rate hovering near zero percent. To fabricate anything at all, the startup had spun a fragile web of subcontractors, farming out electrical, structural, and outfitting work to more than 40 separate outside vendors. A critical piece of metropolitan transport infrastructure had been entrusted to an empty shell.

The Missing $1.1 Million and the Cover-Up Allegations

How an unvetted startup with no workers and no machinery secured a $12.5 million public contract became clearer in early September 2024, when an internal report from Seoul City’s own Audit Committee surfaced. Financial reviews of Gadok’s project disbursements flagged approximately 1.5 billion won (about $1.1 million)—roughly 8.7 percent of the entire contract—in unaccounted outlays.

The municipal audit team concluded that these funds were not legitimate operational expenses. Investigators characterized the money as intermediary broker fees and kickbacks funneled to an influential official inside SH Corporation in exchange for steering the contract to Gadok. The audit division formally drafted an internal referral requesting a full-scale police investigation, armed with witness statements, recorded phone conversations, and specific payment trails.

Yet that request never reached law enforcement. Instead, the explosive document stalled inside City Hall. During recent parliamentary audit hearings, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party—now holding greater oversight leverage under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration—alleged that city leaders deliberately buried the findings. Lawmaker Kim Sung-whan cited internal reports indicating that directives to discard related audit files were issued after Mayor Oh received two in-person briefings on the matter. Lawmaker Park Joo-min blasted the administration, warning the case resembled an orchestrated administrative cover-up to protect senior municipal figures.

Testifying before the National Assembly, Mayor Oh acknowledged directing officials to investigate the allegations strictly, but repeatedly deflected specific questions about the kickback reports, stating he could not recall the details. When pressed on the allegations of document destruction, city officials insisted the records remained intact and that internal reviews were continuing. But for the project's critics, the gap between finding a million-dollar illicit commission and failing to notify the police for over a year told its own story.

Bureaucracy in Denial: A Hollowed-Out Audit Request

Under mounting public pressure earlier this year, the Seoul Metropolitan Government claimed it would clear the air by asking South Korea’s supreme auditing body, the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), to conduct a public-interest audit of the project. That move, however, swiftly backfired.

The BAI summarily dismissed Seoul’s request without an investigation. The reason, revealed during parliamentary questioning, was striking: City Hall’s official petition to the state auditor completely omitted any mention of the 1.5 billion won kickback allegations. The city had formally requested an inquiry into administrative delays while excising the core bribery scandal from the paperwork. While municipal spokespersons argued that unproven suspicions could not be included in the formal filing, lawmakers condemned the maneuver as a deliberate attempt to secure a hollow administrative clearance while keeping investigators away from the financial trail.

Meanwhile, the inevitable reality of the timeline caught up with City Hall. Gadok failed to deliver the fleet by October. The launch has now been postponed to March 2025, with two hulls tentatively promised for late autumn and four more by year's end. The city cited the need for pilot runs and enhanced safety testing, but the public rationale rang hollow after months of insisting the boats would be skimming across the river by autumn.

What to Watch Next on the Han River

The Han River bus project is no longer just a troubled transit initiative; it is an active political liability for one of the conservative opposition's most visible leaders. Several critical fronts will determine where the scandal goes from here:

  • Formal Police and Prosecutorial Inquiries: With parliamentary records now public and the opposition pressing hard, external law enforcement agencies face growing pressure to seize financial records, trace the 1.5 billion won in broker transfers, and interrogate the implicated SH Corporation leadership.
  • Structural and Safety Verifications: Even if the six catamarans are delivered this winter, they face rigorous technical scrutiny. With work scattered across 40 disparate subcontractors, marine safety authorities and Seoul City Council committees will be scrutinizing the hulls for manufacturing shortcuts and structural defects.
  • Political Fallback for City Hall: Mayor Oh built his municipal platform on ambitious waterfront revitalization projects. With national politics firmly dominated by the ruling Democratic Party, opposition lawmakers are likely to use the water bus controversy to challenge the integrity of municipal contracting across Seoul's broader development agenda.

When public transit projects are bent around political timetables, engineering discipline is almost always the first casualty. As the Han River heads into winter, the waters remain quiet—and the questions surrounding the boats supposed to sail them are only growing louder.
