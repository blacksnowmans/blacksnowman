The Intellectual Surgeon: Why Rhyu Si-min’s Criticism of President Lee Jae-myung Matters

November 02, 2026

Imagine a digital square in Seoul, packed not with protesters, but with 110,000 concurrent viewers on a YouTube livestream. The air is thick with tension as Rhyu Si-min, arguably South Korea’s most influential progressive intellectual and a former Minister of Health and Welfare, takes the microphone. For years, Rhyu has been the ideological shield for the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). But today, he is being called a traitor by the very people he once led. The rumors are swirling: has Rhyu Si-min turned "Anti-Lee"? Has he abandoned President Lee Jae-myung?

To an outside observer, this might look like a standard political spat. However, in the context of South Korean politics, Rhyu’s words carry the weight of a tectonic shift. As President Lee Jae-myung’s administration grapples with stagnant approval ratings and internal fractures, Rhyu’s recent critique is not a declaration of war, but a desperate diagnostic report from a surgeon trying to save a patient on the operating table. Understanding this moment requires peeling back the layers of South Korea’s current political landscape, where the stakes involve nothing less than the survival of the progressive movement.

The "Anti-Lee" Frame and the Weight of Loyalty

The term "Ban-myeong" (Anti-Lee Jae-myung) has become a lethal label within the Democratic Party. It is used to purge dissenters and silence those who question the President’s current trajectory. When Rhyu Si-min recently voiced sharp concerns regarding the administration’s handling of public sentiment, conservative media and radical segments of the DPK were quick to slap this label on him. The narrative was simple: the intellectual architect of the movement had finally jumped ship.

Rhyu’s response was both chilling and clarifying. "If I were truly 'Anti-Lee,' it wouldn't end at this level," he remarked during a recent broadcast. For those who know Rhyu’s history, this is not a boast but a statement of fact. Rhyu is known for his "all-or-nothing" approach to political combat. If he intended to destroy the President’s standing, he would not be offering advice; he would be leading the charge for a replacement.

To understand why Rhyu is still firmly in the President’s corner, one must look back to the 2022 election. When Lee Jae-myung narrowly lost his first bid for the presidency, Rhyu Si-min was seen on camera with tears in his eyes, telling Lee, "I love you." He spent the subsequent years defending the President against legal risks, famously comparing the prosecution’s investigations to a "targeted execution" and telling the party not to worry about "decorum" when the opposition is "coming at you with a jackknife." His current criticism is an extension of that protective instinct. It is the "bitter medicine" offered by a comrade who sees a cliff ahead that the leader refuses to acknowledge.

A New Grammar for Public Sentiment: Person, Path, and Policy

Why is the administration struggling? President Lee Jae-myung currently faces a daunting approval rating that hovers around 37.3%, while his disapproval rating has surged past 60%. Rhyu Si-min argues that the government is misreading these numbers. Most pollsters look at specific issues—real estate prices, inflation, or foreign policy—to explain the dip. Rhyu, however, proposes a more sophisticated framework of analysis: the three layers of 'Person, Path, and Policy.'

He draws a historical parallel to the late President Roh Moo-hyun. During Roh’s term, his policy approval was often low, but his "Person" approval—the public’s fundamental trust in his character and sincerity—remained high among his base. This trust acted as a safety net. Rhyu’s warning today is that for President Lee, all three layers are simultaneously under threat.

  • Person: Is the President still perceived as the humble "survivor" who fights for the common man, or has he become distant and arrogant in power?
  • Path (Line): Is the government still following the democratic and progressive values it promised, or has it veered into a pragmatic vacuum?
  • Policy: Are the actual laws being passed improving daily life?

Rhyu’s diagnosis is that when a leader loses the "Person" and "Path" layers, the "Policy" layer doesn't matter. Even a good policy will be viewed with suspicion. He has urged the President’s office to conduct rigorous, internal polling to find out exactly where the disconnect lies, rather than simply dismissing the numbers as "unreliable."

The Danger of "Echo Chamber" Speakers and Internal Isolation

Perhaps Rhyu’s most controversial take is his criticism of the "Pro-Lee Speakers"—the YouTubers and influencers who claim to be the President’s strongest defenders. In South Korea’s hyper-polarized digital landscape, these figures wield immense power. However, Rhyu argues that they are paradoxically isolating President Lee Jae-myung.

These influencers often engage in "witch hunts" against anyone within the party who offers a different perspective, labeling them as "Subak" (Watermelons)—a derogatory term for those who are "green" (Democratic Party color) on the outside but "red" (conservative color) on the inside. Rhyu pointed out that by attacking internal moderates, these speakers are shrinking the President’s tent. "The more they active they are, the more isolated the President becomes within his own camp," Rhyu warned.

"A leader should not be the head of a fandom, but the leader of a nation. When the space for internal democratic discussion is destroyed by aggressive supporters, the leader eventually finds himself standing alone on a shrinking island."

This internal fracturing has led to the rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Rhyu describes this new party as a "lifeboat." Supporters who are frustrated with the DPK’s internal bickering and the President’s perceived lack of direction are jumping off the "mother ship" (the DPK) to find refuge in the lifeboat. While the lifeboat is currently popular, Rhyu warns it cannot cross the ocean. The mother ship must be repaired if the progressive movement is to survive the next general election cycle.

The Courage of the Intellectual and the Demand for Truth

Rhyu Si-min did not hold back when discussing specific figures within the party leadership, such as Representative Park Sun-won. He criticized the tendency of some politicians to rely on conspiracy theories or baseless attacks to rally the base. Rhyu invoked the motto of Lee Hae-chan, a titan of the Democratic Party: "Truthfully, Sincerely, and Urgently" (Jin-seong-jeol).

He argued that politics based on falsehoods or "marketing" will eventually collapse. For Rhyu, the role of an intellectual is to expand the boundaries of what can be said. He noted that by being the first to speak these uncomfortable truths, he hopes to give other commentators the "courage" to speak up. "When you try to shut one person's mouth, two more will open," he said, signaling that the era of blind obedience to the party line must end for the sake of the party's health.

This commitment to truth is reflected in Rhyu’s personal life. Despite the massive success of his broadcast, which generated roughly $26,000 (about 35 million KRW) in "Super Chat" donations in a single session, Rhyu refused to take a penny in appearance fees. He directed the money toward the production of better programs and educational retreats for young supporters. He remains a full-time writer, earning 90% of his income from book royalties, currently working on a new manuscript about "Rebellion" set for release next spring.

What to Watch Next

Rhyu Si-min’s intervention has sent shockwaves through the Blue House (the President’s office) and the DPK headquarters. His "bitter medicine" is a signal that the honeymoon period for the Lee Jae-myung administration is officially over, and the era of critical partnership has begun. Here is what to watch for in the coming months:

  • The "Person" Rebrand: Watch if President Lee Jae-myung shifts his communication style to be more inclusive and less reliant on his core "fandom." Rhyu’s advice suggests the President needs to regain his image as a sincere "man of the people."
  • Internal Purge or Peace: Will the DPK leadership continue to allow radical influencers to attack internal critics, or will they take Rhyu’s advice and open up a space for "rational dissent"?
  • The Lifeboat Effect: Keep an eye on the polling for Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party. If it continues to rise at the expense of the DPK, it will be a clear sign that Rhyu’s "mother ship" analogy was correct and that the DPK is in deep structural trouble.
  • The Spring Book Release: Rhyu’s upcoming book on "Rebellion" is expected to be more than a history lesson; it will likely be a veiled philosophical critique of how power is maintained and lost in modern Korea.

Rhyu Si-min’s message to the supporters who are currently feeling "desperate enough to leave the party" was one of profound comfort through clarity. He didn't tell them everything was fine; he told them exactly why things were going wrong and how they could be fixed. In the volatile world of Korean politics, that kind of honest diagnosis is often more valuable than a thousand campaign slogans.
