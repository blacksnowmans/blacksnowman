Beyond the Goal: What Son Heung-min Can Teach Us About Living with Diabetes

October 16, 2026

When we watch Son Heung-min—South Korea’s captain and a global football icon—sprint past defenders or thread a perfect pass, it is easy to get caught up in the scoreline. But for those looking past the highlight reels, Son’s career longevity offers a masterclass in something far more vital: the fundamental integrity of the human body. His ability to maintain elite performance at his age is not a result of raw talent alone, but of a rigorous, disciplined approach to maintaining his lower-body strength and spinal health. It is a lesson that holds profound implications for anyone managing chronic conditions like diabetes.

Lessons in Movement from the Pitch

The connection between elite sport and everyday health is often overlooked. Recently, football commentator Park Moon-sung, who has spent six years as a promotional ambassador for 'KFHI' (Korea Food for the Hungry International), a major South Korean non-profit organization, highlighted this link through his humanitarian work. By organizing youth football tournaments for children from impoverished regions across 10 countries, Park has witnessed firsthand how physical activity acts as a foundation for broader human potential.

This is where the parallel to Son Heung-min becomes clear. Son’s recent performance—a one-goal, two-assist masterclass that propelled him to the top of the league’s assist charts—is fueled by the same "honest" physical exertion that Park advocates. For individuals struggling with diabetes or sedentary lifestyles, Son’s game is not just entertainment; it is a blueprint. Football requires 90 minutes of constant core stabilization and lower-body engagement. If a player relies on the structural integrity of their spine and legs to perform at the highest level, surely the rest of us can adopt similar principles to reclaim our health.

The Diabetes Dilemma: Diet and the Cost of Care

Managing diabetes is often a psychological battle as much as a biological one. The fear of carbohydrate-induced glucose spikes creates a restrictive, often joyless lifestyle. However, the goal of management should not be total deprivation, but finding a sustainable "palatable balance." Many patients fall into a cycle of frustration and burnout when they view their diet strictly through the lens of restriction rather than nourishment.

This challenge is compounded by the financial realities of modern treatment in South Korea. There has been significant buzz surrounding new, highly effective weight-loss and diabetes-managing injections (often colloquially referred to as "forty injections" due to their approximate monthly cost of 400,000 KRW, or about $300). For many, these medications are life-changing, yet the system is failing them. These drugs are often excluded from private health insurance coverage, and patients face high out-of-pocket costs that rise as dosage requirements increase.

The current policy landscape, under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, faces criticism for treating these medical necessities as aesthetic options rather than life-saving interventions. When the state treats a chronic, debilitating condition with such administrative distance, the burden falls entirely on the individual. Until the government expands insurance coverage and subsidies, patients are left navigating a medical system that often prioritizes bureaucracy over human well-being.

Building a Foundation: Strength and Stability

To combat the systemic hurdles, we must focus on what we can control: our physical foundation. Falls are a primary threat to health in later years, and a simple, highly effective test for stability is the "one-leg balance." Research suggests that the inability to stand on one leg for 10 seconds—with eyes closed and arms folded—is linked to a higher mortality rate within a seven-year period. This simple movement, which takes no time and costs nothing, wakes up the stabilizing muscles around the ankles and forces the vestibular system to sharpen.

Complementing this is the fundamental squat. For the modern office worker, shortened hamstrings and a sedentary lifestyle are recipes for back pain. The key is not to aim for professional-athlete intensity, but for consistent, controlled movement. Squatting while maintaining spinal alignment and keeping one’s head level helps engage the glutes and erector spinae muscles. If you are struggling, using a partner for support or relying on a wall can bridge the gap until your strength improves.

The Two-Minute Routine

Ultimately, health is not built in a gym once a month, but through small, daily investments. To address the stubborn "belly fat" that plagues many, simple rotational core exercises—engaging the obliques by shifting weight while keeping the core braced—can be done in just two minutes. It is about waking up muscle groups that spend the day in a state of atrophy.

The takeaway is clear: medication and medical interventions, while valuable, are only as effective as the physical foundation supporting them. Relying solely on injections without a commitment to movement is like building a house on sand. We must move beyond "surface-level" health management.

What to Watch Next

  • Policy Shifts: Monitor the National Assembly for potential debates on expanding national insurance coverage for advanced diabetes medications, as public pressure grows on the current administration.
  • Consistency Metrics: Start your "10-second one-leg balance" test today. Tracking your improvement over weeks is a more accurate indicator of your long-term health than any single medical check-up.
  • Lifestyle Integration: Look for ways to incorporate "micro-movements" into your workday, such as the two-minute core rotation routine, to combat the physiological impact of long hours spent sitting.

Taking care of your body is not an act of vanity; it is the fundamental price of maintaining autonomy over your own life. As Son Heung-min proves every time he steps onto the pitch, the most spectacular results are born from the most basic, diligent habits.
