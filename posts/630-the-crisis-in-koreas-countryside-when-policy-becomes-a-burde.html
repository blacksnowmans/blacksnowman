The Crisis in Korea’s Countryside: When Policy Becomes a Burden

October 03, 2026

Walking through rural South Korea today, you are just as likely to encounter crumbling, abandoned plots of land as you are active farms. As the nation grapples with a rapidly aging population and the slow death of its rural communities, the government’s response has shifted toward a controversial strategy: aggressive land surveys coupled with a push to convert farmland into solar power plants. While framed as a crusade against speculators, these policies are increasingly criticized for ignoring the practical realities of the people who actually work the soil.

The administration of President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's primary liberal opposition force—has placed the reform of farmland management at the center of its domestic agenda. However, for many farmers and rural observers, the policy feels less like a path to modernization and more like an out-of-touch bureaucratic intrusion that threatens food security and property rights alike.

The Illusion of Reform: Regulation Over Revitalization

To the foreign eye, the South Korean government’s current approach to "farmland reform" might sound like a necessary administrative update. In reality, it is being described by critics as a regressive throwback to the 1970s "Saemaul Undong" (New Village Movement)—an era defined by top-down, state-led rural transformation. While no one disputes the state’s duty to ensure land is managed transparently, the current obsession with nationwide surveys feels like a performative exercise in regulation rather than an effort to solve the systemic issues of rural life.

Farmers today are not crying out for more government surveillance; they are struggling with a labor shortage, rising operational costs, and the literal abandonment of the countryside. By framing the issue through the lens of "cracking down on land speculators," the government is successfully shifting the narrative away from its own failure to provide support. Instead of investing in advanced agricultural technology or higher-value crop production, the administration is focusing on control. This approach treats farmland not as a productive asset that needs nurturing, but as a secondary object to be monitored and repurposed according to state whim.

Solar Power: Innovation or Industrial Graveyard?

The most contentious aspect of the current agenda is the push for "agrivoltaics"—the practice of installing solar panels above active farmland. While the government presents this as a dual-benefit strategy that allows for both food production and energy generation, the implementation has been widely criticized as a "desk-bound" theory that ignores the physical realities of the grid.

The disconnect is staggering. Across the country, solar facilities have been installed at great expense, yet many sit idle—effectively expensive "scrap metal" in the middle of fields—because the local electrical grid is already saturated and cannot transmit the power produced. In one illustrative case in the Wolpyeong village of Yeongam, farmers invested in solar equipment only to find that the necessary connection infrastructure was never built. They were left with the debt and the equipment, but no way to export the energy.

This is not just poor planning; it is a fundamental misunderstanding of the energy transition. Forcing farmers to host solar arrays without first expanding the nation’s transmission capacity is not energy policy—it is a reckless experiment using the livelihoods of the rural poor as the test subjects. When the government spends billions of won (some projects costing millions of dollars) on infrastructure that cannot be used, it reflects a hollow commitment to the very farmers they claim to serve.

What is at Stake for Korea’s Future

South Korea’s food self-sufficiency rate is among the lowest in the developed world. Converting vast swathes of the country's limited arable land into power plants is, therefore, a strategic gamble that could have long-term consequences for national security. When the government treats farmland as a "playground for speculators" or a "parking lot for solar panels," it loses sight of the ultimate purpose of that land: feeding the nation.

True reform would start by addressing the gaps that actually keep farmers awake at night: labor volatility, price instability, and the decline of rural youth populations. If the government’s goal is "fairness"—a term President Lee frequently invokes—that fairness must begin by listening to those who work the land, rather than branding them as potential criminals or forcing them to pivot into industries they are ill-equipped to manage.

What to Watch Next

Moving forward, the success or failure of this policy will depend on two key factors that observers should watch closely:

  • Grid Infrastructure Expansion: Any meaningful shift toward solar energy in rural areas must be preceded by a massive, state-funded upgrade to the national transmission grid. If the government continues to approve new solar installations without addressing the "connection bottleneck," the resulting economic waste will likely become a major political liability for the DPK.
  • The "Speculator" Narrative vs. Reality: Watch for whether the government’s rhetoric on land speculation transitions into actual, transparent legislation. If the investigations continue to target small-scale farmers while appearing to ignore larger, politically connected entities, the public’s perception of "fairness" will erode further, potentially triggering organized pushback from rural advocacy groups.

Ultimately, the health of a nation is rooted in its soil. If the current administration continues to prioritize political slogans and industrial surface-level changes over the basic infrastructure and human needs of the rural community, the long-term impact on Korea’s food sovereignty may be irreversible.
