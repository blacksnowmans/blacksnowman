Five Buyers for 465 Homes: The Hidden Cost Cracking South Korea’s High-Rise Dream

November 22, 2026

In April, a developer opened applications for a brand-new 49-story apartment tower in South Chungcheong Province, west-central South Korea. On paper, the project followed a formula that had sold out housing complexes across the country for years: pitch the tallest building in town as a gleaming regional landmark, split it into family-sized units, and invite buyers into South Korea’s competitive presale lottery. When the application window closed for the 465 units on offer, only five people had signed up.

For decades, the phrase "tallest landmark in the area" acted as an automatic guarantee of demand outside Seoul. Even as South Korea’s housing market cooled in provincial cities, developers bet that buyers in their thirties and forties—the core cohort of first-time homeowners—would still chase skyline views and glossy model-home interiors. That April flop suggests the spell is breaking. Ordinary buyers are starting to look past the sales brochure and do the math on what it actually costs to keep a vertical tower running 15 or 20 years down the road.

Behind that near-empty presale lies a structural problem South Korea has spent twenty years deferring. While builders competed to push residential towers higher and sleeker, almost no one—not developers, not regulators, and not residents—priced in the cost of maintaining them. Now, as the country’s first generation of luxury skyscrapers hits its twenties and a second wave built around 2010 approaches middle age, the bill is coming due.

The 49-Story Loophole and the Shrinking Buyer

Spend a few minutes flipping through housing flyers across South Korea and one oddly specific number appears everywhere: 49 stories. A round 50 would look cleaner on a banner, yet developers routinely shave off a single floor. The reason is purely financial. Under South Korean building and fire laws, 50 stories is the threshold that turns a building into a legal "super high-rise." Crossing that line triggers far stricter safety requirements, including dedicated refuge floors and upgraded firefighting systems, which drive up construction costs and eat into sellable floor space.

Stopping at 49 stories keeps a building legally classified as a "quasi-super-high-rise," a category covering structures of 30 stories or more. Nationwide, South Korea now has roughly 6,500 residential and commercial high-rise buildings of at least 30 floors, along with about 150 apartment towers of 50 floors or higher. Yet fires and mechanical failures do not respect statutory cutoffs. Studies on high-rise fires point to five interlocking hazards—combustible exterior cladding, imperfect fire compartments, delayed alarms and evacuation plans, standpipes and emergency elevators struggling to deliver firefighters and water upward, and smoke-control failures. Removing one floor on paper does nothing to remove those physical risks for the families living inside.

What has changed just as dramatically is who is expected to pay for these towers. Early Korean skyscrapers, like the famous Tower Palace complex in Seoul’s affluent Dogok-dong neighborhood, were built with sprawling 80- to 120-pyeong residences (roughly 2,800 to 4,200 square feet) aimed at conglomerate executives and the ultra-wealthy. By contrast, today’s 49-story projects—especially outside Seoul—are carved into South Korea’s standard family unit of around 84 square meters (about 900 square feet) and marketed to heavily mortgaged salaried workers. The apartments shrank to middle-class proportions, while the infrastructure holding them up remained skyscraper-sized.

A Giant Machine, Not a House

Traditional South Korean apartment blocks of 15 stories or fewer sit firmly on the ground and rely heavily on municipal water and power pressure. A 40- or 50-story tower is a completely different species. As housing analysts in Korea put it, a high-rise is not really a house at all—it is a giant machine.

In South Korea, a reinforced concrete frame can last up to 80 years. Buildings do not fail because their concrete skeletons give out; they fail the way a body ages when its spine stays intact while its nerves, blood vessels, and organs wear down. Pipes, electrical networks, ventilation systems, and elevators all have fixed lifespans and must eventually be stripped out and replaced.

Upkeep costs also do not rise in a straight line with height—they accelerate. Beyond 25 stories, high-pressure pumps cannot push tap water all the way to the top in one shot. Towers require intermediate mechanical rooms and water tanks every 25 floors to re-pump and regulate pressure, adding constant electricity demand, pump wear, and plumbing stress. Meanwhile, buildings of 50 stories cannot be built with conventional thick load-bearing outer walls, which would need to be two to three meters thick at the base to support the weight. Instead, engineers hang glass-and-sealant "curtain walls" off a central structural core. Yet virtually no residential tower in Korea has a realistic plan or budget on file for how to repair or replace an aging glass facade two or three decades after construction.

There is an added friction built into the monthly billing. Upper-floor owners enjoy unobstructed views and higher resale premiums, while residents on the 20th floor of a dense 49-story complex often stare directly at the concrete wall of the next tower over. Because South Korean law assesses long-term repair fees strictly by floor area with no adjustment for height or view, low- and mid-floor households pay the exact same per-square-meter rate as penthouse owners—a recipe for bitter disputes once large repair bills arrive.

$14 a Month for a $23 Million Bill

Every apartment owner in South Korea pays a monthly contribution to a mandatory long-term repair reserve alongside utilities and building management fees. Tenants under Korea’s jeonse lump-sum deposit or monthly rent systems get those payments refunded by the landlord when they move out, while buyers often treat low monthly reserve fees as a selling point. Look at the actual engineering math for a skyscraper, however, and those low bills look less like a bargain and more like an accounting illusion.

  • The real cost of a tower: Maintaining a 60-story, 400-unit apartment building properly over 20 to 30 years requires roughly 32.5 billion won (about $23 million). Split across 400 households, that comes to 81 million won (about $58,000) per home—or around 250,000 won (about $180) every month for two to three decades.
  • The standard benchmark: Ordinary Korean apartment complexes benchmark around 580 won per square meter, or roughly 49,000 won (about $35) a month for an 84-square-meter unit—a quarter to a fifth of what a true high-rise needs.
  • What people actually pay: Across South Korea, the actual average collection is just 239 won per square meter, or roughly 20,000 won (about $14) a month for a standard family unit—less than one-tenth of what a skyscraper’s upkeep demands.

You do not have to wait for a theoretical future to see that math collapse. You can see it in Gangnam. Dogok-dong’s Tower Palace—South Korea’s flagship first-generation luxury skyscraper—recently crossed the 20-year mark, when elevators reach their legal and mechanical replacement deadline. Replacing the complex’s 47 elevators alone came with a contract price of roughly 9.7 billion won (about $6.9 million). Total long-term repair reserves saved by Tower Palace residents over the previous 20 years amounted to 10 billion won (about $7.1 million).

After paying for nothing more than the elevators, the wealthiest high-rise address in the country was left with just 300 million won (about $215,000) in its reserve fund—with aging pipes, HVAC machinery, and exterior repairs still ahead. While replacing a single elevator in a 15-story building costs 70 million to 100 million won ($50,000 to $70,000), high-rise units run 300 million to 1 billion won ($215,000 to $715,000) each, and ultra-high-speed observation elevators like those in Seoul’s 63 Building or Lotte World Tower Signiel cost 3 billion to 4 billion won ($2.1 million to $2.9 million) apiece. In northern Seoul’s Junggye-dong, residents at one 26-story complex already got a taste of deferred maintenance when 10 of its 17 aging elevators were ordered shut down, leaving a man in his seventies to climb 24 flights of stairs with his young granddaughter.

Passing the Bomb—and the Risk of Vertical Slums

Why don’t apartment complexes raise monthly reserves now while the fix is manageable? Under South Korea’s Multi-Family Housing Management Act, reserve collections and spending are supposed to be disclosed on K-APT, the national apartment management database. Yet a comprehensive audit of K-APT filings by Kim Ji-ho, head of the data firm iStory, found that while complexes log routine cash deposits and withdrawals, actual multi-year repair schedules specifying which equipment will need replacement and at what cost are practically blank.

The catch is governance. Reserve rates are set not by builders, but by each complex’s Resident Representative Council. Current owners routinely resist paying 200,000 won or more a month for replacements scheduled 20 years in the future when they plan to sell within ten. Worse, everyone on the council knows that printing a 200,000-won repair fee on the monthly bill hurts resale prices and tarnishes the building’s prestige. So residents and managers look the other way—cashing out at a good price and passing a ticking financial bomb to whoever ends up holding the keys when the machinery fails.

When a building’s reserves run dry, the shock can wreck an entire local market. After the 2021 collapse of the coastal Surfside condominium in Florida, the state passed laws forcing older high-rises to fully fund safety repairs and reserves. Owners accustomed to modest monthly dues were suddenly hit with special assessments running into the equivalent of hundreds of millions of won per household, triggering a wave of distressed sales that froze the market.

In South Korea’s provincial cities, the long-term risk is even starker. In prime Seoul districts, steady capital inflows and affluent buyers can still paper over steep repair bills. In regions without deep office demand—such as the forest of residential skyscrapers packed onto reclaimed land at Busan’s Marine City—aging towers face a real threat of "slumification," the urban decline seen decades ago when Seoul’s pioneering 1968 Sewoon Sangga luxury complex lost its wealthy residents to Gangnam and slid into rapid decay. Unlike 15-story 1970s complexes in Gangnam such as Apgujeong Hyundai or Daechi Eunma, which have struggled for decades just to organize redevelopment, a 49- or 60-story tower already built to its maximum floor-area ratio cannot be profitably torn down and rebuilt 50 years later. Too expensive to repair and unprofitable to rebuild, an underfunded skyscraper has nowhere to go.

What to Watch Next

The April presale flop in South Chungcheong suggests buyers outside Seoul are beginning to price in that trap before signing contracts. Over the next several years, four concrete indicators will show whether South Korea confronts the problem early or lets it break into the open:

  • The 2030–2033 aging wave: Second-generation high-rises built around 2010 are now 15 to 16 years old. Between 2030 and 2033, when they hit the 15-to-20-year replacement window en masse, watch for a spike in elevator shutdowns, pipe failures, and emergency special assessments.
  • Tower Palace’s next repair bill: With 97% of its 20-year reserve spent on elevators alone, how Dogok-dong’s flagship handles its remaining mechanical and facade replacements will set a precedent for every high-rise behind it.
  • More empty 49-story lotteries: Watch regional presale numbers. If 49-story towers split into 84-square-meter middle-class units keep drawing single-digit applications, developers may finally be forced to rethink the regulatory-avoidance skyscraper model.
  • K-APT enforcement: Unless housing regulators compel Resident Representative Councils to file real, itemized engineering schedules on K-APT—and fund reserves accordingly—the gap between 20,000 won a month and reality will only widen.
