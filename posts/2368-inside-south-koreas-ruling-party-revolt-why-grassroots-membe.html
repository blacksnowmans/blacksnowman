Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party Revolt: Why Grassroots Members Are Suing Their Own Leadership

November 16, 2026

In late-night verification spreadsheets and small legal war rooms across Seoul, more than a thousand dues-paying members of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) have been checking bank transfer receipts against court filing lists. Their target is not the conservative opposition, nor the remnants of the previous administration. They are taking one of their own party’s senior leaders to court.

To outsiders, the timing looks baffling. Having defeated the conservative camp and propelled Lee Jae-myung into the presidency—while Yoon Suk-yeol sits in prison following his impeachment and removal from office—the DPK holds the commanding heights of South Korean politics. Yet beneath the surface of governing power, the party is fracturing. According to a sharp critique circulating among grassroots activists and political commentators in the party’s reformist wing, the Lee administration and DPK leadership have begun sidelining the very supporters who carried them to victory, trading structural reform for internal control ahead of the 2028 parliamentary elections.

Whether one views this conflict as a necessary growing pain of a ruling party pivoting toward the political center or as a betrayal of its founding promises, the dispute has moved past angry message boards. With a preliminary injunction filed against senior party figure Rep. Kim Min-seok, an ethics probe targeting a prominent reformist lawmaker, and an organized grassroots “rectification movement” taking shape, the struggle inside the DPK will determine what kind of governing party South Korea actually has.

The Closed-Door Remarks That Triggered an Ethics Purge

The flashpoint came from a private meeting in the Seoul suburb of Namyangju. Last month, Rep. Choi Min-hee—a DPK lawmaker known for leading the party’s aggressive campaigns on broadcasting and media reform, including high-profile battles over the Korea Communications Commission—met behind closed doors with local party members. According to accounts of the gathering cited by her defenders in Korean commentary, Choi spoke candidly about the agonizing political calculations party leaders faced during Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office.

Among the remarks leaked from that meeting were comments suggesting that the president himself had once opposed impeaching Yoon Suk-yeol on the grounds that the party needed to win over moderate voters to capture the presidency and retake power. Almost immediately after those words surfaced, party leadership moved against her. At the instruction of Rep. Kim Min-seok, Choi was referred to the DPK’s Ethics Inspection Team, with plans to send her case to the party’s Ethics Tribunal, accompanied by pressure on her to leave the party voluntarily.

To Choi’s supporters and grassroots critics of the leadership, the reaction was wildly disproportionate—and historically dishonest. To understand why, foreign readers need a brief look back at the chaotic aftermath of South Korea’s late-2024 martial law crisis. Under South Korea’s constitution, passing an impeachment motion requires 200 votes in the 300-seat National Assembly. At the time, the DPK and allied opposition parties were unsure they could clear that 200-seat hurdle, fearing a political backlash and constitutional paralysis if an impeachment vote failed.

Instead of rushing straight to an immediate vote, party leaders initially staged street rallies in central Seoul’s Gwanghwamun Square demanding Yoon Suk-yeol’s resignation. On December 15, then-DPK leader Lee Jae-myung publicly proposed forming a “State Affairs Stabilization Consultative Body” uniting ruling and opposition parties with the government to manage the crisis. Party leadership also formally halted a push to impeach Prime Minister and Acting President Han Duck-soo, arguing that piling on another impeachment would trigger severe administrative chaos.

Grassroots commentators argue that Choi was merely recounting this documented history of strategic caution to sympathetic local members. By stripping away context and painting her as a traitor who criticized the president, critics contend, DPK insiders—amplified by major conservative daily Chosun Ilbo—have weaponized the party’s disciplinary machinery to intimidate internal dissenters. Those critics also point to a perceived double standard: insiders who previously weathered major controversies, such as former lawmaker Jeong Bong-ju, were shielded by party allies, while a frontline media-reform advocate is now facing potential expulsion.

Stalled Prosecution Reform and the Battle for 2028 Nominations

For rebellious rank-and-file members, the treatment of Choi Min-hee is a symptom of a broader retreat from the DPK’s core agenda. For years, dismantling the sweeping investigative powers of South Korea’s prosecution service—and shifting major probes to new institutions such as the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA)—has been the central promise of the progressive camp, especially after years of intense prosecutorial investigations during Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office.

Yet critics within the party’s own base argue that since President Lee Jae-myung took office at Cheong Wa Dae (the presidential office), momentum has stalled. Instead of using the administration’s early political capital to push reform bills through the legislature, presidential aides and new party power brokers are accused of slowing down out of fear of losing moderate voters in opinion polls. In the view of these dissenting commentators, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), now South Korea’s main opposition, is maintaining relevance not through its own political skill, but purely as a beneficiary of DPK supporters’ growing disillusionment.

What fills the vacuum left by stalled policy reform, critics say, is factional maneuvering for the 2028 general election. Under South Korea’s party system, securing a party’s nomination is often the decisive step toward a parliamentary seat, making control over nomination rules the ultimate prize in internal party politics. Grassroots commentators accuse emerging party factions—including a group dubbed the “New Lee Jae-myung” camp, the DPK first-term lawmakers’ caucus known as Deomincho, and the Democratic Innovation Council (Deominju Hyeoksinhoe)—of trying to sideline established reformist figures.

According to these critics, allies of the leadership have floated potential challengers in the constituencies of prominent outspoken lawmakers, including attempting to place former lawmaker Shin Hyun-hyung into the district held by Rep. Jung Chung-rae while steadily undermining Choi Min-hee’s local base. From the leadership’s perspective, broadening the party’s appeal and cultivating fresh faces may look like routine political renewal. To the party’s militant base, however, it looks like a coordinated effort to purge independent voices and build a loyalist nomination cartel ahead of 2028.

Taking the Leadership to Court: 1,083 Members Fight Back

Rather than accept those maneuvers quietly, dissenting members have turned to the courts. Led in part by attorney Lee Jae-il, 1,083 rank-and-file DPK members have joined a preliminary injunction lawsuit at the Seoul Southern District Court seeking to suspend Rep. Kim Min-seok from performing his party duties. Organizers scrambled to cross-check bank deposits against applicant paperwork ahead of a scheduled Tuesday press conference outside the courthouse.

The legal challenge centers on allegations raised by complainants regarding how party leadership power was consolidated during the party’s convention and how candidate nomination authority is being exercised. The frustration fueling the suit is blunt, captured in protest slogans cited in the Korean commentary:

“A president elected by deceiving the people, a president elected by deceiving party members and supporters, and Representative Kim Min-seok who defrauded party members.”

At the same time, a second legal front has opened around pro-reform media voices outside parliament. Kim Han-mae, head of the civic group Citizens’ Action for Judicial Justice, filed a complaint with the Suwon branch of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency targeting reformist outlet Sisa Tapa TV and attempting to implicate former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae. Supporters of the targeted figures note that the underlying allegations were already cleared without indictment three years ago, branding the renewed filing a politically motivated harassment campaign. In response, the reformist camp has filed countersuits accusing Kim Han-mae of false accusation and of violating South Korea’s Attorney-at-Law Act by allegedly acting beyond the bounds of a non-lawyer.

An Identity Crisis and What to Watch Next

Beneath the court filings and ethics referrals lies a deeper argument over the DPK’s political soul. Traditional party loyalists—the grassroots base rooted in the legacies of former progressive presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in—argue that they no longer recognize the coalition running their party. Some critics in the Korean commentary go so far as to claim that the “New Lee Jae-myung” leadership reflects an improbable, opportunistic alliance between the Seongnam-based Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance (historically linked in Korean political memory to far-left figure Lee Seok-ki) and conservative “New Right” elements, united mainly by a desire to hold power.

For these traditionalists, policy disputes—such as simmering controversy over the management and direction of roughly $130 billion (182 trillion won) in National Pension Service reserves—signal that establishment economic orthodoxy is quietly displacing the party’s progressive welfare identity. Most galling of all, grassroots members say, is watching the administration and party leaders label the very voters who delivered them the presidency as “hardliners” or “extremists” whenever they demand accountability.

In response, dissenting members are rallying behind a grassroots “rectification movement” set to build around December 25, aiming to reassert member sovereignty over party decision-making. Whether this rebellion forces a course correction or simply deepens the party’s internal fractures remains an open question. For observers watching South Korean politics from abroad, four concrete developments will signal where the ruling party goes from here:

  • The Seoul Southern District Court’s response: Whether judges entertain or dismiss the preliminary injunction filed by 1,083 DPK members against Rep. Kim Min-seok—and how Tuesday’s courthouse press conference reverberates inside parliament—will test whether internal disputes stay political or become entrenched judicial battles.
  • The Ethics Tribunal’s ruling on Rep. Choi Min-hee: If party leaders follow through with formal sanctions or expulsion over her Namyangju remarks, it could trigger wider defections or open defiance among reformist lawmakers.
  • Action on prosecution reform: Watch whether the Lee Jae-myung administration accelerates legislation regarding the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency to placate its base, or continues prioritizing moderate approval ratings.
  • Momentum of the December 25 rectification movement: If grassroots organizers sustain turnout and fundraising into the new year, DPK leadership may find it far harder to control nomination machinery heading toward the 2028 parliamentary elections.
