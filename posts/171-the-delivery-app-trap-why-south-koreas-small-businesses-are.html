The Delivery App Trap: Why South Korea's Small Businesses Are Working Harder and Earning Less

September 05, 2026

Picture the scene in a tiny neighborhood kitchen anywhere in Seoul. A smartphone chimes on the counter, signaling a new order for a steaming bowl of sundae gukbap (blood sausage soup) or a freshly made plate of kimbap. For the owner, who has been standing over a hot stove for ten hours straight, that sound is both a lifeline and a quiet source of dread. The daily sales tally flashing on the screen looks impressive, even reassuring. But when the owner opens the bank app at the end of the month to check the actual balance, a profound sense of emptiness sets in. Despite bustling kitchens and seemingly endless streams of incoming orders, the bank account barely moves.

This is the strange, hollow reality facing hundreds of thousands of independent merchants across South Korea. In a country where digital convenience is woven into the very fabric of daily life, ordering food through an app has completely displaced the traditional phone call or neighborhood stroll. Yet beneath the slick interfaces of dominant platforms lies a grinding economic paradox: the more popular a restaurant becomes on an app, the harder its owner has to work just to stay afloat.

To understand how South Korea's bustling food service sector ended up trapped in a cycle of high-revenue, low-profit exhaustion, we have to look past the marketing slogans of convenience and examine the hidden architecture of the modern delivery economy.

The Illusion of Top-Line Revenue

When starting a small restaurant or a corner snack bar, the math used to be relatively straightforward. An owner would calculate the cost of raw ingredients, add a reasonable margin for rent and utilities, factor in labor, and set a menu price. Today, however, running a food business in South Korea requires factoring in a constantly expanding category of invisible overhead known simply as "platform tribute."

Consider a standard dish priced at 15,000 won (about $11). To the casual diner browsing on a smartphone, that price covers the food. But for the merchant, a cascade of deductions begins the moment the order is confirmed. There is the base brokerage fee charged by the platform, payment gateway fees, delivery dispatch costs, mandatory advertising fees designed to keep the restaurant visible in search results, and the cost of discount coupons that owners feel pressured to issue just to stay competitive in a crowded digital marketplace.

For low-margin items like simple noodles or traditional soups, this structure turns high volume into a liability. The more meals an owner prepares and ships out, the deeper the losses mount because every single transaction chips away at an already razor-thin margin. Merchants often realize too late that gross revenue is a vanity metric, while net profit—the actual cash left at the bottom of the settlement statement—is the only number that matters for survival.

The Monopoly Pinch and the Cost of Visibility

Part of the problem is that consumer habits in South Korea have been thoroughly institutionalized. Why pick up the phone to call a local restaurant when a few taps on a smartphone can summon a meal within minutes? Faced with a customer base that refuses to browse outside of established digital ecosystems, restaurant owners have virtually no leverage.

Dominating the domestic delivery market are platforms like Baemin (Baedal Minjok, South Korea's dominant food delivery app, operated by Delivery Hero) and Coupang Eats (the food delivery arm of Coupang, South Korean e-commerce giant often styled as the country's Amazon). Even though a typical neighborhood restaurant might list on multiple apps, the vast majority of their digital business funnels through a single dominant player.

This creates a relationship that feels less like a business partnership and more like a hostage situation. No matter how unfair or burdensome the fee hikes become, an owner cannot simply switch off the app. Doing so would mean watching daily orders plummet by half overnight. The platforms draw their immense corporate power directly from that underlying fear of sudden obscurity.

The Myth of Symbiosis and Tiered Fees

In response to mounting public criticism and political pressure from lawmakers, major delivery platforms periodically introduce "coexistence plans," rolling out tiered commission structures that promise lower rates for lower-revenue merchants—such as dropping specific fees to single-digit percentages.

Yet independent merchants frequently find that these adjustments are little more than sophisticated shell games. Lowering one nominal percentage point often coincides with subtle increases in advertising costs, delivery logistics fees, or compulsory participation in promotional discounts elsewhere in the system. The beautiful corporate language of coexistence masks a bottom-line reality that has fundamentally not changed: the platform gets paid first, regardless of whether the kitchen makes a profit or absorbs a loss.

Even more insidious is how these digital middlemen sever the direct link between merchant and customer. When a regular diner orders through an app, their contact information, preferences, and loyalty belong entirely to the platform, not the restaurant. The merchant is reduced to an anonymous fulfillment center—a digital subcontractor barred from building a direct relationship with the very people keeping their lights on.

Reclaiming Control of the Kitchen

South Korea's delivery apps are not going away. If anything, their grip on consumer habits will only tighten as artificial intelligence and logistics automation advance. For independent business owners, surrender or quiet resentment are poor strategies for survival.

The necessary shift begins with perspective. Successful operators are learning to treat delivery apps not as the entirety of their business, but merely as one high-cost sales channel among several. Every customer acquired through an app must be viewed as an acquisition target to be migrated into a direct relationship. Forward-thinking owners are printing flyers for direct pickup, offering exclusive incentives for customers who order directly via phone or independent channels, and building their own customer databases.

Slowing down to recalculate the actual profit margin on every menu item is no longer optional. When up to 30% of gross sales vanishes into platform fees, delivery overhead, and taxes, the remaining income often falls well below South Korea's legal minimum wage for the owner's grueling hours.

What to Watch Next

  • Regulatory interventions: Watch for upcoming legislative hearings and antitrust rulings by South Korean authorities regarding platform commission caps and mandatory fee disclosures.
  • The rise of public alternatives: Monitor the adoption rates of government-backed local delivery apps (such as Public Delivery apps run by municipal governments), which attempt to bypass corporate monopolies with near-zero commission models.
  • Merchant coalition movements: Pay attention to organizing efforts by small business associations pushing for collective bargaining rights against tech platforms.

Ultimately, the kitchen belongs to the cook, not the software engineer. Until independent merchants across South Korea reclaim their own customer data and treat platform dependency with cold financial realism, the chimes of the smartphone will continue to ring out for a system where everyone profits except the person doing the cooking.
