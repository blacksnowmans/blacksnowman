The Intellectual Divorce: Why South Korea’s Liberal Dream is Fracturing

October 06, 2026

In the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, few moments carry as much weight as a former ally turning their back on a leader. This is not the standard "I am disappointed" tweet from a disgruntled voter. It is a public, agonizing admission from Professor Choi Bae-keun, a prominent intellectual who once staked his reputation on President Lee Jae-myung. Standing before the thousands he personally encouraged to support Lee, Choi has effectively declared: "I was deceived."

When someone who stood at the forefront of the campaign effort publicly walks away, it signifies more than a disagreement on policy; it marks a breakdown in the moral contract between a leader and his core supporters. As President Lee’s administration struggles with plummeting approval ratings, this rupture forces us to ask: Is this the natural evolution of power, or has the reformist vision that promised a "Great Harmony" (Daedong-sesang) fundamentally rotted from within?

The Anatomy of a Political Betrayal

Professor Choi was not a critic looking for a reason to attack. He was an architect of the movement. He served as a bridge between the candidate's lofty promises and the public's expectations, frequently appearing on broadcasts and in classrooms to champion Lee’s reform agenda. His exit is not a tactical maneuver; it is a desperate attempt to rectify what he now views as a profound error in judgment.

Choi’s diagnosis is chilling: The reformist goals that once defined the movement have been replaced by a quest for power that is unrecognizable from the blueprints he once discussed with Lee in private. When a leader pivots away from their founding principles, those who invested their political capital into that vision are left with more than just frustration—they are left with the fear that they have helped install a version of power they once fought against.

This betrayal is further underscored by the government's recent performance. Following an earnest-sounding apology during the Chuseok holidays—where the President admitted to failing the public’s desire for a more equitable society—the administration’s subsequent personnel appointments suggested that the apology was less of a transformation and more of a "delay tactic" intended to silence critics until the holiday news cycle passed.

📊 A Warning Sign in the Numbers

According to a recent Realmeter poll commissioned by Energy Economic News, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has sunk to a record low of 43.0%. Meanwhile, his disapproval rating has climbed to 54.1%. This downward trajectory has lasted five consecutive weeks, and the administration has yet to produce a credible plan to reverse the trend.

The 'Zombie' Party and the Revival of Old Guards

The most painful consequence of the administration’s drift is the unintended resurrection of the very political forces they once sought to overcome. By losing the momentum for genuine reform, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal opposition-turned-governing party, has become ineffective. In their incompetence, they have effectively provided an oxygen mask to "insurrectionist" conservative elements who should have been relegated to history.

At the center of this vacuum sits the current party leadership under DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok. Critics argue that the party has been reduced to a "zombie" state. Despite controlling the legislature, the DPK appears paralyzed, failing to offer a clear vision or act as a check on executive overreach. Instead, the leadership seems consumed by internal power dynamics, obsessively focused on positioning themselves for the 2030 presidential election rather than addressing the daily hardships of the citizenry.

When the governing party loses its ideological compass, it ceases to be a movement and becomes a collection of competing egos. For voters watching the two major parties descend into a familiar, unproductive cycle of fighting, the feeling is one of profound exhaustion.

The 'Mugeuk-pa' Label and the Death of Debate

When faced with internal dissent, President Lee’s administration has responded with a tactic that is increasingly common in polarized politics: labeling. The President recently took to social media to denounce his critics as the "Mugeuk-pa" (an umbrella term he used to describe "irresponsible extremists" or "unfounded agitators").

This branding exercise creates a dangerous paradox. In the same breath that the President calls for an end to "unsubstantiated claims and speculation," he engages in it himself by pathologizing his critics, dismissing their dissent as mere "selfishness" or a desire to "monopolize credit for reforms."

This has effectively ended internal debate within the DPK. Whenever a specific issue is raised—be it a member’s past career or their opposition to reducing prosecutorial powers—the administration’s response is rarely to engage with the facts. Instead, it relies on "attitude policing," labeling the dissenter as "extreme" or "divisive." By excluding anyone who dares to hold a dissenting view from the "core," the party has retreated into an echo chamber where only sycophancy is rewarded.

What to Watch Next

The danger for the Lee administration is that a room full of people who only agree with you is a room that is slowly emptying. When the "core" becomes too small, the leadership loses its ability to sense the actual temperature of the public.

  • The DPK’s Reconfiguration: Watch for whether grassroots members begin to demand a leadership change. If the current "zombie" status of the party persists, the upcoming general elections could serve as a devastating referendum on the party’s current direction.
  • The Intellectual Exodus: Professor Choi is likely not the last high-profile supporter to leave. Keep an eye on other academic and civil society figures who championed Lee’s 2022 campaign; if they continue to follow suit, it will signal that the ideological rot is reaching the foundation of the administration.
  • Policy Reversals: The administration may attempt a radical personnel reshuffle to appease public anger. However, without a fundamental change in the President’s attitude toward dissent, such surface-level changes are unlikely to win back the trust of the electorate.

Democracy functions not when a leader is perfect, but when a leader has the courage to treat dissent as a mechanism for correction. If the current administration continues to treat its own allies as enemies, it will not just lose an election—it will lose the very history it once promised to rewrite.
