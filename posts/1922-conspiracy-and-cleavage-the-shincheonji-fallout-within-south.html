Conspiracy and Cleavage: The Shincheonji Fallout Within South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 04, 2026

The air inside the closed-door general assembly of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) on November 3 was thick with a tension that had been simmering for three months. For weeks, the halls of the National Assembly had whispered of a "triple alliance"—a shadowy pact between "anti-Lee" factions, political separatists, and Shincheonji, a controversial religious group often described as a cult. But as Kim Min-seok, a senior leader within the DPK, stood up to speak, the bombshell he dropped was not a revelation of guilt, but a confession of a vacuum. There was no evidence. The conspiracy that had threatened to tear the ruling party apart during its most recent internal elections was, by the party’s own admission, a ghost.

For those outside South Korea, the mention of a religious group in a political internal dispute might seem like a secondary detail. However, in the context of the Democratic Party of Korea—the party of current President Lee Jae-myung—the "Shincheonji label" is the ultimate political scarlet letter. To be accused of conspiring with Shincheonji is not just a critique of policy; it is an accusation of betraying the fundamental values of the party and the public trust. The collapse of this theory has not brought peace; instead, it has ignited a secondary explosion of internal strife that threatens the stability of the ruling party’s leadership.

The Ghost of Shincheonji in Korean Politics

To understand why this matters, one must understand the weight the name "Shincheonji" carries in South Korean society. Formally known as the Shincheonji Church of Jesus, the group gained international notoriety in 2020 as the epicenter of South Korea’s first major COVID-19 outbreak. Domestically, however, it has long been viewed with deep suspicion due to its secretive recruitment tactics and alleged history of infiltrating social and political organizations to further its influence. In the hyper-polarized world of Korean politics, accusing an opponent of being backed by Shincheonji is a "megaton-grade" weapon designed to alienate them from the general electorate.

During the DPK’s recent primary process, a narrative began to take hold: that a "triple secret alliance" had been formed to undermine the leadership of Lee Jae-myung. The theory suggested that lawmakers who were not aligned with Lee (the "anti-Lee" faction) had joined forces with those seeking to split the party and were being buoyed by the organizational power of Shincheonji. This was not just a rumor discussed in dark corners; it was a primary talking point that influenced how party members viewed their own colleagues.

The Democratic Party, which currently holds the presidency under Lee Jae-myung and a significant majority in the legislature, is supposed to be focusing on a heavy domestic agenda. Instead, it spent three months chasing its own tail. The investigation into these claims was meant to provide a definitive answer, but the "no evidence" conclusion reached on November 3 has left many wondering if the investigation was a genuine search for truth or a convenient way to bury a smear campaign that had outlived its usefulness.

An Apology That Fueled the Fire

When Kim Min-seok bowed his head during the private meeting, admitting that the claims of a "triple alliance" lacked any concrete basis, he likely hoped to close the chapter. Reports from inside the room describe him as visibly emotional, his voice cracking as he apologized to those whose reputations had been dragged through the mud. But for those on the receiving end of the accusations, a private apology in a closed-door session felt less like a resolution and more like an insult.

The primary target of the conspiracy theory was Jung Chung-rae, a high-profile and often outspoken lawmaker within the party. Jung’s reaction to the apology was immediate and fierce. He argued that a "quiet" apology for a "loud" and public defamation was unacceptable. The logic is simple: the accusations were used to sway voters and damage political careers in the light of day, so the retraction must be equally public and carry tangible consequences for those who fabricated the claims.

"This is not something that can be 'settled' with a private apology at a party meeting," Jung reportedly told his colleagues. He has since demanded a formal public apology and accountability for what he labels "anti-party activities"—a term that, in the DPK, can lead to disciplinary action or even expulsion.

This conflict highlights a deepening rift within the pro-Lee Jae-myung camp itself. While both Kim and Jung are seen as loyalists to the President, the "Shincheonji incident" has created a internal hierarchy of grievance. It suggests that even within a unified ruling party, the temptation to use extremist rhetoric to marginalize internal rivals is a fire that is difficult to extinguish once lit.

The Failure of the Data and the "Blind-Box" Settlement

One of the most contentious aspects of this fallout is the party leadership’s handling of the "total member survey." Earlier this year, the DPK promised a comprehensive investigation into whether Shincheonji members had actually infiltrated the party ranks in specific regions. This was supposed to be the "smoking gun" or the "clearing of names."

However, during the November 3 meeting, the expected detailed report was missing. The leadership simply announced that the claims of mass infiltration in specific districts were "factually groundless" without providing the underlying data or the methodology of the probe. This has led to accusations of a "blind-box settlement"—a political maneuver where a conclusion is announced without the evidence being shown, leaving both the accusers and the accused in a state of perpetual suspicion.

For a party that prides itself on being "member-centric," this lack of transparency is a significant blow. If the party did the work and found nothing, why not show the numbers? If they found something but chose to hide it for the sake of "party unity," they are sitting on a ticking time bomb. The ambiguity serves no one but the party’s critics, who can now claim that the DPK is either incompetent in its investigations or conspiratorial in its cover-ups.

This matters for the broader political landscape because the DPK is currently the engine of the South Korean government. If the ruling party cannot trust its own internal data or its own colleagues, the friction will inevitably bleed into their legislative work. The "Shincheonji ghost" has effectively paralyzed the party's internal trust, making every future disagreement look like a potential secret plot.

The Opposition’s Counter-Attack and the Path Ahead

While the DPK brawls internally, the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—has sensed blood in the water. The PPP has wasted no time in pointing out the irony of a ruling party that spent months investigating a religious conspiracy theory of its own making. They have called for a formal investigation by state authorities into Kim Min-seok and others, arguing that if there was truly an attempt to link politicians to a cult without evidence, it constitutes a serious breach of election law and a move to deceive the public.

The PPP’s strategy is clear: paint the DPK as a party of "conspiracy theorists" who are more interested in factional infighting than in governing. This is a particularly sharp jab given the current political climate. The DPK is still navigating the aftermath of the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol 씨, the former president whose administration was often criticized for its own lack of transparency. For the DPK to now be accused of "manipulative politics" mirrors the very criticisms they once leveled at Yoon Suk-yeol 씨’s government.

For President Lee Jae-myung, this is a headache he does not need. His administration is attempting to push through critical budget bills and navigate a complex international environment. Having his top lieutenants at each other’s throats over a debunked cult conspiracy is a distraction that erodes his political capital. The longer this drags on, the more it looks like the DPK is struggling to transition from a "resistance party" to a "responsible ruling party."

What to Watch Next

  • The Demand for Public Recantation: Watch whether Kim Min-seok or the party leadership issues a formal, televised apology. If they refuse, Jung Chung-rae and his supporters may escalate their protests, potentially boycotting party functions or leadership meetings.
  • Disciplinary Proceedings: The DPK’s ethics committee may be forced to intervene. If the party decides to punish those who spread the Shincheonji rumors, it could signal a major purge of certain factions. If they do nothing, the "anti-Lee" factions may feel emboldened to use similar tactics in the future.
  • The PPP’s Legal Maneuvers: Look for the People Power Party to file formal complaints with the prosecution. This would move the conflict out of the party halls and into the courts, ensuring that the "Shincheonji" name stays in the headlines for months to come.
  • Legislative Impact: As the National Assembly enters the crucial end-of-year session, see if this internal rift causes delays in key bills. A divided ruling party is often unable to maintain the "party whip," leading to unexpected votes or stalls in committee.

The Shincheonji conspiracy was a fire started for short-term political gain during a primary battle. Now that the fire has burned through the party’s internal trust, the DPK is finding that putting it out is much harder than starting it. In the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, the truth often matters less than the damage caused by the lie.
