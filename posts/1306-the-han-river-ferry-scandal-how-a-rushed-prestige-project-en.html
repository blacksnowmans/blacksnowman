The Han River Ferry Scandal: How a Rushed Prestige Project Entangled Seoul’s Mayor

October 19, 2026

Picture a municipal inspection office inside Seoul City Hall. On the desk sits an internal dossier complete with call recordings, whistleblower statements, exact financial figures, and the full names of public officials. It details hundreds of thousands of dollars in illicit broker kickbacks surrounding one of the capital’s most heavily promoted public transit initiatives: the Hangang Bus, a commuter water-ferry network meant to glide along the Han River. Yet when confronted with the dossier, the man at the very top of the metropolitan government claimed he had only ever heard vague rumors on the grapevine.

Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a senior figure in the conservative People Power Party—South Korea's main opposition party—has spent years trying to position himself as a forward-looking visionary capable of leading the country. Central to that image has been an ambitious makeover of the Han River, transforming it into a hub of tourism and daily river transport. But investigative revelations and whistleblowers have instead turned the 15-billion-won (roughly $11 million) water-taxi and ferry venture into a sprawling corruption investigation, exposing kickbacks, unvetted contractors, shredded public documents, and an astonishing multi-million-dollar cash injection from a city agency that builds public apartments.

The Vanishing Memo: Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s Shifting Memory

At the center of the scandal is a fundamental question of political responsibility: what did the mayor know, and when did he choose to forget it? According to internal accounts, the head of Seoul’s municipal Audit Committee—a seasoned career civil servant rather than an outside political appointee—scheduled a private, one-on-one briefing with Mayor Oh. The audit chief brought an exhaustive report outlining roughly 320 million won (about $240,000) in suspected kickbacks, corroborated by transcripts of phone conversations and witness testimony.

When the scandal burst into public view, Mayor Oh first insisted he had merely caught wind of the issue through casual chatter. As scrutiny mounted and public officials confirmed the formal briefing had indeed taken place, his defense shifted to a more convenient middle ground: he acknowledged recalling the meeting, but insisted he had no memory of ever seeing a physical document. In South Korea's rigid bureaucratic culture, the idea that an audit chief would arrive at a private audience with the mayor to discuss a felony-level bribery racket without placing a single sheet of paper before him stretches credulity. The evasive explanation appears designed to insulate the mayor from legal charges of dereliction of duty, which would attach immediately had he officially received proof of wrongdoing and chosen to bury it.

The city's subsequent maneuvering only heightened suspicions. Seeking to clear the air, Mayor Oh’s administration announced it had voluntarily requested an audit from the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), South Korea’s premier state watchdog, and noted with triumph that the agency had promptly dismissed the petition. But the victory lap backfired spectacularly. Incensed by City Hall’s public spin, the BAI took the extraordinary step of issuing a public press release refuting the mayor's narrative. The watchdog revealed that the city’s petition was a toothless, single-page formality that omitted every substantive piece of evidence—stripping out all mentions of kickbacks, bribes, and the detailed findings gathered by its own internal auditors. By submitting an empty document designed to guarantee a procedural dismissal, the city administration sought a cosmetic seal of approval while actively keeping state inspectors away from the crime scene.

Paper Companies and Missing Docks: How 1 Billion Won Disappeared

If the bureaucratic response was clumsy, the underlying contract award was outright bizarre. When municipal planners originally surveyed the domestic maritime sector to identify shipbuilders capable of constructing the passenger ferries, they drew up a shortlist of 45 verified shipyards with the requisite infrastructure and naval engineering track records. However, these established shipbuilders balked at City Hall's unrealistically compressed delivery deadlines and strict safety specifications, warning that safe, high-grade vessels could not be rushed onto the water on such a breakneck political timetable.

Rather than adjust the rollout schedule, the project went to Gadeok Heavy Industries—a firm that was not even listed among the 45 qualified candidates. More extraordinarily, Gadeok had never built a single complete ship in its corporate history. It possessed neither the dry docks nor the specialized fabrication facilities necessary to construct large-scale passenger vessels. In the razor-thin margins of shipbuilding—where profits rarely exceed 3 to 5 percent even for seasoned yards—Gadeok undertook an economically absurd plan: subcontracting the vessel in disjointed blocks across different workshops, transporting the metal pieces, and assembling them piecemeal.

Investigative reports and audio leaks revealed why such an unqualified bidder crossed the finish line. In recorded phone conversations between an executive at Gadeok and a manager at the operating venture, the shipyard official admitted paying hefty broker fees after intermediaries showed up flashing the personal business cards of senior Seoul municipal project directors. The broker fee alone swallowed 320 million won (about $240,000). Another 330 million won (around $250,000) was channeled to an unregistered phantom entity named Seongwoo ENG under the guise of "sea trial consulting"—an outfit that promptly shut down and dissolved before the boats ever entered commercial service. Alongside further subcontracting leakages exceeding 200 million won, more than 1 billion won (roughly $750,000) in public funds vanished into shell companies and intermediary pockets before the project even launched.

Affordable Housing Funds for River Ferries: The 80 Billion Won Question

The dubious contract was only the opening chapter; the funding mechanism behind the project raised even sharper red flags. A venture of this nature typically relies on private operating consortiums or dedicated transport infrastructure budgets. Instead, the municipal administration turned to the Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation (SH Corporation), a city-run public enterprise established exclusively to build low-income public housing and undertake residential urban renewal for struggling tenants.

In a move that stunned policy analysts, SH Corporation extended an eye-watering credit line of between 80 billion and 90 billion won (approximately $60 million to $67 million) to finance the water bus scheme. The spectacle of a municipal public housing provider bankrolling an unproven maritime tourist project cannot be understood through standard commercial logic. It points to immense, top-down political pressure within City Hall. Mayor Oh needed vessels on the Han River to create a photogenic policy trophy before the next electoral cycle, and when qualified builders walked away and commercial financiers hesitated, captive municipal balance sheets were conscripted to bridge the gap.

Predictably, cutting corners on engineering and oversight led to operational trouble. During mandatory trial runs, the hastily assembled vessels suffered recurring mechanical defects and performance failures. Rather than serving as an efficient alternative transit corridor, the project began to look like an accident waiting to happen, threatening passenger safety long before paying commuters stepped on board.

Shredded Files and Stalled Probes: The Anatomy of a Bureaucratic Cover-Up

The legal aftermath has been marked by foot-dragging and alarming allegations of evidence tampering. Despite early internal alarms, local law enforcement investigations drifted for nearly a year and nine months. The Seoul Metropolitan Government requested a police inquiry only on a late Friday afternoon, after independent journalists began pressing officials with detailed queries on camera.

During the protracted delay, internal whistleblowers stepped forward with claims that working-level officials received explicit instructions to destroy project documentation, delete electronic files, and scrub paper trails connecting senior City Hall decision-makers to the procurement process. In a hierarchical civil service, mid-level section chiefs do not purge records of a flagship mayoral initiative on a whim; such destruction invariably reflects implicit or direct cues from the political leadership. If substantiated, orchestrating the destruction of state records elevates a contracting scandal into a serious criminal enterprise involving spoliation of evidence and abuse of authority.

What to Watch Next

The political context surrounding this scandal has shifted dramatically. Under President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the central government has pledged zero tolerance for municipal corruption and regulatory negligence, contrasting its stance with the lax administrative standards and political turmoil that defined the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol before his impeachment and imprisonment. For Mayor Oh, the capital's top elected conservative, the Hangang Bus saga has rapidly evolved from an ambitious showcase of riverfront urbanism into a critical legal and political hazard.

In the coming months, observers of South Korean municipal politics should keep a close eye on several key developments:

  • The money trail: Whether prosecutors can formally trace the 1 billion won ($750,000) siphoned through brokers and paper entities back to City Hall aides or campaign networks.
  • SH Corporation's board culpability: Potential regulatory investigations into whether the housing corporation’s executives committed breach of trust by lending tens of millions of dollars outside their legal mandate.
  • Whistleblower disclosures on document destruction: Forensic examination of digital servers and internal communications inside City Hall to establish whether the directive to destroy files originated from Mayor Oh’s immediate inner circle.
  • The fate of the fleet: Whether maritime safety regulators will permit the troubled vessels to operate commercially, or whether the entire initiative will be indefinitely docked as a costly monument to hurried political ambition.

South Korea’s modern history offers a painful reminder that whenever safety oversight, procurement integrity, and transparent administration are sacrificed for quick political trophies, ordinary citizens bear the cost. The unfolding investigation into the Han River will test whether the country’s oversight institutions can hold its most prominent urban administrators to account before those compromises lead to real harm on the water.
