The Way of St. James: Why Thousands are Walking 800 Kilometers Toward Themselves

September 07, 2026

There is a path that has cut through time for a millennium, yet instead of fading, it shines more brightly with every passing year. We know it as the Camino de Santiago, or the Way of St. James, the trail once walked by the Apostle James to spread the Gospel. Stretching 800 kilometers (about 500 miles) along the Camino Francés (the French Way), the journey is both a grueling penance and a festival of life, where every morning requires you to confront your own limits and extract beauty from the landscape beyond them. While the only certainty is the direction you are heading, every traveler finds a completely different universe reflected in the people they meet and the terrain they traverse.

This is not a vacation for sightseeing; it is something else entirely. Some come seeking answers at the edge of life’s precipice; others arrive to shed the heavy, accumulated weight of their daily routines. It is a place where "walking wounds" gather to form a solidarity of healing. Here is a record of that intense, silent journey.

The Philosophy of the Albergue

In every village along the route, you will find an Albergue—a pilgrim’s hostel. More than just a place to sleep, these are the essential infrastructure that makes survival on the trail possible. With an average cost of about $15 (20,000 KRW), these hostels prove that this path is meant to be accessible to everyone. Public albergues operate on a strict first-come, first-served basis without reservations, which creates an "accidental community." Pilgrims who move at their own unique speeds find themselves running into the same faces every evening at the edge of town.

Unlike modern travel, which prioritizes convenience, the Camino demands a different pace. The rule of staying for only one night and the reality of dormitory living with complete strangers force us to confront the freedom found in a "minimalist life." While some South Korean municipalities—inspired by the popularity of the Jeju Olle Trail—have attempted to bench-mark this system by adding services like luggage delivery, the true value of an albergue isn't the facility’s luxury. It is the unspoken distance we keep while silently watching over one another’s exhaustion.

The Culinary Resilience of Galicia

As you pass through the desolate Meseta plateau, your body begins to demand extreme levels of energy. This is where Sopa de Alubias (bean soup) becomes a savior. A hearty stew of kidney beans, chorizo, and vegetables, it is the optimal meal for protein recovery. As one pilgrim remarked, "You have to eat healthy food to walk the Camino." For those on the trail, this is not just a meal; it is a ritual to gain the momentum needed to reach the next destination.

Notably, the food here is often saltier than standard Spanish cuisine. The local chefs understand the massive salt loss that comes with sweating for hours on end. Dipping bread into the broth while sharing short, breathless conversations about the road ahead, pilgrims often find the confidence to declare, "The Camino is no problem!"

The Camino is a Process of Healing, Not a Gourmet Tour

While the trail is often marketed as a "culinary journey," the essence of the food here is actually a "history of hardship." Dishes like Cecina (smoked beef) or Botillo (pork sausage) were not born as luxuries; they were created during times of poverty to preserve precious protein. When you understand this context, the food of the Camino becomes more than just nutrition—it becomes a tool of empathy. You are essentially "chewing on" the resilience of the local people.

The Iron Cross: Laying Down Your Burdens

At an altitude of 1,490 meters stands the Cruz de Ferro, or the Iron Cross, the symbolic summit of the route. Here, many pilgrims stop to set down a stone they have carried from home. Watching a traveler place a stone inscribed with the names of his wife and grandchildren is more than just a gesture; it feels like a human struggle to heal personal pain and express love for family at the highest point on the map.

Many people walk this path carrying hidden wounds. The stories of those who weep for lost siblings explain why so many are willing to buy a plane ticket and throw themselves onto this rugged road. At the foot of the cross, these stones are no longer heavy. They are fragments of prayer, wishing for peace and comfort. The phrase "Leder zijn eigen Geluk" (Each to their own happiness) inscribed on a Dutch pilgrim's stone encapsulates the heart of the Camino. It is not a march where everyone must move at the same speed. Sometimes you leave a message in stones for a friend to find, and sometimes you must distance yourself from others to find your own pace.

The Gift of the Atlantic

In the village of O Cebreiro, we see the architectural ingenuity of the region in the Palloza—conical houses thatched with rye straw, built to withstand harsh winds and snow. Similarly, the Hórreo—granaries raised on stone pillars to protect crops from humidity—demonstrate how the ancestors engineered survival in a difficult environment. These are not merely structures; they are life-saving storehouses that have protected family tables for over 200 years.

As you reach the port city of Vigo, you encounter the unexpected: raw oysters. In a country that rarely enjoys raw seafood, Vigo’s oysters are a prized exception. Then there is the percebe (goose barnacle), the most expensive seafood in Galicia. To crack open the shell and taste the briny essence of the sea is to awaken the senses of a pilgrim exhausted by the miles. And, of course, there is the famous Pulpo a la Feira (octopus). Boiled, dipped nine times, and sliced with precision, the dish is a testament to the fact that honest time and patience transform simple ingredients into something profound.

The Camino de Santiago is not just an act of walking; it is a ritual of self-transformation. When pilgrims reach the end and receive their completion certificates, the tears they shed aren't just for the 800 kilometers covered. They are for the kind words from a stranger, the shared piece of empanada during a cold, hungry afternoon, and the realization that the suffering they left at the Iron Cross has finally been converted into "their own happiness."

What to Watch Next: If you are planning your own journey, observe how your physical pace changes as your mental state shifts. Do not obsess over the destination. Start by looking for the "local" in the food you eat and the "community" in the albergues you share. If you feel like you are moving too fast to notice the wildflowers, you are already missing the point of the road.
