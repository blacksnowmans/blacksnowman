South Korea Pushes Crypto Tax to 2029: Inside the National Assembly’s Fourth Retreat

November 12, 2026

Around 6:00 PM on November 11, the doors of a closed-door committee room inside South Korea’s National Assembly opened to reveal a rare moment of swift bipartisan harmony. The tax subcommittee of the Strategy and Finance Committee—the parliamentary body tasked with setting the nation's fiscal rules—had just reached a provisional agreement. After hours of quiet deliberation, lawmakers from both the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the main opposition People Power Party agreed to accept a government proposal to delay the country’s long-debated cryptocurrency capital gains tax by another two years.

For millions of retail investors in one of the world's most active digital asset markets, the decision offered an immediate sigh of relief. But for market observers and tax policy experts, the agreement marked an extraordinary fourth retreat on a law first enacted years ago. By pushing the start date out to 2029, South Korea’s political establishment has once again demonstrated how difficult it is to enforce tax discipline on a vocal, youth-heavy demographic during uncertain economic times.

The move highlights the delicate balancing act between the tax authority’s core mantra—“where there is income, there must be tax”—and the practical realities of an unprepared reporting infrastructure and political anxiety over alienating voters. While the delay provides short-term market stability, it leaves several crucial technical and regulatory questions completely unanswered.

The Fourth Retreat: How Politics Trumped Tax Principles

The saga of South Korea's crypto tax is unprecedented in the country's modern legislative history. When the framework was initially passed in 2021, taxation was slated to begin in 2022. That deadline was quickly pushed to 2023, then delayed again to 2025, and has now been deferred until 2029. Pushing a statutory tax regime back four separate times underscores the sheer political sensitivity surrounding digital assets in South Korea.

Going into the November 11 subcommittee meeting, the debate appeared primed for friction. Proponents of keeping the original schedule pointed to growing fiscal deficits and the principle of tax equity, arguing that crypto traders should not enjoy tax-free gains while traditional stock market investors face oversight. However, once lawmakers began examining the clause-by-clause details behind closed doors, resistance evaporated.

The administration of President Lee Jae-myung presented a formal proposal for a two-year deferral, citing market stability and incomplete reporting mechanisms. Both the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party accepted the framework without major conflict. Beneath the official explanations about market infrastructure lies a pragmatic political reality: neither side wanted to take the blame for taxing millions of retail investors, particularly those in their 20s and 30s who view cryptocurrency as one of their primary avenues for wealth generation.

Rather than risking a voter backlash, politicians from across the aisle chose the path of least resistance. The result is that South Korea’s crypto tax regime is once again floating in administrative limbo, leaving the government's long-term tax strategy facing questions over consistency and predictability.

Unresolved Mechanics: Loss Carry-Forwards and Foreign Exchanges

While lawmakers reached a broad consensus on pushing the timeline to 2029, they completely failed to resolve the technical sticking points that have plagued the tax bill since its inception. Foremost among these is the issue of loss carry-forward deductions (sonsil iwol gongje).

In traditional equity markets, investors who lose money in one tax year can carry those losses forward to offset future gains, ensuring they are only taxed on net profits over time. Given the extreme volatility of crypto assets—where an investor might make large profits one year after suffering heavy losses the year before—traders have demanded similar provisions. Without loss carry-forwards, an investor could face heavy tax liabilities on a profitable year despite remaining net-negative overall.

During the subcommittee session, lawmakers acknowledged the need for such a mechanism in principle. However, they could not agree on key specifics, including:

  • The deduction timeline: How many years investors should be allowed to carry losses forward.
  • Verification standards: How taxpayers can objectively prove past losses without a centralized clearing agency.
  • Data integration: How to consolidate trading data across multiple platforms.

Compounding this problem is the inadequacy of the current tax reporting infrastructure. To collect taxes fairly, the National Tax Service requires real-time, comprehensive data on acquisition prices, transaction histories, and selling values from trading platforms. While major domestic Korean-won fiat exchanges have upgraded their IT systems, tracking crypto-to-crypto trades, decentralized finance (DeFi) protocols, private cold wallets, and offshore exchanges remains technically elusive.

If South Korea were to enforce taxation under current conditions, compliant investors using domestic exchanges would bear the brunt of tax collection, while traders utilizing overseas platforms or private wallets could easily evade oversight. Reluctant to create a system that effectively penalizes domestic compliance, the subcommittee chose to shelve the data-reporting guidelines for future legislative sessions.

Prioritizing Market Protection Over Taxation

During the subcommittee discussions, lawmakers repeatedly stressed that building a robust legal framework to protect investors must take priority over collecting revenue. This argument hinges on the ongoing rollout of South Korea’s multi-stage digital asset legislation.

The country has already implemented the first phase of this regulatory framework, the Virtual Asset User Protection Act, which focuses primarily on curbing unfair trading practices, market manipulation, and safeguarding user deposits. However, lawmakers from both parties agreed that a comprehensive "Stage 2" basic law on digital assets is urgently needed before any tax regime can be credibly enforced.

This second phase is expected to establish comprehensive standards for:

  • Token issuance and exchange listing criteria.
  • Mandatory corporate disclosures and transparency rules for crypto projects.
  • Licensing and entry requirements for virtual asset service providers.

Proponents of the delay argue that demanding tax compliance from investors while the underlying market remains under-regulated puts the cart before the horse. Establishing institutional protections first ensures that investors trade within a fair, transparent environment before the state claims a percentage of their earnings. However, drafting and passing such a massive piece of legislation involves navigating complex industry interests, meaning the regulatory timeline could easily face its own delays.

What to Watch Next: The Legislative Path and Beyond

Although the November 11 subcommittee decision represents a major milestone, the deferral is not yet officially law. For the two-year tax delay to take legal effect, the bill must clear several remaining parliamentary hurdles:

1. Strategy and Finance Committee Full Session: The full committee must formally adopt and vote on the subcommittee’s provisional agreement.

2. Legislation and Judiciary Committee: The bill will undergo legal review to ensure systematic consistency with existing statutes.

3. National Assembly Plenary Session: The final vote will take place on the floor of the National Assembly, likely bundled alongside year-end budget-related tax bills.

Given the strong alignment between the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party, the bill is expected to pass the plenary session without significant resistance before the legislative year ends.

The broader question is whether the 2029 deadline will hold. By repeatedly choosing short-term political expedience over policy execution, South Korea’s political class has created a pattern of kicking the regulatory can down the road. For the new deadline to mean anything, the government and the National Assembly must use the next two years to build a sophisticated reporting infrastructure and pass the secondary market protection laws. Without those structural foundations, 2029 may simply bring a fifth delay.
