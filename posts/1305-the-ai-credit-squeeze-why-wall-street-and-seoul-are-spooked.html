The AI Credit Squeeze: Why Wall Street and Seoul Are Spooked by Debt, Power, and Phantom Demand

October 19, 2026

On the surface, Wall Street looked almost stable as the weekend approached. The Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped a modest 0.3%, presenting an illusion of steady resilience. Beneath that placid exterior, however, tech equities were undergoing a brutal sell-off. The Nasdaq Composite dropped more than 2%, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index plummeted 3.8%—with every single one of its 30 member stocks closing in the red. For investors who have navigated past market cycles, the eerie disconnect between headline indexes and the underlying plumbing of technology finance felt less like a routine dip and more like a structural tightening of liquidity.

The panic quickly rippled across the Pacific to South Korea. The South Korean stock market is uniquely exposed to global technology infrastructure spending, as its corporate titans—including major memory chipmakers and equipment suppliers—sit at the very front line of the artificial intelligence supply chain. As Korean technology stocks edged toward their previous lows, some commentators invoked the specter of "Black Monday," the historical 1987 crash that shook global finance 39 years ago. Yet a closer look at the data reveals that this sell-off is not a random panic. Instead, it is a credit bottleneck caused by a widening gap between massive capital expenditure plans, physical power grid constraints, and the actual pace of cash recovery.

The Hidden Alarm in the Bond Market: Spiking CDS Premiums

The immediate spark for this market pullback did not ignite on stock exchange trading floors. It emerged from the quiet corners of the fixed-income market—specifically in the market for Credit Default Swaps (CDS). A CDS premium serves as an insurance policy for bondholders against corporate default; when a company’s balance sheet weakens or its borrowing costs become unsustainable, its CDS premium spikes vertical.

In a single trading session, the five-year CDS premium for software and cloud giant Oracle surged by 26 basis points to reach 288 basis points (2.88 percentage points). Considering that Oracle’s cost of default protection had held stable around 150 basis points over the past five years, this sudden jump signaled that debt markets were demanding a much higher risk premium. Historically, corporate borrowing rates were calculated simply by adding a spread to Treasury yields. Today, with benchmark yields already elevated, companies like Oracle face an effective borrowing penalty of nearly 3% on top of existing debt service burdens that already sit near 8% to 9% annually.

The pressure is even more pronounced elsewhere in the technology ecosystem:

  • SoftBank Group: The Japanese conglomerate led by Masayoshi Son saw its five-year CDS premium jump 24 basis points to an extreme 424 basis points (4.24 percentage points), forcing any new debt issuance into double-digit interest rates.
  • CoreWeave: The unlisted "neocloud" provider, which has aggressively built out GPU-based infrastructure, saw its CDS premium break above 1,000 basis points—a territory typical of speculative, distressed debt. Carrying roughly $50 billion in debt commitments while remaining unprofitable, CoreWeave saw its corporate bonds maturing in 2037 fall to 77 cents on the dollar.

While equity investors spent the past year focused on revenue backlogs and long-term artificial intelligence narrative stories, bondholders began quietly closing their doors, questioning whether operating cash flows can keep pace with ballooning interest payments.

This credit strain quickly bled into tech stocks. The 3.8% single-day crash in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index formed part of an eight-day consecutive losing streak for semiconductor equities. Historically, the longest losing streak for the sector stands at nine consecutive sessions, recorded in July 1996 and May 2006. Crucially, the tech-heavy Nasdaq fell significantly more than the small-cap Russell 2000 index, demonstrating that the sell-off was driven by leverage reduction and valuation recalibration within technology, rather than a broad, economy-wide recession fear.

Phantom Demand and the Power Grid Bottleneck

To understand why this infrastructure expansion hit a wall, one must look at a classic supply chain concept: the Bullwhip Effect. When a retailer sells 10 items but receives only eight due to shortages, it may order 15 items next time to ensure supply. If it then receives nine, it might order 20. The end-user demand remains roughly 10 units, but distorted signals amplify order volumes as they travel up through wholesalers to manufacturers.

South Korean markets learned this lesson painfully in recent years. In 2022, Samsung Electronics recorded a staggering operating profit of nearly 60 trillion won (about $45 billion) amid pandemic-driven technology buying. Just one year later, in 2023, operating profits cratered to around 6 trillion won as temporary demand evaporated, leaving the company holding massive inventories and heavy depreciation costs. Today, memory component prices for desktop PC builders have doubled or tripled compared to late last year. Yet global consumer end-demand has not tripled; rather, fear of component shortages created a temporary layer of artificial, phantom demand.

This bullwhip dynamic is now playing out in the physical infrastructure required for AI data centers, where power availability has become the ultimate bottleneck:

  • Active Capacity vs. Queued Requests: Total operational data center power capacity across the United States currently stands at approximately 57 gigawatts (GW). These existing facilities enjoy high utilization rates and robust rental margins. However, pending applications submitted to state power grids for new data center connections have ballooned to 550 GW—nearly ten times the total currently operating.
  • Astronomical Buildout Costs: Building 1 GW of data center capacity in South Korea is estimated at 15 trillion won (about $11 billion). In the U.S., where specialized labor, land, and substation procurement costs have surged, building 1 GW costs upwards of $15 billion (20 trillion won). Funding 550 GW of applications would require over $8 trillion—a sum that exceeds available global capital.

Because developers face uncertain grid approval timelines, they submit duplicate applications across multiple states, hoping at least one will be approved. This structural overlap has created a massive 550 GW illusion.

The physical realities of grid capacity back up this concern. Research firm Gartner projects global data center power consumption will reach 565 TWh by 2026, with the U.S. alone consuming 36% (204 TWh)—more than 25 times South Korea's total data center power consumption of 8.2 TWh. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory warns that U.S. data center power demand could reach 74 to 132 GW by 2028, consuming up to 12% of the total national power supply.

Circular Financing and the Treasury Yield Boomerang

To secure power grid slots and finance construction, technology firms must demonstrate massive capital readiness. Private equity funds and institutional lenders, facing an avalanche of borrower requests, have seized pricing power, driving up credit spreads and lending rates.

At the same time, the baseline benchmark—the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield—rose to 5.098%. This yield movement was fueled not only by federal budget deficits but also by major technology firms shifting their capital strategies. Historically, mega-cap giants like Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta funded capital projects entirely out of free cash flow. Recently, however, these ultra-high-grade companies began issuing high volumes of corporate bonds to fund data center expansion. When investors can purchase corporate bonds from Microsoft or Google offering yields 0.5 percentage points higher than Treasuries, demand for government debt softens.

To attract buyers, the U.S. Treasury must offer higher yields, which in turn pushes corporate borrowing costs even higher. This feedback loop has pushed borrowing rates for tier-two technology companies and neocloud startups into the mid-to-high teens.

"What broke on sentiment will only brake when earnings come out."

Despite these funding frictions, the underlying operating fundamentals of core AI infrastructure remain intact. Oracle, for example, reported a massive cloud and AI infrastructure backlog of $600 billion. The tension lies in timing: CapEx outlays occur immediately, whereas quarterly revenue collection scales over years as servers, transformers, and GPU clusters are delivered and billed. While Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang noted that short-term volatility will pass, bond markets are forcing tech companies to prove that their cash flow generation can cover their elevated cost of debt.

Geopolitics, $110 Oil, and Monetary Policy Locks

Compounding tech-specific credit stress is a deteriorating macroeconomic environment. Typically, growth stocks benefit when interest rates fall due to economic cooling. Currently, however, equities are falling even as short-term rate expectations stall, because markets are grappling simultaneously with corporate credit risks and rekindled commodity inflation.

Crude oil prices have surged, with West Texas Intermediate (WTI) reaching $99.02 per barrel and Brent crude climbing to $109.69 per barrel. Ukrainian drone strikes against Russian refining facilities have disrupted global refined product supply, while Houthi attacks in the Middle East have targeted merchant shipping near the Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea lanes, alongside an attack in Aden that left five dead at the presidential palace.

Proposed U.S. legislation led by Republican Senator Lindsey Graham seeks strict sanctions and tariffs on nations importing Russian energy. While Secretary of State Marco Rubio noted a 60-day grace period to review potential impacts on the global economy, energy markets are already pricing in potential diesel shortages and supply chain friction.

This inflationary pressure limits the flexibility of central bankers. Federal Reserve Governor Kevin Warsh—widely cited as a top candidate for future Fed Chair—recently stated that the central bank "cannot tolerate inflation accelerating further," dampening market hopes for monetary easing.

What to Watch Next

As markets navigate this convergence of credit tightening, power bottlenecks, and geopolitical inflation, investors should track three concrete indicators to determine when stability will return:

  • Corporate CDS Premiums: Watch whether 5-year CDS spreads for key infrastructure spenders (such as Oracle and SoftBank) begin to compress back toward historical averages, signaling that bond markets are re-opening to tech issuance.
  • Data Center Interconnect Approvals: Look for utility regulator updates regarding actual grid interconnection approvals versus pending application queues to see if phantom demand is being purged from state power pipelines.
  • Quarterly Cash Flow Ratios: Focus on the gap between reported sales backlogs and realized operating cash flow in upcoming quarterly earnings from mega-cap cloud providers and chipmakers.
