South Korea's Political Theater Leaves Ordinary Citizens Behind

October 14, 2026

When you watch the daily drama unfolding in South Korean politics right now, it feels less like governance and more like a grueling national listening test. A few years ago, the entire country was forced to obsess over deciphering a hot-mic controversy involving foreign leaders. Today, the obsession has shifted inward. Citizens pore over social media posts sentence by sentence, playing a national guessing game trying to figure out which politician is trying to assassinate another's character. Politics has long ceased to be about caring for the public and has instead degenerated into a sharp-edged tool for political elites to carve each other up.

The Language of Power Versus the Reality of Daily Life

The latest round of this political shadowboxing began with a Facebook post by South Korean President Lee Jae-myung. Amid swirling rumors and sharp public debate about whether the president had quietly dropped his watch, Lee took to social media to write: "It is difficult for someone who has lived a life of ease, born to privileged parents and spending their entire career as a high-ranking official, to understand the lives and perceptions of weary ordinary citizens." The subtext was a sharp rebuke directed at those who supposedly enjoy luxury paid for by taxpayers while simultaneously slashing modest welfare budgets.

Reading words like that naturally brings specific figures to mind. It forces you to wonder whether people sitting at the very peak of state power can genuinely comprehend the despair of those who skip meals because they cannot afford a few thousand won for fruit, or those contemplating giving up because they can no longer bear financial ruin. "Why do you even do politics?" is a question every politician should carve into their bones. Unfortunately, today’s political arena has completely forgotten the answer to that question, obsessing instead on brandishing knives against rivals.

Compounding this disconnect, reports broke that President Lee and his family visited the Taereung Country Club—a government-run golf course—on a weekend, an outing the presidential office unusually publicized. While the administration defended the trip by noting that the president issued disaster management orders regarding a ceiling collapse in Daegu during the round, the optics of playing golf on a weekend jar heavily with his populist rhetoric about sharing the pain of ordinary people.

Playing golf on a holiday is not illegal, and the Taereung course is open to the public. But as critics have pointed out, the issue is not legality; it is empathy. When public servants are expected to handle national crises without weekend breaks, seeing a leader enjoy a family golf outing feels jarringly out of touch. Furthermore, legitimate questions linger: How were the security vehicles mobilized? Were the golf expenses paid out of pocket or with public funds? Was there preferential treatment in booking? If a strict standard was demanded of imprisoned former President Park Geun-hye and impeached former President Yoon Suk Yeol, that same rigorous yardstick must apply to President Lee. If the progressive camp turns a blind eye to moral laxity and a sense of entitlement, its previous criticisms lose all moral authority.

Regional Hypocrisy: Luxury Residences Amid Fiscal Emergencies

This disconnect between rhetoric and reality is not confined to the central government. Local administrations are providing plenty of reasons for public fury as well. Gyeonggi Province, the country's most populous province, recently declared a fiscal emergency and instituted sweeping budget cuts. Yet right in the middle of this austerity push, Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae leased a luxury apartment in Gwanggyo worth over 1.2 billion won (about $900,000) as an official residence, and acquired a brand-new electric vehicle worth 77 million won (about $58,000) as her official gubernatorial car through a private contract.

During the National Assembly's parliamentary audit, this contradiction took center stage. Opposition lawmakers—and even members of Choo's own party—demanded the objective basis for the province's fiscal emergency declaration. Choo defended the moves, arguing that the residence was leased on a key-money deposit basis (jeonse) and would be returned upon contract expiration, and that a large province required a long commute. She claimed the vehicle was chosen to cut maintenance costs as an eco-friendly model.

Inside the provincial government office, however, the reaction is bitterly cold. Nearly half of the official quarters allocated to junior staff in the southern part of the province are located in rundown buildings constructed in the 1990s—an era when people harbored modest, honest hopes for the future that current government offices fail to match. Employees toil away in cramped spaces, enduring winter cold without proper air conditioning, while their governor moves into a million-dollar luxury apartment and rides in a brand-new luxury EV. When budget priorities are ordered this way under the banner of fiscal crisis, public anger is inevitable.

Worse still, these painful budget cuts have not stopped at official luxuries; they have slashed public safety nets. Gyeonggi Province drastically reduced funding for automated traffic enforcement equipment to meet its fiscal savings goals. As a result, nearly 1,400 speed and signal cameras across southern Gyeonggi—including 562 units in designated school zones—faced a breakdown because they missed mandatory statutory inspections.

Handling safety after a disaster strikes is merely reactive politics, not administration. Using budget constraints as an excuse to gamble with children's commutes to school is entirely unacceptable. When over 30,000 citizens signed a petition demanding Governor Choo's resignation, the provincial office rushed to mark the petition as "answered" well before the deadline, recycling a generic spokesperson briefing. It was a transparent tactic to stifle unfavorable public opinion.

Internal Fighting and Misdirected Targets in the Opposition

Meanwhile, the opposition parties are squandering their energy on internal factional warfare rather than holding the administration accountable. The Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea's main liberal opposition, has launched an aggressive offensive against Lee Jong-keun, a lawyer and husband of Rebuilding Korea Party lawmaker Park Eun-jung. Lee, a former senior prosecutor, faced allegations that he violated the Attorney Act by representing clients involved in the massive "V-Global" cryptocurrency fraud scheme after retiring from the prosecution. Although the police already dismissed the case without formal charges, the Democratic Party has demanded a strict reinvestigation, claiming the initial probe was a whitewash.

Of course, former public officials leveraging their past positions to secure lucrative defense work after retirement is a deeply entrenched form of legal corruption that deserves severe punishment. But the timing and the target raise questions. Amid a parliamentary audit packed with pressing state scandals, why are opposition parties tearing each other apart and making a target out of a minor opposition lawmaker?

This misdirected targeting extends to other figures as well. YouTube channels and fringe online spaces have recently flooded with bizarre, unsubstantiated attacks against prominent progressive writer Rhyu Si-min, attempting to link him to past corporate controversies. Mainstream legacy media outlets have completely ignored these claims, recognizing them as malicious fake news. Yet political actors continue to weaponize trivial details—such as Rhyu receiving a modest speaking fee of 300,000 to 400,000 won (about $230 to $300)—to manufacture a moral scandal. In a world where popular idols pull in millions for a single appearance, weaponizing an intellectual's modest speaking fee is petty in the extreme.

The Isolation of Reformers and the Strains on the Real Economy

The pettiness of the political class reaches its peak when reformers who have long spoken out are targeted by their own side. Im Eun-jung, a senior prosecutor at the Gwangju District Prosecutors' Office known for blowing the whistle on internal corruption and prosecutorial misconduct, recently faced heavy criticism from administration officials claiming she views her oversight role as a political weapon rather than an investigative one.

This is a stunning irony. Im endured years of professional hardship precisely because she exposed the rotten underbelly of the prosecution. Had she prioritized personal comfort, she could have stayed silent, played along with the institutional ladder, retired to private practice, and cashed in on the very network corruption she fought against. Turning the state's apparatus against those who stood up to injustice is a textbook case of blaming the victim.

While politicians throw punches over turf wars and online rumors, ordinary South Koreans are being driven to the edge of a cliff. The government highlights statistical data showing strong economic growth, but if you ask neighborhood small-business owners, the answer is a sigh. On the ground, people whisper that times are harder now than they were under the rule of imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk Yeol.

Sure, walking through trendy neighborhoods like Seongsu-dong reveals bustling streets and long lines at trendy restaurants. But that is only a selective slice of the national economy. We must not fall into the trap of statistical averages. The health of a few large corporations does not warm the cold reality faced by neighborhood merchants buckling under skyrocketing rents, high interest rates, and utility bills.

💭 Editor's Note

The longer you watch South Korean politics, the more profound your sense of sorrow becomes. Whether conservative or progressive, the moment a faction grasps power, the pain of ordinary citizens is pushed to the back burner. Politics collapses into tactical calculations aimed entirely at protecting elite privileges and crushing rivals. Reformers like Im Eun-jung are consumed as political tools and discarded, while neighborhood merchants collapse beneath the shadow of macroeconomic statistics.

We must ask ourselves: Was the democratic progress we fought for merely designed to host a feast for entrenched elites? When will politics that genuinely wipes away the tears of the ordinary citizen finally arrive in Seoul?

The sharp edge of state power should never be pointed at political rivals; it should touch the cold, tired hands of ordinary people weathering a harsh economic storm. Until the political class wakes up to that basic truth, we will keep filtering out the noise of this giant political listening test and surviving the grind of everyday life.
