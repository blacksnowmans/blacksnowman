Cracks in the Coalition: Rhyu Si-min, Kim Min-seok, and the Struggle Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party

October 29, 2026

Inside the fiercely active online communities that form the digital backbone of South Korea’s liberal movement—hubs like Ddanzi Ilbo, Clien, and Damoang—the mood in recent weeks has curdled from post-election triumph into bitter acrimony. Grassroots members of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) are openly questioning their own leadership. When that frustration sparked whispers of a mass defection, one of the progressive bloc’s most respected public intellectuals decided he could no longer stay silent.

Rhyu Si-min—an author, former minister, and towering commentator who served under the late President Roh Moo-hyun—delivered a sharp, unvarnished rebuke to party leader Kim Min-seok and the DPK apparatus. His intervention, aired across progressive political channels including Park Si-young TV, was not merely an emotional venting of grievances. It was an alarm bell warning that the ruling party is eroding its own procedural democracy. Yet his counsel to enraged party loyalists was surprisingly disciplined: do not abandon the ship.

Fractured Legitimacy: Convention Rules and the Limits of Power

The seeds of the current crisis were planted during the Democratic Party’s national convention, when party leaders abruptly overhauled internal voting mechanisms. The leadership pushed through an unfamiliar preferential voting system just ahead of the contest, discarding long-standing procedural customs and consensus. The move immediately triggered accusations that backroom power brokers were tilting the playing field to favor specific establishment figures, chiefly current leader Kim Min-seok.

Altering election rules on the eve of a vote fundamentally compromises the procedural legitimacy of the victor. In political science and social choice theory, this dilemma is famously framed by Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem, formulated by Nobel laureate Kenneth Arrow. The theorem demonstrates that under reasonable criteria, it is impossible to convert individual ranked preferences into an absolute, flawless community-wide ranking without restricting options or assuming a dictator. No electoral formula—whether preferential balloting or a traditional two-round runoff system—is universally or inherently superior.

Runoff elections, for example, preserve a dynamic window where public sentiment can shift dramatically between rounds, as demonstrated in high-stakes contests like Brazil’s presidential race. By packaging a sudden switch to preferential balloting as an objective improvement, party leaders were widely perceived as dressing up political engineering in democratic rhetoric. Winning an election under modified rules does not deliver a mandate; it hangs an enduring legitimacy deficit around the leader’s neck.

Yet the primary outcome delivered an unexpected twist. Despite an unlevel playing field and open establishment backing for Kim Min-seok, non-mainstream candidates captured an astonishing 46% of the vote. In the modern history of the DPK, such a massive protest vote against an entrenched leadership slate is virtually unprecedented. Party members used their ballots to send an unmistakable warning: party sovereignty belongs to the grassroots, not to committee rooms.

High Visibility, Low Favorability: The Crisis Facing Kim Min-seok

The blowback against Kim Min-seok’s leadership has materialized swiftly in hard public opinion data. A monthly online sentiment report compiled by WinG Korea Consulting revealed devastating numbers for the newly installed party leader’s first month in office.

Rather than driving forward key legislative reforms or bread-and-butter economic policies, Kim’s public footprint was dominated almost entirely by political brawling. Outside of President Lee Jae-myung, the political figure most frequently mentioned alongside Kim was Han Dong-hoon, a prominent conservative lawmaker with the main opposition People Power Party (PPP), followed by figures like Cho Kuk, Choo Mi-ae, and Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The metrics reflected intense noise with little goodwill:

  • Attention Index vs. Favorability: While Kim’s attention index soared to 86, his favorability index plunged to a dismal 17.
  • Volume and Negative Ratios: Overall online mentions exceeded 190,000—a 71.7% surge from the previous month. However, negative mentions reached 60,631, outnumbering positive mentions (12,097) by a ratio of roughly five to one.
  • Grassroots Backlash: The harshest criticism originated not from conservative opponents, but from the ruling party’s core progressive online communities, where negative sentiment reached six times the positive volume throughout September.

Grassroots supporters accused Kim of running an insular, self-serving political operation that actively erodes the approval ratings of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. When party broadcast channels abruptly raised sensational rhetoric about historical impeachments in mid-September, negative online posts surged past 3,300 in a single day. Clashes with conservative lawmakers also backfired, inadvertently resurrecting decade-old controversies from Kim’s past that damaged the party’s standing.

Compounding the problem was the leadership’s mishandling of judicial reform. Kim took center stage to demand the resignation of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over appointments, a feud that generated a peak of 6,864 mentions on September 22. Yet the barrage produced no meaningful legislative progress. Paired with public missteps surrounding failed cabinet appointments, such as the dropped nomination of Kim Seung-won for Minister of Justice, the leadership appeared to trade patient policymaking for aggressive, headline-grabbing soundbites.

The Temptation of Executive Hubris

Rhyu Si-min’s critique cut deeper than tactical errors; it questioned how power itself is exercised within a democratic republic. Political observers often romanticize high office as a seat of profound personal loneliness. Drawing on his memories of Roh Moo-hyun, Rhyu noted that Roh’s inner turmoil was born of relentless self-scrutiny—a constant questioning of whether declining public trust meant his continued tenure harmed the nation.

Raw political power, however, operates on a very different psychological frequency. As Max Weber famously observed, power is the ability to carry out one’s own will even against the resistance of others. When rulers encounter institutional friction or dissent within their own ranks, their instinctive reaction is rarely reflective sorrow; it is irritation and anger. Leaders who fail to master that impulse risk treating democratic friction as personal obstruction.

This dynamic holds serious consequences for the Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party majority. The South Korean presidency commands immense constitutional powers, including executive authority, statutory vetoes, and command over the military. If public authority is wielded out of spite or a refusal to hear criticism, governance breaks down.

"If we lower our standards for criticism simply because we supported the president, the democracy we claim to protect becomes nothing more than a slogan that changes depending on who holds power."

The warning is grounded in South Korea’s recent history. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, critics were repeatedly cast as anti-state agitators, vetoes were weaponized, and democratic norms were discarded. That path led directly to Mr. Yoon’s historic impeachment, removal from office, and eventual imprisonment. Progressivism loses its moral and political foundation if it replicates the authoritarian habits of the administration it fought to replace.

Why Core Supporters Must Not Abandon Ship

Faced with a heavy-handed party leadership, a faction of disillusioned DPK rank-and-file members has considered tearing up their party cards and moving to alternative options, most notably the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Cho Kuk. Rhyu firmly discouraged such a migration, laying out a pragmatic distinction between political roles.

The Rebuilding Korea Party, Rhyu argued, serves as an agile icebreaker or an emergency lifeboat, cutting through institutional inertia on the front lines. But it lacks the hull and tonnage required to carry an entire nation across volatile political waters. The Democratic Party of Korea remains the only vessel large enough to secure executive power and deliver nationwide systemic change. Leaping overboard because one dislikes the current captain or navigator is an act of strategic self-defeat.

The ruling coalition also faces an ideological tug-of-war between "extension" and "reconstruction." Core supporters who rallied behind the DPK expected an ambitious expansion built upon the foundations laid by the Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in administrations—namely, the completion of prosecutorial reform, social safety nets, and corporate accountability. Instead, the leadership has flirted with a muddled reconstruction, softening positions on real-estate taxation and reform to appease moderate and conservative swing voters. Neglecting the base to chase elusive centrist support risks losing both.

What to Watch Next

The simmering revolt within the DPK is not an ordinary party dispute; it is an argument over who owns South Korea’s ruling party. As political maneuvering intensifies ahead of upcoming electoral tests, several indicators will determine whether the leadership stabilizes or fractures:

  • Grassroots Member Retention: Watch whether monthly dues-paying party members maintain their registration or accelerate defections toward smaller progressive parties. A plateau in party membership rolls will signal that Rhyu’s call to stay and fight from within has resonated.
  • Legislative Reform Priorities: Observe whether the DPK leadership pivots away from public confrontations with judicial figures and returns to concrete statutory pushes on prosecutorial authority and economic reform.
  • Leadership Course Correction: Any recalibration of Kim Min-seok’s media strategy—moving from personal sparring with conservative figures toward cooperative alignment with President Lee Jae-myung’s policy agenda—will reveal whether the party leadership recognizes the severity of its collapsing favorability ratings.

Party leaders can engineer procedural rules to protect their perches, but they cannot manufacture genuine loyalty. For South Korea’s progressive base, the current strategy is one of patient endurance: staying inside the party, maintaining their voting rights, and waiting for the inevitable moment when the ballot box restores accountability to the rank and file.
