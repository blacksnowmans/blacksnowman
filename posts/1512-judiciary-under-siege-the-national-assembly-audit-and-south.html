Judiciary Under Siege: The National Assembly Audit and South Korea’s Season of Political Warfare

October 27, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, the air is thick with the scent of document toner and the palpable tension of high-stakes political theater. It is late October, the season of the Gukgam—the annual audit of state affairs where lawmakers theoretically scrutinize the government's performance over the past year. But in 2024, the policy folders have been pushed aside. Instead, the cameras are focused on a series of explosive judicial rulings and personal scandals that have turned South Korea’s legislative body into a battlefield.

To an outside observer, the scene might look like standard partisan bickering. However, what is currently unfolding is a fundamental clash between the executive, the legislature, and the judiciary. At the center of the storm is the Democratic Party of South Korea, the current ruling party led by President Lee Jae-myung, and their escalating war of words with the judiciary following a controversial acquittal of a key political rival. On the other side, the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), is fighting back with allegations against the First Lady and warnings of street protests. This is not just a disagreement over policy; it is a struggle to define the boundaries of power in a post-impeachment era.

The Verdict That Shook Yeouido

The catalyst for the most recent explosion of fury was the appellate court's decision regarding Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul and a prominent figure in the conservative opposition. On October 26, the court upheld a "not guilty" verdict for Oh in a case involving allegations of illegal political fund-raising—specifically related to the "Myung Tae-kyun gate," a scandal involving a political broker who allegedly handled campaign expenses for various conservative politicians.

For the ruling Democratic Party, the acquittal was a bitter pill to swallow. They argue that the court applied an overly lenient standard to Mayor Oh, effectively granting him "judicial immunity" despite evidence of third-party payments during his election campaign. The rhetoric from the ruling camp has been unusually harsh. Some Democratic lawmakers labeled the written judgment "a mess," while others went as far as to suggest that South Korea should replace human judges with "AI judges" to eliminate what they perceive as political bias and arbitrary decision-making.

The fallout has led the Democratic Party to move toward legislative retaliation. They are currently drafting an amendment to the Political Fund Act to clarify and tighten the responsibilities regarding third-party payments, ensuring that politicians cannot hide behind legal technicalities when their campaign bills are footed by others. This move signifies a growing trend in Korean politics: when the ruling party is dissatisfied with a court’s interpretation of the law, they seek to rewrite the law itself to box the judiciary in. It is a high-stakes game of "legislative check" that risks eroding the traditional independence of the courts.

Threats of Impeachment and the Battle for the Supreme Court

The anger directed at Mayor Oh’s acquittal has quickly climbed the judicial ladder to the very top. Democratic Party leadership and smaller allied groups, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party, have begun discussing the possible impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The Chief Justice, who leads the Supreme Court of Korea—the nation's court of last resort—is being accused by the ruling camp of failing to maintain the neutrality of the lower courts.

Kim Yong-min, a prominent Democratic lawmaker, has already introduced a bill aimed at curbing the Chief Justice's power. The bill would mandate that the Chief Justice recommend at least two candidates for every Supreme Court vacancy, effectively diluting his individual influence over the composition of the bench. By targeting the leadership of the judiciary, the ruling party is signaling that the era of judicial "sanctuary" is over.

The opposition People Power Party has reacted with predictable alarm, framing these moves as an "assault on democracy." PPP leadership warned that if the ruling party attempts to impeach the Chief Justice or appoints special prosecutors to investigate "fabricated prosecutions," they will take the fight to the streets. "If an impeachment motion is rejected by the Constitutional Court, the ruling party leadership should resign en masse and face the judgment of the people," one opposition leader stated. The threat of large-scale street protests—a potent force in Korean political history—suggests that the conflict is moving beyond the walls of the National Assembly and into the public square.

First Lady Allegations and the Ghost of the Blue House

While the ruling party grills the judiciary, the opposition is aiming its sights at the President's spouse. During the audit of the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, PPP lawmaker Joo Jin-woo raised allegations of "Imperial Golf" lessons involving First Lady Kim Hye-kyung. The opposition claims that the First Lady received private lessons from a famous professional golfer under questionable circumstances.

The Democratic Party and the Presidential Office immediately fired back, calling the claims "vile black propaganda" and "groundless rumors." The clash grew so heated that the committee session had to be suspended for 25 minutes as lawmakers traded insults. This focus on the "First Lady risk" has become a recurring theme in Korean politics, with both sides often targeting the families of their rivals to score points when policy debates fail to gain traction.

Adding to the surreal atmosphere of the audit was the revelation regarding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former leader who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently serving time in prison. It was revealed during the audit that Mr. Yoon and his wife have accumulated approximately 2.2 billion KRW (about $1.6 million) in "prison funds"—donations and support money sent by their ardent supporters.

"The fact that a person removed for violating the constitution and engaging in state-affairs interference can collect over $1.6 million while in a cell is a distortion of the justice system," one lawmaker remarked during the session.

The Ministry of Justice has stated it is reviewing the current regulations on prisoner funds, as the massive sum has sparked public outcry over "special treatment" for high-profile inmates who maintain a cult-like following even behind bars.

Reforming the "Power Organs" and National Sovereignty

Beyond the personal scandals, the audit touched on the structural reorganization of South Korea’s investigative agencies. A major point of contention is the role of the newly proposed Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO). The ruling Democratic Party wants the head of the MCIO to attend Cabinet meetings to ensure "transparent communication" and "responsible administration."

The opposition PPP is vehemently opposed, arguing that having the head of a powerful investigative body sit at the President's table destroys the agency's political neutrality. Han Dong-hoon, the former head of the PPP’s emergency committee, compared the move to "openly declaring an illicit affair," suggesting that the agency would become a tool for the President to target political enemies. However, Minister of the Interior and Safety Yoon Ho-jung countered that the old system, where prosecutors operated in "secretive rooms" without formal oversight, was far more dangerous to democracy.

Finally, the audit touched on the sensitive issue of national security and the transfer of Wartime Operational Control (OPCON). Currently, in the event of a war, a U.S. general would lead the combined forces of the two countries. The ruling Democratic Party has pushed for a "sovereignty-first" approach, arguing that a world-class military like South Korea's should have the right to command its own troops. The opposition remains cautious, fearing that a premature transfer could weaken the alliance with the United States and leave the country vulnerable. This debate underscores a growing rift in how the two sides view South Korea's place on the global stage—one side pushing for greater autonomy, the other for traditional security guarantees.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly audit wraps up, the focus will shift from shouting matches to concrete appointments and legislative battles. There are three key areas to watch in the coming weeks:

  • The Justice Minister Appointment: The President is expected to narrow down candidates for the Minister of Justice, likely choosing from former prosecutors Park Gyun-taek or Yang Bu-nam. Since both served as lawyers for President Lee Jae-myung in the past, their confirmation hearings will be a flashpoint for the "prosecution reform" debate.
  • The "Prison Fund" Legislation: Expect the Ministry of Justice to introduce new caps or regulations on the amount of money high-profile inmates like Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol can receive, a move that will likely be cheered by the ruling party and decried by the opposition's base.
  • The Fate of the Chief Justice: Watch whether the Democratic Party follows through on the impeachment threat against Cho Hee-dae. An actual impeachment motion would paralyze the Supreme Court and likely trigger the massive street protests the opposition has promised.

In the end, the 2024 audit has shown that in South Korea, the law is not a static set of rules but a fluid front line in an ongoing political war. Whether this leads to genuine reform or a total breakdown of judicial trust remains the defining question for the Lee Jae-myung administration.
