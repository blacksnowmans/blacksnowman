South Korea’s Nationwide Farmland Crackdown Sparks Fear and Reform

September 27, 2026

Rumors spread quickly through South Korea's farming communities that the government was coming to seize their land. Elderly villagers who had worked their ancestral plots for decades, alongside young people hoping to return home to farm, worried they would be hit with sudden land disposal orders and evicted overnight. The panic grew loud enough to force emergency high-level meetings between the ruling party and government officials, exposing a deep reservoir of anxiety in the countryside.

The government insists the panic is entirely unfounded. Song Miryeong, head of the Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs (MAFRA), pushed back directly in interviews, stating that the rumors simply are not true. Yet the friction reveals a profound clash between modern state administration and the realities of an aging rural population. At the center of the storm is South Korea’s first-ever nationwide, comprehensive survey of all agricultural land since the republic was founded—a sweeping audit that inadvertently triggered widespread fear.

Understanding the Nationwide Farmland Survey

To foreign observers, a government audit of agricultural land might sound routine. In South Korea, however, the word "comprehensive survey" carries heavy weight. It signals that regulators are turning over every stone to root out legal violations and hand down administrative punishments. Following land speculation scandals involving employees of the Korea Land and Housing Corporation (LH)—a state-owned urban development agency—public demand surged to overhaul South Korea's agricultural land system. Authorities launched the database project to crack down on illegal speculation while correcting minor, everyday infractions rather than punishing farmers.

Despite these stated intentions, local communication broke down. Whispers mutated into panic: the state was confiscating property, and taking a break from farming meant risking a total loss. This disconnect highlights a glaring gap between policymakers in Seoul and the people living off the land.

The root of the tension lies in the country's aging countryside crashing against the 30-year-old Farmland Act, enacted in January 1996. The law is anchored by a constitutional principle known as gyeongjayeujeon—the rule that only those who actually farm can own agricultural land. But three decades later, South Korea's rural areas are aging faster than almost anywhere else in the world, and inherited farmland has multiplied. With nearly 70% of farmers aged 65 or older, expecting every single owner to personally wield a hoe is an impossible standard. The fact that the government had never mapped out a precise, national inventory of its own farmland until now demonstrates just how outdated the system has become.

Decoding the Numbers: What the Initial Findings Actually Mean

The initial phase of the audit yielded a startling statistic: 27% of the agricultural land parcels with changed ownership titles showed signs of potential violations. Upon seeing this high number, farmers feared their property rights were about to vanish. Officials have repeatedly stressed, however, that these flagged parcels do not mean automatic guilt or immediate land seizure.

Instead, the figure represents properties marked for deeper investigation. Illegal leasing made up the largest share at 21.1%, followed by unauthorized fallow land at 11.6%, illegal conversion at 9.0%, and ownership restriction breaches at 1.4%. Many of these are administrative mismatches—for example, a plot is registered as self-farmed, but fertilizer purchase records are missing, requiring officials to check whether the owner is genuinely farming. Field teams are utilizing everything from text message alerts to drones and satellites to review remote plots before any final decisions are made.

Crucially, the government has carved out clear protections for inherited land and elderly farmers. Inherited plots up to 1 hectare (about 3,000 pyeong, or roughly 2.4 acres) are fully permitted to be leased out between individuals. Furthermore, elderly farmers forced to leave fields fallow due to declining health are granted legitimate exemptions.

Targeting Speculators While Offering Relief to Everyday Landowners

While shielding good-faith landowners, the government has aimed its regulatory weapons at speculators using capital to manipulate the agricultural market. Authorities have defined three clear categories of illegal speculation: acquiring land in restricted zones through falsified farm management plans while actually leaving the plots idle; corporate entities registered as agricultural businesses operating real estate speculation or storage businesses instead; and speculative syndicates where dozens or even hundreds of people buy large tracts of farmland in fractional shares and abandon them.

For these bad actors, a one-year correction period is followed by a disposal order and steep financial penalties if ignored. At the same time, the government plans to introduce a special legislative measure later this year to retroactively legalize minor historical violations caused by outdated regulations, giving relief to farmers who built necessary structures that previously fell outside legal boundaries.

Supporting this shift is the state-run Korea Farmland Bank. To help farmers who want to sell plots but cannot find buyers, the law was amended to require the Farmland Bank to purchase eligible disposal land. The state's farmland acquisition budget was increased by 25% to about 2.26 trillion won (approximately $1.6 billion). The state plans to purchase these plots and lease them first to young farmers before offering them for sale, attempting to build a pipeline to bring youth into agriculture.

Shifting Toward Practical Land Use as Rural Areas Shrink

With only 19% of South Korea's population living outside major urban centers, rural depopulation is an existential crisis. Clinging blindly to the constitutional myth of absolute self-farming while ignoring aging demographics and non-farming heirs will only leave behind abandoned fields. Policymakers must shift from a rigid ownership-focused model to a pragmatic framework centered on productive land use.

Just as Japan utilized agricultural committees to completely open up leasing and streamline local planning, South Korea needs bold institutional innovation. The ongoing debate over reforming the Farmland Act cannot stop at harsher punishments for idle land; it must carefully evaluate adopting flexible, negative-regulation systems that allow corporations, diverse legal entities, and urbanites dreaming of rural life to utilize land productively.

What to Watch Next

  • Implementation of Special Legislation: Watch for the introduction of the special measure designed to retroactively legalize minor, practical zoning infractions for farmers caught in decades-old regulatory traps.
  • Korea Farmland Bank Activity: Track whether the expanded 2.26 trillion-won ($1.6 billion) budget effectively absorbs unmarketable land and successfully transfers it to young, incoming farmers through the "lease-first, sell-later" program.
  • Local Communications and Relief Centers: Observe if the continued operation of tenant farmer protection and reporting centers successfully quells local panic and bridges the gap between central policy goals and rural realities.

The sharp edge of state regulation must target outdated rules and bad-faith speculators, not the everyday farmers sustaining the countryside.
