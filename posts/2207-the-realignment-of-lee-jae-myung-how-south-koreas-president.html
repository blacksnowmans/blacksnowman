The Realignment of Lee Jae-myung: How South Korea's President Abandoned the Progressive Vanguard

November 11, 2026

When Lee Jae-myung swept into the presidency, he carried the hopes of a South Korean progressive movement that had weathered one of the most turbulent chapters in the nation's democratic history. For years, Lee had fashioned himself as an unapologetic champion of the underdog, brandishing the populist banner of eok-gang-bu-yak—a classical maxim meaning "restrain the strong and support the weak"—alongside promises of an egalitarian commonwealth (daedong sesang). To his base, he seemed a blunt-talking, fearless reformer willing to dismantle the entrenched conservative establishment, rein in family-run conglomerates (chaebols), and reform the judiciary.

Yet inside the Blue House—the South Korean presidential office—an unsettling transformation has unfolded. The leader who once thrilled grass-roots activists by confronting the conservative elite has aggressively pivoted toward the very forces he pledged to tame. Disillusionment among progressives is giving way to open grievance: from wooing veteran figures of the conservative establishment to echoing nativist rhetoric against foreign migrant workers, President Lee is charting a course that many of his longtime loyalists view not as shrewd pragmatic governance, but as outright political betrayal.

From Seongnam Maverick to the Pinnacle of Power

To grasp the depth of this current fracture, one must trace Lee's rise through South Korea's fractured political terrain. In the early 2010s, under the conservative administration of Lee Myung-bak, the progressive camp was in complete disarray. Following the death of former President Roh Moo-hyun, liberal lawmakers and activists labeled themselves "ruined outcasts," paralyzed by factional guilt and electoral defeats. Into that vacuum stepped Lee Jae-myung.

Beginning as the mayor of Seongnam, a satellite city southeast of Seoul, Lee possessed an instinctive talent for stagecraft. During his municipal tenure, he thrust himself into national headlines by declaring a moratorium on the city's debt—a dramatic maneuver that council critics argued was vastly overstated for political effect, only for Lee to later take credit for a triumphant fiscal turnaround. Regardless of the administrative controversy, the spectacle worked. While conventional liberal figures inside the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—such as former Prime Minister Lee Nak-yon—presented a cautious, patrician conservatism, Lee positioned himself as an energetic disruptor.

Over the following decade, as progressive rivals fell away due to scandal or tragedy—including the death of Justice Party icon Roh Hoe-chan and the political downfall of former South Chungcheong Governor Ahn Hee-jung—Lee systematically captured the leadership of the liberal bloc. He borrowed the emotive legacy of Roh Moo-hyun, claiming to be the authentic heir to democratic reform. Yet, as his critics point out, the egalitarian slogans were always a tool for personal political survival rather than an unshakeable ideological anchor.

The 2022 Lesson and the Turn Toward Compromise

The turning point in Lee's worldview arrived with his razor-thin defeat in the 2022 presidential election. The shock of that loss convinced Lee and his closest strategists that progressive purity would never reliably crack the 50 percent threshold needed for enduring rule. Although he ultimately secured the presidency after the tumultuous impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol, the trauma of previous electoral bottlenecks left a cynical imprint.

Lee's private conclusion was stark: real structural reform of South Korean society is exhausting, triggers massive resistance from the elite, and alienates the moderate center. Consequently, his administration concluded that staying within the traditional progressive tent was a losing game. Compromising with entrenched interest groups, in his estimation, offered an easier path to holding power.

This recalculation has sparked fierce friction inside the ruling Democratic Party. Lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae and Kim Yong-min, who have consistently pushed for aggressive restructuring of the state prosecution service and sweeping corporate reforms, find themselves sidelined. The president has grown weary of demands from the core progressive base, treating ardent activists not as the engine of his movement, but as dogmatic, uncompensated burdens who hamper his broader appeal.

More troubling to observers has been the administration's willingness to flirt with right-wing populism. Facing persistent economic anxiety among young South Koreans, the government has drifted into scapegoating. Rather than directly tackling youth wage stagnation, presidential communications have quietly tapped into online anti-immigrant sentiment, echoing claims that foreign migrant workers suppress local wages and steal entry-level jobs. By leaning into nativist frames and highlighting divisions regarding multicultural families, Lee has borrowed tactics straight out of the global far-right playbook to shore up flagging approval numbers.

Courting the Elite: A Cynical Vision of the 'Structural Majority'

Behind closed doors, the intellectual rationalization for this shift rests on a deeply cynical reading of Korean history. Lee's political philosophy has come to mirror a classic oligarchical thesis: that like the Andong Kim clan who monopolized power during the late Joseon Dynasty, South Korea is always run by an organized, disciplined elite. In this view, the broad masses are largely passive spectators; real governance requires an alliance with the forces that hold institutional leverage.

Lee's political engineering increasingly resembles a bid to replicate the perpetual-governance model of Japan's Liberal Democratic Party (LDP)—a grand coalition where the ruling center shares power with traditional elites. To assemble this coalition, Lee has made overtures to veteran figures associated with the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), reaching out to veteran conservative policymakers and strategists such as Kim Sung-sik, Lee Seok-yeon, Yoon Yeo-joon, and Lee Byung-tae. The strategic message from the Blue House is unmistakable: progressives are an inherent minority in South Korean power dynamics, and lasting rule requires partnering with the corporate and administrative establishment.

This dynamic was laid bare during recent press interactions prior to the Chuseok holiday. In off-the-record exchanges with beat reporters, the president openly distanced himself from his core supporters, insisting that the progressive movement lacks the institutional muscle to govern alone. Promises to make everyday citizens the true sovereigns of the republic have given way to courting the legacy conservative press—such as the Chosun, JoongAng, and Dong-A dailies—and easing scrutiny on South Korea's massive family-run chaebols.

Alienation, Stagnation, and the Erosion of Power

This realignment carries severe political costs. During the previous liberal administration of Moon Jae-in, an impassioned, volunteer digital army actively defended the government's policies against hostile media attacks. By discarding that grass-roots base, Lee has dismantled his own protective shield. The newer cohort around him, dubbed the "New Lee Jae-myung" loyalists, is viewed as transactional—an opportunistic circle united by access rather than ideological dedication.

The isolation appears to be taking a toll on governance. Confronted by falling poll ratings and sharper parliamentary scrutiny, the president's public temperament has turned brittle. When challenged by opposition figures, Lee has resorted to coarse retorts, dismissing criticism with the crude proverb that "a pig sees only pigs"—a defensive reaction that erodes the dignity expected of the head of state.

Simultaneously, the administration's economic gamble has produced little relief for ordinary households. While headline macroeconomic figures boast strong semiconductor exports, South Korea faces a punishing "K-shaped" economic divide. Youth employment has contracted for two consecutive years, and the government's economic policies have tilted toward business-friendly concessions that mirror the conservative playbook. The paradox is acute: the conservative People Power Party, without needing to advance visionary policies of its own, is reaping the electoral windfall simply by mobilizing anti-Lee sentiment.

What to Watch Next

As President Lee Jae-myung's term advances, the tension between his campaign rhetoric and governing reality will define the political horizon. Observers should watch several critical dynamics:

  • Factions in the Democratic Party: Watch how progressive backbenchers and reformist heavyweights respond to executive appointments and legislative priorities. If the Blue House continues to ice out prosecution and chaebol reform, a formal factional rupture within the ruling party could paralyze the legislative agenda.
  • Voter Retaliation in Local and By-Elections: Core progressive turnout is the bedrock of liberal election victories. If disillusioned grass-roots voters withhold their participation or support third-party alternatives, the ruling party faces severe vulnerability in metropolitan areas.
  • Policy on Foreign Labor and Social Welfare: Pay close attention to whether the administration's rhetoric translates into restrictive immigration decrees or rollbacks on multicultural support initiatives, which would signal a permanent embrace of culture-war politics to distract from economic pressures.

Lee sought to build an unassailable "structural majority" by borrowing the clothes of his former adversaries. Yet by alienating the loyal constituency that carried him to the Blue House, he risks finding himself insulated in a political fortress of his own design—governing on borrowed time without a base to protect him.
