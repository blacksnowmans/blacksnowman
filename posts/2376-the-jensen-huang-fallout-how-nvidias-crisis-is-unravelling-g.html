The Jensen Huang Fallout: How Nvidia’s Crisis Is Unravelling Global Tech and Shaking South Korea’s Chip Titans

November 16, 2026

For years, the story of the artificial intelligence boom had a single, unmistakable face: Jensen Huang standing onstage in his signature leather jacket, unveiling processing hardware that tech giants bought up as fast as silicon foundries could manufacture it. That reality dissolved almost overnight. The sudden resignation of Nvidia’s chief executive—triggered by personal scandals and explosive allegations of circular financial transactions to artificially inflate GPU prices—has sent shockwaves through Silicon Valley, Wall Street, and the semiconductor hubs of East Asia.

What began as a leadership crisis at a single American firm is rapidly escalating into a systemic realignment of the global AI hardware ecosystem. With Nvidia’s upcoming earnings report looming next week, global markets are bracing for volatile trading. For international observers watching South Korea—the world's memory semiconductor powerhouse—the crisis at Nvidia is far more than an American corporate drama; it is a structural challenge that threatens supply contracts, inflates operational costs, and exposes key Asian tech titans to intense regulatory crossfire.

An Empire Without Its Emperor: Scandals, Petitions, and Wall Street Bets

The immediate aftermath of Huang’s departure has laid bare just how dependent Nvidia’s market supremacy was on his personal standing. As details of the scandal emerged alongside allegations of irregular financial trades, the executive office was abruptly emptied. The shock among investors and tech enthusiasts was swift: an online petition demanding Huang’s reinstatement as CEO surpassed 115,000 signatures within days, illustrating the intense retail and institutional anxiety over a leaderless Nvidia.

Industry leaders have voiced grave concerns about the structural void. Elon Musk issued a stark warning, noting that without Huang’s deep-seated relationships and deal-making instincts, Nvidia risks losing vital contracts with its major corporate clients. For tech conglomerates that committed billions of dollars in capital expenditure based on Nvidia’s long-term technology roadmaps, the sudden absence of its visionary chief creates immediate contract and planning risks.

Meanwhile, institutional investors are positioning themselves for a paradigm shift. Michael Burry, the hedge fund manager famed for predicting the 2008 housing crash in The Big Short, significantly expanded his short bets against Nvidia. Burry has openly bet on a deep stock decline, signaling that the primary beneficiaries of Huang’s departure will be direct competitors capable of absorbing displaced demand. As market sentiment fractures, Nvidia’s dominance is facing its most severe test since the dawn of the generative AI era.

The Price-Fixing Allegations and a $34 Billion Infrastructure Crisis

Beyond the executive turnover, the core operational scandal threatens to dismantle the pricing structures that built Nvidia’s record-breaking margins. Investigators and industry analysts are scrutinizing allegations that Nvidia utilized circular financial transactions—a scheme where funds are routed through affiliated entities to fabricate artificial demand—to maintain elevated pricing for its graphics processing units (GPUs).

During the peak of the global AI chip shortage, hyper-scalers and early-stage startups alike had little choice but to pay premium prices for high-end accelerators. If regulatory investigations confirm that GPU prices were systematically manipulated through opaque financing arrangements, Nvidia faces severe legal liabilities and forced price recalibrations that will squeeze its historical profitability.

The fallout has spilled directly into international trade and supply chain networks. To navigate stringent U.S. government export controls aimed at restricting advanced AI hardware sales to China, complex routing systems had been established through Southeast Asian data centers. In the wake of Huang's exit and intensified scrutiny from the U.S. Congress, this indirect supply line is breaking down.

A staggering $34 billion worth of planned data center investments across Southeast Asia now faces cancellation or indefinite freeze. Facilities designed to house thousands of high-performance servers are standing idle as legal channels tighten and access to modified chips vanishes. This sudden blockage of indirect export routes has severed a critical revenue valve for Nvidia, while leaving regional infrastructure partners saddled with massive capital commitments.

South Korea’s Memory Giants and the Bold Play of 'Seohak Ants'

The reverberations of Nvidia's breakdown hit South Korea with acute force. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—the twin titans of the South Korean semiconductor industry—produce the High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) chips essential for running high-performance AI accelerators. SK Hynix, in particular, has maintained an exclusive supply relationship with Nvidia, powering its top-tier computing architectures.

With Nvidia’s internal investment strategies in flux and chip redesigns potentially delayed, South Korean manufacturers face serious risks to their cost structures. Any slowdown in Nvidia's deployment schedule threatens to disrupt HBM delivery timelines, forcing South Korean firms to absorb excess inventory costs and renegotiate contract prices. Furthermore, heightened scrutiny from Washington is intensifying political pressure on South Korean chipmakers to expand expensive domestic manufacturing facilities within the U.S., adding another layer of financial strain.

"If Nvidia's major architecture rollouts stall due to leadership chaos, the immediate burden cascades down to high-bandwidth memory suppliers in South Korea who have structured their entire capital expenditure plans around Nvidia's aggressive roadmap."

Despite these mounting headwinds, South Korean retail investors have taken an extraordinarily aggressive stance. Known locally as Seohak Ants (서학개미)—a term coined for South Korean individual investors who trade heavily in foreign equities, primarily U.S. technology stocks—these traders viewed the sharp dip in Nvidia’s share price not as a warning sign, but as a generational buying opportunity.

South Korean retail accounts poured roughly 450 billion won (approximately $330 million) into Nvidia stock immediately following news of the scandal. This massive capital inflow reflects a deep-seated conviction among Korean retail investors that Nvidia remains an irreplaceable tech monopoly, even as institutional short sellers bet heavily on its downfall.

Rival Surge, Mega-IPOs, and What to Watch Next

As Nvidia stumbles, rival chipmakers are rapidly capitalizing on the power vacuum. Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) and Broadcom have emerged as the primary beneficiaries of this market realignment. Big Tech customers eager to derisk their hardware dependencies are accelerating orders with alternative vendors, converting what was once an Nvidia-dominated monopoly into a multi-polar competitive field.

At the same time, the broader AI investment landscape is undergoing a major recalibration. Foreign AI startup Anthropic is aggressively advancing plans for a massive $1.4 trillion initial public offering (IPO). A deal of this magnitude threatens to siphon substantial institutional liquidity away from traditional hardware stocks, redirecting capital toward software and foundation model developers just as hardware valuations come under fire.

As the market approaches next week's earnings release, several key indicators will determine whether the current crisis is a temporary shock or the beginning of a permanent shift in tech leadership:

  • Nvidia’s Q3 Guidance and Shipment Volumes: Wall Street will look past headline revenue numbers to inspect actual GPU shipment volumes and forward guidance, searching for early signs of order cancellations or margin compression resulting from the circular trading probe.
  • U.S. Congressional Action on Export Controls: Observers must monitor whether Washington enacts stricter statutory limits on indirect hardware exports through Southeast Asia, which would permanently shutter the $34 billion regional data center buildout.
  • South Korean HBM Supply Adjustments: Watch for upcoming strategic announcements from Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix regarding their HBM production schedules and capital expenditure plans in the U.S.
  • Diplomatic Positioning: Political analysts are closely watching upcoming U.S.-China bilateral summits. While reports indicate that former U.S. President Donald Trump's advisors do not plan to address the Jensen Huang scandal directly during upcoming diplomatic engagements, any shifting regulatory rhetoric could alter the compliance landscape for global chipmakers.

The golden era of unassailable hardware monopolies in the AI sector has met its most complex challenge yet. As Nvidia navigates regulatory investigations and a vacant corner office, the global semiconductor supply chain—stretching from Silicon Valley design boards to South Korean fabrication plants—is entering an era defined by fractured dominance and intense geopolitical competition.
