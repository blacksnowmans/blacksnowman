Why South Korea’s Stock Market is Lagging While the U.S. Hits Record Highs

October 07, 2026

Spend any time on Korean stock forums or reading economic news comment sections, and you will hear a recurring lament: "The U.S. market is hitting record highs every single day, so why is our market in this state?" As Samsung Electronics, South Korea’s flagship tech giant, prepares to announce its earnings, the atmosphere is thick with a blend of expectation and dread. To understand the current situation, we must look past the surface-level frustration and address why foreign investors have reduced their holdings in Korean stocks to a 13-year low.

The Nvidia Rally: Proof of Growth or a Bubble?

To understand the global backdrop, one must look at Nvidia. According to recent analysis by DBS, Singapore’s largest bank, Nvidia’s 12-month forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio sits at around 15. This is a far cry from the 100x P/E ratios seen during the dot-com bubble, which is why many on Wall Street remain confident in the current rally. While skeptics cry "bubble," it is hard to ignore a company where data center revenue accounts for nearly 90% of total sales with a 75% profit margin. The market has shifted from betting on "dreams" to valuing "actual performance."

As Nvidia’s dominant position strengthens, tech giants like Google, Amazon, and Meta are scrambling to design their own proprietary chips (ASIC). This isn’t necessarily a threat to the ecosystem but a sign of its expansion. In this environment, partners like Broadcom and Marvell Technology have emerged as key players. Marvell’s recent projection of $70 billion to $90 billion in revenue for fiscal year 2031 is not mere hubris; it reflects an explosion in demand from big tech companies that want to move beyond Nvidia’s standard offerings.

For South Korean semiconductor firms, this creates both opportunity and peril. While they are critical suppliers of HBM (High Bandwidth Memory), the question is how long they can maintain their "moat." Tech giants are even signing direct contracts with nuclear power companies to solve their energy shortages, showing a survival instinct that says, "I can build it myself." Investors should keep an eye on the "accounting trap"—the practice of extending the lifespan of infrastructure assets to artificially inflate profits. When the time comes to verify actual profitability, those who ignored the shadows behind the optimism may face a reckoning.

Samsung Electronics: The 100 Trillion Won Question

Foreign ownership of Samsung Electronics has fallen to the 46% range, the lowest level in 13 years. Why are foreigners dumping shares while the company is heading toward record-breaking performance? The answer is simple: they fear the stock will fall further, or they have already taken their profits elsewhere. Currently, the Korean market’s relative attractiveness has diminished compared to U.S. tech stocks.

It is statistically common to see foreigners sell during periods of corporate share buybacks, as they use the liquidity provided by the buyback to exit their positions. Once they see the "safety net" of a buyback, they rebalance their portfolios—a standard move for institutional investors. However, short-term supply-demand imbalances do not shake the fundamentals. Market forecasts suggest Samsung could see operating profits of 106 trillion won (about $76 billion) in the third quarter, proving it remains a massive cash cow.

Looking ahead, some analysts predict that next year, Samsung could enter a "600 trillion won" era of profitability, fueled by the rising price and market share of HBM. There is also the possibility that Samsung will increase its shareholder returns fourfold, which would push dividend yields to 6–7% and provide a strong floor for the stock price. This shift toward prioritizing shareholder returns is a necessary step toward aligning with global standards. While there are valid criticisms regarding the founding family’s stock-collateralized loans, the fact that the company is finally distributing a significant portion of its profits is a critical first step in restoring market trust.

Finding a Path Through the Volatility

Investors often lose sleep because their conviction is shaky or they have invested more than they can afford. True long-term investing begins with blocking out the market's noise. Rather than obsessing over whether foreigners are buying or selling on a given day, investors should focus on whether the company will remain central to the market three to five years from now.

We are currently at an inflection point. The imbalances in supply and demand will eventually resolve, and the company's fundamental value will be reflected in its share price. As we observe the current landscape, it is worth noting that South Korean companies are maturing alongside the broader social and technological shifts envisioned by the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung. Just as Samsung has spent decades building its competitive moat, investors must be willing to dig their own moat of patience.

Do not try to force the stock price to your will. Instead, maintain a long-term perspective and accompany the company as it realizes its future value. The volatility is a test, but for those who focus on the essential value of the businesses they hold, the path forward remains clear.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the actual earnings report. If the operating profit stays above the 100 trillion won "psychological defense line," the exodus of foreign capital is likely to reverse as the numbers force a reassessment of the company’s value.
