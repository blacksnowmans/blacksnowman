The $25 Billion Gamble: Why Seoul’s Yongsan Development Plan Risks Becoming a Ghost Town

October 10, 2026

Imagine standing in the heart of Seoul, staring at a massive, empty expanse of land that was once home to a foreign military base. For decades, this land in Yongsan has been the "last great frontier" of the South Korean capital. Recently, the Seoul Metropolitan Government unveiled an ambitious plan to transform this site into the “Yongsan International Business District.” The blueprint is staggering: a vertical city featuring a 100-story landmark skyscraper, with a total floor area 4.4 times larger than New York’s Hudson Yards. The goal is to generate over $25 billion (32.6 trillion won) in economic output and create 146,000 jobs by the early 2030s.

On paper, it looks like a visionary project to propel Seoul into the global top five. But beneath the polished renders lies a fundamental disconnect from reality. While the government of President Lee Jae-myung pushes forward with this massive infrastructure project, the world has undergone a tectonic shift over the last decade—a shift accelerated by a global pandemic and the AI revolution. Building a forest of 100-story office towers in an era where the very nature of “the office” is being dismantled is no longer just a bold bet; it is a dangerous, obsolete strategy that threatens to saddle the city with a monumental economic burden.

The Death of the Traditional Office

The Yongsan plan relies on a skeleton designed over a decade ago. While officials argue that the administrative processes have been followed, they are ignoring the new economic reality. First, the pandemic proved that work can be decentralized. Second, AI is drastically reducing the need for human personnel in traditional office roles.

The shift is visible globally. In Austin, Texas, and La Défense in Paris, office vacancy rates have surged past 25%. Employees are increasingly demanding hybrid work, with surveys indicating that nearly 60% of US workers prefer to be in the office only two or three days a week. Even as some companies attempt to force a return to the office, the competitive nature of talent acquisition ensures that flexible work is here to stay. Who, then, will fill these millions of square feet of luxury office space in Yongsan? The era of multinational corporations paying astronomical rents to house thousands of employees in prime real estate is effectively over.

The impact of AI only compounds this issue. Companies are aggressively cutting costs, and headcount reductions are the first order of business. Amazon, for instance, has embarked on a massive restructuring that includes plans to significantly reduce its workforce by 2033–2034, focusing heavily on AI-driven automation. When experts like Professor Park Jung-ho—a former research fellow at the Korea Development Institute—skeptically ask if even a handful of employees would actually occupy a new "Asia Headquarters" in Yongsan, the point is clear: the demand for massive office footprints is evaporating.

The Risk of a National Economic Disaster

If Seoul insists on flooding the market with more commercial space while vacancy rates in existing districts like Jongno and Euljiro are already climbing, the results could be catastrophic. We are already seeing the warning signs: new office buildings like G1 SEOUL are struggling with vacancy rates as high as 86%.

Adding a massive supply of premium offices to the market during a period of high interest rates and volatile project financing (PF) will only destabilize the sector further. If these towers are built and remain empty, it won’t just be a failure of urban planning—it will be a massive drain on the national economy. Furthermore, if companies simply relocate from older districts in central Seoul to the shiny, new Yongsan towers, the existing commercial centers will be hollowed out, leading to a "zero-sum" game that destroys the urban ecosystem of established neighborhoods.

A New Urban Grammar for the 21st Century

This is not to say that the Yongsan site lacks value. On the contrary, it is arguably the most valuable piece of real estate in the country. Because the land has been kept largely vacant due to its complex history—passing from Qing dynasty troops to Imperial Japanese forces, and finally to the U.S. military—it provides a rare, clean slate in the middle of a dense metropolis.

The challenge is to move away from the "office-only" mentality. As cities like New York and Paris have realized, the solution to the office vacancy crisis is not to build more offices, but to convert or repurpose space for mixed-use residential and innovative industrial needs. New York has been busy converting financial district office buildings into residential apartments, while France has passed legislation to incentivize the conversion of office space into housing.

Seoul suffers from chronic housing shortages and high real estate prices. Why not prioritize high-quality residential space integrated with incubators for AI-driven startups and smart-city infrastructure? Modern architecture has already proven that residential and commercial spaces can coexist in a single building with distinct, private access routes—as seen in some of Seoul’s own luxury mixed-use developments.

The Lee Jae-myung administration and the Seoul Metropolitan Government need to demonstrate the courage to halt. Continuing a project simply because it was started under previous administrations—like the era of the now-imprisoned and disgraced former leader, the late-regime figurehead often associated with failed development policies—is a path to failure. Being able to stop, re-evaluate, and pivot when the facts change is the mark of true leadership.

What to Watch Next

  • Policy Revisions: Watch for whether the city government introduces residential or mixed-use quotas into the Yongsan master plan. If they stick to a 100% office-oriented approach, the risk of a "white elephant" project will continue to rise.
  • Vacancy Trends: Monitor the quarterly office market reports for Seoul (such as those from RSQUARE). If vacancy rates in the CBD continue to climb, the pressure to abandon the "office-first" Yongsan model will intensify.
  • Public Discourse: Expect increased debate in the National Assembly regarding the financial viability of the project. As taxpayers, the public must decide whether this space should be a monument to 2010s-era planning or a functional, flexible hub for the future.

The Yongsan project is currently at a crossroads. By clinging to a 10-year-old dream of skyscrapers, we risk building the ghost towns of the future. By embracing a new urban grammar—one that prioritizes people, high-tech innovation, and residential necessity—we might actually salvage the last great opportunity in the heart of Seoul.
