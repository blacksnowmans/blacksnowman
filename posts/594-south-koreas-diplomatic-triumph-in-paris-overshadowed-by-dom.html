South Korea's Diplomatic Triumph in Paris Overshadowed by Domestic Turmoil

September 10, 2026

When you watch the evening news in Seoul, the contrast can give you whiplash. On one screen, images beam in from Paris, where South Korea and France are marking 140 years of diplomatic ties by upgrading their relationship to a "global strategic partnership" for the first time in 22 years. On the other screen, the familiar, muddy undertow of Korean domestic politics pulls you right back down: lingering investigations from previous administrations, bitter infighting within political parties, and a sliding approval rating for President Lee Jae-myung's administration. It is a striking reminder that a nation's government must fly with two wings—one reaching outward to the world, and one anchoring itself in the soil at home.

Before analysts can fairly parse the president's achievements abroad, the airwaves are flooded with post-mortems on botched prosecutions and sliding poll numbers. But behind the glittering lights of Paris and the gritty political bickering in Seoul lies a deeper story about where South Korea stands today—caught between its rising status as a global heavyweight and the persistent, self-inflicted wounds of its domestic political class.

The Tangled Wreckage of Past Prosecutions and Party Fractures

The messy reality of domestic politics was laid bare recently as commentators dissected the investigative tactics used during the previous administration of Yoon Suk Yeol, South Korea's former conservative president. Transcripts and testimonies revealed that prosecutors knew a key personal relationship involved a different individual entirely, yet prosecutors allegedly manufactured separate investigation reports in a desperate bid to link Democratic Party leader Lee Jae-myung to the case. This politically motivated push—coinciding with the Suwon District Office's handling of North Korea remittance allegations—ultimately fizzled out, resulting in weak dispositions of non-prosecution and suspended indictments.

Adding a layer of farce to the affair, former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon—who oversaw the prosecution during his time in office—publicly demanded via social media that the current Justice Minister explain why the case ended in a suspended indictment. To many observers, including some within the conservative People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party), watching a former minister fail to clean up his own department's mess and then point fingers at others felt like an open gift to the liberal opposition. It is a textbook example of political self-harm: using state investigative powers for political vendettas, only to watch the strategy collapse into public embarrassment.

Meanwhile, the liberal ruling Democratic Party is hardly immune to internal friction. Lingering factionalism from recent party conventions has spawned sharp, bitter rhetoric that drains the party's energy. President Lee has signaled a need for restraint and a culture of mutual respect—meeting with former President Moon Jae-in in an effort to mend fences—but deep-seated distrust is hard to wash away overnight. When a party spends its energy on internal sniping while professing a desire for reform, it breeds profound fatigue among the public.

Deconstructing the Approval Ratings and the Myth of Realignment

Inside the Blue House (the executive office and official residence of the South Korean president), strategists are wrestling with a slide in approval ratings that has persisted for over two months. The slump cannot be pinned on a single factor; it is a toxic blend of political missteps, policy errors, and tone-deaf communication. Chief among the political risks are the unresolved party fractures, while the fatal blow to public sentiment came from economic and policy miscalculations that hit ordinary citizens hard.

Policy confusion surrounding real estate tax regulations, compounded by a stock market shock earlier in the spring, dealt a harsh blow to retail investors—the "ants" who form a massive voting bloc. When the stock market was soaring toward the 9,000-point mark, the booming market was the Lee administration's strongest shield. But when share prices dipped, public frustration inevitably turned on policymakers. Governing is easy when the economy is roaring, but handling market volatility with honesty and practical alternatives is the true test of competence.

Amid these struggles, some commentators have floated theories of an impending political realignment, pointing to remarks made during President Lee's meeting with former President Moon. But past scenarios of forced mergers or political horse-trading make little sense in the current landscape. With the ruling party holding a commanding legislative presence and leading in public sentiment, there is no structural rationale for desperate political realignments. What the president meant by a "structural majority" is not a temporary electoral trick, but a fundamental reshaping of South Korea's political topography—moving past the old ideological tilt that heavily favored conservatives to build a durable base rooted in policy results and public trust.

A Diplomatic Milestone: The Strategic Partnership with France

Step away from the domestic noise, however, and South Korea's standing on the global stage tells a vastly different story. On the occasion of the 140th anniversary of bilateral relations, South Korea and France have elevated their ties to a "global strategic partnership" after a 22-year hiatus. During a joint press conference in Paris, French President Emmanuel Macron explicitly underscored the two nations' tight alignment on major international security and stability challenges.

This goes far beyond ceremonial diplomacy. The visit cemented real-world security cooperation, building on the revision of the military intelligence protection agreement agreed upon at an earlier summit. Joint exercises, such as the South Korean and French air forces' upcoming "Pegasus" drills and upcoming naval port calls, prove that this partnership is written in actions, not just diplomatic communiqués. For South Korea to forge this level of deep strategic coordination with a traditional European powerhouse like France is a clear testament to how far the nation's global status has evolved.

The scope of cooperation extends all the way to the volatile waters of the Middle East. President Macron highlighted the freedom of navigation and discussed multinational missions involving partners like the UK to reopen and secure the Strait of Hormuz. In an unstable global order where supply chains and energy corridors constantly shudder, South Korea is stepping up alongside France as a co-architect of international security. Beyond traditional security, the two nations inked a landmark 1 billion euro (about $1.1 billion) partnership to foster the video and film industries, blending economic and cultural diplomacy into a modern survival strategy for an uncertain world.

K-Culture in Paris and the Human Side of Summit Diplomacy

Away from the formal halls of power, the human side of state visits often reveals the true texture of international relations. Traveling journalists in Paris reported on local hotspots like the restaurant *Vendredi gourmand*, where inventive French menus incorporate traditional Korean ingredients like gimbal, wild herbs, and seasoned acorn jelly. Even notoriously hard-to-please food critics have broken into genuine praise, proving that Korean cuisine has moved far beyond a niche comfort food for expatriates to become a dominant cultural force in the heart of global gastronomy.

Even small, humorous mishaps find their way into the margins of high-stakes diplomacy—such as an episode where President Lee briefly misplaced his backpack amid a grueling schedule of meetings with the French National Assembly, the OECD, and local diaspora groups. While critics and late-night talk shows might chuckle, these human moments underscore the relentless, grinding pace of modern statecraft. Behind the stiff suits and flashing cameras, individuals are working grueling hours to advance the nation's interests.

What to Watch Next

As the dust settles on the Paris summit, several concrete developments will signal whether these diplomatic and domestic storylines translate into lasting change:

  • Follow-Through on Military Drills: Watch for the execution of the upcoming "Pegasus" joint air drills and naval port exchanges between South Korea and France in the coming weeks to measure the practical depth of the new strategic partnership.
  • Economic Policy Corrections: Monitor whether the Blue House and economic ministries roll out tangible relief or structural adjustments for retail investors and real estate markets to address the root causes of the administration's sliding approval ratings.
  • Ruling Party Discipline: Pay close attention to whether the Democratic Party can successfully quell its internal factionalism and present a unified front as upcoming legislative battles approach.

South Korea remains a nation living in two parallel realities: one bogged down by the bitter, inward-looking habits of partisan politics, and another soaring outward to forge critical alliances on the world stage. How soon the country can bridge that gap will determine whether its global achievements can finally heal the divisions at home.
