Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party Crisis: Ideological Drift, Online Echo Chambers, and the Threat to Prosecution Reform

September 24, 2026

Walking through Seoul's political landscape today feels entirely different from the celebratory mood that accompanied the inauguration of President Lee Jae-myung. Back then, supporters believed that sweeping aside decades of conservative rule would usher in an era of decisive, principled reform. Instead, political gatherings and online chat rooms are filled with sighs of frustration. Observers are asking a sharp question: how can a party that fought so hard to capture power find itself tearing apart from the inside only months into its term? The answer lies in a toxic mix of internet-driven politics, friction between the ruling party and the executive branch, and a growing sense of betrayal among the coalition's core supporters.

The Rise of "Femco Politics" and the Party Leader's Dilemma

At the center of this internal turbulence is Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal party). Rather than acting with the heavy-handed authority expected of a governing party leader, Kim has increasingly drawn criticism for practicing what critics call "Femco politics"—reacting knee-jerk style to memes and volatile sentiment on male-dominated online discussion boards like FMKorea, rather than sticking to principled party platforms.

This dynamic was laid bare when Kim publicly defended controversial personnel decisions made by the presidential office, arguing that the ultimate decision-maker’s burdens must be respected. To critics within his own camp, this sounded less like strategic discipline and more like complete capitulation. When ruling party lawmakers begin asking aloud, "Did we elect a king instead of a president?", it signals a breakdown in internal democracy. Instead of shaping the national debate, the party leadership appears to be acting as a rubber stamp for the Blue House.

This subservience has dealt a severe blow to the momentum behind prosecution reform, the core mandate that propelled Lee Jae-myung to the presidency. Generations of progressive voters demanded this reform to dismantle the unchecked power of state investigative agencies, specifically through the complete separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, alongside the elimination of supplementary investigation rights. Yet, key deadlines have slipped, and the appointment of figures with controversial past ties to previous conservative administrations has exposed deep fractures between party reformers and the leadership.

Erosion of the Separation of Powers and the Blue House Proxy Debate

The institutional danger here goes beyond bruised egos; it strikes at the heart of South Korea’s constitutional framework. The legislature was designed to serve as an independent check on the executive branch. When ruling party leaders abandon that oversight role to function as a praetorian guard for the president, they invite public backlash.

This tension recently spilled over into public view when veteran public intellectual Rhyu Si-min launched a scathing critique of the administration. Commenting on the president's remarks shifting the ultimate responsibility for reform onto his own shoulders, Rhyu noted that legislative power does not belong to the executive, declaring that such rhetoric sounds like something only a monarch would say.

While such direct attacks on the executive would normally trigger fierce retaliation from party loyalists, Rhyu’s comments struck a chord. Core Democratic Party supporters widely agreed, recognizing that the party had lost its independent voice. As administrative and legislative branches hardened into a rigid, top-down hierarchy reminiscent of South Korea's authoritarian past, voters who view internal democracy as an article of faith felt a profound sense of alienation.

Compounding the problem, the party’s online defenders have largely abandoned serious argumentation in favor of light mockery and defensive memes, failing to mount an effective intellectual defense against conservative attacks. Incidents like a recent clash where a cabinet minister bluntly told a Democratic lawmaker to take personal responsibility for a newly drafted bill—famously dubbed a "lawmaker's law"—only underscored how the executive branch views the legislature: as a subcontractor expected to rubber-stamp government guidelines rather than an equal partner.

Falling Poll Numbers and the Alienation of Core Supporters

Political miscalculations ultimately show up in the data. A regular weekly opinion poll conducted by Every Research for Every News illustrated a dramatic shift in public sentiment. The Democratic Party's approval rating plunged by 10.2 percentage points down to 31.7%, while the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, climbed 9.2 percentage points to 36.6%, pulling ahead within the margin of error.

This drop is not merely a temporary post-election cool-down. Traditional liberal strongholds—particularly voters in their 40s and 50s, as well as the Honam region in the country's southwest—are showing clear signs of fraying support. The combination of unresolved policy friction, administrative missteps, and a party leadership incapable of managing internal dissent has driven traditional supporters into silent disaffection.

Kim Min-seok’s current trajectory risks alienating virtually every faction of the broader progressive coalition. By marginalizing reform-minded lawmakers, alienating the base associated with prominent progressive figures, and ignoring the warnings of seasoned organizers, the leadership is effectively playing a dangerous game of political self-sabotage. If the core coalition that brought the government to power decides to walk away in frustration, the current administration's foundation will crumble.

What to Watch Next

As conservative legacy media outlets turn up the pressure by demanding a sweeping overhaul of the presidential staff and reviving past controversies surrounding key presidential aides, the Democratic leadership must decide whether to change course or double down.

  • Upcoming Legislative Fights: Watch how the party handles upcoming votes on pending prosecution reform bills and whether reformist lawmakers successfully claw back independent legislative authority from the executive branch.
  • Polling Trajectory: Monitor whether the recent drop in approval ratings stabilizes or solidifies into a long-term trend, particularly in key demographic regions like the greater Seoul area and traditional liberal provinces.
  • Intra-Party Coalitions: Pay attention to public statements from party heavyweights and factional leaders. If internal pushback against the current leadership turns from private grumbling into organized dissent, a major party leadership challenge could emerge ahead of upcoming local elections.

Politics remains a battle for the public trust, and that trust cannot be maintained through internet echo chambers or unquestioning obedience. Unless the ruling party bridges the widening gap between its leadership and its base, the current slump will prove to be only the beginning of a much deeper political reckoning.
