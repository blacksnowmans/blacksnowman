The Stepping Stone Revolt: Why South Korea’s Progressive Base Is Flocking to Cho Kuk

November 10, 2026

When an incumbent president’s approval rating drops to a career low, the standard playbook in South Korean politics assumes that the conservative opposition is reaping the rewards. Yet the latest tremor rattling the political landscape in Seoul reveals a far more complex internal crisis. President Lee Jae-myung, representing the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea, has watched his support sink to dangerous depths. But the voters walking away are not crossing over to the conservative People Power Party. Instead, they are stepping sideways into the open arms of the Rebuilding Korea Party, a fiercely combative third party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk.

For observers outside South Korea, this intra-bloc movement offers a rare window into the psychology of the country’s progressive electorate. Rather than an outright conservative resurgence, what is unfolding is an open rebellion by hardcore liberal voters against an administration they view as sluggish, compromised, and overly cautious.

The Slump: Why Lee Jae-myung’s Base Is Shifting

The warning signs arrived with stark clarity in recent polling conducted by Research View. President Lee Jae-myung’s positive job performance rating dropped by 2.9 percentage points in a week to hit 34.7%, setting an all-time low for his presidency. In South Korean governance, slipping into the mid-30% range indicates that an administration has shed nearly all moderate and swing voters, leaving behind only its hardened partisan base.

Particularly alarming for the Blue House is the steep decline among voters in their fifties. Long considered the demographic backbone of the liberal establishment—a generation shaped by South Korea’s pro-democracy movements of the late 1980s—voters in their fifties tend to judge politics through the pragmatic lens of governance and livelihood. Their withdrawal suggests widespread disappointment with the administration’s practical achievements and overall strategic direction.

This presidential slide dragged the Democratic Party down with it. The ruling party saw its support fall by more than two points, trailing the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, outside the statistical margin of error. Even more telling was the negative voter sentiment: when asked to name the political party they would least like to vote for, more than half of all respondents pointed to the Democratic Party. Crossing the 50% threshold in outright voter rejection mirrors the trajectory seen during the unraveling of the previous conservative administration, effectively shutting off the party’s ability to attract undecided moderates.

Meanwhile, the Rebuilding Korea Party surged to 8.6% overall—more than doubling its support compared to the period leading up to local elections—and secured over 20% backing among self-identified progressives. The math is undeniable: the ruling party's core voters are staging an exodus, and Cho Kuk is the primary beneficiary.

The “Stepping Stone” Theory and the Combative Liberal

To understand why this support has moved so swiftly, political commentator and author Rhyu Si-min offered a revealing metaphor during an appearance on the online political channel Park Si-young TV. Rhyu described the relationship between the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party as a "shallow stream crossed by stepping stones."

Between the Democratic Party and the conservative People Power Party lies a wide, impassable river like the Han River. But between the Democrats and Cho Kuk’s party, voters can simply hop across the rocks without getting their feet wet. It is a shared ecosystem.

At the center of this migration are South Korea’s "combative liberals"—a vocal, highly engaged segment of the progressive coalition that demands swift, uncompromising action on judicial, prosecutorial, and media reforms. To these voters, the Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic majority in the National Assembly have spent their governing mandate bogged down in political calculus and toothless calls for bipartisan compromise. The collective sigh from this flank is straightforward: Why can't you govern with some fire?

The Rebuilding Korea Party meets that demand with unvarnished ideological clarity. While critics brand the party's platform as polarizing, frustrated partisans view it as an antidote to Democratic hesitation. Crucially, as Rhyu pointed out, answering a pollster’s call to back Cho Kuk does not mean these voters have abandoned the administration permanently. Rather, it is a deliberate protest—a conditional defection designed to jolt President Lee and party leaders into action. If the Democratic Party demonstrates clear resolve and delivers on structural reforms, these voters can easily skip back across the stones. But if their complaints are brushed aside, temporary protest risks hardening into a permanent split.

Lessons from the Past and the Fragility of Power

The current impasse also illustrates a timeless rule of political power. As Rhyu observed, power by its very nature seeks to perpetuate and consolidate itself. Those sitting inside the ruling circle frequently fall victim to echo chambers, tuning out constructive criticism while mistaking internal loyalty for broad public approval.

South Korea offers an immediate cautionary precedent. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the conservative president systematically dismantled his own winning coalition. By driving out younger reformers like former party leader Lee Jun-seok and marginalizing consolidation partners like Ahn Cheol-soo, Mr. Yoon sought to reshape the ruling party around personal loyalists. That failure of political stewardship ultimately triggered widespread public revolt, ending in his impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment.

The lesson for President Lee is that no leader governs on individual charisma alone. A president sits atop a foundation built by decades of collective partisan mobilization. Once a leader takes that base for granted or allows party elites to focus entirely on factional preservation, the foundation buckles.

Furthermore, when public trust collapses, conventional policy announcements lose their utility. Introducing new economic stimulus packages, real estate measures, or livelihood subsidies fails to move the needle when voters no longer trust the administration's motives. Under an atmosphere of skepticism, even sensible policies are dismissed as short-term electoral pandering. Recovering from a 34% approval rating requires political humility, accountability, and genuine reform rather than an aggressive public relations spin.

From 12 Seats to the 200-Seat Battlefield

The Rebuilding Korea Party’s current leverage stems from its rapid, unconventional ascent. Launched in February 2024 at a press conference in Busan, the party was founded by Cho Kuk under the banner of directly confronting what he characterized as prosecutorial overreach. When election authorities ruled that the party could not use his personal name directly, organizers framed the name around the Sino-Korean word for "homeland" or "fatherland" (Cho-guk), adopting the English title "Rebuilding Korea Party."

Within a month of its launch, the upstart party surpassed 100,000 members. It crafted a proportional representation slate focused on sharp expertise and institutional confrontation:

  • Park Eun-jung, a former prosecutor who took high-profile internal stands against entrenched power within the prosecution service, secured the number-one spot.
  • Shin Jang-sik, a well-known progressive attorney and commentator, joined as the party’s first recruited talent.
  • The list incorporated specialized technocrats and former senior officials, including Google IT expert Lee Hae-min, former Seoul Institute president Seo Wang-jin, former Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service head Kim Sun-min, and former Korea National Diplomatic Academy chancellor Kim Joon-hyung.

In the 22nd General Election, this platform struck a powerful chord. The party captured 24.25% of the nationwide proportional vote, securing 12 seats in the National Assembly. While the Democratic Party dominated local constituencies, Cho Kuk’s outfit became an essential legislative "icebreaker"—pushing radical reform bills on prosecutorial restructuring that the ruling party was often hesitant to sponsor directly.

Yet this dynamic carries existential stakes for the broader liberal movement. Influential progressive commentator Kim Eo-jun has repeatedly warned that if infighting and disaffection continue to drag down overall approval ratings, the entire bloc could face disaster. In South Korea's unicameral parliament, 200 votes out of 300 constitute the supermajority threshold required to pass constitutional amendments, override presidential vetoes, or approve impeachment motions. If the Democratic Party remains widely rejected by more than half the public, holding or expanding that crucial leverage becomes mathematically impossible.

What to Watch Next

As the political calendar advances, the relationship between the Blue House, the Democratic Party, and Cho Kuk will determine whether South Korea's ruling coalition stabilizes or fragments further. Observers should track three key developments:

  • The Administration's Reform Calendar: Watch whether President Lee Jae-myung pivots toward aggressive legislative goals—particularly on long-delayed prosecutorial and media reform measures—to placate "combative liberals," or doubles down on centrist economic messaging to recover his standing in the polls.
  • The 50s Demographic Trajectory: Future polling will reveal whether the collapse among voters in their fifties is cyclical or structural. A failure to recover support among this key cohort will signal that the administration's broader economic program is losing credibility.
  • Tension in the National Assembly: Pay close attention to how the 12 lawmakers of the Rebuilding Korea Party leverage their position. Will they operate as coordinated legislative partners to pull the Democratic Party to the left, or will competitive posturing over local and future general election turf turn this shallow stream into an acrimonious divide?
