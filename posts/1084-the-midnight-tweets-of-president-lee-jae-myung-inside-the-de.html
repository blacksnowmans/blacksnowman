The Midnight Tweets of President Lee Jae-myung: Inside the Deepening Friction in South Korea's Ruling Camp

October 13, 2026

Late at night in Seoul, long after the presidential aides have cleared out of their offices, South Korean politics often takes an unpredictable turn on a smartphone screen. Instead of the choreographed press statements and measured diplomatic prose typical of an executive branch, public discourse has increasingly been jolted by personal, emotionally charged dispatches posted directly to X (formerly Twitter) by President Lee Jae-myung. When the leader of South Korea recently lashed out at critics using the term "filth" (omul), it was not just another passing online spat. It catalyzed a profound sense of exhaustion across the political spectrum and raised difficult questions about governance inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

For an administration that came into office promising to restore institutional stability and reset the political tone following the chaotic collapse and impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol—who is now serving time in prison—the descent into vitriolic online sparring feels painfully familiar. Rather than moving past the factional vendettas that have long paralyzed South Korean statecraft, the current administration finds itself bogged down in self-inflicted wounds, strange political compromises, and bitter internal battles.

The President's Digital Bullhorn and the Perils of Personal Feuds

To understand why a single online insult has generated such intense blowback, one has to appreciate the constitutional stature of the South Korean presidency. The office is endowed with sweeping executive powers, and by custom, the occupant is expected to embody national unity once elected, rising above the partisan mud-slinging of the National Assembly. When President Lee Jae-myung repeatedly takes to social media late at night to trade barbs with commentators, critics, and political adversaries, that institutional veneer cracks.

Labeling public criticism or political opponents as "filth" crosses a line that many veteran observers find difficult to defend. In the past, political figures who used crude language toward the press or the presidential press corps were eventually compelled to issue public apologies. Yet the current administration's reluctance to acknowledge the impropriety of the "filth" comment has compounded the damage. When a sitting president uses an informal personal platform as an emotional release valve, it signals to voters that the executive office is driven by personal grievance rather than deliberate policy. Analysts increasingly attribute the recent erosion of presidential approval ratings not to grand policy failures, but to this steady stream of impulsive digital confrontations.

The tragedy for the progressive bloc is that this self-sabotage is occurring while holding a dominant mandate. Instead of leveraging power to enact structural reforms, the administration is spending precious political capital defending off-the-cuff late-night posts that alienate moderate voters and deepen domestic cynicism.

Gift Boxes, Backroom Courtship, and the Shadow of Yoon Suk-yeol

Equally puzzling to observers of the ruling Democratic Party is the administration's clumsy attempt to win over hostile media voices. In South Korea, seasonal holiday gift boxes—especially during Chuseok, the autumn harvest festival—are deeply symbolic. Historically, presidents used these hampers filled with regional agricultural specialties to send subtle political signals of reconciliation and regional harmony.

Former President Roh Moo-hyun famously paired black raspberry wine from Mount Jiri with traditional confections from Hapcheon, bridging historical regional divides. The administration of Moon Jae-in championed eco-friendly, zero-waste packaging to underscore environmental priorities. Even conservative administrations navigated these cultural waters carefully: Lee Myung-bak once had to hastily swap out gifts of fresh anchovies for traditional tea sets to avoid offending the Buddhist monastic community, while Park Geun-hye recalibrated holiday gift costs to strictly adhere to the Kim Young-ran Act, the nation’s stringent anti-graft law.

Under the current government, however, the gift-giving apparatus appears to have transformed from a symbol of cultural diplomacy into an overt, transactional effort to placate conservative pundits. The presidential office has faced harsh scrutiny for aggressively dispatching commemorative watches and luxury gift sets to prominent right-wing commentators in an effort to blunt their daily broadsides. Far from softening the opposition, this outreach has backfired spectacularly. Reports and political rumors suggest that conservative influencers have simply pocketed the overtures while escalating their demands—even audaciously suggesting they will only cease their relentless criticism if the administration moves to grant a presidential pardon to the imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol.

Attempting to appease hardline right-wing critics through personal favors has left the administration looking desperate. To progressive supporters, watching their leadership bend over backward to court commentators who yearn for the restoration of the previous conservative order feels like an incomprehensible betrayal of principle.

Purges, Public Airwaves, and the Stifling of Youth Politics

While the administration attempts back-channel conciliation with conservative pundits, its attitude toward internal party dissent has been unsparing. Rather than focusing investigative energy and parliamentary oversight on confronting the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, key figures within the ruling camp seem preoccupied with settling scores inside their own tent.

Appointees with a track record of issuing harsh ultimatums to fellow party members have been slotted into influential roles across public broadcasting networks, fueling concerns about state media independence. Simultaneously, the imminent appointment of figures like Kim Ji-yong to head the newly restructured Central Serious Crime Investigation Office has stoked anxieties that state investigative power is being calibrated to neutralize political rivals rather than deliver blind justice. When power turns inward to prune internal dissent, it invariably signals an administration governed by paranoia rather than confidence.

This factional rigidity was on full display during the Democratic Party's recent youth speech contest, organized to mark the party's 71st anniversary. Promoted as an open forum for members aged 18 to 39 to debate democracy, peace, and social inclusion, the event instead exposed the hollow way modern political machines treat younger generations. Rather than offering real solutions to the crushing realities confronting young Koreans—skyrocketing urban housing costs, extreme job market precarity, and the world's lowest fertility rate—the stage was set to reward ideological conformity.

"Instead of handing young people a genuine microphone, the establishment turns them into parrots tasked with regurgitating the venomous rhetoric of party elders."

When young politicians feel compelled to take the stage and chant aggressive factional slogans—declaring that internal factions must be wiped out before diplomatic peace can be achieved—youth politics ceases to be an engine of renewal. It becomes an audition for patronage, alienating the very demographic the party desperately needs to retain.

The 15-Year Tax Hunt and the Partisan Echo Chamber

This obsession with factional squabbling has also distorted how the public consumes genuine governmental achievements. A striking case in point was the recent announcement by the National Tax Service (NTS). Led by Commissioner Lim Kwang-hyun, the tax agency's high-value delinquent task force achieved a historic milestone: recovering 100 billion won (about $75 million) in cash and securing collateral over oil tankers valued at more than 300 billion won (about $225 million), out of a staggering 400 billion won ($300 million) tax debt tied to the notorious shipping magnate Kwon Hyuk.

Pursuing hidden assets across foreign flags of convenience for 15 years, and coordinating with the Board of Audit and Inspection to clear legal hurdles, was an extraordinary administrative victory for tax justice. Yet the way this triumph was processed by South Korea's hyper-partisan YouTube ecosystem revealed the intellectual rot of contemporary political commentary. Dozens of political channels instantly flooded the internet with nearly identical, granular "behind-the-scenes" leaks, presenting administrative detective work as dramatic factional warfare.

In the past, political commentary channels drew hundreds of thousands of viewers by diving into substantive policy debates and serious intellectual discussions. Today, that depth has been largely supplanted by rapid-fire partisan gossip designed to trigger algorithmic dopamine hits. By reducing systemic institutional victories to conspiratorial theater, the media ecosystem starves citizens of meaningful policy analysis and further traps political leaders in a hall of digital mirrors.

Six Steps Away From the Brink: What to Watch Next

Political analysts and reform-minded scholars, such as Professor Jeon Kang-soo, argue that the administration can still reverse its sliding fortunes, but only through immediate and humbling course corrections. A clear path forward has been articulated across six key measures:

  • Break with factional fixers: Formally distance the executive office from hyper-partisan loyalists such as Kim Min-seok and aggressive inner-circle factions that promote ideological purity tests.
  • Put down the smartphone at night: Cease late-night, unvetted posting on X and YouTube. The president must prioritize rest, policy study, and deliberation over instant digital reactions.
  • Restore institutional communications: Retire personal accounts as policy channels. All presidential positions should be delivered through vetted, official spokesperson briefings.
  • Acknowledge past contradictions: Systematically review contradictory policy stances and communicate honestly with the public when approaches must shift.
  • Protect prosecutorial reforms: End the perceived weaponization of judicial appointments, ensuring new investigative bureaus are run by independent professionals rather than political loyalists.
  • Deliver a sincere public apology: Directly address the public and independent critics, acknowledging that referring to citizens or political opponents as "filth" was an unacceptable breach of presidential decorum.

The coming weeks will reveal whether President Lee Jae-myung and his inner circle possess the self-awareness required to step back from the edge. Observers should watch two key indicators: whether the administration proceeds with polarizing appointments to lead the Central Serious Crime Investigation Office, and whether the president formally retracts his disparaging online rhetoric. Power in South Korea is fleeting, and public patience is notoriously thin. If the leadership continues to confuse personal grievances with governance, the reckoning at the ballot box will be swift and unforgiving.
