Friendly Fire in Seoul: Inside the Democratic Party’s Civil War Over Reform and Jung Chung-rae

October 31, 2026

When South Korea emerged from the constitutional crisis sparked by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s botched martial law declaration, the path ahead for the liberal opposition seemed historically clear. Following Mr. Yoon’s dramatic impeachment and subsequent imprisonment, the center-left Democratic Party of Korea reclaimed executive power under President Lee Jae-myung. With the conservative People Power Party relegated to the opposition benches and discredited by its association with the unconstitutional decree, the new administration appeared poised to enact sweeping institutional reforms with an uncontested legislative majority.

Yet inside the National Assembly halls of Yeouido, the atmosphere within the ruling party is far from triumphalist. Instead, the Democratic Party is grappling with vicious internal infighting, plateauing approval numbers, and a war of attrition between entrenched party elites and reform-minded leaders. At the center of this storm sits lawmaker Jung Chung-rae, a veteran pugilist whose recent rise has exposed the deep, structural fault lines dividing South Korea’s ruling coalition.

From Committee Enforcer to Internal Target: The Paradox of Jung Chung-rae

For more than two decades, Jung Chung-rae has served as one of the most polarizing figures in Korean progressive politics. To the conservative establishment, he has long been public enemy number one—an unapologetic, aggressive polemicist whose verbal broadsides made him a frequent target of conservative media outlets and the People Power Party. Within his own party, however, his combative style was treated as an invaluable asset whenever the liberals found themselves on the defensive.

That dynamic was on full display during the darkest hours of Mr. Yoon’s tenure. As chairman of the National Assembly’s powerful Legislation and Judiciary Committee—the institutional bottleneck through which almost all critical legislation must pass—Jung was the Democratic Party’s primary battering ram. During the martial law decree and the frantic push for constitutional impeachment, Jung stood on the front lines, managing committee procedures and leading the charge against executive overreach. At the time, progressive commentators, party officials, and grassroots supporters universally celebrated his tenacity.

The praise curdled into hostility almost the moment the external threat receded. Once the Lee Jae-myung administration was inaugurated and Jung began positioning himself for the party’s supreme leadership, the nature of the attacks against him changed entirely. The most damaging salvos were no longer arriving from conservative opponents, but from within the ranks of his own party.

As Jung himself recently remarked to colleagues:

“When I served as the Judiciary Committee chairman fighting the opposing trench, no one inside our camp tried to demonize me. Everyone was applauding. The attacks only began when I stepped up to contest the party leadership.”

This dynamic illustrates a familiar pattern in Korean party politics: frontline combatants are lionized when battling external rivals, only to be branded dangerous loose cannons the second they seek to translate that battlefield prominence into institutional party control.

The ‘Faction’ Label as a Weapon Against Internal Reform

The primary weapon deployed against Jung and his allies on the Democratic Party’s Supreme Council is factional pigeonholing. Critics within the party and sympathetic commentators have taken to branding these reformist leaders as the “pro-Jung faction” (chin-cheong-gye). In Korean political discourse, attaching the prefix chin- (meaning "pro-") to an individual's name is rarely an objective analytical description; it is an accusation designed to reduce policy-driven initiatives to self-serving clique politics.

By labeling reformist Supreme Council members as a personal coterie, internal opponents seek to delegitimize their proposed structural overhauls. What these lawmakers present as a mandate from the party base—such as strengthening grassroots voting weight and expediting judicial cleanups—is instead dismissed as factional maneuvers intended to consolidate Jung’s personal machine.

This factional trench warfare is creating profound disillusionment among the Democratic Party’s newer members. Over the past year, tens of thousands of citizens joined the party not out of unconditional affection for its establishment, but out of defensive necessity. Many viewed the Democratic Party not as a perfect vessel, but as the only functioning constitutional firewall capable of checking autocratic backsliding.

As one grassroots member who officially registered in early 2024 put it, joining the party was not an endorsement of perfection:

“It was not the best choice, but in that moment, the Democratic Party was the only realistic vehicle standing between constitutional democracy and collapse. We chose it because it was the only road available, not because it was spotless.”

For these rank-and-file members, seeing senior party figures expend their energy on factional sabotage rather than enacting promised reforms is an alarming regression to the pre-crisis status quo.

Disinformation, Plateauing Polls, and the Cho Kuk Option

As the internal leadership contest intensifies, the smear campaigns directed at Jung have crossed into outright absurdity. One viral rumor spread through online communities and hostile YouTube channels claimed that Jung was closely related to the leader of the notorious JMS religious cult, alleging that the sect founder was his uncle simply because the two men shared a surname and regional roots.

The smear grew so noisy that investigative journalists from The Chosun Ilbo and Weekly Chosun—mainstays of South Korea’s conservative media landscape, who hold no affection for Jung—actually dispatched reporters to examine the official genealogical registry of the Hadong Jung clan. The physical records confirmed that the rumor was completely baseless. The episode provided a stark illustration of Yeouido's current information climate: internal progressive whisper networks had circulated a fabricated smear so crude that even the opposition press ended up debunking it.

Underneath this toxic noise lies genuine electoral anxiety. Despite holding the presidency under Lee Jae-myung, the Democratic Party’s public approval ratings have hit a stubborn plateau. Many backbench lawmakers privately worry that if the ruling party fails to deliver clear, tangible improvements to governance and the economy, public disillusionment will set in rapidly. Voters who mobilized to punish Mr. Yoon’s administration are now evaluating the Democratic Party on its executive performance rather than its opposition credentials.

To break this political deadlock, reformist lawmakers have begun openly advocating for formal strategic coordination—or even an outright merger—with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the hardline reformist party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. Proponents argue that bringing Cho’s unapologetic, reform-focused coalition formally into the fold would rejuvenate the ruling party’s stalled agenda and silence conservative elements inside the party apparatus who favor cautious incrementalism.

What Lies Ahead: Grassroots Mobilization vs. Elite Inertia

The outcome of this internal struggle will determine the trajectory of the Lee Jae-myung presidency’s legislative agenda. For the Democratic Party to function as an effective governing party, it must resolve the friction between its institutional establishment and its mobilized base.

Some party operatives have attempted to engineer artificial support organizations—attempting to manufacture a top-down version of "Nosamo," the legendary voluntary fan club that catapulted the late President Roh Moo-hyun to power in 2002. Yet history suggests such efforts invariably falter. Nosamo succeeded precisely because it was an authentic, bottom-up movement of ordinary citizens refusing to bow to party bosses, not a bureaucratic instrument handed down from Yeouido headquarters.

Jung Chung-rae’s response to the campaign against him has leaned heavily into this dynamic. Rather than engaging in a mudslinging match over every internet fabrication, his strategy relies on what he terms the "equal and opposite reaction" of informed party members. While the party has initiated legal filings against malicious defamation, the political defense is being waged in online comment sections, grassroots committees, and local rallies where rank-and-file members are actively rejecting factional smear campaigns.

In the coming months, two key developments will signal which faction is winning this contest: first, whether the Democratic Party formalizes an institutional alliance or merger talks with Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party; and second, whether the party’s upcoming internal elections reward grassroots-backed reformists or the traditional factional machine. If the ruling party allows itself to be consumed by internal witch hunts, it risks squandering the mandate granted to it after one of the most perilous constitutional crises in South Korea’s modern history.
