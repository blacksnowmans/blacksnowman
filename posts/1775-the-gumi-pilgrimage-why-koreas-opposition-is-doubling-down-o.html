The Gumi Pilgrimage: Why Korea’s Opposition is Doubling Down on the Past

October 31, 2026

The air in Gumi, an industrial city in North Gyeongsang Province, carries a specific weight for South Korean conservatives. It is the birthplace of Park Chung-hee, the military strongman who ruled the country for nearly two decades and is credited with the "Miracle on the Han River." For a politician in the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, a visit to this modest thatched-roof house is more than a photo opportunity—it is a ritual of survival. On October 30, Jang Dong-hyeok, the representative of the PPP, stood before the portrait of the late leader, seeking more than just historical inspiration. He was seeking a way to anchor a party that feels increasingly adrift in the era of the Lee Jae-myung administration.

Jang’s visit comes at a precarious moment. While the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) consolidated power following the landmark impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the conservative opposition has struggled to find a coherent identity. Instead of pivoting toward the center to reclaim the moderate voters who abandoned them during the chaotic final days of the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol administration, Jang Dong-hyeok has chosen a different path: a retreat into the fortress of the hard-right. By invoking the legacy of Park Chung-hee and simultaneously reigniting incendiary claims of election fraud, Jang is signaling that the PPP’s strategy for the 2026 elections will not be built on policy innovation, but on grievance and the mobilization of the "concrete" conservative base.

The Shrine of Modernization and the Politics of Nostalgia

To understand why Jang Dong-hyeok traveled to Gumi, one must understand the unique status of Park Chung-hee in the Korean psyche. To his critics, he was a dictator who suppressed labor and human rights; to his supporters, he is the "Father of Modernization" who transformed a war-torn agrarian society into an industrial powerhouse. In the current political climate, where the conservative party feels besieged by the DPK-led government, Park’s image serves as a powerful shield.

During his visit, Jang was vocal in his defense of this legacy. "Even if history is distorted, the history of our fatherland’s modernization will not be shaken," he declared. This rhetoric is a direct jab at the current government’s efforts to re-evaluate the authoritarian period. By positioning himself as the protector of "true" Korean history, Jang is attempting to unify a fractured party under a single, emotive banner. However, for many international observers and younger Korean voters, this focus on the 1960s and 70s feels disconnected from the pressing issues of the 2020s, such as the housing crisis, record-low birth rates, and the rapidly shifting tech landscape.

Jang’s actions in Gumi went beyond rhetoric. He engaged in volunteer work in nearby rural villages, attempting to project an image of a "leader of the people." Yet, it was his message in the visitor’s log that caught the eye of political analysts: "Those who stop do not win." While ostensibly a call for persistence against the ruling party, it is widely interpreted as a declaration of internal war. Jang is currently facing significant pressure from within his own party to step down or reform, and this message was a clear signal that he intends to hold onto the leadership at any cost. This "siege mentality" is becoming the defining characteristic of the PPP’s current leadership.

The Stolen Election Narrative and the Attack on the NEC

The most controversial aspect of Jang’s recent maneuvers is his decision to lean into election conspiracy theories. Following his visit to the Park Chung-hee residence, Jang intensified his attacks on the National Election Commission (NEC), the independent agency responsible for overseeing the country’s voting processes. He has called for a "Special Prosecutor" (teuk-geom)—a high-level independent investigative body usually reserved for major state scandals—to probe alleged irregularities in previous elections.

Jang’s language has been remarkably blunt. He accused the NEC of "acting like thieves, running away with ballot boxes," and claimed that the current investigations are a sham. He is demanding a "scientific verification" of physical ballots, a move that mimics the "Stop the Steal" rhetoric seen in other global democracies. This is a dangerous gamble. The NEC is a constitutional institution, and undermining its credibility is often seen as an attack on the foundations of South Korean democracy itself.

Interestingly, Jang has recently begun to shift his terminology slightly, a move that suggests he is aware of the legal and social risks of his position. While he previously spoke definitively of "fraud," he now uses the phrase "whether it was fraud or simple mismanagement (busil-seon-geo), the truth must be revealed." This subtle hedging allows him to keep the fire of the conspiracy-minded base burning while providing a slim degree of plausible deniability should legal challenges arise. However, this "calculated ambiguity" has drawn sharp criticism from the ruling DPK. Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the Democratic Party’s leadership, dismissed Jang’s claims as a "cheap trick" designed solely to protect his grip on party power rather than to improve electoral integrity.

A House Divided: The Conservative Civil War

Jang Dong-hyeok’s hardline pivot has not only alienated the ruling party but has also deepened the fissures within the conservative camp. The People Power Party is currently split into three main factions, each represented by a prominent figure in recent polling. According to a recent survey by Daehan Economy, the "suitability" for the next conservative leader is a tight three-way race:

  • Oh Se-hoon (19.2%): The Mayor of Seoul, who represents the moderate, metropolitan wing of the party. He focuses on pragmatic governance and has been vocal about distancing the party from extreme right-wing conspiracy theories.
  • Jang Dong-hyeok (15.4%): The current representative, holding the support of the traditional, rural, and hardline base.
  • Han Dong-hoon (13.9%): The former interim leader, who remains a popular figure among those who want a "cleaner" but still aggressive opposition style.

The fact that Oh Se-hoon is leading, even within the margin of error, is a significant problem for Jang. Oh has reportedly begun "distancing" himself from Jang’s leadership, with sources close to the Mayor suggesting that the party cannot win a general election if it remains obsessed with election fraud claims and 1970s nostalgia. Even traditional conservative icons are turning on Jang. Cho Gap-je, a veteran conservative journalist known for his fierce anti-communism, recently mocked Jang’s logic. Cho argued that if Jang truly believed the elections were fraudulent, he should be calling for the resignation of conservative local officials like Oh Se-hoon who were elected in the same cycles. "You can't have it both ways," Cho remarked, highlighting the logical inconsistency that plagues the "stolen election" narrative.

This internal friction creates a strategic trap. To stay in power as the party representative, Jang needs the 15% of the hardcore base that believes in the conspiracy theories. But by catering to that 15%, he makes the party toxic to the 20-30% of moderate swing voters needed to actually win a national election against President Lee Jae-myung’s DPK. The PPP is effectively choosing between internal stability and external viability.

The Shadow of the Past and the Road to 2026

The current political landscape in Korea is still heavily defined by the fallout of the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol administration. Mr. Yoon’s impeachment and subsequent imprisonment left a power vacuum in the conservative movement that has yet to be filled by a unifying figure. In this vacuum, figures like Jang Dong-hyeok are reverting to the tactics of the "Yoon Suk-yeol era"—confrontational rhetoric, the judicialization of politics, and the demonization of state institutions.

However, the effectiveness of these tactics is waning. During the Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol 재임 시절 (time in office), the country saw a period of extreme polarization that many citizens are now eager to move past. President Lee Jae-myung’s government has capitalized on this fatigue, positioning itself as the party of stability and "livelihood politics" (minsaeng). When Jang Dong-hyeok spends his days in Gumi talking about ballot boxes and the 1960s, he inadvertently strengthens the DPK’s narrative that the conservatives are a party of the past, incapable of governing a modern, high-tech democracy.

The "scientific verification" of ballots that Jang demands would come at a significant social and financial cost. More importantly, it risks further eroding trust in the democratic process among a populace that has already been through the trauma of a presidential impeachment. If the main opposition party refuses to accept the legitimacy of the system, the resulting political instability could affect Korea’s international standing and market confidence, especially as the country navigates a complex geopolitical environment involving its neighbors and the global supply chain.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will determine whether the PPP continues its slide into the political wilderness or finds a way back to the center. There are several concrete developments to monitor:

  • The Leadership Challenge: Watch for a formal move by the Oh Se-hoon or Han Dong-hoon factions to trigger a leadership race. Jang’s "no surrender" stance makes a quiet transition unlikely.
  • The Special Prosecutor Bill: The PPP is expected to formally introduce a bill for a special prosecutor to investigate the NEC. The DPK will almost certainly block this in the National Assembly, but the debate itself will be a barometer of how far the PPP is willing to push the fraud narrative.
  • Public Opinion Shifts: If Jang’s approval ratings continue to stagnate while the party’s overall support drops, the pressure from the party’s financial donors and regional chapters in the "Gyeongsang" heartland may finally force a change.

South Korea’s democracy has proven its resilience in the face of massive upheaval before. However, the health of that democracy depends on having a "loyal opposition"—a party that criticizes the government fiercely but respects the rules of the game. By choosing the incense of Gumi and the rhetoric of election denial, Jang Dong-hyeok is testing that resilience in ways that may haunt the Korean conservative movement for years to come.
