Justice on Trial: South Korea’s Week of Judicial and Political Reckoning

November 19, 2026

The corridors of the National Assembly in Seoul are rarely quiet, but on Monday, November 18, the air was particularly thick with the scent of a brewing storm. As the Democratic Party (DP), South Korea’s ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, pushed forward with its judicial reform agenda, a series of court rulings and hearing schedules collided to create a high-stakes political theater. For an observer outside Korea, the events might seem like a flurry of disjointed legal battles. However, they are all chapters in a single story: the ongoing struggle to redefine the nation’s legal boundaries following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The day was marked by three major developments. First, the confirmation hearing for the next Minister of Justice was set for November 24, a role crucial for President Lee’s administration. Second, a lower court acquitted Choo Kyung-ho, the floor leader of the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), of charges related to the martial law crisis during the previous administration. Third, in a move toward unprecedented transparency, the Supreme Court announced it would live-stream the final verdicts for Kim Keon-hee (the wife of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol) and Han Duck-soo (the former Prime Minister). This confluence of events has pushed the standoff between the government and the opposition to its most extreme point this year.

The Battle for the Ministry of Justice

The Ministry of Justice has long been the most sensitive seat in the Korean cabinet. It is the bridge between the executive branch and the prosecution—an agency that, in South Korea, holds immense power to initiate investigations that can make or break political careers. On Monday, the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee officially adopted a plan to hold a confirmation hearing for Lee Yong-cheol, the nominee for Minister of Justice, on November 24.

This is not just a routine appointment. For the Lee Jae-myung government, Lee Yong-cheol is the designated architect of "prosecution reform." This phrase, a cornerstone of the current administration’s platform, refers to the systematic effort to strip the prosecution of its investigative powers and transform it into a body primarily focused on indictments and human rights oversight. The nominee is seen as the "right person at the right time" to finish this task, which the government argues is necessary to prevent the legal system from being used as a political tool—a charge frequently leveled against the previous era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The main opposition People Power Party has already signaled a "total war" for the hearing. They intend to scrutinize the nominee’s past, his moral standing, and his specific roadmap for the ministry. More importantly, the PPP views this appointment as a test of the Lee administration’s judicial philosophy. For the opposition, "reform" is a euphemism for "neutering" investigations into current power players. With the hearing set for a Sunday, an unusual timing that reflects the urgency of the situation, the public will be watching to see if the nominee can survive a barrage of questions regarding the separation of powers and the future of Korean law enforcement.

A Controversial Acquittal and the Martial Law Shadow

While the National Assembly was debating the next minister, a courtroom in Seoul delivered a verdict that sent shockwaves through the political establishment. Choo Kyung-ho, the PPP’s floor leader, was found not guilty in his first trial on charges of obstructing the lifting of a martial law order. To understand why this matters, one must recall the final, chaotic months of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency, where attempts to declare martial law to suppress protests led to charges of insurrection against several top officials.

The court’s reasoning for the acquittal was technical but significant: it ruled that there was no objective proof that Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol had issued specific instructions or requests to Choo to block the lifting of the order. Essentially, the court found that the prosecution failed to provide a "smoking gun" linking the current opposition leader to the illegal actions of the then-President. While legally sound under the principle of "evidence-based judgment," the ruling has been met with fierce backlash from the ruling party.

The Democratic Party immediately labeled the verdict a "historical betrayal." They argue that the judiciary is hiding behind legal technicalities to grant immunity to those who threatened the foundation of Korean democracy. Within the ruling camp, there is a growing sentiment that the judiciary is becoming increasingly conservative under Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Some members of the minor Rebuilding Korea Party have even floated the idea of impeaching Chief Justice Cho, arguing that the courts have lost their ability to self-regulate and are now shielding remnants of the previous "insurrectionist" regime. This acquittal has not calmed the political waters; it has turned a legal dispute into a fundamental debate about the legitimacy of the judicial system itself.

Sunlight in the Courtroom: Live-Streaming the Elite

Perhaps the most modern and jarring development is the Supreme Court’s decision to live-stream the final (third-instance) verdicts for Kim Keon-hee and Han Duck-soo on November 19. In South Korea, broadcasting court proceedings is extremely rare, usually reserved for cases of immense national importance where public interest outweighs the privacy of the defendants. The court’s decision to allow cameras is a clear signal that it recognizes the deep public mistrust currently plaguing the legal system.

The cases involve Kim Keon-hee, who has faced various allegations ranging from stock manipulation to ethical breaches during her time as first lady, and Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Interestingly, both defendants are expected to be absent from the courtroom—a legal right in South Korea for final verdicts—meaning the public will watch a judge read their fate to an empty dock.

This transparency is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it allows the public to hear the legal logic directly from the bench, potentially dispelling rumors of "backroom deals." On the other hand, it turns the judiciary into a media spectacle. The stakes are particularly high for Kim Keon-hee; her verdict will likely set the tone for several other ongoing investigations. If she is acquitted, the opposition will claim the court is biased; if she is convicted, it will be seen as the final nail in the coffin of the previous administration’s legacy. By broadcasting the event, the Supreme Court is essentially saying, "Watch us and judge for yourselves," a risky move for an institution currently under fire from all sides.

The "Activism Privilege" Standoff in the Assembly

Beyond the courtrooms, a legislative battle is brewing over the Democratic Meritorious Persons Act. This proposed law seeks to provide official recognition and financial support to those who were injured or killed during South Korea’s various pro-democracy movements—particularly those in the 1980s that eventually led to the end of military rule.

The Democratic Party views this as a long-overdue act of justice to honor those who built the nation's democracy. However, the People Power Party has dubbed it the "Movement Activists’ Privilege Act." They argue that the criteria for becoming a "meritorious person" are too vague and could be used to funnel taxpayer money to the ruling party’s political base—the aging generation of student activists who now form the core of the DP.

The PPP has threatened a filibuster (a marathon speech intended to delay a vote) if the bill is brought to the floor on November 19. In South Korea, a filibuster can effectively paralyze the entire legislature, halting even urgent economic bills. This ideological clash highlights a deep-seated divide in Korean society: one side sees the 1980s activists as the heroes of the nation, while the other sees them as a "new elite" that has become just as entrenched and entitled as the dictators they once fought.

What to Watch Next

The coming days will act as a barometer for the stability of the Lee Jae-myung administration and the resilience of the Korean opposition. Here are the concrete milestones to monitor:

  • November 19 (Tuesday): The Supreme Court’s live broadcast of the Kim Keon-hee and Han Duck-soo verdicts. Pay close attention to the specific legal reasoning the judge uses—this will be the "law of the land" for any future investigations into the previous administration.
  • November 19 (Tuesday): The National Assembly plenary session. Watch for whether the PPP actually begins a filibuster. If they do, the legislative process could be frozen for days, signaling a complete breakdown in communication between the two major parties.
  • November 24 (Sunday): The Lee Yong-cheol confirmation hearing. This will be the most direct confrontation between the DP’s reform agenda and the PPP’s defensive strategy. Look for how the nominee handles questions about the "political neutrality" of the prosecution.

For those watching from abroad, these events reveal a South Korea that is still aggressively processing its recent past. The transition from the "Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol era" to the "Lee Jae-myung government" was not just a change of leaders, but a total shift in the country's political and judicial gravity. This week, we will see whether that new gravity can hold, or if the system will continue to fracture under the pressure of its own history.
