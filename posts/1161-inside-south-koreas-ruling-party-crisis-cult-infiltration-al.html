Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Crisis: Cult Infiltration Allegations, Judicial Showdowns, and the Struggle for Livelihood Politics

October 15, 2026

October in Yeouido—South Korea's political heartland, home to the National Assembly—is rarely quiet, but mid-October brought a convergence of shocks that laid bare the volatile currents running through the ruling party. As the annual government audit entered its mid-point, the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's center-left ruling party, found itself grappling with a multi-front war: an explosive internal scandal involving thousands of members tied to a notorious religious sect, a direct and intensifying confrontation with the judiciary, and an urgent push to prove its competence on everyday economic and social issues.

For observers outside South Korea, watching these developments offers a rare window into the fierce, high-stakes mechanics of Korean party politics, where moral authority, grassroots organizing, and institutional power collide on a daily basis.

The Shincheonji Infiltration Scandal and Factional Fractures

The morning of October 14 brought what was easily the most damaging headline of the week for the ruling party: allegations that Shincheonji, a controversial religious movement widely classified in South Korea as a cult, had systematically planted more than 30,000 members into the party to manipulate internal primary elections.

During a Supreme Council meeting, Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok publicly confirmed that the party had uncovered substantial evidence of organized mass enrollments and testimonies pointing to primary interference. Senior party figures like Supreme Council member Jeong Chung-rae demanded a thorough investigation and a formal referral for criminal prosecution, framing the issue as an existential threat to democratic integrity. The conservative opposition, the People Power Party, quickly seized on the opening, demanding a special counsel investigation and raids on party membership registries.

Yet the external pressure was matched, if not exceeded, by internal discord. As party leaders debated how to handle the fallout, a bitter public feud erupted over leaked closed-door meeting details. Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee lambasted the leak as a severe act of betrayal, while other factional leaders clashed openly with party chief Kim Min-seok over unverified claims circulating in the press. For a ruling party whose public support has stagnated, this kind of public infighting risks squandering its political capital precisely when unity is most required.

Taking Aim at the Judiciary and the Opposition

While managing the religious sect controversy, the Democratic Party simultaneously ratcheted up its pressure on the judicial branch to its absolute limit. Party leader Kim Min-seok issued a stern warning to Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de, declaring that the time for the top judge to preserve his honor had passed and announcing that the party is actively exploring all options—including impeachment—to hold him accountable for what it terms systematic violations of the constitution and laws.

The push for judicial accountability is backed by progressive civic groups who argue that a judiciary tainted by political controversy has lost public trust. Naturally, the opposition People Power Party has fired back, accusing the ruling camp of shredding the constitutional principle of the separation of powers. But the ruling party's leadership appears determined to press ahead, framing the showdown as a necessary reckoning to clear away old institutional alignments.

At the same time, ruling party floor leader Han Byung-do lashed out at the opposition for aggressive rhetoric and misconduct during the ongoing parliamentary audits. Accusing opposition lawmakers of prioritizing viral insults over the ability to govern, the ruling party urged a change in tone while accelerating its own legislative agenda, including post-audit reforms and laws governing investigative procedures.

Shifting Focus to Regional Livelihoods and Practical Policy

Away from the high-drama clashes in Seoul, local party chapters and regional governments attempted to ground the day's politics in tangible public needs. In Hwaseong, a growing city south of Seoul, local Democratic Party officials held a joint policy consultation with municipal leaders, hammering out 15 livelihood-focused local projects ranging from ecological waterfront spaces to children's science museums and vital transit upgrades.

On a national policy level, party leadership rolled out concrete measures aimed at easing the financial burdens on younger citizens, announcing plans to expand free vaccinations for cervical cancer to all women aged 18 to 26, alongside ongoing pushes for stronger protections against relationship-related crimes and temporary tax relief on everyday goods.

These policy rollouts reflect a hard truth recognized by strategists across the political spectrum: while ideological battles and institutional purges dominate the headlines, voters ultimately judge political parties by the quality of their daily lives.

"Politics must eventually return to its core purpose of making people's daily lives easier. No amount of grand political rhetoric can replace the tangible relief of fixing local problems one by one."

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves deeper into the autumn political calendar, several critical markers will determine whether the Democratic Party can successfully navigate this turbulence:

  • The Handling of the Shincheonji Probe: Whether the party can transparently investigate and excise the alleged 30,000-plus cult-affiliated members without triggering a destructive civil war between party factions.
  • Judicial Showdown Momentum: How far the ruling party actually takes its threats of impeachment against the Supreme Court Chief Justice, and whether this escalates into a prolonged constitutional crisis.
  • Legislative Delivery: Whether the party can translate its aggressive reform agenda into concrete parliamentary outcomes before public fatigue over political infighting drains its momentum entirely.

For a ruling party balancing high-stakes institutional reform against internal dissent, the margin for error is shrinking. The ultimate test will not be how fiercely it fights its political opponents, but whether it can restore public trust through transparency and tangible results.
