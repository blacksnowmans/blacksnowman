The Lula Lesson: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Must Abandon the Myth of the Moderate

October 27, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, a peculiar silence has settled over the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). Despite holding the presidency under Lee Jae-myung and maintaining a firm grip on the legislative agenda, the air is thick with a sense of stagnation. The "Sajida" (refreshing cider-like) energy that propelled President Lee Jae-myung to power—driven by his reputation for bold, uncompromising execution—seems to have hit a plateau. Outside, the core supporters who once marched in the streets to demand the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president now serving a prison sentence, are beginning to grow cold. They watch as the government hesitates, seemingly paralyzed by the fear of offending the "moderate" voter or triggering a backlash from the conservative press.

It is in this climate of hesitation that the political ghost of Brazil’s Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has begun to haunt the South Korean left. The lesson from Lula’s dramatic comeback victory is as sharp as a razor: if you try to please everyone, you end up trusted by no one. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, which currently finds itself at a crossroads between bold reform and cautious centrism, the Brazilian experience offers a stern warning. Victory does not come to those who dilute their identity to seek a non-existent middle ground; it comes to those who provide a physical, material reason for their base to show up at the ballot box.

The Miracle of the 80.8 Percent Turnout

To understand why the Brazilian election matters to a politician in Seoul, one must look at the sheer impossibility of Lula’s reversal. After the first round of voting, Lula trailed the right-wing incumbent, Jair Bolsonaro, by 2.2 million votes. Conventional political wisdom suggested that the right was surging and that Lula’s path to the presidency was blocked by a wall of conservative momentum. Yet, when the final tallies were counted, Lula had not only closed the gap but won by a margin of 4.4 million votes, securing 51.76% of the total.

The secret was not a sudden shift in the hearts of right-wing voters. It was a historic 80.8% voter turnout—the highest since 2006. While political pundits often talk about "winning over" the other side, Lula’s camp realized that elections are won by the people who actually show up. They identified that the biggest barrier to victory wasn't political disagreement, but physical and economic exhaustion. In the poverty-stricken Northeast and among the urban youth, the cost of a bus ticket was a literal barrier to democracy.

Lula’s response was a masterclass in material politics. He didn't just offer speeches about the "future of Brazil"; he pushed for a constitutional amendment to provide free public transportation on election weekends. He promised to cancel the federal tax debts of small-scale entrepreneurs and pledged a 15% increase in "Bolsa Família," Brazil's landmark social welfare program. These weren't abstract ideological goals; they were tangible, "cider-like" solutions that made it economically rational for a poor person to spend their Sunday at a polling station. For the DPK, currently struggling to energize a base that feels the "impeachment spring" has turned into a "governance winter," the message is clear: the base moves when the policy touches their wallet and their daily survival.

The Fatal Temptation of Appeasement

In South Korea, the term "moderate expansion" (jungdo hwakjang) is treated like a holy grail by political consultants. The logic is that the core base is already "in the bag," so the party must move toward the center to capture the swing voters. However, Lula’s recent speeches offer a scathing critique of this strategy. He has warned that the first commandment for a progressive leader must be consistency.

"We must not win on one set of promises and then implement a different set of policies once in power," Lula warned. "When we try to suit everyone's taste, we lose everyone's trust."

This resonates deeply with the current frustrations surrounding the Lee Jae-myung administration. Many supporters feel that the government, wary of the influence of major conservative outlets like the Chosun Ilbo—the country's oldest and most influential daily—has softened its stance on key reforms. Whether it is the overhaul of the prosecution service or media reform, the perception of "hesitation" is more damaging to a leader’s standing than the actual opposition of the right. When a reformist government starts seeking the approval of the very establishment it promised to change, it creates a vacuum of trust that even the best economic indicators cannot fill.

The Lee administration must recognize that the "moderate" voter is often a phantom. Political analysts like Jeong Jun-hee and writers like Rhyu Si-min have frequently argued that the middle ground is not a fixed location. Rather, it is a group of undecided citizens who are eventually pulled toward the side that demonstrates the most conviction and clarity. Like a magnet, a strong, consistent political core attracts the fragments in the middle. A weak, oscillating core loses both the fragments and its own foundation.

Weathering the Market’s Tantrum

One reason the DPK hesitates is the fear of market volatility. The South Korean economy is deeply integrated into global financial networks, and the "Korea Discount"—the tendency for Korean stocks to be undervalued due to geopolitical risks and corporate governance issues—is a constant concern for the Blue House (the presidential office).

Lula’s victory provides a grim preview of what happens when a reformist leader takes charge. Immediately following the news of his win, the Brazilian Bovespa index plummeted by over 17%, the largest drop since 1998, triggering a "circuit breaker" that halted trading. The Brazilian Real fell by 10%, and interest rates on 10-year government bonds surged past 15%. Global capital, which had bet on the pro-business policies of the right, threw a tantrum. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) issued dire warnings about Brazil’s fiscal deficit hitting 9% of GDP, and business leaders estimated that Lula’s welfare promises would cost at least 50 billion reais (about $10 billion or 12.3 trillion won).

The lesson for President Lee Jae-myung is that this backlash is not a sign of policy failure, but an inevitable part of the reform process. Established interests and global capital will always react negatively to a redistribution of power and resources. If a government retreats at the first sign of a stock market dip or a critical editorial from a financial newspaper, it forfeits its mandate. Lula did not abandon his promises to the poor to soothe the nerves of São Paulo’s bankers. He understood that his political survival depended on the trust of the millions who voted for a change in their material conditions, not the approval of the few who benefited from the status quo.

Overcoming the Sunk Cost Fallacy

Perhaps the most academic, yet practical, lesson from the Brazilian election involves the "Sunk Cost Fallacy." In behavioral economics, this is the tendency to continue an endeavor once an investment in money, effort, or time has been made, even if the current situation suggests that abandonment is the better option. The late Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman famously noted that he never let sunk costs dictate his future; if a path was wrong, he turned back immediately.

When Lula’s team realized their first-round strategy was failing to stop the right-wing momentum, they didn't double down out of a sense of pride. They tore up their digital strategy, replaced their campaign leadership, and pivoted entirely to a ground-game focused on the specific economic grievances of the youth and the working class. They didn't care about the time and money already spent on the old narrative; they cared about winning the next round.

The DPK is currently at risk of falling into this very trap. There is a tendency to stick to the "polite" politics of the past, hoping that the ghost of the Moon Jae-in era’s "kind governance" will eventually win over the public. But the public that impeached Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol did not do so because they wanted "politeness." They did so because they wanted a fundamental restructuring of Korean society. Sticking to a failing strategy of "gentle reform" just because it was the party’s brand for years is a classic sunk cost error. The Lee Jae-myung administration needs the courage to "flip the table" and return to the aggressive, results-oriented style that defined the President’s earlier career.

What to Watch: The Road to the Midterms

As the Lee Jae-myung government moves into its next phase, the "Lula Lesson" will be tested in real-time. To maintain its grip on power and fulfill its promises, the DPK must shift its focus from "not offending the right" to "energizing the left."

The first concrete sign to watch for will be the upcoming legislative session regarding social safety nets and labor rights. Will the government push for a bold "basic income" framework or a "Lee Jae-myung style" debt relief program for small businesses, despite the inevitable cries of "populism" from the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition? If the administration chooses the path of compromise, it may find its core base staying home during the next local and legislative elections.

Secondly, keep an eye on the government’s response to the conservative media’s framing of the economy. If the Blue House begins to prioritize the "Bovespa-style" metrics of the KOSPI (the Korean stock market index) over the "Bolsa Família-style" metrics of household debt and real wages, it will be a signal that the Lula Lesson has been ignored.

The Brazilian election was not a romantic "Pink Tide" victory that happened by accident. It was a gritty, calculated, and uncompromising battle for the soul of the working class. If President Lee Jae-myung and the DPK continue to hesitate in the shadow of the "middle ground," they may find that by the time they decide to act, the voters who once carried them to power have already walked away.
