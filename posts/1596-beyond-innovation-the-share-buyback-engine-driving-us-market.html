Beyond Innovation: The Share Buyback Engine Driving US Markets and the Search for Korea’s Missing Value

October 28, 2026

Imagine you are a retail investor in Seoul, often referred to as a "Seohak Gaemi" (Western Ant)—a nickname for Korean individual investors who aggressively buy US stocks. You watch the S&P 500, the benchmark index for the 500 largest companies in the United States, dip momentarily only to roar back to life with relentless momentum. You might assume this resilience is solely the result of American technological innovation or the global dominance of Silicon Valley. While innovation is the fuel, the engine itself is something far more mechanical and calculated: the "magic" of Earnings Per Share (EPS) and the systematic destruction of stock supply.

The secret to why the US market feels like a "safe haven" compared to the volatile South Korean market lies in how companies treat their cash. While Korean conglomerates have historically hoarded cash or used it to cement family control, American giants like Apple have spent the last decade perfecting a system of returning value to shareholders. It is not merely about making money; it is about "burning" it—specifically, buying back their own shares from the open market and permanently canceling (retiring) them. This process creates a scarcity that supports stock prices even when the broader economy stutters.

As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung moves to implement sweeping capital market reforms in South Korea, understanding this American mechanism has become essential. For years, Korean investors have suffered from the "Korea Discount"—a phenomenon where South Korean stocks trade at lower valuations than their global peers due to poor corporate governance and weak shareholder returns. By examining the 800 trillion won (about $600 billion) bonfire Apple built with its own shares, we can see exactly what the Korean market has been missing and what the current "Value-up" program aims to fix.

The EPS Engine: Why Scarcity Beats Growth

To understand why the S&P 500 remains so resilient, one must look past total net profit and focus on Earnings Per Share (EPS). The math is simple but profound: EPS is calculated by dividing a company’s total profit by the number of outstanding shares. Most investors focus on the numerator (profit), assuming that a company's value grows only if it earns more money. However, American companies have mastered the art of manipulating the denominator (the number of shares).

When a company like Apple or Microsoft uses its excess cash to buy back and cancel its own shares, the "denominator" shrinks. Even if the company’s total profit remains flat, the profit attributed to each remaining share rises. This is the "magic" of EPS. It creates a structural tailwind for stock prices. For the investor, it is like owning a pizza: if the pizza stays the same size but the number of people sharing it is cut in half, your individual slice just doubled in value. This is the fundamental reason why US stock indices often recover faster from corrections than their international counterparts; the supply of shares is constantly being sucked out of the market by the companies themselves.

This strategy also changes the psychology of the market. In a typical supply-and-demand environment, prices are determined by external buyers. However, in the US market, the companies themselves are often the largest and most consistent "buyers." When prices drop, these companies see their own stock as a "bargain" and accelerate buybacks, creating a powerful floor for the share price. This systematic support is what allows American investors to maintain a long-term perspective. They aren't just betting on the next iPhone; they are betting on a financial machine designed to increase the value of their holdings regardless of short-term market fluctuations.

The Apple Case Study: A $600 Billion Lesson

The most staggering example of this philosophy in action is Apple. Over the past decade, Apple’s stock price has increased roughly nine-fold. While the success of the iPhone and the growth of its services ecosystem are critical factors, they only tell half the story. The other half is found in the company's massive shareholder return program. Over ten years, Apple has spent more than 800 trillion won (about $600 billion) buying back and canceling its own shares. To put that in perspective, that is a sum larger than the annual national budget of many developed countries.

By spending this capital, Apple essentially removed more than 30% of its outstanding shares from the market. This means that even if Apple’s total company value had remained unchanged over that decade, the value of a single share would have risen by roughly 40% simply due to the reduction in supply. When you combine this "scarcity premium" with Apple's actual earnings growth, you get the explosive 900% return that has made it a cornerstone of global portfolios.

This approach is significantly more tax-efficient than traditional cash dividends. In many jurisdictions, including the US and South Korea, cash dividends are subject to immediate income tax. However, share buybacks and cancellations drive up the stock price, allowing shareholders to benefit from capital gains. These gains are only taxed when the investor chooses to sell, allowing their wealth to compound tax-free in the meantime. For the long-term investor, Apple’s strategy isn't just a "buyback"; it is a sophisticated wealth-preservation mechanism that aligns the interests of the CEO with the smallest retail investor.

The "Korea Discount" and the Treasury Share Trap

If the American model is so effective, why haven't South Korean companies followed suit? The answer lies in the unique and often criticized structure of the chaebol—the large, family-controlled conglomerates like Samsung and Hyundai that dominate the Korean economy. In South Korea, when a company buys back its own shares, it rarely "cancels" them. Instead, it holds them in its "treasury." These are known as treasury shares, but in the Korean context, they often serve a more cynical purpose.

"There is a fundamental difference between US companies canceling shares and Korean chaebols hoarding them in a treasury," market analysts often point out. In Korea, these shares remain "alive." They sit on the balance sheet as potential "overhang"—supply that could be dumped back onto the market at any time, Diluting the value for existing shareholders. Even worse, these shares are often used as tools for "management defense." During mergers or hostile takeover attempts, a chaebol family might exchange these treasury shares with a friendly partner to gain voting rights that the family wouldn't otherwise have.

This is often called the "Magic of Treasury Shares." It allows controlling families to maintain power over vast empires with relatively small personal stakes, often at the expense of minority shareholders. Because these shares are not canceled, they do not boost EPS. They do not create scarcity. They do not provide a floor for the stock price. This lack of genuine shareholder return is a primary driver of the "Korea Discount." Foreign investors are hesitant to pay a premium for Korean stocks when they know the company’s cash might be used to protect a chairman’s control rather than reward the people who actually own the stock.

Politics and the Push for "Value-up" Reforms

The tide, however, is beginning to turn. The administration of President Lee Jae-myung has made "capital market advancement" a central pillar of its economic agenda. The government, led by the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party), is pushing for a "Value-up" program modeled partly on successful reforms seen in Japan. The goal is to force or incentivize Korean companies to improve their governance and increase shareholder returns to global standards.

A key part of this discussion is the amendment of the Commercial Code. Currently, Korean law states that directors owe a "duty of loyalty" to the company, not necessarily to the shareholders. This legal loophole has allowed boards to approve deals that benefit a parent company or a controlling family while hurting minority investors. President Lee’s government is advocating for a shift that would require directors to consider the interests of all shareholders, a move that would make "fake" buybacks much harder to justify.

This is a sharp departure from the previous era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment and removal from office. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, the market was often characterized by opacity and a lack of decisive action against unfair trade practices. The current administration argues that for the "Seohak Gaemi" to return their capital to the domestic market (the KOSPI), the "rules of the game" must be fair. If a company buys shares with shareholder money, those shares should be burned, not hoarded in a chairman’s vault.

We are already seeing the first signs of change. Leaders like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (the world’s second-largest memory chip maker) have begun to increase dividends and, crucially, engage in share cancellations. This isn't necessarily because these executives have had a change of heart; it is because the cultural and political pressure is becoming irresistible. Investors are no longer silent, and the government is providing the legislative teeth to back them up.

What to Watch: The Future of the Korean Market

The road to a "Korean S&P 500" is still long, but the blueprint is clear. For South Korea to shed the "Korea Discount" and create a market where "long-term investment" is a rational strategy rather than a gamble, it must adopt the American discipline of share cancellation. As an observer of the Korean market, there are three concrete developments to watch in the coming months:

  • The Commercial Code Amendment: Watch for whether the Democratic Party can successfully pass the "Duty of Loyalty" amendment. This would be the single most significant legal shift in Korean corporate history, effectively ending the era of boards ignoring minority shareholders.
  • Treasury Share Mandatory Cancellation: There is growing legislative momentum to require companies to cancel any shares they buy back within a certain timeframe. If this becomes law, the "Treasury Share Trap" will effectively be closed.
  • Samsung and SK Hynix’s Capital Allocation: As these giants compete in the high-stakes AI semiconductor market, their dividend and buyback policies will set the tone for the rest of the KOSPI. If they commit to American-style cancellation, the rest of the market will likely follow.

Ultimately, the strength of the US market isn't just about the brilliance of its engineers; it's about the transparency of its financial system and the respect it accords to capital. For too long, Korean companies have treated shareholders as an afterthought. Under the current administration's reform push, the message to the 14 million retail investors in Korea is clear: the goal is a market where a stock price reflects the true value of the company, and where a share buyback is a promise of value, not a tool for control. If Korea can finally start "burning" its shares like Apple, the "Korea Discount" may finally go up in smoke.

"American investors don't have more patience than us; they simply have companies that give them a reason to stay. It's time Korean companies did the same."
