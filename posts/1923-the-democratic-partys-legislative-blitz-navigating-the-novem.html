The Democratic Party’s Legislative Blitz: Navigating the November Parliamentary Agenda

November 04, 2026

The halls of the National Assembly in Seoul are bracing for a high-stakes legislative showdown this month. With the arrival of November, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung—has launched an aggressive legislative campaign. Their goal is clear: to solidify the administrative momentum of the new government and address long-standing societal grievances through a series of sweeping policy shifts.

The atmosphere in Yeouido, the heart of South Korean politics, is charged. As the Democratic Party prepares to fast-track controversial bills, the main opposition, the People Power Party (the primary conservative party), has vowed to stall proceedings using the filibuster. Despite this resistance, the ruling party has committed to holding weekly plenary sessions to ensure their agenda reaches the finish line.

The “Fabricated Prosecution” Special Counsel Act

At the center of the storm is the “Fabricated Prosecution” Special Counsel Act. Adopted as an official party platform, this bill is designed to investigate the conduct of the prosecution service during the tenure of the now-impeached and imprisoned former leader, Yoon Seok-yeol. The Democratic Party alleges that during that period, the prosecution acted as a political tool, targeting specific politicians with falsified evidence and aggressive, politically motivated indictments.

The proposed special counsel investigation would last for 200 days. While the opposition decries the bill as a “shielding” tactic intended to protect President Lee Jae-myung from past legal investigations, the ruling party views it as a necessary cleansing of the judicial system. For the Democratic Party, restoring public trust in the rule of law is not just a policy goal—it is a symbolic act of justice to address the abuses of the previous administration. With the party signaling its intent to force a vote, the stage is set for a major clash between the ruling bloc and the People Power Party, which has already threatened unlimited debates to paralyze the process.

Taxation and the Digital Economy: A Two-Year Stay for Crypto

Beyond judicial reform, the Democratic Party is making pragmatic moves regarding the economy, specifically targeting the burgeoning digital asset market. Lawmakers from the party's Strategy and Finance Committee have officially recommended a two-year extension on the taxation of virtual assets. Originally slated to begin next year, the party argues that the current infrastructure is insufficient and that implementing the tax now would only trigger market volatility and confusion for the roughly 7 million cryptocurrency investors in South Korea.

This decision reflects a broader shift toward "pragmatic governance." By delaying the tax, the Lee Jae-myung administration aims to avoid alienating younger voters—a key demographic in the crypto space—while using the two-year window to build a more transparent and robust regulatory framework. It is a strategic pivot: rather than prioritizing immediate tax revenue, the government is betting that fostering market stability will yield better long-term economic dividends.

Labor and Agriculture: Addressing Structural Disparities

The Democratic Party is also pushing for two significant social reforms: the extension of the mandatory retirement age and the introduction of a direct election system for the head of the National Agricultural Cooperative Federation, commonly known as Nonghyup.

  • Retirement Age: As South Korea faces the challenges of a rapidly aging society, the party is aiming to pass legislation to extend the retirement age within the year. The objective is to mitigate the “income cliff” faced by the elderly and address labor shortages, though the move requires delicate negotiations regarding wage structures and youth employment.
  • Agricultural Reform: The passage of an amendment to the Agricultural Cooperative Act, which would allow member farmers to directly elect the head of the federation by 2028, marks a victory for rural advocates. By ending the tradition of a small group of delegates holding all the power, the reform aims to make the cooperative more accountable to the farmers it serves.

For the ruling party, these initiatives represent the core of their "protect the weak" (eokgangbuyak) philosophy—leveraging state power to support marginalized laborers and farmers against entrenched institutional interests.

Looking Ahead: The Budget and Beyond

The most concrete test for the Democratic Party’s legislative prowess will be the 2025 national budget. The party has pledged to adhere to the statutory deadline of December 2, aiming to pass a budget that fuels economic recovery and social welfare. While the People Power Party has threatened to slash the budget by approximately 30 trillion won (about $21.5 billion) to curb spending, the ruling party is digging in, determined to protect expenditures related to job creation and social safety nets.

What to watch next:

  • The Filibuster Battles: Watch for the intensity of the People Power Party’s resistance during the weekly Thursday plenary sessions. If the opposition maintains a full-scale blockade, the Democratic Party’s ability to force through its core bills will determine the speed of the government’s transition.
  • Economic Indicators: As the crypto tax extension gains traction, keep an eye on market response. A positive reaction from investors could provide the ruling party with a significant political win and a reputation for competent economic management.
  • Budget Negotiations: The final week of November will be pivotal. Whether the ruling party can navigate the budget through the Assembly without severe compromises will be the true measure of their legislative control and political influence under the new administration.

The coming weeks will not only define the legislative legacy of this session but will also signal how effectively the Democratic Party can translate its parliamentary majority into tangible changes for the average citizen. As the dust settles on these debates, the public will be looking to see if these efforts lead to a more equitable and stable society, or merely deeper political division.
