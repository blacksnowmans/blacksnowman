The 2026 KOSPI Collapse: Navigating the End of the AI Narrative in South Korea

November 23, 2026

Imagine waking up on a Monday morning in Seoul, checking your smartphone, and seeing a sea of blue. In the South Korean stock market, blue signifies a price drop—the opposite of the red used in Western markets—and on November 23, 2026, the screens are dripping with it. The KOSPI, South Korea’s benchmark price index, has just erased an entire year of gains in a matter of days. The panic is palpable in the "Ant" communities—the nickname for Korea’s ubiquitous retail investors—as multi-billion dollar valuations vanish into thin air.

This is not just a routine market correction; it is a systemic shock. On November 20, the Nasdaq in the United States suffered a "Second Black Friday," plummeting 5.28% in a single session. The ripples from that crash have turned into a tsunami in Asia. As the Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea scramble to stabilize the economy, investors are faced with a brutal choice: succumb to the primitive urge to sell everything in a panic, or execute a cold, mechanical survival strategy. In this environment, the goal isn't to predict the next bounce; it is to determine exactly where you stand before the water gets any deeper.

The Forced Liquidation Crisis: Why Korean Retail Investors are Trapped

The current carnage on the Korea Exchange (KRX) is being exacerbated by a uniquely Korean phenomenon: the sheer volume of "Ant" investors trading on borrowed time and money. In recent weeks, as the market began its descent, retail investors—often referred to as Donghak Gae-mi (Eastern Ant Investors) for their domestic focus—poured roughly $10.6 billion (14.7 trillion KRW) into the market. This is nearly four times the amount seen in previous downturns, suggesting a desperate attempt to "buy the dip" that has instead turned into a trap.

The problem is the quality of this capital. According to the Korea Financial Investment Association, the balance of credit trading—money borrowed from brokerages to buy stocks—has ballooned to over $24.4 billion (33.7 trillion KRW). Meanwhile, the actual cash reserves held in investor accounts have shriveled. This creates a "house of cards" scenario. When the market drops past a certain point, brokerages automatically sell off these investors' shares to cover the loans—a process known as bandae-maemae or forced liquidation.

We are already seeing the blood in the streets. In just the last two weeks, forced liquidations have reached nearly $217 million (300 billion KRW). On some days, over $58 million (80 billion KRW) in shares are dumped onto the market at the opening bell because investors couldn't meet their margin calls. This creates a feedback loop: forced selling drives prices lower, which triggers more forced selling. For the high-risk investor trading on credit, the strategy now isn't about profit; it's about setting a "firewall" line—selling a third of your position voluntarily before the brokerage does it for you at the worst possible price.

For those not using leverage, the trap is psychological. Investors often engage in "reactive trading," hitting the sell button only when the fear becomes unbearable, usually near the bottom, and then FOMO-buying (Fear Of Missing Out) back in during a temporary "dead cat bounce." To survive this, professional analysts in Seoul are advocating for a four-tiered protocol based on one's debt-to-equity ratio, urging investors to write down their exit prices on paper and execute them like a machine, regardless of the headlines.

The Cracks in the AI Myth: From Nvidia to the Anthropic Failure

To understand why the KOSPI is bleeding so profusely, one must look at the "AI Narrative" that has sustained global markets for the past four years. That narrative has officially cracked. The Nasdaq, which had been on a historic bull run, has now turned negative for the year. The catalyst was the spectacular fall of Nvidia, the "King of AI." Once the world’s most valuable company, Nvidia has seen its stock price halved, dropping 48% from its peak and falling to the fourth-largest company by market cap.

The shockwaves hit Korea particularly hard because the nation’s economy is essentially a giant bet on the semiconductor supply chain. When the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) launched an investigation into Nvidia’s accounting practices, it didn't just hurt American tech; it signaled to Korean investors that the "AI Gold Rush" might be built on shaky ground. Rumors regarding CEO Jensen Huang’s leadership and potential talent departures only added fuel to the fire. In Korea, where Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix dominate the index, an Nvidia crisis is a national crisis.

The final blow to market sentiment came from the "Anthropic Tragedy." Anthropic, an AI startup that was supposed to be the next great hope for the industry, saw its stock price collapse back to its IPO levels within just two days of trading. The market had bought into the "narrative" that these companies would double or quadruple their earnings every year. When Anthropic failed to provide a clear roadmap for actual profitability, the "Short" sellers and hedge funds pounced.

This has left Masayoshi Son and SoftBank in a precarious position. SoftBank had invested roughly $65 billion (90 trillion KRW) into AI ventures like OpenAI, expecting a series of massive Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) to provide an "exit party." Instead, SoftBank’s own stock has plummeted to a third of its 52-week high. The market is no longer interested in companies that sell "shovels and pickaxes" for the AI gold mine; it is now cruelly asking: "Who is actually making cash from AI?"

"Hedge fund sell-buttons usually trigger at a 3% drop. But in the current semiconductor and AI sectors, we are seeing 10% plunges. This means we have entered a secondary wave of liquidations where funds are completely unwinding their positions."

The Great Disconnect: Record Profits Met with Total Market Distrust

The most baffling aspect of the current Korean crisis is the "fundamental gap." If you look at the factory floors of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, you wouldn't know there was a crisis. From November 1 to November 20, 2026, South Korea’s exports hit $77.4 billion. Semiconductors alone accounted for $38.5 billion of that—nearly half of the nation's total exports. Daily export averages are at an all-time high of $2.4 billion.

On paper, the numbers are staggering. Samsung Electronics is on track for a fourth-quarter operating profit of $94 billion (130 trillion KRW), while SK Hynix is expected to hit $76 billion (105 trillion KRW). Both companies claim their production lines are sold out through 2028 and 2029. By any traditional metric, these stocks are historically undervalued.

However, the market is currently ignoring these numbers. This is a crisis of multiples—the premium investors are willing to pay for future earnings. When the "AI Narrative" was strong, investors might pay 20 times earnings for Samsung. Now, gripped by fear and rising interest rates, they might only be willing to pay 5 times. The reality of record-breaking exports is being drowned out by a macro-economic "gravity" caused by the U.S. Federal Reserve’s hawkish stance.

The Fed is signaling a potential 0.5% rate hike in December to combat stubborn inflation. In a high-interest-rate environment, the cost of capital for massive data center projects—the primary buyers of Korean chips—skyrockets. Investors are looking past the "Sold Out" signs and asking what happens in 2030. Furthermore, global investment banks have begun cutting their price targets for Korean chipmakers, citing potential "order cuts" from Chinese tech giants amid ongoing U.S.-China trade friction. While these analyst downgrades are often "lagging indicators"—meaning they cut the target only after the price has already fallen—they act as a megaphone for negative sentiment, causing retail investors to panic-sell despite the strong fundamentals.

Historical Resilience and the Lee Jae-myung Administration's Response

As the market reaches what many hope is the "deepest part of the valley," the Lee Jae-myung administration is preparing to deploy the Jeung-an Fund (Stock Market Stabilization Fund). This is a traditional Korean policy tool where institutional investors and banks contribute capital to support the market during extreme volatility. While the Democratic Party of Korea emphasizes that this is a necessary safety net to prevent a total collapse of the middle class’s wealth, critics often wonder if such "verbal interventions" are enough to stop a global tide.

To find perspective, one must look at the history of the KOSPI. The legendary investor Howard Marks once warned about crossing a river that is "four feet deep on average," noting that a six-foot-tall man can still drown if there is a ten-foot hole in the middle. Most Korean investors are currently in that ten-foot hole. However, history shows that the Korean market is remarkably resilient. Looking back at the four major crises in South Korea’s history—the 1997 IMF Crisis, the 2000 Dot-com Bubble, the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, and the 2020 Pandemic—a clear pattern emerges.

  • 1997 Asian Financial Crisis: Took 16 months to recover, but the market rose 126% from the bottom.
  • 2008 Global Financial Crisis: Took 23 months to recover, with a 103% gain from the low.
  • Average Recovery: Across all major crashes, the KOSPI typically takes about 18 months to return to previous highs, with an average gain of 87% from the bottom.

The lesson for the international observer is that the current pain is likely a "valuation reset" rather than a terminal decline of the Korean tech sector. The companies are still making record profits; it is the price of those profits that is being recalibrated by global fear.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the final weeks of 2026, there are three concrete signals to monitor for a potential bottom:

  • The "Blackout" Period: Watch for the end of November, when the U.S. Federal Reserve enters its "blackout" period (where officials cannot speak publicly) before the December meeting. This often leads to a temporary reduction in market noise.
  • The Bank of Korea’s Rate Decision: On November 26, the Bank of Korea will meet. Whether they choose to hold or cut rates will be a major signal to the market about the government’s confidence in domestic stability.
  • Margin Debt Levels: A true market bottom in Korea is usually signaled when the credit trading balance (currently $24.4 billion) drops significantly, indicating that the "weak hands" have been flushed out and the forced liquidations have ended.

For now, the KOSPI remains a battlefield. The "AI Myth" has been replaced by a "Profit Reality," and for the "Ants" of Seoul, the coming months will be a grueling test of whether they can stay afloat long enough to see the other side of the river.
