Beyond the Screen: How South Korea Is Betting on ‘Physical AI’ to Transform Its Factories, Shipyards, and Streets

November 28, 2026

Picture walking through a sprawling Korean shipyard on the southern coast. Heavy steel plates groan under colossal gantry cranes, sparks fly from automatic welding rigs, and the air smells of sea salt and hot metal. For decades, this high-stakes environment relied entirely on the grit and experience of human shipbuilders. Today, however, the eye at the top of the crane and the torch on the assembly line are increasingly guided by artificial intelligence that can see, adapt, and physically react to unpredictable surroundings in real time.

Artificial intelligence is no longer confined to chat windows, code editors, or digital avatars. In South Korea, the technology conversation has decisively shifted to Physical AI—the integration of advanced machine learning into real-world mechanical hardware, ranging from industrial cobots and 3D LiDAR sensors to urban delivery rovers and heavy maritime equipment. Backed by an aggressive policy drive from the Lee Jae-myung administration under its national AX (AI Transformation) banner, the nation's telecom carriers, heavy industrial conglomerates, and technology startups are rushing to embed physical intelligence directly into the real economy.

Connectivity as the Infrastructure Backbone: 5G Standalone and Autonomous Logistics

For physical AI to operate safely alongside humans in dynamic environments, seamless connectivity is an absolute prerequisite. While a text-generating model can tolerate a brief network latency spike, a 150-kilogram autonomous delivery robot navigating a crowded pedestrian sidewalk in Seoul cannot afford a single moment of disconnect.

Addressing this operational bottleneck, LG Uplus, South Korea's third-largest mobile carrier, recently conducted real-world trials of physical AI delivery robots powered by 5G Standalone (5G SA) networks in partnership with Hyundai Motor and Kia, South Korea's premier automotive group. Unlike conventional non-standalone 5G setups that rely on legacy 4G LTE infrastructure for core network functions, 5G SA provides a dedicated, ultra-low-latency channel tailored for autonomous machines. In high-density urban zones or commercial centers where signal interference typically causes delivery rovers to stall, 5G SA ensures real-time map updates and uninterrupted obstacle avoidance.

This network foundation is critical for last-mile delivery startups such as Doomdoom, which is developing multi-modal logistics networks combining aerial drones and ground rovers. Backed by national R&D investments reaching 11.7 billion won (about $8.5 million), these initiatives show that market leadership in autonomous logistics is moving beyond mechanical design toward network stability and continuous uptime.

The global stakes are rising quickly. Chinese electric vehicle manufacturer Xpeng recently unveiled its integrated physical AI architecture and announced plans to launch humanoid robots next year. By transferring autonomous driving algorithms developed for electric cars directly into bipedal robotics, Xpeng is executing a strategy that puts it in direct competition with Tesla's Optimus project. Treating electric vehicles as "wheeled robots" enables automotive manufacturers to port spatial data into humanoid forms—presenting a competitive challenge to traditional robotics companies and forcing South Korean firms to look beyond hardware supply toward fully integrated software platforms.

The Factory's New Brain: Self-Healing AI and Data Automation

While urban logistics demands robust connectivity pipelines, heavy manufacturing requires cognitive flexibility. Traditional factory automation relied on rigid programming: if a work piece shifted out of alignment by a few millimeters, the entire assembly line halted until a human operator stepped in.

To overcome this limitation, South Korean robot software firm Clobot has launched an international joint R&D initiative valued at 11.7 billion won (about $8.5 million) alongside researchers from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). The collaboration centers on developing "self-healing" manufacturing intelligence—AI software that equips industrial robots to detect sudden disruptions or positional errors on the factory floor and autonomously adjust their physical actions to keep working without human intervention.

Building effective physical AI requires vast volumes of structured real-world data, which has long been a key operational bottleneck. To address this, INT—an industrial startup spun off from Sogang University—has partnered with LG CNS, the IT service arm of LG Group, to build an automated data infrastructure platform for physical AI. By automating the collection, generation, and validation of physical data, the platform aims to accelerate the deployment of autonomous systems across complex manufacturing environments.

Sensing hardware is advancing alongside software. Local sensor developer SOS Lab has introduced its spatial intelligence platform, "SPADDY," which uses 3D LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) technology to grant robots high-precision spatial awareness. By combining 3D LiDAR vision with automated training data pipelines, South Korea's tech sector is seeking to digitize the implicit skills of veteran human craftspeople—offering a practical countermeasure to manufacturing labor shortages driven by demographic trends.

From Shipyards to Regional Hubs: South Korea's Industrial AX Pivot

The push for physical AI is actively reshaping South Korea's traditional industrial regions. Members of the presidential Presidential Committee on AI Strategy recently visited HD Hyundai Samho, one of South Korea's leading shipbuilders, to review the implementation of physical AI across complex yard operations. Shipyards represent one of the toughest environments for robotics due to vast open-air layouts, unpredictable weather, and hazardous tasks like high-temperature welding, anti-fouling painting, and heavy material transport.

Under the Lee Jae-myung administration's AX initiative, industrial hubs across the Yeongnam region—including Busan and Ulsan—are converting heavy industrial facilities into practical testbeds. Regional academic and industry groups, including National Pukyong University and the Busan IT Industry Association, have organized forums focused on deploying physical AI across maritime and offshore plant facilities. The goal is not merely adding isolated robotic arms, but creating end-to-end intelligent shipyards that analyze real-time data from initial design to final delivery.

To ensure AI infrastructure extends beyond the Greater Seoul area, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport selected the district surrounding Jeonju Station in North Jeolla Province as a final candidate for its Urban Convergence Special Zone. The initiative aims to build a regional physical AI hub where residents interact with autonomous delivery rovers and intelligent transport infrastructure in daily life.

This regional focus is paired with growing academic involvement. Institutions including Hanyang University ERICA, Handong Global University, and Sogang University are organizing symposia to align engineering research with industrial needs. Handong Global University's "Glocal Forum" highlighted the importance of combining tech development with entrepreneurial execution, while KAIST (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology) recently opened its multi-disciplinary research facilities to display projects ranging from physical robotics to AI-generated film.

The 2027 Roadmap and the Legislative Horizon

To consolidate these efforts, the Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) and the Ministry of SMEs and Startups (MSS) have committed to a national campaign targeting 2027 to accelerate AI transformation across manufacturing and logistics. The policy framework includes expanded R&D funding, broader regulatory sandboxes for live testing, and targeted assistance to help small and medium-sized enterprises adopt physical AI systems.

Setting a concrete 2027 milestone signals a clear direction for private investors, equipment makers, and research universities. For South Korea—an economy built on electronics, automotive manufacturing, and heavy industry—physical AI serves as a strategic bridge connecting its legacy hardware base with modern software intelligence.

However, technological progress is moving faster than the surrounding regulatory framework. Key legal questions regarding liability when an autonomous machine causes workplace damage, physical safety standards, and the formal legal status of physical AI units remain unresolved. Industry leaders caution that without updated statutory guidelines, enterprise adoption could face friction from compliance uncertainty.

Technology is already active on the factory floor, but legacy regulatory frameworks risk slowing down innovation. Clear legal guidelines and liability standards are urgently needed.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward its 2027 industrial transformation targets, several key developments will indicate how effectively physical AI is taking root:

  • Legislative action on robot liability: Watch for proposals in the National Assembly establishing clear safety protocols and legal liability standards for autonomous industrial rovers and field equipment.
  • Commercial rollout of 5G SA networks: Track whether LG Uplus and competing domestic carriers expand dedicated 5G Standalone network slicing for industrial complexes and urban logistics hubs.
  • Academic-industry milestones: Keep an eye on regional research initiatives, such as Hanyang University ERICA's planned 2026 Physical AI Symposium, for updates on real-world industrial deployments.
  • Maritime automation benchmarks: Monitor productivity and safety metrics from HD Hyundai Samho and the Yeongnam maritime belt as physical AI assumes higher workloads in welding, painting, and heavy lifting.
