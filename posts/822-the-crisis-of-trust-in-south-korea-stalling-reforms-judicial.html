The Crisis of Trust in South Korea: Stalling Reforms, Judicial Privilege, and the Pitfalls of Ruling Party Politics

October 08, 2026

For observers watching South Korea's political landscape unfold from afar, the current atmosphere feels unsettlingly familiar yet distinctively tense. A new administration came to power promising sweeping reform and a break from the past, waving the banner of democratic renewal. Yet, step inside the current political reality, and the picture is one of fractured trust, a sluggish approval rating, and a political elite increasingly insulated by its own rhetoric. From fierce battles over the judiciary to the sharp, defensive language echoing from the top down, a persistent question hangs over the country: Is South Korea's democratic machinery actually moving forward, or is it spinning its wheels?

Stalling Approval Ratings and the Lost Window for Bold Reform

When President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the center-left Democratic Party of Korea—held a high-profile press conference, it offered a brief window to reset a sliding approval rating. In South Korean politics, the public rarely looks for mere formal apologies or carefully scripted explanations; citizens are watching for the aggressive, structural changes promised during campaigns. Unfortunately, that window of opportunity slammed shut almost as quickly as it opened.

The core miscalculation lay in the ruling party’s failure to deliver swift, heavy-hitting follow-up measures. In a political system where the president bears immense structural weight, the ruling party is expected to carry the momentum forward—amplifying the message, taking the political heat, and driving legislative overhauls. Instead, the golden hour was lost to hesitation and internal management. If the administration and its allies do not bring out heavy policy weapons soon—such as tackling the controversial legacy of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae or forcing structural judicial overhauls—stopping the hemorrhage of public support will remain nearly impossible.

For the Democratic Party, the challenge is proving that it is fundamentally different from the conservative forces it replaced. Critics inside and outside the party argue that instead of acting as the vanguard of reform, the party has grown overly cautious, prioritizing internal peace over radical institutional change. When the political force tasked with uprooting old privileges begins pulling its punches, even its core base starts to look away.

The 'Imperial Supreme Court' and the Resistance to Judicial Reform

Nowhere is the stalling of reform more evident—or more consequential—than in the country's judicial branch. South Korea’s legal system has long operated under a heavily vertical, centralized structure anchored by the Supreme Court and the National Court Administration. Activist groups, including People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy and Minbyun (Lawyers for a Democratic Society), have long argued that this concentration of administrative power in a single Chief Justice creates an institutional breeding ground for judicial corruption and political maneuvering.

The ghosts of past judicial scandals under former leaders still haunt public trust, proving that the courts have largely lost their capacity to police themselves. Leaving the "Imperial Supreme Court" model intact invites future abuse. Experts argue that complete abolition of the National Court Administration, the establishment of an independent judicial committee to separate personnel management rights, and the shielding of Supreme Court nominee recommendation committees from political pressure are absolute prerequisites for judicial democratization.

Furthermore, critics point out that a trial structure dominated solely by professional judges is inherently prone to insularity. Proposals to expand citizen-led trials—mirroring Germany’s *Schöffengericht* system or Anglo-American jury models—frequently hit a brick wall built by a self-protective legal cartel. Until routine administrative tasks like local registry offices are stripped from the courts, and until ordinary citizens are given genuine authority over factual determinations in serious trials, the concept of judicial justice will remain out of reach for everyday South Koreans.

Beneath these policy debates lies a deeper cultural resentment. Revelations from parliamentary audits alleging that senior judges enjoyed extravagant perks—including wine parties funded by hundreds of thousands of dollars in special activity funds—while ordinary citizens struggle through economic hardship have stoked widespread fury. When top judicial figures brush off accountability with evasive answers, it reinforces the public perception that the judiciary views itself as an untouchable, elite caste.

Judicial Inconsistencies and the Erosion of Public Faith

This deep-seated skepticism toward the courts was laid bare once again by dramatic developments in the legal proceedings surrounding imprisoned former leader Yoon Suk-yeol. Yoon, who was impeached and removed from office, faced heavy legal scrutiny alongside political broker Myung Tae-kyun over allegations of illegally receiving free opinion polls and interfering in candidate nominations. While a lower court initially handed down a two-year prison sentence—flickering a brief spark of hope for accountability—an appeals court abruptly overturned the verdict and handed down an acquittal.

The legal reasoning behind the reversal—arguing that providing opinion polls under political funds law is difficult to legally classify as a tangible material benefit—did little to satisfy public common sense. To ordinary citizens watching a sequence of powerful figures escape legal penalties through technicalities, the judiciary appears to apply a remarkably lenient yardstick when dealing with high-level political corruption.

Ironically, the legal gymnastics required to acquit Yoon also relied on shifting perceptions of Myung Tae-kyun, previously dismissed by many as a mere braggart and con artist. By treating Myung’s claims as credible enough to form the basis of an acquittal, the court inadvertently legitimized assertions that could prove fatal in separate, ongoing legal battles involving other high-profile conservative figures, such as Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon. These pendulum-swinging decisions do little to stabilize public faith in the rule of law, leaving the legal system looking increasingly politicized.

The Danger of Hyper-Partisanship and the Silencing of Internal Dissent

As institutional friction mounts, the tone of political communication from the top has also taken a sharp, combative turn. President Lee recently took to social media to lash out against what he termed "irresponsible extremists"—critics within and outside the party who challenge government lines without minimum grounding—accusing them of stoking unfounded panic.

While leaders turning to social media is nothing new, the sheer volume and timing of these digital salvos risk looking less like genuine civic communication and more like top-down message enforcement. When a ruling administration treats respectful, internal dissent or progressive policy critiques as the work of dangerous radicals, it poisons the well of healthy intra-party democracy. Senior party figures echoing the need to "rein in" excessive grassroots demands risk coming across as profoundly arrogant.

History suggests that when a governing force tries to muzzle its own most passionate, reform-minded supporters while placating the very vested interests that stand to lose from change, it bleeds its own vitality. Popular anger is not easily insulated just because a government claims the progressive label. If the administration continues to rely on defensive framing and communication walls, that political blowback will inevitably return to its doorstep.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this complex political and judicial crossroads, several concrete developments will signal where the country is heading next:

  • Legislative Moves on Judicial Overhaul: Watch whether opposition and ruling lawmakers actually introduce concrete bills to dismantle the National Court Administration and reform judicial appointment committees, or if the rhetoric fades once media attention shifts.
  • Ruling Party Dynamics: Pay attention to whether the Democratic Party stifles internal criticism or allows space for grassroots reformists to voice dissent without being branded as political extremists.
  • Further Legal Rulings: Monitor upcoming appellate and Supreme Court decisions regarding related political finance and nomination cases, which will serve as a definitive litmus test for whether the judiciary can claw back public credibility.
  • Presidential Communication Shifts: Observe if the administration alters its digital outreach strategy to embrace broad-based civic dialogue rather than doubling down on defensive partisan messaging.
