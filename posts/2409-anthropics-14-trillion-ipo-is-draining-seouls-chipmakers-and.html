Anthropic’s $1.4 Trillion IPO Is Draining Seoul’s Chipmakers and Locking Out Korean Investors

November 17, 2026

Three days before Anthropic makes its Nasdaq debut on November 19, the ripple effects are already reshaping trading floors in Seoul. On November 16, as global institutional investors scrambled to raise cash for an initial public offering carrying a rumored $1.4 trillion valuation and order books six to nearly seven times oversubscribed, South Korea’s stock market split along a sharp fault line. Shares of SK Telecom, one of South Korea’s dominant wireless carriers, climbed as investors hunted for a proxy stake in the AI developer. At the exact same moment, foreign funds dumped Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the two memory-chip giants that anchor Seoul’s benchmark stock index, treating them less like semiconductor bellwethers and more like automated teller machines for IPO cash.

For international observers, the episode highlights how a mega-listing in New York can destabilize Asia’s most liquid tech markets before a single share changes hands. For South Korean investors—who have become some of the most aggressive retail buyers of U.S. tech stocks in the world—it also brings a stinging realization: while institutional money and even retail buyers in neighboring Japan have a window into the offering, Korea has been shut out.

A $1.4 Trillion Bet Built on a Compute Dilemma

To understand why global capital is rushing toward Anthropic at a pace reportedly exceeding the frenzy around SpaceX’s IPO, it helps to look at the sheer scale of the numbers circulating ahead of the listing. Market estimates put Anthropic’s valuation at $1.4 trillion, with fundraising and offering figures ranging from $64 billion to nearly $90 billion (121 trillion won) and total investment commitments and financing expectations stretching as high as $900 billion. Wealthy Asian investors are reportedly willing to pay premiums just to secure an allocation.

Behind that appetite is a dramatic shift in market sentiment. Skepticism over whether generative AI can justify its costs had been weighing on tech equities, and big tech firms are now watching to see whether Anthropic’s debut breaks that pessimism—or exposes a historic bubble. Even Anthropic’s own leadership has framed the listing less as a victory lap than as an operational necessity.

Developing and scaling Anthropic’s flagship generative AI model, Claude, requires roughly $890 billion (1,200 trillion won) in total investment. Computing commitments alone stand at around $891 billion (1,203 trillion won), with nearly $70 billion (94 trillion won) slated to be spent within a single year. Ahead of the IPO, both Google and Nvidia stepped in with private investments of roughly $9.6 billion (13 trillion won) each. Anthropic’s chief executive has acknowledged that securing compute is the company’s central dilemma and a primary reason for going public, noting the bind of having to commit to massive infrastructure spending long before profits are clear—while pledging not to push the IPO valuation a single dollar higher.

The governance terms emerging ahead of the listing underscore how tightly the company’s insiders intend to hold the reins:

  • Founder windfalls and control: Anthropic’s seven founders each stand to hold equity worth roughly $17.8 billion (24 trillion won) at listing, while a founder-controlled entity will retain 50.1 percent of voting rights afterward.
  • A staged supply overhang: Some 15.5 billion existing shares will be subject to lock-up restrictions that expire in phases rather than all at once, limiting immediate post-IPO float while creating recurring supply tests down the line.

Geopolitics, the Seoul Shutout, and the Three-Times Leverage Trap

Even as global capital floods in, Anthropic’s underwriters have drawn strict geopolitical boundaries around who gets to participate. Chinese and Hong Kong institutional investors have been effectively barred from the IPO allocation. The decision reflects U.S. defense regulations, heightened sensitivity over AI as a strategic industry, and lingering anxiety across American tech circles over China following the scandal surrounding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. With the IPO falling during a packed diplomatic week that includes a U.S.-China summit, national security screening has moved directly into the IPO book-building process.

More surprising to market watchers in Seoul, however, is that South Korean retail investors have also been left empty-handed. While subscription channels for the offering opened in Japan, South Korea was excluded, and major South Korean brokerages stayed out of the underwriting syndicate—reportedly deterred by the precedent and burdens of SpaceX’s earlier IPO.

In Korea, retail investors who actively trade overseas equities—known locally as Seohak Gaemi, or "Western-learning ants"—have become a major market force. Being locked out of the primary offering means they cannot buy at the IPO price and must instead compete on the open market once trading begins in New York. That frustration has already spawned a rush toward workarounds. Investors are debating when and in what weightings major exchange-traded funds (ETFs) will add Anthropic after the listing. Worse, leveraged financial products offering three times exposure to Anthropic-linked themes have already surfaced before the company has even debuted, prompting warnings from market analysts that retail buyers chasing quick gains could suffer severe losses if early post-IPO trading turns volatile.

Seoul’s Stock Market Paradox: SK Telecom Rallies While Chipmakers Bleed

Inside South Korea’s own equity market, the pre-IPO shockwave has produced starkly opposite winners and losers. Because individual Korean investors cannot participate in the offering directly, many turned to SK Telecom as an indirect proxy. SK Telecom holds an early equity stake in Anthropic, and the reassessment of that stake’s value lifted its shares on November 16. Yet the rally also places a spotlight on Chey Tae-won, chairman of SK Group, the sprawling conglomerate that controls SK Telecom and SK Hynix. While a paper gain on an early AI bet offers short-term momentum, investors are watching to see whether SK can convert its AI investments into recurring revenue and earnings inside its core businesses.

Meanwhile, South Korea’s two most important stocks—Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—are absorbing the brunt of the global cash scramble. Together, the two memory-chip manufacturers dominate the KOSPI, South Korea’s benchmark stock index, and are among the most heavily owned Asian equities by foreign institutional investors. As Asian markets slid broadly on November 16, foreign selling hit both chipmakers hard.

In Yeouido, Seoul’s financial district, brokers fear a repeat of the "supply-demand black hole" that followed SpaceX’s IPO, when foreign investors net-sold roughly $30 billion (41 trillion won) worth of South Korean shares. The dynamic has little to do with semiconductor fundamentals. When global funds need billions in liquid cash to settle mega-IPO allocations, they sell whatever large, easily tradable holdings they have on hand—and Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix frequently sit at the top of that list.

In the short term, Korea’s flagship memory makers are being sold to fund a New York IPO. Over the medium term, however, the billions Anthropic is raising must flow straight back into AI servers and memory chips.

That paradox leaves long-term semiconductor bulls divided. If Anthropic’s roughly $891 billion in computing commitments and the broader $900 billion wave of AI investment expectations turn into real data-center orders, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix stand to be among the primary hardware beneficiaries. For now, however, liquidity flows are overpowering fundamentals.

An "AI Superweek" Rattles Global Futures and Bitcoin

Anthropic’s debut is not happening in isolation. Wall Street is navigating what traders are calling an "AI Superweek," as Anthropic’s listing collides with Nvidia’s quarterly earnings—an event unfolding under the unusual shadow of "Nvidia without Jensen Huang" amid his ongoing scandal—alongside rising U.S. Treasury yields. The combination has left global equity futures on edge, with S&P 500 futures hovering weakly and both Nasdaq futures and Canada’s TSX futures facing downward pressure.

The cash squeeze has spilled into cryptocurrencies as well. By November 16, Bitcoin had erased nearly all of the gains it built up since August, caught between higher bond yields and the pull of a $64 billion IPO draining liquidity out of risk assets—a pattern that also played out around SpaceX’s listing. Still, some traders note that if Nvidia’s earnings and Anthropic’s debut pass without a major shock, the cash currently parked on the sidelines for IPO subscriptions could rotate swiftly back into beaten-down risk assets.

What to Watch Next

The coming days will determine whether Anthropic’s listing marks a turning point for global AI valuations or simply another round of liquidity whiplash for Asia’s markets. Four concrete signals stand out:

  • November 19 opening trade and valuation hold: Whether Anthropic trades smoothly above its $1.4 trillion benchmark on day one, and how quickly the market digests the staged lock-up schedule on 15.5 billion insider shares.
  • Foreign net flows in Seoul: Whether foreign selling in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix subsides once IPO allocations are settled, or deepens into another $30 billion-style exodus from the KOSPI.
  • ETF inclusion and retail leverage: How much weight major ETFs actually assign to Anthropic after listing, and whether Korean regulators or brokerages rein in speculative three-times leveraged products aimed at locked-out retail investors.
  • Post-Superweek liquidity rotation: Whether the cash pulled out of Bitcoin and global futures returns once Nvidia’s results and Anthropic’s debut are in the rearview mirror.
