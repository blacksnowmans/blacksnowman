South Korea’s Parental Leave Controversy: Studying 80 Hours a Week for a New Career on the State’s Dime

November 27, 2026

An online post recently sent South Korean social media and workplace forums into a frenzy. A woman in her thirties posted a detailed account of how she had successfully passed the nation’s notoriously difficult Certified Tax Accountant exam. But what captured the public's attention was not her academic discipline—it was her timing. She accomplished the feat while on officially paid, government-subsidized parental leave.

According to her own narrative, she extended her parental leave to devote roughly 80 hours a week—from 6:30 AM to 10:30 PM—to private academy classes and exam preparation. Meanwhile, she delegated primary childcare duties to her extended family, spending as little as two hours a week directly taking care of her child. All the while, she collected state parental leave benefits that can reach up to 2.5 million KRW (approximately $1,800) per month.

Outraged readers promptly reported her to the Ministry of Employment and Labor, accusing her of fraudulent receipt of welfare benefits. The incident has laid bare deep anxieties in South Korea, touching on the ethics of state benefit misuse, workplace burdens placed on colleagues who cover for absent staff, and the immense pressure young South Koreans feel to constantly upgrade their career credentials.

The Paid Leave Dilemma: Personal Upskilling vs. Public Misuse

In South Korea, paid parental leave is funded through employment insurance and tax revenues to help working parents care for infants without facing financial ruin. However, taking leave often places heavy stress on remaining co-workers, who must absorb the extra workload when companies fail to hire temporary replacements. For many office workers, seeing a colleague use leave meant for childcare to pull 80-hour study weeks felt like a breach of social trust.

Online debate split rapidly into two camps:

  • The Critics: Opponents argue that parental benefit funds are explicitly designed to support child-rearing. Using state money and workplace allowances to prepare for a career pivot is an abuse of public resources, and anyone planning full-time study should simply resign.
  • The Defenders: Sympathizers argue that if a family manages to cover childcare responsibilities through grandparents or relatives, how a parent spends their off-hours falls under personal discretion and professional development.

The Ministry of Employment and Labor is now reviewing the case after receiving formal complaints. Because parental leave payouts carry a legal maximum of 2.5 million KRW ($1,800) a month, the ministry’s determination on whether intensive test preparation constitutes "fraudulent receipt" (부정수급) will set a vital precedent for workplaces across the country.

Carrots and Incentives: How Korea Is Trying to Buy Its Way Out of a Demographic Crisis

This controversy unfolds against the backdrop of South Korea’s staggering demographic collapse. The nation's natural population continues to shrink—with deaths exceeding births by 40,000 annually. In response, the government and local municipalities are rolling out aggressive financial incentives to ease the financial burden of raising children.

Among the latest measures highlighted by parenting media are targeted housing and work adjustments:

  • Newborn Special Low-Interest Loans: Households that have given birth within the past two years can access mortgage and rental loans at ultra-low interest rates starting in the 1% annual range.
  • Extended Reduced Hours: Policies allowing parents to work reduced daily hours have been expanded to cover parents with children up to the 6th grade of elementary school.
  • Expanded Public Housing: Public housing supply in the Seoul Metropolitan Area will expand to 56,000 units next year, a 44% increase over this year. Youth public transit cost-rebate programs ("Modu Card") are set to launch in December.

Private corporate culture is also being nudged to adapt. The Ministry of Employment and Labor recently named 124 corporate entities as "2026 Excellent Work-Life Balance Companies." Notable examples highlight how policy shifts produce real numbers:

Corporate Impact in Numbers: Cosmetics manufacturer Kolmar Korea made a one-month paternal leave mandatory for new fathers. Within two years, male employee leave usage surged 18-fold while company turnover dropped by 1.9%. The firm also provides a 40 million KRW (about $29,000) cash bonus for employees who have triplets. Other recognized firms include Tonymoly, which enforced automatic PC shut-off systems and flexible hours, and NS Home Shopping, which recorded a 100% post-leave return rate.

Local governments are making equally massive financial commitments. South Chungcheong Province reviewed an ambitious demographic strategy allocating 5.9415 trillion KRW (about $4.3 billion) over two years (2026–2027). Meanwhile, the coastal city of Sokcho is committing 28.2 billion KRW (about $20 million) to open a comprehensive child-rearing support complex by late 2029. The central government is also moving to reduce regional disparities by expanding public postpartum care centers, which currently number just 31 nationwide.

Systemic Cracks: Empty Daycares, Safety Hazards, and Toddler Burnout

Despite these high-dollar initiatives, local child-care networks are buckling under low birth rates and administrative gaps. As the cohort of annual births hovering under 300,000 reaches school age, school boards are facing a staggering 9.5 trillion KRW (about $6.8 billion) budget deficit, forcing painful restructuring of teaching staff and school facilities.

At the grassroots level, daycare centers are struggling to survive:

  • Daycare Closures in Ulsan: In Ulsan, where birth rates have plummeted, losing even a single enrolled toddler can cut off municipal wage subsidies for staff. The city is now forming a emergency task force to test new operating models, such as allowing facilities in new housing developments to temporarily pause toddler classes and operate exclusively for infants.
  • Support Gaps for Twins: Demand for state child-minding services among twin families rose 33% over two years. However, government-subsidized care hours for twins remain identical to those for single-child households, leaving parents of multiples overwhelmed.
  • Safety Negligence: Inspecting authorities recently discovered 44-day-past-expiration pork in a daycare refrigerator in Bupyeong, Incheon. In nearby Yeonsu-gu, local council members criticized municipal oversight after 13 private daycares were found to contain unaddressed asbestos.

At the same time, educational authorities are sounding the alarm over the psychological pressure placed on infants. A joint policy forum held by the Ministry of Education and the Korea Institute of Child Care and Education (KICCE) urged parents to stop pushing toddlers into private cram schools (사교육) and intensive early academic prep.

Researchers presented findings showing that forced early cognitive training leads to severe fatigue and destroys long-term learning motivation. Medical experts similarly warned that excessive early screen time and digital device exposure significantly hinder language, cognitive, and emotional milestones in young children.

What to Watch Next

For observers of South Korea’s social trends, policy shifts, and family welfare, several immediate markers will show where these issues are heading:

  • Ministry of Labor Ruling on Parental Leave Study: Keep an eye on the labor ministry's pending determination regarding the tax accountant candidate. A formal ruling of "fraudulent drawdown" could lead to benefit clawbacks and stricter employer audits for staff taking extended parental leave.
  • Kindergarten Enrollment Deadlines: General admission results for the 2027 kindergarten school year were published on November 26. Families in Korea must confirm their registration via official portals immediately to prevent losing their spots.
  • Expositions and Welfare Rollouts: The 50th Daegu Baby & Kids Fair (Daegu BEKI) will run at the EXCO center from December 3 to December 6. Meanwhile, parents should check their eligibility for the upcoming youth transit rebate cards launching in December and verify local municipal schedules if routine Parental Benefit (부모급여) payouts experienced administrative delays.
