The Ghost of Namsan: How a South Korean Spy Agency Tortured a Professor and Haunted His Brother

October 19, 2026

In October 1973, an elite young intelligence officer walked into a meeting with full faith in the state he served. Choi Jong-sun had graduated at the top of his class from the ninth recruit training cycle of the Korean Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA), South Korea's all-powerful spy service. Trained rigorously for over a year and assigned to the agency’s internal inspection bureau, he believed that defending the nation from communism was not just a career, but a moral duty. When fellow KCIA officers approached him with polite smiles, asking if his elder brother—a distinguished legal scholar—might visit their headquarters on Mount Namsan in central Seoul to share insights on Korean students in West Germany, Choi did not hesitate. He persuaded his brother to go.

Three days later, Professor Choi Jong-gil of Seoul National University School of Law was dead. The agency claimed the legal scholar had confessed to espionage and leaped to his death from a restroom window in the Namsan interrogation complex. In truth, he had been beaten and subjected to electric shocks until his body gave out, after which his interrogators threw his corpse out the window to disguise a fatal torture session as suicide. For Choi Jong-sun, that betrayal marked the beginning of a lifelong agony—and an undercover campaign from within the agency to preserve the truth before the dictatorship could erase it forever.

A Puppet Prosecution and the Shadow of Namsan

To understand how an academic could vanish into an interrogation basement and emerge as a fabricated corpse, one must understand the absolute machinery of the Yushin ("Revitalization") dictatorship led by President Park Chung-hee during the 1970s. On paper, the Republic of Korea maintained a modern judicial framework. The Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office operated with judges, prosecutors, and formal statutory procedures. Yet that formal apparatus functioned merely as a cosmetic front.

In practice, real authority was concentrated in the KCIA's headquarters on Mount Namsan. The agency acted above the constitution, drafting the scripts for political and national security cases before sending them down to civilian prosecutors. Prosecutors were relegated to rubber-stamping agency-manufactured confessions, signing indictments crafted in windowless detention rooms, and presenting evidence obtained through savage physical torment. When the secret police hold both the whip and the legal monopoly on coercion, formal statutes become little more than instruments to entrench the regime.

This dynamic was not confined to obscure dissidents. Major historical flashpoints—such as the August 15, 1974 assassination attempt on Park Chung-hee by Mun Se-gwang, which resulted in the death of First Lady Yuk Young-soo—were managed behind a dense fog of internal secrecy, handled by specialized covert squads like the agency's Songjaeban unit. Official investigation files were repeatedly manipulated to serve political survival. Decades later, historians and documentarians trying to untangle these episodes have discovered that relying uncritically on contemporary state records means adopting the distorted narrative written by the interrogators themselves.

The Tragic Betrayal of Professor Choi Jong-gil

Professor Choi Jong-gil was one of South Korea's brightest legal minds, having completed advanced legal studies at the University of Tübingen in West Germany. That European pedigree made him an irresistible target for the KCIA's Counterintelligence Bureau 5, Section 10 (Operations). The bureau was constructing a massive narrative of an overseas espionage network, and they needed prominent figures to legitimize it.

The operation's section chief, Lieutenant Colonel Ahn Heung-ryeong—an army officer seconded from the Defense Security Command—approached the younger brother with deliberate courtesy, framing the interview as an informal consultation. Trusting the agency he served, the young inspection officer vouched for his organization. It took only seventy-two hours for that trust to destroy his family.

Refusing to betray his conscience or endorse a fabricated spy ring, Professor Choi endured horrific beatings and electric torture in the subterranean interrogation chambers of Namsan. When he succumbed, the agency’s leadership initiated an immediate cover-up. A chain of command extending from interrogators Kim Sang-hoon, Choi Chul-kwon, and Ko Byung-hoon up through Section Chief Ahn, Task Force Chief Jang Seong-rok, Counterintelligence Bureau Director Ahn Gyeong-sang, Assistant Deputy Director Cho Il-je, and KCIA Deputy Director Kim Chi-yeol (who would later serve as Minister of Justice) coordinated the story. They announced that Professor Choi had admitted to espionage before leaping from a bathroom window.

"The unbearable guilt of having delivered my own brother to the agency stayed with me every day of my life, and that guilt was one of the driving forces that compelled me to write down the truth."

The tragedy proved that the dictatorship’s violence did not merely strike down critics; it weaponized the loyalty and filial bonds of its own officials, turning family members into unwitting accomplices.

From Elite Agent to Monitored Target: Life Under the Code Name 'Assassin'

Choi Jong-sun learned of his brother's death while working out of an office in Seoul's central Sogong-dong neighborhood. When the news arrived, a furious colleague in the room shouted that they should declare open war against the agency's leadership. But Choi knew the agency too well. An emotional, suicidal confrontation would accomplish nothing and endanger the surviving relatives. He intervened, calmed his colleague, and resolved to step directly into the lion's mouth to shield his family and collect the facts.

Returning to the agency knowing his brother had been murdered by his own colleagues turned Choi into a living liability for the KCIA high command. Internal security classified him as an acute danger, assigning him the surveillance code name "Assassin." The agency placed him under unrelenting surveillance: wiretaps hissed on his telephone lines, and unidentified operatives shadowed his footsteps through the backstreets of Seoul.

Under constant threat of being dragged into a basement himself, Choi utilized the very surveillance tradecraft he had been taught to slip past monitors and collect testimonies, names, and logs. Years later, those clandestine notes formed the foundation of his memoir, Speak, the Living (Sanja-yeo Malhara), a detailed internal whistleblowing account that laid bare the mechanics of the torture room. His written testimony would later serve as the direct inspiration for the Korean investigative feature film The Assassins, bringing an insider's view of state violence into the modern public consciousness.

Censorship, Blank Front Pages, and Economic Revenge

The physical torture on Mount Namsan was backed by an equally brutal stranglehold over the public square. Throughout the Yushin era, KCIA agents and Ministry of Culture and Public Information officials maintained permanent desks inside newsrooms, armed with red pens to excise reporting on labor unrest, student demonstrations, and unlawful detentions.

At major dailies like Dong-a Ilbo, editors scrambled to fill unexpected holes when censors struck entire columns shortly before print deadlines. Often, the presses ran with stark white boxes—blank newsprint that signaled to readers that the state had suppressed the truth. When journalists rose up in the historic October 24, 1974 Declaration for Freedom of the Press, the Park regime retaliated with corporate economic warfare, hauling major advertisers to the KCIA to intimidate them into canceling contracts. Citizens responded by purchasing small, personal advertisements of encouragement in what became South Korea's iconic "Blank Advertisement Crisis," financing the paper line by line until mass dismissals crushed the newsroom.

For reporters, watching colleagues dragged into dungeons while their own pens were paralyzed produced an enduring collective trauma. As one veteran journalist later recalled, reporters wept tears of blood with nowhere to turn, knowing that an isolated press left the wider public completely defenseless against arbitrary arrests.

What to Watch Next

Decades after democratization, South Korea has officially acknowledged the torture and unlawful death of Professor Choi Jong-gil through the Presidential Truth Commission on Suspicious Deaths. Yet the institutional lessons of the Choi brothers' tragedy remain deeply relevant in contemporary South Korean politics. As discussions persist regarding the proper division of authority between the National Intelligence Service (the modern successor to the KCIA), the prosecution service, and national police investigators, several critical points remain on the public radar:

  • Demilitarization and Civilian Oversight of Intelligence: Watch whether the National Assembly introduces further statutory guardrails to keep the National Intelligence Service strictly focused on foreign and North Korean intelligence, preventing any covert encroachment into domestic politics or civilian monitoring.
  • Prosecutorial Reform and Investigative Separation: South Korea's ongoing debate over separating criminal indictment authority from direct investigative power stems directly from historical memories of the prosecution acting as a political tool. The Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung continues to emphasize civilian oversight of law enforcement, while the main opposition People Power Party argues against destabilizing established prosecutorial procedures.
  • Restitution and Historical Archives: Continued archival declassification efforts around 1970s security cases will test the judicial system's willingness to review lingering historical convictions based on tainted investigative files.

The tragic journey of Choi Jong-sun demonstrates that institutions granted unchecked power will inevitably consume even their most dedicated servants. As long as state agencies possess the capacity to act in secret, vigilance over legal procedure and press freedom remains the only bulwark against the return of the shadows of Namsan.
