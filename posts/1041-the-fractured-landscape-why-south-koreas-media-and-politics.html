The Fractured Landscape: Why South Korea’s Media and Politics are Reaching a Boiling Point

September 23, 2026

For many in South Korea, the daily news cycle has become a source of profound exhaustion. From the high-stakes sentencing of military commanders involved in the recent insurrection to the abrupt resignation of ministerial nominees and a relentless series of diplomatic crises, the country feels as if it is being battered by an endless wave of instability. Behind these headlines, a deeper question remains: Where is South Korean society actually headed, and what does the current chaos reveal about the true state of public sentiment?

To understand the current climate, we must look beyond the official talking points and examine how the public is consuming information and interpreting the crises of the day. A recent, authoritative look at the 2026 media landscape offers a window into this shifting reality.

The Rise of "Broadcasting Without a Transmitter"

The most striking indicator of how Korea’s media landscape has fundamentally transformed is the 2026 Media Credibility Survey, conducted jointly by SisaIN (a prominent liberal news magazine) and Gallup Korea. The survey found that "Kim Ou-joon’s News Factory," a digital-native YouTube program, has secured the top spot as the most trusted news source in the country (6.3%). It outperformed traditional giants like MBC’s Newsdesk (5.9%) and KBS’s News9 (4.9%).

For Kim Ou-joon, a veteran journalist, this milestone represents the realization of a goal he set years ago: to create a "broadcasting station without a transmission tower." While he originally planned for this digital platform to rival traditional terrestrial broadcasters within five years, he achieved it in just three years and nine months. This shift signals that the monopoly on agenda-setting once held by legacy media and conservative newspapers has fully splintered. The public has proven that they no longer view traditional outlets as the sole gatekeepers of truth.

This is further reflected in the rankings for trusted journalists. While former JTBC anchor Son Suk-hee remains the most trusted individual, figures like Kim Ou-joon and author Rhyu Si-min—commentators who wear their political philosophies on their sleeves—now command higher trust than those who claim "mechanical neutrality." In a period of political turmoil, citizens are gravitating toward voices that provide sharp, unapologetic analysis rather than those who attempt to balance opposing sides for the sake of appearances.

The Trap of Passive Diplomacy

On the international stage, South Korea finds itself in a precarious position. Ahead of the U.S.-China summit scheduled for late September 2026, the global order is shifting. Analysis from the Land Liberty Institute suggests that for the first time, AI regulation will be a core agenda item alongside traditional trade and investment. While China once struggled under U.S. sanctions, it has gained the strategic confidence to push back, leading to a much more intense "arm-wrestling" match in Washington and Beijing.

Against this backdrop, experts have sharply criticized the current South Korean government’s approach as dangerously passive. The criticism is that while other nations come to the table with complex strategies, South Korea is stuck in a defensive crouch, obsessing over "damage control" rather than asserting its own national interests. As the U.S. and China redefine their geopolitical boundaries, Korea’s lack of an independent, proactive strategy—what some call "autonomous diplomacy"—has left it sidelined, merely reacting to the demands of major powers rather than shaping the regional conversation.

The Reckoning for Insurrectionist Commanders

Domestically, the judicial system is finally holding those involved in the recent, abortive military insurrection accountable. The Seoul Central District Court recently saw prosecutors demand heavy sentences for former military commanders—up to 30 years in prison for figures like former Capital Defense Commander Lee Jin-woo and former Counterintelligence Commander Yeo In-hyung. These officers were accused of colluding with former President Yoon Suk-yeol to stage an illegal martial law declaration.

The trial has revealed a stark contrast in character among the accused. While most have attempted to shift blame onto their subordinates or pleaded ignorance, former Special Warfare Commander Kwak Jong-geun stood out by admitting his wrongdoing and apologizing to the public. His admission, and the subsequent plea for leniency from families of the 1980 Gwangju Uprising victims, provided a rare moment of moral clarity in an otherwise dark chapter.

The court’s scrutiny has been particularly harsh toward those who claimed they were "just following orders." The judiciary has made it clear: the tragedy was only stopped because of the passive resistance of rank-and-file soldiers and the heroic intervention of ordinary citizens who blocked tanks with their own bodies. This trial serves as a grim warning that in a democracy, "obeying orders" is no defense for undermining the constitutional order.

A Crisis of Trust in the Judiciary

Finally, the judiciary itself is reeling from a scandal involving Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Investigations have revealed that the Supreme Court spent approximately $190,000 (260 million won) in official business expenses by using deceptive accounting practices. The records show the court consistently inflated the number of attendees at luxury restaurants—specifically high-end omakase spots—to bypass spending limits per person.

While the court administration dismissed these as "simple clerical errors," the scale and repetition of these "mistakes" have severely damaged the institution's moral authority. For a body that demands strict adherence to the law from the public, engaging in such petty, systemic corruption has been a massive blow to public trust. It raises the uncomfortable question: If the guardians of the law do not respect the spirit of the law, how can they maintain the legitimacy required to judge the nation?

What to Watch Next

  • Political Polls: Monitor whether the recent rebound in President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings (hovering around 37%) continues as the administration navigates these domestic and foreign crises.
  • Diplomatic Maneuvering: Watch for the government's rhetoric during the upcoming UN General Assembly. Any signal of a shift toward "autonomous diplomacy" will be a key indicator of whether the administration is changing its tactical approach.
  • Judicial Reform: Look for whether the Supreme Court faces genuine internal reform or further calls for external oversight regarding its budget management, which will serve as a bellwether for the public’s eroding faith in judicial integrity.
