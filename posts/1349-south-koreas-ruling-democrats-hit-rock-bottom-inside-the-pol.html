South Korea’s Ruling Democrats Hit Rock Bottom: Inside the Polling Collapse of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 20, 2026

Inside the National Assembly in Seoul, the mood among lawmakers of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has shifted from quiet unease to open alarm. For months, party strategists had reassured themselves that governing fatigue was a temporary phase and that their progressive coalition would eventually rally around President Lee Jae-myung. But the latest survey from Realmeter, one of South Korea’s most closely watched polling agencies, delivered numbers that shattered those illusions: the Democratic Party has plunged to the lowest public approval rating in its history, trailing the conservative opposition across almost every key demographic and geographic battleground in the country.

For observers outside South Korea, this collapse might appear puzzling. After the dramatic impeachment and removal of former conservative leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently serving a prison sentence—the Democratic Party returned to power with an ambitious progressive mandate. Yet, in its second year of governance, the Lee Jae-myung administration finds itself besieged not by the external maneuvers of its opponents, but by deep internal fractures, growing alienation among young voters, and a mounting public sense that its reform agenda has lost both direction and competence.

The Numbers Behind the Collapse: A Historic Low for the Ruling Party

The headline polling figures tell a stark story of political reversal. According to recent Realmeter data, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main opposition party, has surged to 47.3% support. The Democratic Party, meanwhile, has sunk to historical lows, struggling far behind. Smaller opposition parties have failed to absorb the disaffected voters: the Cho Kuk Innovation Party (Rebuilding Korea Party) registered just 4.4%, the left-wing Progressive Party took 1.5%, and the center-right Reform Party sat at 0.7%. Rather than fragmenting into alternative progressive movements, public sentiment has consolidated sharply behind the conservative opposition.

Equally devastating are the personal ratings for the Democratic Party’s leadership. Party Representative Kim Min-seok recorded a positive job performance rating of only 28.0%, with a mere 11.7% saying he is doing "very well." In contrast, his negative rating climbed to an overwhelming 66.3%. When nearly half of self-identified progressive voters express dissatisfaction with the head of the ruling party, the problem ceases to be a messaging glitch; it becomes an existential leadership crisis.

Inside the party, the blame game has already begun. The coalition is fraying along factional fault lines, particularly between the "pro-Myung" loyalists tied directly to President Lee Jae-myung and rival camps such as the "pro-Cheong" faction. High-profile personnel disputes, such as the contentious nomination of Kim Ji-yong to head the newly established Major Crimes Investigation Agency, have reinforced the perception that the ruling party is consumed by internal turf wars rather than bread-and-butter governance. To ordinary citizens, the party's focus looks less like national stewardship and more like parochial infighting.

The Demographic Fault Line: Losing the Capital and the Youth

To understand the depth of the ruling party’s crisis, one must look at South Korea's electoral geography. For more than a decade, the greater capital region—encompassing Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province, home to roughly half of the nation's 51 million people—has served as the structural fortress of the progressive bloc. Today, that fortress is crumbling.

In Seoul, the People Power Party leads the Democratic Party by an astonishing margin of 45.7% to 26.7%. While the broader Gyeonggi and Incheon area remains relatively competitive at 41.4% for the PPP versus 39.5% for the DPK, the trend is unmistakable. Across the entire peninsula, only the southwestern Honam region (traditionally the Democratic Party's geographic heartland in the Jeolla provinces) still holds a pro-DPK majority. Every other region has swung toward the conservative opposition.

Even more perilous for the Democrats is the wholesale flight of voters in their twenties and thirties—the so-called "2030 generation." In both age brackets, support for the People Power Party has crossed 50%, outpacing the Democratic Party by more than double:

  • Generational Disillusionment: Younger voters, who previously formed a vital part of the progressive coalition, have turned away in droves. Their frustration is not ideological; it stems from a lack of tangible economic fairness, stagnant employment prospects, and perceived double standards in political appointments.
  • The Flight of Moderates: As recently as May, self-identified political moderates favored the Democratic Party. Today, that crucial swing bloc has shifted to the PPP, which commands 37.7% of centrists.
  • Electoral Math: Lawmakers within the ruling party are now openly warning that if these trends hold into the upcoming general elections, the Democratic Party risks falling below 120 seats in the 300-member National Assembly, potentially wiping out their legislative leverage entirely.

The concept of a "structural majority"—an idea long championed by both former President Moon Jae-in and President Lee Jae-myung—assumed that changing demographics would permanently favor the progressive camp. That theory is now being dismantled by the very generation it relied upon.

The Factional Rift and the Limits of "Pacing" Reform

Faced with plunging poll numbers, President Lee Jae-myung recently held an emergency press conference, acknowledging that "our preparation and management were insufficient" and proposing to adjust the "speed" of his reform agenda. Yet that diagnosis has drawn sharp criticism from political analysts and allies alike.

"When your driving is headed in the wrong direction, turning the steering wheel is the solution; merely slowing down will not prevent an accident. If the destination itself is flawed, you must reset the destination."

The critique strikes at the heart of the administration’s strategy. Framing the government’s troubles as a matter of "moving too fast" implies that the underlying policies are sound and that public resistance is simply an obstacle to be managed. In reality, critics argue, the administration lacks a coherent, transparent roadmap that explains who is accountable for past missteps and what concrete results citizens should expect. A ruling party cannot govern solely using the passionate language of core ideological conviction; it must rely on the language of administrative responsibility.

Furthermore, attempts to rally the progressive base through high-stakes partisan confrontations—such as controversial efforts to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—have backfired among moderate voters. While aggressive political battles might momentarily appease hardline supporters, they heighten institutional instability and accelerate the flight of uncommitted voters. Genuine national unity cannot mean granting political immunity to one's own allies while weaponizing accountability against opponents; it requires consistency, where misconduct is handled strictly regardless of party affiliation.

Communication vs. Governance: What Comes Next

President Lee has long prided himself on direct, unmediated engagement with the public, frequently turning to social media platforms to communicate policy decisions and address complaints. While direct outreach has its merits, observers increasingly point out that public relations cannot substitute for institutional reform.

The fundamental test of communication is not how many posts an administration publishes or how approachable a leader appears online. The test is whether citizen feedback leads to measurable improvements in state bureaucracy, whether uncomfortable questions are answered honestly, and whether administrative errors are corrected swiftly. Without clear institutional accountability, direct messaging risks degenerating into unilateral publicity—an approach that may energize dedicated online followers but fails to convince a skeptical wider public.

As the administration navigates its second year, several key developments will determine whether the Democratic Party can recover from this historic nadir:

  • Cabinet and Leadership Shakeups: With party chief Kim Min-seok facing severe negative ratings, pressure will intensify for an overhaul of both party leadership and presidential advisory staff to signal a genuine break from past errors.
  • Legislative Reorientation: Observers must watch whether the administration shifts from contentious institutional battles toward practical, kitchen-table economic policies that speak directly to the grievances of moderate and 2030 voters.
  • Managing Intra-Party Warfare: Whether the pro-Lee faction can establish an equitable working relationship with dissenting party wings will determine if the ruling party enters the next election united or irreconcilably fractured.

Slowing down an erratic engine is not enough to regain the public's confidence. For President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party, the coming months will test whether they possess the humility to re-examine their course, accept accountability for misjudgments, and offer voters tangible outcomes rather than defensive rhetoric.
