The Democratic Party’s Strategic Crisis: Why Popular Support is Slipping Under Kim Min-seok

October 09, 2026

Barely a year into the current administration, the gears of South Korean politics are grinding with a jarring, discordant sound. When Kim Min-seok, the newly elected leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country’s ruling party—took the helm just two months ago, he made a bold pledge: he would turn around the party’s and the government’s sagging approval ratings within 100 days. Instead of a recovery, however, the public is witnessing a series of alarming fractures that have left even core supporters bewildered.

Watching the current state of affairs, one cannot help but sigh at the sheer tactical ineptitude on display. This is not a matter of complex political engineering; the basic flow of recent events suggests a leadership team that is completely disconnected from the electorate. The question is no longer whether they can achieve their goals, but whether their current strategy is even grounded in reality.

The Fragile Path of Kim Min-seok

Kim Min-seok’s promise to boost approval ratings in 100 days already looks like a gamble doomed to fail. Critics, including political analysts like Lee Jeong-won, have tried to frame the party's recent moves—targeting the investigative powers of the prosecution and reshuffling the Corruption Investigation Office—as part of a grand, calculated puzzle. However, stripping away the complex interpretations, the reality is far simpler: the party’s current operational strategy is aimless.

The frustration within the party is palpable. When people whisper, "How could they be this incompetent?" it isn't just the opposition throwing stones; it is a manifestation of despair from within the DPK's own base. The leadership seems to be repeating the same tired scripts while the ground beneath them continues to shift. By focusing on internal consolidation rather than reading the "floor" of public sentiment, Kim is effectively isolating the party rather than expanding its reach.

Even more baffling is Kim’s recent rhetoric regarding the "undermining of presidential leadership." Kim has lashed out at hardline members of his own party who are pushing for aggressive prosecutorial reforms, claiming they are endangering the leadership of President Lee Jae-myung. This is a classic case of projection. When the party’s own leader ignores the fact that the President’s approval rating is suffering due to repeated policy missteps, attacking his own colleagues—rather than addressing the substance of their concerns—is not leadership; it is a lack of self-awareness.

The contradiction is best illustrated by the DPK's prosecutorial reform task force. This group spent over $700,000 (1 billion KRW) in public funds over the last year, including roughly $90,000 (130 million KRW) just on advisory fees, yet failed to produce a final government-sanctioned proposal. To protect such a stagnant organization while chastising members for "hurting the President’s leadership" for demanding actual results is a peak of hypocrisy that the public is noticing.

The Warning Signs of "Inevitable Failure"

This atmosphere of dysfunction was recently highlighted by Rhyu Si-min, a prominent liberal commentator and intellectual. In a recent appearance on a popular YouTube political program, Rhyu argued that President Lee Jae-myung does not truly desire a complete separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, and is instead attempting to "reconstruct" the ruling party in an artificial way. Rhyu warned that this path leads to an "inevitable failure."

Rhyu’s critique was sharp: he characterized the planning team supporting the President as woefully underskilled, creating massive political risks. "I am deeply anxious," he remarked. "When you consider why the President is so obsessively pushing this, it is hard not to see it as part of an attempt to rebuild the party structure to his liking."

Predictably, the DPK establishment reacted with hostility. Figures like Representative Park Jie-won and Minister of Planning and Budget Park Hong-geun dismissed Rhyu’s remarks as "curses" and "language not fit for a comrade." Yet, their reflexive defensiveness fails to answer the core issue: why did a prominent liberal voice feel the need to offer such a dire warning? By labeling any dissent as a "curse," the leadership is choosing to ignore the structural rot that Rhyu identified.

This desperation is perhaps most clearly seen in the reckless push to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. After candidate Song Young-gil floated the idea, Kim Min-seok embraced it as a "winning card." It was, in reality, a massive strategic blunder. The judiciary is a constitutional pillar, and treating it as a pawn in a power struggle has only served to alienate moderate voters. This move, lacking sound legal basis or public consensus, has become a political time bomb that is blowing up in the DPK’s face.

The Collapse of 'Minty 07'

The party’s lack of planning has also manifested in its digital communication strategy. On October 7th, the main writer for "Minty 07," the flagship program of the DPK’s official YouTube channel, abruptly resigned after only one month. The departure was billed as a result of "exhaustion," but the reality is that the project was a failure from the start.

The channel attempted to rely on high-profile talent from other successful political YouTube shows, marketing the launch with aggressive press releases. But in digital media, name recognition is no substitute for content quality. The program quickly became a platform for hostility and ridicule rather than constructive communication. Even as viewership tanked, the leadership’s response was to lengthen the runtime, proving they fundamentally misunderstand why their audience is turning away.

This failure mirrors the larger crisis of the party: an organization so blinded by internal defense and power dynamics that it has lost the ability to speak to the public. As the DPK focuses on silencing critics and obsessing over short-term survival, it is losing the very "structural majority" it claims to possess.

What to Watch Next

  • Local Election Preparations: Keep a close eye on how the DPK handles the upcoming Seoul mayoral race. With disapproval ratings in Seoul among voters in their 30s reaching 70%, the party is in a precarious position.
  • Internal Dissent: Watch for further friction between the "pro-Lee" faction and reformists who feel the current party leadership is leading them toward an electoral cliff.
  • Government Policy Shifts: Any change in the "prosecutorial reform" agenda or a sudden pivot to actual livelihood policies will be a clear indicator of whether the leadership is finally feeling the heat from public polling.

The Democratic Party is at a crossroads. Unless it abandons the "us versus them" mentality and begins to address its own institutional incompetence, the promises of recovery will remain nothing more than empty rhetoric.
