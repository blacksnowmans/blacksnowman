How South Korea's Prosecution Reform Became a Weapon of Power

October 12, 2026

For anyone observing South Korean politics from afar, the machinery of government often appears to run on grand promises of reform. On the official website of the South Korean presidency, a list known as the "123 State Affairs Tasks" remains prominently displayed. Item number three on that list carries immense weight: completing prosecution reform by entirely separating investigative and indicting powers.

The logic behind this promise, championed by the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal party), was straightforward. For decades, South Korea's prosecution service held an immense, virtually unchecked concentration of power, frequently weaponized for political ends. The blueprint to dismantle this system involved dissolving the traditional prosecutors' office and replacing it with specialized bodies—an indictment office and a separate investigation agency—to build a permanent firewall between those who investigate crimes and those who decide to prosecute them. It was meant to be the ultimate safeguard for democratic accountability.

Yet, reality has fractured that blueprint. As President Lee Jae-myung's administration has settled into power, its actual trajectory has turned away from dismantling prosecutorial overreach. Instead, critics argue, the administration has mastered the art of wearing the badge of reform while quietly harnessing the prosecution's machinery for its own ends. For outside observers trying to understand why South Korean progressive politics is currently tearing itself apart from the inside, the story of this broken promise is where it all begins.

The Erosion of 'Irreversible' Institutional Reform

To understand the depth of the current disillusionment, one must look at what was promised regarding the Ministry of Justice. A core pillar of the liberal platform was the "irreversible de-prosecution" of the ministry—a commitment to systematically reduce the presence of career prosecutors and dispatched prosecutors, replacing them with civilian legal administrators to break the institutional dominance of the prosecutorial caste.

Today, a glance at the Ministry of Justice's organizational chart tells a different story. From the ministry's spokesperson to the planning, legal affairs, criminal justice, and human rights bureaus, career prosecutors remain firmly entrenched in key positions. In fact, critics note that even compared to the era of the disgraced former conservative leader Yoon Seok-youl—who was impeached and is currently imprisoned—these positions are now filled with prosecutors just as firmly, if not more so.

Recent structural changes have only compounded these contradictions. Under a major overhaul of the criminal justice system, the traditional prosecution service was officially restructured, with total personnel dropping from roughly 10,700 down to about 8,400. Yet, the quota of actual prosecutors remained untouched at 2,292. Critics point out that maintaining the exact same number of prosecutors while nominally shrinking the agency's overall footprint creates a top-heavy, institutional anomaly. Furthermore, the rapid bifurcation of investigative and indicting agencies has created bureaucratic friction, leading to delayed case handling and growing concerns that complex financial and technological crimes are slipping through the cracks of a disjointed system.

Internal Purges and the Rise of a New Factional Politics

The friction is not merely bureaucratic; it has spilled over into a bitter ideological civil war within the liberal camp itself. As the administration faces mounting pressure from civil society and reform-minded party members to honor its original pledges, President Lee’s camp has increasingly dismissed dissenters as "extremists" or political outliers.

This dismissive rhetoric has filtered down to a vocal, aggressive new base of online and political supporters often referred to as the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction. Rather than focusing their political fire on the conservative opposition, the People Power Party, this faction has increasingly turned its blades inward. Traditional reformists, moderate voices within the Democratic Party, and figures seeking to protect the party’s historical identity have found themselves branded as internal enemies.

Critics argue that this factional aggression has adopted the very tactics once deployed by the conservative right—weaponizing coordinated online campaigns, pushing dubious narratives, and demanding absolute ideological conformity. When leaders within the progressive movement attempt to raise rational objections to how investigative powers are being handled, they are met with fierce retaliation. For longtime members of the Democratic Party who trace their lineage back to decades of fighting military dictatorships, watching a progressive government utilize party-loyalist factions to crush internal debate feels like a profound betrayal of their movement's soul.

Recycling the Tools of the Past

The most glaring irony of the current political landscape is the recycling of old investigative tools against new targets. The administration’s critics are quick to point out the double standard surrounding past high-profile scandals. For instance, while certain controversial past investigations are quietly glossed over, other legacy cases—such as the contentious troubled-asset investigations involving the firm Lime and Sillajen—are being selectively re-examined or utilized when politically convenient.

Sillajen, a biotech firm whose high-profile collapse years ago triggered sweeping political and financial storms, had already been thoroughly scrutinized by prosecutors during previous administrations under heavy political direction. The decision by elements within the current progressive ecosystem to revive these old investigative playbooks has sparked widespread alarm. When a democratic government begins dusting off the very prosecutorial weapons it once vowed to dismantle just to settle internal scores or manage political fallout, the lines between reformer and authoritarian begin to blur.

Legal experts and civic watchdogs warn that institutionalizing this kind of selective justice creates a dangerous precedent. The public did not vote to hand over the levers of prosecutorial power so that a new ruling elite could simply change the locks on the executive offices and take the helm of the machine.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this complex political transition, several key developments will indicate whether the administration changes course or doubles down on its current trajectory:

  • The Implementation of New Investigative Agencies: Monitor how the newly established investigative and indicting bodies actually function in practice, particularly regarding whether independent oversight prevents political interference.
  • Intra-Party Power Struggles: Watch for fractures within the Democratic Party as moderate lawmakers and traditional reformists push back against pressure from hardline factions.
  • Civil Society Pushback: Pay attention to legal reform groups and progressive civic organizations, whose willingness to publicly criticize the administration will test the government's democratic responsiveness.

The fundamental question facing South Korean democracy is no longer just whether the prosecution can be reformed, but whether the political forces that claimed the mantle of reform can resist the intoxicating pull of the very power they swore to dismantle.
