South Korea’s Political Turbulence: From ‘Emperor Golf’ Scandals to Labor Policy U-turns

October 28, 2026

To understand the current state of South Korean politics, one must look past the superficial headlines and into the structural frictions defining the Lee Jae-myung administration. Since the impeachment and subsequent imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol, South Korea has seen a transition of power to President Lee Jae-myung and his party, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the current ruling party. However, the legacy of political mudslinging and the remnants of a rigid, often dysfunctional judicial system continue to hamper the new government’s progress.

From the collapse of meritless "Emperor Golf" allegations to the internal contradictions of the government's own labor policies, the political landscape remains polarized. As an outside observer, it is crucial to sift through the noise and identify which issues reflect genuine policy dilemmas and which are merely the echoes of a deeply entrenched, adversarial political culture.

The 'Emperor Golf' Allegations: When Fake News Meets the Courts

Political disinformation remains a significant tax on South Korea’s social cohesion. A recent case involving Joo Jin-woo, a lawmaker from the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative opposition), highlights the cost of reckless accusations. Joo alleged that the former First Lady engaged in "Emperor Golf"—claiming she received private lessons from professional golfer Lydia Ko and enjoyed exclusive access to a clubhouse during a thunderstorm. The claims were widely viewed as a political "self-sabotage" attempt.

Under scrutiny, the allegations disintegrated. There was no evidence of lessons from Lydia Ko, nor was there any basis for the claims regarding exclusive access. Legal experts suggest that if this case proceeds to trial, the probability of a defamation conviction—likely carrying a fine of over $700 (1 million KRW)—is nearly 100%. Attempting to circumvent legal accountability through private, behind-the-scenes apologies, as some in the opposition have attempted, serves only to underline the lack of substance in their initial claims. For the political class, this serves as a stark reminder: in the digital age, the failure to perform basic fact-checking is a direct assault on one’s own political viability.

Judicial Accountability and the 'Law Distortion' Charge

The belief that the judiciary is a sacred, untouchable entity is being challenged. A major development recently saw Judge Joo Hye-geun, who presided over the appeals trial of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, accused of "distorting the law." This is a new legal mechanism designed to hold judges accountable for rulings that deviate from the law for political or subjective reasons.

When an AI-based legal analysis program reviewed the judge's 9-page ruling, it identified significant logical errors and inconsistencies. For too long, the refrain "a judge has ruled, so it must be final" has shielded the judiciary from necessary oversight. If the courts are to be trusted, they must be transparent. The legal pushback against Judge Joo is a litmus test for South Korea’s legal reform movement, signaling that judicial independence does not equate to immunity from logical and ethical scrutiny.

The Labor Policy Paradox: The 52-Hour Workweek vs. the 4.5-Day Promise

Perhaps the most baffling contradiction in the new administration is its labor policy. President Lee Jae-myung campaigned on the promise of introducing a 4.5-day workweek. Yet, the government is currently pushing for a "special exemption" to the 52-hour workweek for semiconductor "mega clusters." The policy would allow research and white-collar professionals to work beyond the 52-hour limit, provided they consent and receive authorization from the Minister of Employment and Labor.

This "exemption-heavy" approach has drawn sharp criticism, both for its substance and its methodology. By attempting to force through these changes via administrative decrees—much like the "decree-based politics" criticized during the Yoon Suk-yeol era—the administration risks alienating its core base. The Minister of Employment and Labor, Kim Young-hoon, who has a long history in labor activism, has publicly expressed that innovation and workers' rights should not be mutually exclusive. If the government continues to bypass social dialogue, it risks losing the credibility of its labor-friendly platform.

Economic Rhetoric and the Trap of Social Division

Tensions also erupted recently when President Lee Jae-myung made comments regarding foreign laborers, suggesting that their work does not significantly benefit the national economy because wages are sent abroad. This comment was not only met with immediate backlash but was also factually flawed: government data shows that approximately 43% of foreign workers' wages are spent domestically, contributing directly to local consumption and internal circulation.

The incident escalated when the President publicly criticized the newspaper Hankyoreh for omitting the word "significantly" from his statement in a headline. The resulting spat—involving the President "pointing out" a media outlet and the outlet eventually issuing a correction—exposed a volatile relationship between the executive branch and the press. Rather than engaging in productive policy debates, the administration and the media seem trapped in a cycle of reactive, petty conflict that serves neither the public interest nor the stability of the state.

What to Watch Next

  • The Outcome of the 'Law Distortion' Case: Keep an eye on whether the judiciary adopts more transparent, AI-audited ruling procedures to regain public trust.
  • Labor Policy Resolution: Will the government stick to its 4.5-day workweek promise, or will the push for semiconductor industry exemptions lead to a fracture within the ruling party or the resignation of Labor Minister Kim?
  • Administrative Maturity: Watch to see if the President's office moves toward a more mature stance on media relations, or if the "us vs. them" rhetoric continues to overshadow substantive national policy.
