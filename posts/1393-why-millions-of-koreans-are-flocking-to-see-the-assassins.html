Why Millions of Koreans Are Flocking to See "The Assassins"

October 22, 2026

The cinema is packed, and the atmosphere is heavy with more than just popcorn and soda. The Assassins, a gripping new film, has just crossed the 3-million-viewer mark in its fifth week—a significant feat in the South Korean market, representing roughly $21 million (30 billion KRW) in ticket sales. But this is not just a standard blockbuster success. The audiences filing into these theaters aren't here for mindless spectacle or political gossip; they are here to confront a ghost from the nation's past.

The film centers on the 1974 assassination of First Lady Yuk Young-soo, a tragedy that occurred during the "Yushin" era—the brutal, authoritarian dictatorship of Park Chung-hee. Despite aggressive campaigns from the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative opposition) to boycott the film and labeling it "historically distorted," the public has voted with their wallets. Even as the film’s cast and director faced threats that forced the cancellation of events at the Busan International Film Festival, the theaters remained full. This success is a profound signal from the public: they are hungry for the truth that was suppressed half a century ago.

Resurrecting the Spirit of 1974

At its core, The Assassins is a procedural thriller based on the 2005 investigative reporting by MBC’s documentary series Now It Can Be Told. It dissects the official narrative of the 1974 shooting, which blamed a lone gunman, Mun Se-gwang. The film highlights the glaring inconsistencies—missing ballistics evidence, conflicting reports on the number of shots fired, and mysterious details regarding the shooter’s entry into the country—that the state spent decades trying to bury.

But the film is more than a detective story; it is a tribute to the journalists of that era who, despite working under the suffocating censorship of the military regime, refused to stop writing. During the mid-70s, the "People's Revolutionary Party" incident saw eight individuals executed a mere 18 hours after the Supreme Court upheld their death sentences—a judicial murder that shocked the nation. Major newspapers like the Chosun Ilbo, Dong-A Ilbo, and JoongAng Ilbo remained silent, paralyzed by state pressure. That silence became a festering wound of shame for the journalists of that generation.

The Night the Pens Rose Up

The climax of the film—and the turning point in South Korean media history—is the October 24, 1974, Declaration of Freedom of the Press. After years of state-sponsored gag orders, reporters at the Dong-A Ilbo finally reached a breaking point. On the night of "UN Day," they locked themselves in the newsroom to stage an all-night sit-in. They demanded that the "intelligence agents" (state censors embedded in newsrooms) leave immediately. It was a declaration of war against the regime's control.

The repercussions were swift and cruel. The regime pressured advertisers to pull their business from the Dong-A Ilbo, leading to the infamous "blank advertising" era, where citizens bought small spaces in the paper just to express support for the journalists. Eventually, the reporters were fired en masse. Yet, rather than fading away, these journalists formed the Dong-A Committee for the Defense of Freedom of the Press and the Chosun Committee, beginning a decades-long struggle that would serve as the bedrock for the later democratic movement.

From Underground Resistance to a New Kind of Newspaper

The legacy of these fired journalists is immense. In the 1980s, after being pushed out of their jobs, they founded the magazine Mal. It was this magazine that exposed the military junta’s "Reporting Guidelines"—a secret document dictating exactly what news should be manipulated—which cracked the foundation of the Chun Doo-hwan regime. Their ultimate goal was never just a magazine; they viewed it as a "stepping stone" toward creating a true citizen-owned newspaper. That dream was realized in 1988 with the founding of The Hankyoreh, the world’s first newspaper funded entirely by public shareholders.

By 2014, on the 40th anniversary of their original declaration, these veteran journalists established the Freedom of the Press Foundation. It was a necessary lifeline for younger reporters who, during the administration of the now-imprisoned Yun Suk-yeol, faced renewed crackdowns on public broadcasting and constant pressure to align with the regime's interests.

A Profession at a Crossroads

Today, the landscape of Korean media is fractured. We see the rise of "polinurnalists"—anchors who hop directly from the news desk into the ruling party’s offices—and the toxic grip of clickbait algorithms. While the overt dictatorship of the 70s has vanished, it has been replaced by a more insidious form of media control: commercialization and extreme political polarization.

The success of The Assassins reminds us of the fundamental purpose of journalism. As the late Song Geon-ho, the legendary figure who inspired the film, once said: "If I must choose between being faithful to my employer and being faithful to my profession, I will choose my profession."

The film’s 3 million viewers are effectively asking today’s reporters a difficult question: Whose freedom are you protecting? Are you a merchant selling outrage for clicks, or are you the voice for those who have been silenced? As South Korea navigates the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung and the shifting political landscape, the demand for a press that serves the people—not the powers that be—has never been more urgent.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on how the Freedom of the Press Foundation interacts with current legislative debates regarding public broadcasting governance. As the media environment struggles to balance tech-driven news consumption with traditional editorial responsibility, the historical precedent set in 1974 remains the ultimate yardstick for what "fair" and "free" journalism truly looks like.
