The Evolutionary Trap: Why Wild Animals Thrive on Sugar While Humans Face a Diabetes Crisis

October 09, 2026

Imagine yourself on a quiet evening: the workday is done, you have settled onto your sofa, and you reach for a bowl of fresh fruit. It feels like a healthy choice, yet you cannot help but sigh when you think about your last checkup or the number on the scale. Now, consider the hummingbird or the fruit bat. These animals consume a diet heavy in nectar and sugar, yet they do not suffer from obesity or diabetes. They live in a completely different set of evolutionary rules. To understand why our modern bodies are struggling so much, we must look at the insights of Choi Jae-chun, a renowned Korean evolutionary biologist, who explores how the chasm between wild survival and modern convenience is wreaking havoc on our health.

The Evolutionary Mismatch: Genetics vs. Modern Life

The core question is simple: If animals like nectar-feeding birds and fruit bats consume massive amounts of sugar, why aren't they diabetic? For a long time, this was a mystery. However, recent research published in journals like Science—involving teams from Harvard, the Max Planck Institute, and Goethe University—has provided a breakthrough. By analyzing the genes of sugar-dependent birds, researchers discovered that these creatures have evolved specific metabolic adaptations over their entire history. Their bodies are "pre-set" to handle high-sugar diets.

Humans, while sharing ancestry with primates, followed a different, more painful evolutionary path. About six million years ago, our ancestors left the forests for the grasslands and eventually turned to agriculture. While farming allowed us to secure a stable supply of grains and sugars, our genes did not evolve quickly enough to keep pace with this dietary shift. We are, in effect, trying to survive in a world overflowing with processed sugars and fruits while our bodies remain programmed for a lifestyle of scarcity. We lack the metabolic "insurance" that wild animals enjoy, leaving us vulnerable to the modern metabolic plague.

Movement as a Survival Imperative

Why do primates in the jungles of India, like the Javan gibbon, eat fruit all day and remain healthy? The answer isn't just genetics; it is their constant activity. While the modern human habit involves finishing dinner, sitting on the sofa to scroll through a phone, and then going straight to bed, wild animals are in constant motion.

Consider the hummingbird. Its ability to hover mid-air before a flower is not a leisurely act; it is an extreme feat of physical exertion. They are burning energy at a rate that would be impossible to sustain if they weren't metabolically optimized for it. As Choi Jae-chun notes, "For a bird, every moment spent in the air is an intense workout. They don't have time to worry about diabetes." For these creatures, the choice is binary: move or fall. Their physical health is a byproduct of a lifestyle where stillness leads to immediate death.

In contrast, human society has evolved toward the removal of movement. We have engineered our lives so that we can open doors with a button, have food delivered to our doorstep, and work for hours without ever leaving our chairs. We have traded our ancestral need for motion for the comfort of the couch. However, biology does not recognize "comfort" as a virtue. To our ancient, biologically wired systems, this lack of movement is a signal of stagnation that invites disease.

Breaking the Myth: Should We "Save" Our Joints?

A common excuse for avoiding exercise as we age is the belief that joints are like machine parts: if you use them, they wear out. Choi Jae-chun, who has walked an average of 10,000 steps a day for over two decades—a round trip of about 7 kilometers (roughly 4.3 miles) between his home and Ewha Womans University—is a living testament to the fallacy of this idea. He has found that his joints are more resilient for the movement, not less.

Even when considering higher-impact activities like running, the fear of "wearing out" the knees persists. However, medical professionals often clarify that, when done correctly, running is not a destructive force but a restorative one. The human body is designed to be used. When we move, we strengthen our muscles and joints. When we remain sedentary, we facilitate the very decline we are trying to avoid.

This is particularly relevant in South Korea, where the metabolic health crisis is deepening. With nearly 6 million people in the country now diagnosed with diabetes—and over 20 million if you include the pre-diabetic population—the rapid increase in metabolic diseases among younger demographics is a glaring warning. It is not merely an aging issue; it is a lifestyle collapse.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward the future of health policy and individual well-being, the message from evolutionary biology is clear: we cannot medicate our way out of a lifestyle problem. While modern treatments like GLP-1 receptor agonists are medical marvels, they are not a substitute for the fundamental need to move and eat according to our evolutionary heritage.

  • Policy Trends: Keep an eye on how the government, led by the Democratic Party under President Lee Jae-myung, addresses the rising healthcare costs associated with metabolic diseases. Expect more emphasis on preventive public health initiatives rather than just reactive medical care.
  • Lifestyle Shifts: Watch for the "reverse-eating" trend (eating vegetables before carbohydrates) and the integration of traditional Korean diets—rich in fermented vegetables and grains—as a functional defense against metabolic syndrome.
  • The "Sedentary" Reckoning: As more research confirms the toxicity of excessive sedentary behavior, look for shifts in corporate and urban design. The next "wellness" movement will not be about expensive supplements, but about environmental changes that force us to stand up and walk.

Ultimately, the lesson is one of humility. We are biological organisms that have been shaped by millions of years of movement. Trying to live in a state of permanent "comfort" is an evolutionary contradiction. The next time you find yourself stuck at your desk or glued to your sofa, remember the hummingbird. You don't need a gym membership to start your recovery; you just need to stand up and take the first step. Your body was built for the journey, not the chair.
