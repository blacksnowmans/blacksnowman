The Dangerous Illusions of South Korea’s Ruling Bloc: Why Purity Tests and Economic Blind Spots Threaten the Progressive Government

November 09, 2026

Walk down the commercial avenues of Seoul, and you will see vibrant banners hung by the Democratic Party of Korea—South Korea's ruling progressive party—bearing an ambitious slogan: "The Golden Age of Democracy Has Arrived." Just a year after a harrowing constitutional crisis ended with the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the nation’s progressive movement holds both the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung and a commanding majority in the National Assembly. To an outside observer, South Korea’s progressives appear to be sitting at the apex of political dominance, bolstered by headlines of record-breaking export figures driven by global demand for artificial intelligence chips.

Yet beneath this triumphant facade, a far more fragile reality is taking hold across South Korea’s storefronts, factory floors, and legislative halls. Rather than capitalizing on their mandate to enact structural economic relief, key factions within the ruling camp have turned inward, spending precious political capital on ideological purity tests and blind loyalty competitions. While party insiders celebrate political victories and herald macroeconomic indicators, ordinary citizens face a worsening cost-of-living crisis, historic wage inequality, and soaring debt. By branding internal critics as traitors and ignoring everyday hardship, South Korea's governing coalition is rapidly eroding the broad alliance that brought it to power.

Loyalty Tests and Toxic Acronyms: How Internal Purges Are Fracturing the Coalition

The most alarming symptom of the ruling bloc's current malaise is the rise of aggressive loyalty politics within the progressive camp. Online movement loyalists and party hardliners have increasingly adopted partisan "split tactics" (gallachigi)—divisive factional political maneuvers aimed at identifying and excommunicating internal dissenters. Anyone who dares to raise policy concerns or suggest that the administration isn’t infallible risks being stamped with a scarlet letter: labeled as "Anti-Lee" (opposed to President Lee Jae-myung) and targeted for political purges.

This toxic culture is illustrated by the rapid evolution of online hit-lists targeting prominent figures across the progressive ecosystem. Party activists initially coined the derogatory acronym Moon-Cho-Teol-Rae-Yoo to lump together former President Moon Jae-in, Cho Kuk (leader of the aligned progressive Rebuilding Korea Party), media personality Kim Ou-joon, senior Democratic Party lawmaker Jung Chung-rae, and political commentator Rhyu Si-min. These figures were collectively accused of plotting against President Lee, despite the fact that none of them had actively opposed the president during or after the election campaign.

The absurdity of these purity tests became fully apparent when President Lee held a friendly high-profile meeting with former President Moon. Overnight, hardliners dropped "Moon" from the blacklist, replacing him with former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae to forge a new target acronym: Cho-Chuk-Teol-Rae-Yoo. Yesterday’s alleged traitor was suddenly rehabilitated simply for sharing a photo with the president, while today’s mild critic was cast into political exile.

This relentless narrowing of the political tent is proving destructive. According to recent public opinion metrics, an alarming 28.4% of citizens who previously supported the president have now withdrawn their support. When a ruling coalition treats its own internal "Red Team"—those offering constructive criticism to prevent executive overreach—as ungrateful enemies, it alienates the very swing voters and moderate allies necessary to maintain a governing majority.

The Semiconductor Illusion: Record Exports Masking Small-Business Collapse

While ruling party leader Kim Min-seok and party officials point to high-level economic statistics to claim that a democratic golden age has arrived, these figures mask severe structural distress in the domestic economy. South Korea recently celebrated a record-setting $120 billion in annual semiconductor exports. However, this headline growth creates a dangerous optical illusion. The boom is overwhelmingly concentrated in just two tech titans: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

The semiconductor sector is notoriously capital-intensive but has low employment elasticity, meaning vast export revenue generates relatively few new jobs and fails to trickle down into the wider domestic economy. Removing Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix from the equation reveals a bleak picture of corporate South Korea:

  • In the second quarter, the operating profit margin for all other listed companies fell by 6.2 percentage points to 16.9%.
  • One out of every four listed companies in South Korea (25%) operated at a financial loss.
  • The profit margin gap between large conglomerates and small-and-medium enterprises (SMEs) widened to 13.8 percentage points—the widest discrepancy since data collection began in 2015.

For South Korea's self-employed business owners—the backbone of the domestic service economy in dining, lodging, and retail—the crisis has reached levels reminiscent of the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis. Prolonged sluggish domestic consumption has pushed loan delinquency rates among self-employed workers to a 13-year high. Small-business owners across the country are shuttering their shops and slipping into credit insolvency, making the ruling party's celebratory banners feel wildly out of touch with daily life.

Structural Inequality by the Numbers: Wage Gaps and Youth Precarity

The disconnect between political rhetoric and lived reality is equally stark in the labor market. South Korea's socioeconomic inequality has hardened into historic structural divides, backed by sobering official statistics:

The average monthly wage for regular full-time workers stands at approximately 4.05 million won (about $2,900), whereas non-regular and temporary workers earn just 2.11 million won (about $1,500). This monthly wage gap of 1.94 million won (approx. $1,400) is the largest ever recorded in South Korean statistical history, while non-regular workers now comprise a record high of 38.5% of the total workforce.

This precarity falls heaviest on younger generations. During the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, 17.4% of young workers entering the labor market (those born between 1969 and 1978) started their careers in non-regular, temporary positions. Today, despite South Korea’s status as an advanced economy, 33.5% of young workers born between 1989 and 1998 enter the workforce in unstable non-regular jobs—nearly double the rate of the IMF crisis generation. This economic insecurity explains the deep political cynicism among young voters toward hyper-partisan political feuds.

Labor organizations, including public-sector non-regular workers affiliated with the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU), recently gathered at the National Assembly to demand structural reform. Workers across essential services—from postal contractors and tollgate operators to municipal counselors on suicide prevention hotlines and power plant staff facing upcoming plant closures—reported that minimum wages have effectively become maximum wages. Despite the establishment of public service committees, government initiatives remain hamstrung by temporary five-year sunset clauses, leaving hundreds of thousands of public-sector workers trapped in perpetual employment insecurity.

Political Missteps: From Agricultural Audits to the 'Indictment Cancellation' Fiasco

Instead of addressing these deep socioeconomic divides, the ruling party has repeatedly stumbled by prioritizing political signaling over pragmatic governance. A prime example occurred in the progressive heartland of the Honam region, where the government executed a rigid, top-down audit of agricultural land ownership. The heavy-handed audit caused farmland asset values to plunge while leaving farmers saddled with pre-existing bank loans, sparking widespread agricultural distress. Yet, ruling party lawmakers remained silent on the issue until external progressive critics publicly demanded policy corrections.

An even more egregious misstep was the formation of the "Presidential Indictment Cancellation Group" (Gong-chi-mo), a caucus of 100 Democratic Party National Assembly members. The group pooled their collective influence to demand the immediate legal cancellation of all criminal indictments filed against President Lee Jae-myung during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency.

To a public suffering from inflation and debt, the sight of 100 ruling-party lawmakers prioritizing the president’s personal legal battles looked like a complete abandonment of public livelihood concerns. Party hardliners initially attacked internal critics of the group as "Anti-Lee" traitors. However, the political fallout grew so severe that President Lee Jae-myung himself was forced to step in at a press conference, ordering lawmakers to remove the indictment cancellation clause from pending special prosecutor legislation. The episode wasted valuable legislative momentum on an aggressive show of partisan loyalty that ultimately backfired.

What to Watch Next: The One-Year Golden Window Before the 2028 Elections

South Korea’s progressive camp faces a critical juncture. The historical grace period granted by voters for overcoming the previous crisis and seeing Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol imprisoned has expired. Citizens are no longer evaluating the administration on past political battles, but on current economic conditions.

Analysts estimate that the ruling bloc has a "golden window" of roughly one year—until November 2025—to shift focus from political infighting to tangible structural reforms. If the administration fails to deliver visible results in reducing labor market inequality, alleviating small-business debt, and creating stable youth employment, it risks facing severe voter backlash in the April 2028 National Assembly elections.

In the coming months, international observers should monitor three key indicators:

  • Party Culture and Coalition Unity: Whether Democratic Party leadership moves away from online factional purges and re-establishes a collaborative, big-tent coalition with partner groups like Cho Kuk's Rebuilding Korea Party.
  • Targeted Small-Business and Labor Relief: Whether the National Assembly transitions from partisan legislative battles to passing structural labor reforms, such as making public worker committees permanent and addressing minimum wage caps for non-regular workers.
  • Economic Policy Pivot: Whether the government relies less on headline semiconductor export statistics and introduces targeted intervention to address the 13.8% profit margin gap dividing conglomerates from domestic small businesses.

Political dominance in a democracy is rarely preserved through enforced ideological uniformity. If South Korea's governing progressives wish to secure a lasting legislative majority in 2028, they must stop demanding absolute loyalty within their ranks and start responding to the economic pain outside their windows.
