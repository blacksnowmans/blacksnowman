The Childcare Crisis: Between Expansion and Safety in South Korea

November 16, 2026

Imagine a mother in Gangneung, a coastal city in South Korea, opening a mobile app to check the CCTV feed of her one-year-old child’s daycare center. She expects to see nap time or a quiet meal. Instead, the footage reveals a nightmare. Over the course of several weeks, teachers are seen stuffing the child’s mouth with tissues used to blow noses, striking the toddler with heavy metal tumblers, and intentionally withholding food. Most chilling of all is the sight of other staff members standing by, laughing and watching the abuse unfold as if it were entertainment.

This recent scandal, involving over 320 documented instances of abuse against a single infant, has sent shockwaves through a country already grappling with a demographic collapse. For the Lee Jae-myung administration and the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party), the incident is a grim reminder that building more daycare centers is only half the battle. As South Korea pours resources into "publicizing" childcare to encourage young couples to have children, the gap between the quantity of facilities and the quality of supervision has become a chasm that many families are falling through.

The Failure of Internal Oversight and the Push for Transparency

The Gangneung case is not merely a story of "bad apples." It highlights a systemic failure of the internal "self-purification" mechanisms within Korean childcare centers. In many private facilities, the hierarchy is rigid, and the pressure to maintain a clean public image often leads to a culture of silence. When multiple teachers are caught on camera laughing while an infant is mistreated, it suggests that the professional ethics of the environment have completely eroded. The fact that 320 incidents occurred before the abuse was caught demonstrates that current oversight—often limited to occasional, scheduled inspections—is insufficient.

In response, Park Seong-min, a lawmaker from the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative opposition party), has moved to introduce legislation aimed at breaking this wall of silence. The proposed bill would mandate regular evaluations of childcare services and, crucially, require the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family to make these results public. Currently, while evaluations exist, the data is often opaque or difficult for parents to navigate. By forcing transparency, the bill aims to give parents the "right to know" before they entrust their children to a specific provider.

This legislative push reflects a shift in the Korean public’s expectations. In the past, childcare was seen as a private family matter or a localized service. Now, it is increasingly viewed as a critical piece of national infrastructure that requires the same level of rigorous, data-driven oversight as the financial or medical sectors. If passed, the bill would create a quantitative ranking system, effectively using market pressure and public shame to force low-performing centers to improve or shut down.

The Rush Toward Public Daycare Expansion

While safety dominates the headlines, local governments are simultaneously racing to meet the high demand for "public" childcare. In South Korea, daycare centers are generally divided into Guk-gong-rip (state-run or public) and Min-gan (private). Public centers are the gold standard for parents; they typically offer better-trained staff, lower turnover, and more standardized educational programs. However, they are also notoriously difficult to get into, with waiting lists that can stretch for years.

To bypass the massive costs and time required to build new facilities from scratch, many municipalities are adopting a "long-term lease" model. Daejeon Seo-gu, a major administrative district in central South Korea, recently announced it would convert three private daycare centers into public ones using this method. This brings the total number of public centers in the district to 50. By leasing existing private buildings and remodeling them to meet state standards, the government can expand the public safety net much faster than through traditional construction.

Similarly, Asan in South Chungcheong Province and Okcheon in North Chungcheong Province have recently renewed their contracts with existing public providers. These moves are designed to ensure "continuity of care." For a toddler, a change in the managing entity of their daycare can mean new teachers and a disruptive change in routine. By securing five-year extensions for trusted operators, these local governments are prioritizing emotional stability for the children—a subtle but vital component of quality childcare that often gets lost in high-level policy debates.

The "Care Desert": Regional Gaps and the Night-Shift Struggle

Despite the expansion in major cities, a geographical "care desert" is emerging in South Korea’s rural and island regions. This is most evident in the disparity of late-night and emergency care. For double-income families where parents work irregular hours or night shifts, standard daycare hours (usually ending by 7:00 PM) are insufficient. To address this, the government supports "extended-night" daycare centers that stay open later.

However, the distribution of these centers is wildly uneven. In Incheon, a massive port city near Seoul, there are 338 daycare centers offering extended hours. Yet, a closer look at the data reveals a disturbing trend: in the rural island districts of Ganghwa-gun and Ongjin-gun, the number of such facilities is exactly zero. This means that a parent working a late shift on an island has no formal public safety net, while a parent in the city center has dozens of options.

"We cannot leave childcare blind spots to the whims of the market," noted one local advocate. "In areas with declining populations, private centers won't offer late-night care because it isn't profitable. This is where the government must step in with direct budget injections to ensure that a child’s safety and a parent’s career aren't determined by their zip code."

On the other side of the spectrum, Yongin—a rapidly growing tech hub—is attempting to bridge this gap with the "365-day Hourly Care" model. This initiative, currently under discussion in the city council, would provide emergency childcare even on weekends and holidays. For a country trying to boost its birth rate, this "niche care" is arguably more important than standard daytime slots. It targets the "panic moments" of parenting—when a child is sick, a babysitter cancels, or a work emergency arises—which often drive parents to despair or discourage them from having a second child.

Professionalizing the Workforce: Beyond Supervision

The ultimate quality of childcare rests not on the buildings, but on the people inside them. South Korean childcare workers have long complained of low wages, high stress, and a lack of societal respect. When teachers are overworked and undervalued, the risk of burnout—and by extension, the risk of abuse or neglect—skyrockets. Recognizing this, several municipalities are shifting their focus toward teacher welfare and professional development.

In Sacheon, a city in the south, the local government recently held a ceremony to honor 12 "outstanding childcare professionals." While seemingly a small gesture, such awards are part of a broader strategy to improve the "prestige" of the profession. Meanwhile, Daegu Suseong-gu held a seminar for daycare directors focused on "Sustainable Future Education," moving the conversation from mere supervision to high-level pedagogy. The idea is that if directors view themselves as educators rather than just facility managers, they will foster a better environment for both teachers and children.

In Danyang, a mountainous region known for its natural beauty, the local government is even looking to the environment to improve childcare. They recently signed an agreement with the Korea Childcare Association to use the city’s "Fish Garden" and natural parks as "eco-classrooms." By moving children out of cramped indoor rooms and into nature, the city hopes to reduce the stress levels of both students and staff, creating a "healing" approach to early childhood education that is rare in the high-pressure urban centers of South Korea.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves into the final months of the year, several key developments will determine the trajectory of its childcare system:

  • Legislative Progress: Watch for the debate over Rep. Park Seong-min’s bill in the National Assembly. Its passage would mark a major turning point in how much data the government is willing to share with parents regarding daycare quality.
  • The "Care Desert" Budget: During the upcoming budget cycles, keep an eye on whether the Lee Jae-myung government allocates specific "minimum density" funding for rural areas like Incheon’s islands. Without subsidized hubs, the regional gap will only widen.
  • Implementation of "365 Care": The success or failure of Yongin’s weekend and holiday care pilot will likely serve as a blueprint for the rest of the country. If it proves popular and manageable, expect a nationwide rollout of "24/7" childcare infrastructure.
  • Judicial Outcomes in Gangneung: The legal consequences for the teachers and the director in the Gangneung abuse case will be a litmus test for the "Immediate Removal" policy. Public sentiment is currently demanding that facilities involved in such extensive abuse be permanently shuttered without the chance for rebranding or reopening.

South Korea’s childcare policy is currently a race between expansion and safety. While the government is successfully adding more slots and "publicizing" the system, the horror in Gangneung serves as a stark warning: without radical transparency and a focus on the well-being of the teachers themselves, more daycare centers may simply mean more opportunities for the system to fail the very children it was built to protect.
