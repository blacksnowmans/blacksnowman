The Megacity Gamble: How the Ruling Democratic Party is Redrawing South Korea’s Economic Map

November 12, 2026

The air outside the Gwangju Metropolitan City Hall was thick with a familiar, localized tension. Inside, the top brass of the Democratic Party—South Korea’s ruling party under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—were gathered for a "field leadership meeting," a high-profile exercise in regional outreach. They arrived bearing promises of semiconductor clusters and "mega-city" legislation. But just beyond the doors, the cheering of party supporters was punctuated by the rhythmic chanting of protesters from the city’s southwestern districts. They weren’t interested in abstract tech hubs; they wanted a medical school, and they wanted it now.

This scene, which played out on November 11, captures the delicate balancing act currently facing the Lee Jae-myung government. In a country where nearly half the population lives in or around Seoul, the "regional problem" is no longer just about fairness; it is about survival. For the Democratic Party, which consolidated power following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the challenge is twofold: they must satisfy their traditional power base in the Honam region (the southwest) while demonstrating a statesmanlike ability to govern the conservative heartland of Yeongnam (the southeast) through pragmatic cooperation.

The Honam Heartland: Between Industrial Dreams and Medical Reality

To understand why the Democratic Party leadership chose Gwangju for their first major regional push of the month, one must understand Honam. Comprising Gwangju and the Jeonnam and Jeonbuk provinces, this region is the ideological and electoral bedrock of the Democratic Party. However, for decades, residents here have felt that their loyalty has been rewarded with slower industrial development compared to the industrial corridors of the east.

During the meeting, Kim Min-seok, a member of the party’s Supreme Council, laid out a vision for a "Semiconductor Cluster" in Gwangju and Jeonnam. In the Korean context, a semiconductor cluster is the ultimate prize; it signifies high-paying jobs, infrastructure investment, and a guarantee that the region will not be left behind in the Fourth Industrial Revolution. The leadership also pledged to move forward with the relocation of the Gwangju military airport—a project worth billions of dollars that has been stalled for years due to disputes over where the noisy, expansive facility should be moved.

However, the party’s attempt to project a message of "visionary growth" was complicated by a very immediate, human demand. For years, the southwestern part of Jeonnam has been one of the few areas in South Korea without a dedicated medical school, forcing residents to travel hours for specialized care. While the government has proposed building a branch of the Chonnam National University Hospital, locals dismissed this as a "stopgap measure."

"A branch hospital is a trick," protesters shouted outside the meeting hall. "Give the southwest a proper national medical school."

The friction highlights a growing problem for the ruling party: when you hold the presidency and a commanding majority in the National Assembly, "we are trying" is no longer an acceptable answer to your core supporters. Seo Mi-hwa, another member of the party leadership, acknowledged the "deep wounds" felt by residents during the long, bureaucratic process of medical school site selection. The stakes are high; if the Democratic Party cannot deliver on basic infrastructure like healthcare in its own backyard, its promises of "semiconductor glory" may start to ring hollow.

The "Grand Coalition" for the Southeast

While the Democratic Party was busy tending to its base in the west, an even more surprising diplomatic dance was happening with the leaders of the TK region (Daegu and Gyeongbuk)—the traditional fortress of the opposition People Power Party. In a display of "bipartisan pragmatism" that would have been unthinkable during the height of the political turmoil surrounding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment, Lee Cheol-woo, the Governor of Gyeongbuk, and Choo Kyung-ho, the Mayor of Daegu, met with the Democratic Party leadership to ask for help.

Their request centered on two massive projects: the construction of the Daegu-Gyeongbuk New Airport and a historic "Administrative Integration" that would essentially merge the city of Daegu and the surrounding Gyeongbuk province into a single administrative entity. The goal is to create a "second capital" capable of competing with the Seoul metropolitan area for investment and talent.

For the Democratic Party, supporting these projects is a strategic masterstroke. By signaling that they will "positively review" the legal and financial frameworks for the TK Airport, the ruling party is positioning itself as the "party of national performance" rather than just a regional faction. They are effectively telling conservative voters in the southeast: "The opposition might share your ideology, but we are the ones who can actually build your airport."

However, this outreach is not without its internal contradictions. Even as the national leadership talked cooperation, the Democratic Party’s provincial branch in Gyeongbuk was slamming Governor Lee Cheol-woo for his administration’s handling of "Basic Income" pilot programs. The local DP branch accused the Governor of "robbing residents of their benefits" by excluding certain districts from the program. This friction shows that while the top-level "big deal" for airports and integration might move forward, the ideological battle over the welfare state continues unabated at the local level.

Grassroots Economics: The Battle for the "Living Wage"

Away from the billion-dollar airport projects and semiconductor clusters, a quieter but equally significant battle is being waged in the local councils of Daegu. Currently, Daegu is the only major city in South Korea where not a single one of its nine districts has adopted a "Living Wage" ordinance.

A Living Wage (saenghwal imgeum) in South Korea is a policy where local governments mandate a higher hourly rate for municipal employees and contracted workers than the national minimum wage, calculated based on the actual cost of living in that specific area. While common in Seoul and Gyeonggi province, it has faced stiff resistance in the more conservative south.

On November 11, Democratic Party councilors from five different districts in Daegu announced a joint initiative to force these ordinances through their respective councils. This is a classic "bottom-up" political strategy. By focusing on the "zero" status of the living wage in Daegu, the DP is attempting to frame the local People Power Party leadership as indifferent to the struggles of low-income workers. If they succeed, it provides a powerful talking point for the next election cycle: that the Democratic Party is the primary driver of wage growth even in "enemy territory."

Budget Season and the "Small City" Conflicts

As the National Assembly moves into the critical year-end budget review period, these regional demands are coalescing into a massive "shopping list" for the Lee Jae-myung administration. In Daejeon, the city government and the local Democratic Party chapter have formed a "joint front" to ensure that the city’s key infrastructure projects don't see their funding slashed in the final rounds of negotiations.

However, the day’s news also served as a reminder that local politics in Korea remains a contact sport. In Gyeryong, Democratic Party councilors held a fiery press conference demanding that the city’s mayor (from the opposition party) answer for ongoing investigations into his administration. In Geoje and Seongnam, sessions were derailed by accusations of "power trips" and personal scandals between rival party members.

These "small-town" skirmishes are more than just distractions. They represent the friction points where the high-minded rhetoric of "regional balance" meets the reality of local power struggles. For a reader outside Korea, these might seem like minor local disputes, but in a centralized system like South Korea’s, a scandal in a city council can often stall a national-level development project for months.

What to Watch Next

The Democratic Party’s flurry of regional activity on November 11 marks the beginning of a high-stakes legislative season. As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung attempts to solidify its post-impeachment mandate, the success or failure of these regional promises will determine the political map for years to come.

  • The National Assembly Budget Vote: Watch whether the "positive review" of the Daegu-Gyeongbuk New Airport translates into specific line items in the 2025 budget. If the funding is absent, expect the "bipartisan" atmosphere to evaporate instantly.
  • The Medical School Decision: The government must soon decide whether to grant a full national medical school to the southwest or stick with the "branch hospital" plan. This will be the ultimate litmus test for the DP’s relationship with its Honam base.
  • Administrative Integration Legislation: The proposed merger of Daegu and Gyeongbuk requires a special law. If the Democratic Party fast-tracks this, it could signal a new era of "super-regional" governance that bypasses the traditional provincial system.
  • Living Wage Outcomes: Keep an eye on the Daegu district councils. If the Democratic minority can successfully pressure the conservative majority into passing living wage ordinances, it will mark a significant shift in the political discourse of the TK region.

In the end, the Democratic Party is betting that it can solve the "Seoul Republic" problem by building several other "Republics" in the south. It is an expensive, politically fraught, and logistically daunting gamble. But for a ruling party looking to move past the chaos of the previous era, it may be the only way to prove that they can govern the whole of the peninsula, not just the parts that voted for them.
