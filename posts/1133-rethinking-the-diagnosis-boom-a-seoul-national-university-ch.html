Rethinking the Diagnosis Boom: A Seoul National University Child Psychiatrist on Korea’s Developmental Anxiety

October 14, 2026

In South Korea’s hyper-competitive neighborhoods, parental anxiety often begins long before a child ever takes a standardized test. In community message boards and pediatric clinic waiting rooms across Seoul, conversations increasingly revolve around subtle behavioral markers: Does an eighteen-month-old make sustained eye contact? Why is a toddler still lining up miniature cars instead of babbling full sentences? Is a spirited kindergartener simply energetic, or are they showing early signs of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)?

Developmental and neurodivergent conditions, once discussed in whispers or treated as rare tragedies, have become mainstream concerns. According to prominent child and adolescent psychiatrist Dr. Kim Bung-nyun of Seoul National University Hospital—the country’s premier medical institution—the diagnosis rates tell a striking story. Conditions once thought to affect one in two hundred children are now diagnosed in two to three out of every hundred. Looking at international benchmarks like the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the prevalence of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) among eight-year-olds has reached 3.2 percent, or roughly one in thirty-one—a 4.8-fold surge compared to 2000.

Yet as Dr. Kim argued in recent broadcast remarks, simply lamenting that "children have changed" misses the true picture. The explosive increase in diagnoses reflects a complex collision of broadening clinical definitions, heightened public awareness, modern environmental factors, and an unforgiving societal tempo that leaves little room for children who follow their own internal clocks.

Diagnostic Expansion, Environmental Suspects, and the Rush to Label

To understand the current surge in autism diagnoses, one must distinguish between true clinical increase and changing diagnostic yardsticks. As highlighted by developmental psychologists such as Professor Emerita Uta Frith of University College London (UCL), the historical criteria for autism were once restricted to children with profound developmental and cognitive impairments. Over the past decade, that definition has expanded dramatically to include mild traits in individuals with completely intact speech and cognitive learning abilities.

In short, part of the statistical spike is not a sudden epidemic of a new disorder, but the result of casting a much wider diagnostic net. At the same time, Dr. Kim points out that environmental factors warrant serious scrutiny. Endocrine-disrupting chemicals found widely in everyday plastics—specifically bisphenol A (BPA) and phthalates—have been shown to negatively impact neurodevelopment, particularly in the brain regions responsible for social communication.

However, the lack of definitive biological markers creates its own set of complications:

  • Subjective evaluation: Without blood tests or brain scans to confirm ASD, diagnoses rely heavily on clinical observation, leaving wide room for subjective clinician judgment.
  • Social media amplification: A flood of self-diagnosis checklists on digital platforms causes anxious parents to misinterpret standard developmental variations as clinical pathology.
  • Resource misallocation: As the diagnostic boundaries stretch, public and therapeutic resources risk being diverted away from children with severe impairments who require intensive, lifelong support.

Inside the child's mind, the classic behavioral triad of autism—deficits in social bonding, delayed language acquisition, and the presence of compulsive, repetitive actions—often functions as a shield rather than a defiance of norms. Repetitive motions like spinning in place, hopping, or endlessly aligning toys are frequently misread by adults as stubbornness. In reality, they are self-soothing defense mechanisms deployed by a child whose brain is overwhelmed by sensory and communicative overload.

The Power of Waiting: A Case Study in Non-Linear Growth

The standard instinct for anxious parents is to force immediate correction—enrolling toddlers in rapid-fire speech therapy or drilling vocabulary flashcards. Dr. Kim counters that treating neurodivergent children requires abandoning adult timelines and discovering the unique entry points through which a child is willing to connect.

He recalls the case of a 30-month-old toddler who could not utter a single word, leaving the parents in despair. While standard verbal interventions produced no response, clinical observers noticed that the child had an intense, exceptional affinity for music. Rather than drilling words, therapists introduced children's songs into structured play sessions involving both mother and child.

"Respecting a child's inherent developmental timetable and unique characteristics is not just one treatment strategy among many; it is the core foundation of care."

The results unfolded gradually but fundamentally. As musical play accumulated, the child began making voluntary eye contact, actively seeking out physical embraces, and expressing joy in maternal touch. Only after this emotional rapport was cemented did the mother begin weaving gentle speech into their routines—and only then did the child begin verbalizing. The intervention succeeded not by forcing the child into a predetermined mold, but by meeting them where their focus naturally lay.

The Pathologizing of Childhood Energy and Adult Reckonings

The rush to diagnose is perhaps even more pronounced in the realm of ADHD. South Korean society, known for placing children in formal childcare and academic settings at remarkably early ages, frequently mistakes typical child vigor for a psychiatric disorder.

Neurodevelopmentally, self-regulation mechanisms in the human brain typically mature between 60 to 72 months, extending up to 84 months (ages five to seven). This biological timeline is precisely why elementary schooling across the globe traditionally commences around age seven; it is only at this stage that children possess the baseline executive function required for sustained classroom attention. Yet, when lively toddlers placed in institutional daycare act out, parents and educators often jump to conclusions.

Dr. Kim notes with dismay that true early-onset ADHD accounts for less than one percent of cases. Labeling vibrant, active temperaments as a medical disorder creates a vicious cycle: children are treated as difficult, marginalized by peers and teachers, and subsequently develop secondary behavioral problems that make them appear even more symptomatic.

Conversely, the story takes an entirely different turn in adulthood. While roughly 50 percent of childhood ADHD diagnoses resolve as the brain matures, the remaining half persist into adult life. For grown adults struggling with chronic disorganization, missed deadlines, persistent tardiness, emotional volatility, and sudden outbursts of anger, a formal diagnosis often brings profound relief rather than shame.

Many adults who spend decades internalizing labels like "lazy," "unmotivated," or "careless" find that receiving a clinical explanation lifts an enormous psychological burden. It offers a structured framework to seek medication, behavioral coaching, and executive-function training, transforming self-hatred into self-management.

The Adolescent Storm: Pruning Prefrontal Cortexes and Overheated Amygdalas

The developmental struggle does not end in early childhood; it re-emerges with full force during puberty. Parents of teenagers often watch their previously affectionate children turn sullen, rebellious, and confrontational almost overnight, interpreting the shift as personal disrespect. Neurobiology, however, reveals an intense internal crisis.

During adolescence, the brain undergoes two massive structural disruptions simultaneously:

  • Prefrontal cortex pruning: The brain's rational control center begins aggressively shedding excess neural connections to streamline adult cognition. This temporary instability peaks between the sixth grade of elementary school and second year of middle school for girls, and between the first year of middle school and second year of high school for boys.
  • Amygdala hyperactivation: Fueled by hormonal surges, the brain's emotional hub becomes hyper-reactive, generating sudden spikes of fear, anxiety, and aggression.

In essence, an adolescent is operating a high-powered vehicle with a malfunctioning brake system while the accelerator is pinned to the floor. When combined with the natural developmental urge to shift allegiance from parents to peer groups, conflict is inevitable.

Dr. Kim advises parents to view their children through an entirely different lens: not as possessions to be micromanaged, but as "honored guests" who have arrived in their home. A host treats a precious guest with hospitality, respects their personal space, and understands that their stay is temporary. Framing the parental role as preparing an independent soul to eventually depart allows families to replace defensive anger with patience and empathy.

What to Watch Next

As discussions around neurodiversity grow in South Korea, several policy and cultural shifts will determine whether the medical and educational systems adapt effectively:

  • Diagnostic precision and standardization: Watch whether South Korean psychiatric associations and public health authorities establish clearer screening guidelines to differentiate between high-energy temperaments and clinical ADHD, preventing premature diagnoses in early childhood centers.
  • Public school integration and therapy resources: Observe whether the Ministry of Education expands specialized support personnel inside regular classrooms, ensuring that public resources are channeled toward children who genuinely require developmental interventions.
  • Adult mental health access: Monitor the growth of adult ADHD clinics and workplace accommodations, as destigmatization encourages more working professionals to seek clinical evaluation rather than suffering chronic career derailment in silence.
