Why Record Profits Can’t Save Samsung: The Vicious Cycle of the Korea Discount

November 05, 2026

Imagine you are a retail investor in Seoul, staring at your smartphone screen during your lunch break. For weeks, you have read the headlines: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the twin titans of the global semiconductor industry, have just posted robust earnings. Demand for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) is skyrocketing thanks to the AI boom. By all traditional logic, your portfolio should be glowing green. Instead, it is a sea of "blue"—the color used in the Korean markets to denote a price drop. You are watching the value of the nation’s most successful companies evaporate in real-time, seemingly disconnected from the reality of their balance sheets.

This is the "Samsung Paradox" currently gripping the KOSPI (the Korea Composite Stock Price Index, South Korea's benchmark market). Despite solid fundamentals, the stock prices of Korea’s leading tech firms are being pulverized. To an outsider, it looks like a market in collapse; to those on the ground, it is the culmination of a "Korea Discount" that has become a structural cage. While the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's ruling liberal party) is working to restore democratic procedures and mend diplomatic ties with the North, the market is still nursing deep wounds from the previous era. The "prosecutor-style" economic pressure and market interference that characterized the time when Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi was in power left a legacy of institutional distrust that today’s record-breaking chip sales cannot easily overcome.

The Invisible War: Global Hedge Funds and the "Degrossing" Trap

If the earnings are good, why is the stock falling? According to Kang Kwan-woo, CEO of The Premier and a prominent market analyst, the answer lies not in the factories of Suwon, but in the trading desks of New York and London. We are currently witnessing an unprecedented offensive by global hedge funds. These institutional players are "shorting" the Korean market—betting that prices will fall—at a scale rarely seen in the history of the KOSPI.

A primary driver of this is a phenomenon known as "degrossing." When global volatility spikes or political uncertainty looms, hedge funds move to reduce their overall exposure to risky assets. Because South Korea is a highly liquid "emerging market" with large-cap stocks like Samsung, it often serves as the "ATM of Asia." When a fund needs to raise cash or hedge against a downturn in the U.S. tech sector, they sell Samsung. It is not a reflection of Samsung’s quality, but of its convenience as a source of liquidity.

Furthermore, the market is currently trapped in a "negative spiral." Many funds that leveraged heavily to bet on the AI revolution have seen their returns decimated. As their losses mount, they are forced to liquidate their remaining winners to meet margin calls. This creates a mechanical selling pressure that ignores P/E ratios or profit guidance. "The market has reached a tipping point where retail investors are simply saying, 'I can't do this anymore,'" says Kang. The psychological trauma of watching stocks slide despite good news is driving a mass exodus of domestic capital toward the U.S. markets, further hollowing out the KOSPI’s support base.

The Red Tape Prison: Why Samsung Can’t Buy Back Its Own Success

In a healthy market, a company with excess cash and a languishing stock price would simply buy back its own shares and cancel them (stock cancellation), thereby increasing the value of the remaining shares. For Samsung Electronics, this should be a simple fix. However, in South Korea, this path is blocked by a formidable legal wall: the Act on the Structural Improvement of the Financial Industry, commonly known as Geumsan-beop.

This law enforces the "Separation of Finance and Industry," a rule designed to prevent industrial giants from using their financial affiliates as private piggy banks. The complication for Samsung is that its insurance arms—Samsung Life Insurance and Samsung Fire & Marine Insurance—hold significant stakes in Samsung Electronics. Under current law, a financial institution cannot own more than 10% of a non-financial company. If Samsung Electronics were to buy back and cancel a large portion of its shares, the total number of outstanding shares would decrease. This would cause the percentage owned by the insurance affiliates to "accidentally" rise above the 10% threshold, putting the company in immediate violation of the law.

This creates a bizarre stalemate. Samsung earns billions of dollars in profit, yet it cannot use that money to effectively boost its stock price through cancellations without triggering a massive regulatory crisis or being forced into a fire sale of its own shares. While the Lee Jae-myung government has signaled a willingness to discuss easing these regulations to solve the "Korea Discount," the political sensitivity of appearing to give "favors" to chaebols (the family-run conglomerates that dominate the economy) makes any quick fix unlikely. Without the ability to return value to shareholders as aggressively as their American peers like Apple or Nvidia, Korean stocks remain perpetually "cheap" for a reason.

The Myth of the "Peak-out" and the Ghost of Solidigm

Adding to the structural hurdles is a prevailing narrative among global analysts that the semiconductor industry has reached its "peak." This "peak-out" theory suggests that the massive investments in AI infrastructure have already hit their ceiling and that earnings will only decline from here. However, a look at the data suggests this pessimism is drastically overblown. Current valuations for Samsung are pricing in a scenario where earnings would have to be cut in half every year until 2030—a catastrophic outlook that has no basis in current demand cycles.

SK Hynix, the world's second-largest memory maker, faces its own specific "discount" related to its 2020 acquisition of Intel’s NAND flash business, now known as Solidigm. The deal, worth about $9 billion (roughly 12 trillion won), was intended to make Hynix a leader in data center storage. Instead, due to geopolitical shifts and a downturn in the NAND market, Solidigm has become a weight around the company’s neck. Recent rumors about the board of directors considering a total shutdown of the business have rattled investors. While the company’s official stance remains ambiguous, the lack of transparency regarding Solidigm’s future has provided short-sellers with the perfect ammunition to suppress SK Hynix’s valuation, even as its HBM business leads the world.

Furthermore, the U.S. policy of "reshoring"—bringing manufacturing back to American soil—is being used as a rhetorical club against Korean tech. Yet, as Kang Kwan-woo points out, the reality of building advanced fabs in the U.S. is fraught with high costs and a lack of skilled labor. Companies like Samsung and SK Hynix are essentially "buying time," making symbolic investments in the U.S. to satisfy political pressure while keeping their most efficient and profitable production lines in Korea. The "peak-out" is less a reality of physics and more a product of market sentiment and geopolitical posturing.

What to Watch Next: Catalysts for a Reversal

The current state of the Korean market is a classic "valuation gap." Samsung Electronics is trading at a Price-to-Book Ratio (PBR) of less than 1.1, meaning the market values the company at barely more than the cost of its physical assets. For a global leader in the most important technology of the 21st century, this is an absurdity born of structural flaws and temporary supply-demand distortions.

To see a reversal, investors should watch three specific indicators:

  • Regulatory Reform: Will the Lee Jae-myung administration move forward with specific exemptions for Geumsan-beop? If Samsung is given a legal "green light" to cancel treasury shares without penalizing its affiliates, it would be the single largest catalyst for a price surge in a decade.
  • Foreign Exchange and MSCI Weighting: Korea’s weight in the MSCI Emerging Markets index recently spiked, forcing passive global funds to sell Korean stocks to maintain their balance. Once this rebalancing is complete and the won stabilizes against the dollar, the mechanical selling pressure from "long money" should ease.
  • The AI "Second Wave": As U.S. Big Tech companies transition from "spending" on AI to "earning" from it, their Free Cash Flow (FCF) will be the litmus test. If Microsoft, Google, and Meta show that AI is indeed a profit machine in the next 1-2 years, the "peak-out" theory will collapse, forcing hedge funds to cover their massive short positions in Korean chipmakers.

The "Korea Discount" was not built in a day, and it will not disappear overnight. It is a product of 1990s-era regulations, the scars of the Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi era’s economic mismanagement, and a global financial system that treats Seoul as a high-speed ATM. However, for the patient investor, the gap between Samsung’s "blue" screen and its "gold" balance sheet represents one of the most significant—and frustrating—opportunities in the global market today.
