The Mentor Turns Critic: How an Internal Revolt Is Exposing Cracks in Lee Jae-myung’s Administration

October 19, 2026

When an outside opposition attacks an administration, South Korean politics treats it as routine political theater. But when the intellectual architect of a president’s economic philosophy takes to a live broadcast to issue a tearful public apology for helping put that president in power, the ground beneath the ruling bloc shifts violently. On October 19, Professor Choi Bae-geun, an influential heterodox economist who laid the analytical foundation for President Lee Jae-myung’s signature economic agenda, delivered an unprecedented broadside against the very leader he once mentored. Accusing the administration of relying on organized online harassment—what he termed modern "digital Red Guards"—Choi warned that the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is descending into an intolerant, personalized rule that threatens the norms of democratic governance.

To foreign observers, South Korea’s ruling progressives might appear unified after regaining the presidency in the wake of the political upheaval that saw former president Yoon Suk-yeol impeached, ousted from office, and imprisoned. Yet beneath the veneer of legislative dominance lies a bitter struggle over the party’s soul. Professor Choi’s public denunciation is not merely an academic disagreement over interest rates or fiscal policy. It is an indictment of how political power is maintained, how dissent is crushed inside the governing tent, and how the Democratic Party is actively dismantling its historical coalition.

The Rupture: Why an Architect of Lee’s Agenda Is Calling Him a Digital Autocrat

Professor Choi Bae-geun was not just another surrogate during the presidential race; he was the primary intellectual validator of Lee Jae-myung’s policy vision, notably defending controversial concepts like "Basic Finance"—a system aimed at expanding low-interest public credit to vulnerable citizens. When the campaign struggled, Choi stepped in to build economic credibility and rally progressive voters. That history makes his October 19 remarks a political earthquake.

The core of Choi’s accusation is that institutional governance has been supplanted by emotional, direct-to-supporter communication that borders on digital terror. He pointed directly to President Lee’s personal activity on social media platform X (formerly Twitter). In South Korean political slang, the term jwa-pyo jjik-gi—literally "dropping coordinates"—refers to public figures signaling to hardcore internet fandoms where to direct mass harassment campaigns. According to Choi, when the head of state posts unvetted personal grievances or amplifies attacks against internal critics, those statements function as operational marching orders for hyper-partisan loyalists.

Choi described the result as a systematic "chilling effect." Anyone within the progressive camp who raises questions regarding policy execution or reform strategy—including high-profile figures such as former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae or the reform-minded minor faction, the Rebuilding Korea Party—faces immediate, organized character assassination online. Rather than operating as the head of an institutional cabinet, Choi argued, the presidency has degraded into a megaphone for online fandoms, treating healthy internal critique as treasonous heresy.

The Leaked Rumor: The Bank of Korea Episode and the Anatomy of an Internal Smear

The breaking point between the president and his former economic tutor centered on an alleged whispering campaign involving the leadership of the Bank of Korea (BOK), the country’s central bank. Political circles were recently rocked by an anonymous leak claiming that Choi had arrogantly lobbied for the governor’s seat at the central bank, with subsequent commentary from the presidential orbit dismissing him as an unqualified radical hungry for titles.

Choi used his broadcast to expose how the narrative was manufactured. According to his account, it was the presidential office itself that approached him first. The senior presidential secretary for personnel asked Choi to recommend candidates for the BOK governorship ahead of the post’s upcoming vacancy. Choi later spoke directly with the president. Worried that the administration’s core pledge to overhaul credit access for ordinary citizens was being abandoned, Choi advised the president to appoint a cohesive team dedicated to public financial reform—adding that if nobody else was willing to take on the political fight, he would step forward himself.

That private advisory conversation was subsequently distorted, stripped of context, and leaked to friendly commentators as a smear: that Choi had tried to strong-arm the administration into handing him the central bank chairmanship. For an interaction known only to Choi and top presidential aides to be weaponized in this manner suggests deliberate public-relations engineering by the presidential office. Choi responded with bitter self-reflection, publicly apologizing to South Korean citizens for misjudging Lee's character during the campaign and helping to elect an administration that now casts its own allies aside.

Reshaping the Democratic Party: Purging the Old Guard for a Loyal Base

To understand the ferocity of this conflict, one must examine the deep factional fault lines within South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party. Historically, the party’s modern identity was built on two successive pillars: the loyalists of the late reformist president Roh Moo-hyun (the "pro-Roh" faction) and those of his successor Moon Jae-in (the "pro-Moon" faction). For decades, these interconnected networks formed the emotional and organizational backbone of mainstream South Korean progressivism.

Under Lee Jae-myung, however, the party has undertaken an aggressive campaign to replace this legacy. Loyalists refer to this shift as inaugurating the "New Lee Jae-myung" era, explicitly claiming they owe no political debt to the Roh or Moon traditions. Using supportive digital media creators and sympathetic political YouTubers, hardliners have sought to marginalize historic icons of the movement—including author and former cabinet minister Rhyu Si-min and former justice minister Cho Kuk—framing veteran party figures as exhausted elites who must be displaced by unquestioning loyalists.

The plan, however, has run into stubborn resistance. When internal party mechanisms—including strategic party convention reforms designed to cement permanent factional majorities—threatened to drive disaffected moderates and traditional progressives out of the party, senior figures intervened. High-ranking party official Jung Chung-rae and author Rhyu Si-min publicly urged the rank-and-file not to defect en masse, insisting that leaving would surrender decades of institutional history to factional capture. With rank-and-file members staying put to fight for control, the presidential circle’s impatience has spilled over into public venting, fueling visible friction between the executive branch and traditional progressive intellectuals.

Bread vs. Power: Budget Austerity for Firefighters, Millions for Enforcement

The philosophical rupture is reflected directly in government balance sheets. Throughout his career, President Lee ran on the populist slogan eok-gang-bu-yak—a classic Korean political idiom meaning "restraining the strong and elevating the weak." Yet critics inside and outside the party point to the latest national budget as proof that priorities have shifted from public welfare toward institutional enforcement.

In the upcoming budget, the administration slashed the National Fire Safety Grant by roughly 27 percent—a cut of approximately 289.7 billion won (about $215 million), lowering total funding to 760.5 billion won (roughly $565 million). Field disaster operations budgets for the National 119 Rescue Headquarters were reduced by 15.8 billion won (around $11.7 million), alongside a 17.6 billion won cut to emergency medical response support. The official explanation offered by the government—that safety grant revenues fell due to declining cigarette consumption—was widely criticized as an evasive excuse that ignored public safety commitments made after recent line-of-duty deaths of first responders.

In stark contrast, executive priority projects received instant backing. The government swiftly drew 6.83 billion won (about $5.1 million) from discretionary reserve funds for the newly formed Major Crimes Investigation Office, an enforcement agency established to shift investigative authority away from the traditional state prosecutor corps. For progressive critics like Choi, slashing life-saving equipment budgets for frontline workers while effortlessly funding new state investigative organs lays bare an uncomfortable reality: an administration consolidating punitive power while practicing austerity on basic public protections.

What to Watch Next

The escalation of public attacks against long-standing allies signals that South Korea’s ruling camp has entered a volatile period of internal consolidation. As the political calendar moves forward, observers should watch three concrete developments:

  • Cabinet and Judicial Staffing: The appointment of a former National Tax Service chief as the president's Chief of Staff raised immediate red flags among civil libertarians, who fear financial audits could be weaponized against internal party dissidents. Watch whether future personnel appointments lean further toward compliance and surveillance backgrounds rather than seasoned political conciliators.
  • Party Integration Pushback: Polling indicates sharp resistance within the Democratic base—over 41 percent of supporters in recent surveys oppose absorbing smaller progressive allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party under heavy-handed executive terms. Watch whether legislative backbenchers begin quietly rebelling against executive branch directives during committee votes.
  • The Treatment of Alternative Leaders: As the presidential circle targets potential future standard-bearers like Choo Mi-ae over administrative statements, track whether these attacks backfire by galvanizing the party's alienated traditional base around an alternative leadership slate ahead of upcoming local elections.
