How a President Abroad and a Struggling Party Are Fueling South Korea's Political Malaise

October 03, 2026

Holidays in South Korea are supposed to offer a momentary pause from the relentless pace of political life, a chance for citizens to step away from the noise and catch their breath. Yet the recent holiday break brought no such relief. Instead, citizens scrolling through their feeds were greeted by a surreal parade of presidential outbursts from overseas, plunging approval ratings, and bitter infighting within the ruling party. For an administration that came to power promising reform and institutional modernization, the current moment feels less like a government at work and more like a political apparatus drifting entirely off course.

A Diplomatic Disaster in Mexico: Petty Feuds and Bizarre History

Foreign trips are typically choreographed to project statesmanship and national pride. But President Lee Jae-myung managed to turn a routine gathering with expatriates in Mexico City into an unfiltered airing of domestic grievances. Speaking to an audience of Korean citizens abroad, Lee abruptly pivoted from addressing consular paperwork to launching a bitter, personal attack against Yu Si-min, a prominent liberal commentator and former politician. Yu had recently criticized the administration's prosecution reform plans on a political YouTube broadcast, accusing the government of misleading the public. The slight clearly stung; the president used an international stage to publicly settle scores, dragging up decades-old debates over the National Security Act—legislation restricting pro-North Korean activities—to target Yu's past political activism.

The spectacle did not stop at personal vendettas. Attempting to defend his administration's legislative battles, Lee offered a bizarre historical aside, warning that moving too fast and too far brings backlash, much like the French Revolution, which he casually dismissed as a failure. To lecture expatriates half a world away by misrepresenting a foundational moment of European democracy left political observers bewildered. The blunder highlighted a growing pattern within the administration: when cornered by critics, the nation's chief executive displays an alarming lack of diplomatic discipline, treating the global stage as an extension of domestic political combat.

This emotional volatility has effectively hollowed out whatever credibility remained in the president's earlier attempts at public contrition. Public apologies and televised town halls ring hollow when the country's leader uses his first trip abroad afterward to nurse grudges and lecture his critics. Rather than projecting strength, these outbursts convey a deep-seated insecurity, leaving the administration increasingly isolated behind a wall of its own making.

The Ruling Party's Plunging Numbers and the Rise of Partisan Echo Chambers

Back home, the political machinery of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the country's primary liberal party, is showing severe structural strains. Recent polling data paints a grim picture for Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok, whose disapproval rating has climbed past 51%, while his overall approval sits at an anemic 29%. For a party holding the reins of power, these numbers are a flashing red light, signaling a mass defection of the moderate and rational voters who tipped past elections.

More alarming for the party leadership is the erosion of its core base. Support for Kim among self-identified Democratic Party loyalists dropped sharply from 65% to 55% in a matter of weeks. Rather than engaging in introspection or addressing the policy failures driving voters away, party insiders have dismissed the drop as the work of outside subversives. This defensive bunker mentality has created an environment where internal dissent is treated as treason, leaving the party increasingly dominated by radicalized, unthinking factions.

As pragmatic voices are sidelined, the party risks cementing its transformation into a insular echo chamber. Critics within political commentary circles have taken to describing this factional capture derisively, noting how blind obedience to leadership has replaced strategic thinking. For a party ostensibly dedicated to progressive reform, the obsession with internal loyalty and positioning for future party primaries has entirely overshadowed the actual business of governance.

An Accidental Opposition: When the Main Conservative Party Disciplines the President

Perhaps the most bizarre twist in recent weeks involved a social media misstep by the president himself. Lee's official account had shared posts critical of the prior liberal administration—content heavily laced with far-right rhetoric typically associated with radical online communities like Ilbe. The posts sat unnoticed by administration defenders until Jang Dong-hyuk, leader of the main conservative opposition People Power Party, publicly called out the account and demanded answers.

Only after the conservative opposition made the issue a major public scandal did the presidential office scramble to scrub the posts and issue quiet retractions. The irony was not lost on the public. When liberal supporters and progressive commentators begged the administration to stop amplifying toxic online fringes, the president ignored them entirely. Yet the moment a conservative opposition leader pointed a finger, the administration scrambled to comply. Social media quickly filled with dark humor about how the only effective brake on the president's erratic behavior is the leader of the opposition party.

This dynamic underscores a profound institutional failure: the ruling camp has completely lost its internal capacity for self-correction. With the presidential office insulated by loyalists and unwilling to listen to constructive criticism from within its own coalition, it has outsourced the function of accountability to its bitterest political rivals.

The Battle Over Prosecution Reform and the Politics of Retribution

As the administration prepares to launch the newly restructured prosecution service—a flagship reform meant to strip investigative powers from prosecutors and divide institutional authority—the process has degenerated into a scramble for bureaucratic spoils. What began as a grand vision for democratic accountability has turned into an internal turf war over who will control the newly created investigative apparatus.

For the administration, these powerful legal institutions increasingly function less as tools of public service and more as instruments to settle old scores and shield political allies from legal exposure. The constant reshuffling of personnel and the fixation on punitive measures against perceived enemies have exhausted the public's patience. When reform is weaponized into a mechanism for political survival, the foundational promises of the democratic transition begin to erode.

What to Watch Next

  • Prosecution Overhaul Rollout: Watch how the newly minted investigative agencies are staffed in the coming weeks; the choice of personnel will signal whether the administration intends genuine institutional balance or total political control.
  • Polling Trajectories: Monitor upcoming weekly polls to see if the Democratic Party's base stabilization efforts halt the slide or if the 29% approval mark hardens into a permanent ceiling.
  • Intra-Party Dissent: Pay attention to whether sidelined reformist factions within the liberal camp begin organizing formal challenges against the current party leadership ahead of upcoming local political alignments.
