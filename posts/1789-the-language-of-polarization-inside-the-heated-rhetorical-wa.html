The Language of Polarization: Inside the Heated Rhetorical War of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

November 01, 2026

To walk through Yeouido, the Seoul district that houses the National Assembly, or to scan the headlines coming out of the presidential office in Yongsan these days is to witness a political landscape where words are no longer tools of persuasion, but weapons of total war. On October 31, the atmosphere in South Korean politics reached a fever pitch, characterized not by policy debates or legislative compromise, but by a series of sharp, visceral exchanges that reveal the deep-seated animosity between the ruling and opposition camps.

At the center of this storm is President Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DP), which currently holds power. Facing a relentless barrage of criticism from the opposition regarding his frequent overseas diplomatic tours, the President chose to abandon the traditional "dignity of the office" in favor of a raw, confrontational social media presence. The resulting fallout has dominated the national conversation, dragging issues of diplomacy, personal conduct, and judicial independence into a mud-slinging match that leaves little room for the concerns of the average citizen struggling with a cooling economy.

The "Pigs and Buddhas" Rhetoric: Diplomacy Under Fire

The primary flashpoint of the day was the President’s defense of his international itinerary. The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has consistently framed President Lee’s foreign trips as "extravagant shopping tours" and mere "photo opportunities." In response, President Lee took to social media to vent his frustration, describing his schedule as "nosebleed-inducing" hard work. However, it was one specific phrase that set the political world on fire: "Pigs only see other pigs."

To a foreign observer, this might sound like a playground insult. To a Korean reader, it is a pointed reference to a famous exchange between the founder of the Joseon Dynasty and his monk advisor, where the monk noted that to the eyes of a pig, everyone looks like a pig, but to the eyes of a Buddha, everyone looks like a Buddha. By using this phrase, President Lee was effectively telling his critics that their accusations of "shopping and sightseeing" were merely a reflection of their own shallow intentions and inability to grasp the complexities of national interest.

While the President’s supporters cheered this "candid" communication, the backlash was swift. The opposition PPP argued that the President was essentially calling the citizens who criticize him "pigs." Within the ruling Democratic Party, there are quiet whispers of concern that the President’s "raw" messaging is backfiring. When the head of state engages in such visceral wordplay, the substance of the diplomatic achievements—whether they involve supply chain security or energy cooperation—gets buried under the headlines of the insult. This rhetorical escalation comes at a precarious time, as the President’s approval ratings have recently dipped back into the 30% range, a psychological "danger zone" in Korean politics that often signals a loss of momentum for the administration.

"Emperor Golf" and the Politics of Personal Conduct

As the President defended his professional conduct, the opposition shifted its focus to his personal life and family. Jang Dong-hyeok, a high-ranking representative of the PPP, launched a scathing attack on the President’s family, centering on allegations of luxury leisure activities. Jang characterized the family’s behavior as "Nuisance Golf" (min-pye golf), a step above the commonly used term "Emperor Golf," which refers to elites who use their power to clear out courses or receive special treatment.

"It’s not just 'Emperor Golf' anymore; they’ve become a 'Nuisance Family,'" Jang remarked, targeting the President’s claims of exhaustion from his overseas trips. "He talks about getting IV drips and having nosebleeds after going to Japan to play drums with the Prime Minister. If he’s that tired, he should just stop."

In South Korea, golf has long been a politically sensitive "litmus test" for elitism. While the sport has become more popularized, the image of a political leader or their family enjoying a round of golf while the public struggles with inflation is a potent weapon for the opposition. By framing the President’s family as a "nuisance" to the public, the PPP is attempting to tap into the "relative deprivation" felt by voters. This strategy is partly a response to the recent parliamentary audits, which many analysts felt ended "without a bang" for the opposition. Lacking a legislative "smoking gun" to cripple the administration, the PPP has turned to character assassination and the scrutiny of private schedules to maintain pressure on the Blue House.

The Visa Scandal: A Proxy War Over the U.S. Alliance

Diplomacy and domestic politics collided in a bizarre incident involving Representative Jin Sung-joon, a prominent member of the ruling Democratic Party. Reports surfaced that Representative Jin’s visa to enter the United States had been delayed or initially denied, with the travel document only being issued three days before his scheduled departure. In the highly charged atmosphere of Seoul, a routine administrative delay for a visa was immediately transformed into a referendum on the health of the South Korea-U.S. alliance.

The opposition PPP seized on the delay as proof of a "diplomatic catastrophe," suggesting that the U.S. government’s hesitation to grant a visa to a high-ranking member of the ruling party indicated a lack of trust in the Lee Jae-myung administration. They went as far as to question whether past legal records or political stances were the cause, framing the incident as a national embarrassment.

The Democratic Party hit back with equal fervor, labeling the PPP’s reaction as "Toadyism" (sadae-juui)—a historically loaded term in Korea referring to a submissive attitude toward great powers. The DP argued that for a domestic political party to cheer for the diplomatic humiliation of their own country’s representative just to score points is a "self-harming act" that undermines national interest. They maintained that visa delays are a common administrative occurrence and that the opposition was acting like "vassals" obsessed with the whims of Washington. This exchange highlights a recurring theme in Korean politics: the U.S. relationship is so central to national identity that even a passport stamp can become a battlefield for ideological purity.

Judicial Independence and the "Animal Farm" Conflict

The final front of the day’s conflict moved to the halls of justice. The Democratic Party has been pushing for a formal "state investigation" into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, a move the opposition views as a direct attempt to intimidate the judiciary and protect the President from ongoing legal challenges. The PPP has characterized this as "Judicial Destruction," accusing the DP of trying to conduct a "people’s trial" to remove political obstacles.

The tension within the legal community became public when a sitting judge posted on an internal court network, referencing George Orwell’s Animal Farm to criticize the political pressure being placed on the judiciary. The PPP praised the judge for his "courageous" stance against the ruling party’s overreach, while the DP dismissed the judge as a "bodyguard" for the Chief Justice.

Simultaneously, a dispute erupted over the storage of ballots from recent elections. The opposition raised concerns about ballots being stored at facilities in Olympic Park (a major public park in Seoul), hinting at potential irregularities or "election fraud." The Democratic Party reacted with fury, accusing the PPP of clinging to "conspiracy theories" to keep their base mobilized and protect their party leader’s position. The fact that the physical storage location of ballot boxes has become a point of contention suggests a worrying breakdown in the basic trust required for a functioning democracy.

What to Watch Next

As the rhetoric cools from the October 31 peak, the underlying issues remain unresolved. The "war of words" is likely to transition into a "war of the checkbook" as the National Assembly enters its budget deliberation season. Here is what to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • Approval Rating Floors: If President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings stay in the 30% range or dip further, expect him to either double down on his "raw" social media communication to solidify his base or pivot toward a major populist economic policy to regain the middle ground.
  • The Chief Justice Investigation: Whether the DP actually follows through with a state investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae will be a major indicator of how far they are willing to go to reshape the judiciary. A formal move here would likely trigger a massive protest from the legal community and the opposition.
  • Diplomatic Outcomes: The President has defended his "nosebleed-inducing" trips as essential. The public will soon look for concrete results—such as finalized trade deals or investment commitments—to see if the "pigs and Buddhas" defense holds any water.
  • The "Election Integrity" Narrative: If the PPP continues to push the narrative of ballot mismanagement at Olympic Park, it could signal a shift toward a more "hard-right" strategy, potentially alienating moderate voters but deepening the loyalty of their core supporters.

In a political environment where the President refers to his critics as "pigs" and the opposition calls the President’s family a "nuisance," the space for constructive governance is shrinking. For the foreign observer and the domestic voter alike, the question is no longer who wins the argument, but whether the institutions of the state can survive the language used to defend them.
