18 Million Prompts: How ChatGPT Rewired South Korea’s Daily Life and Workplace Culture

November 09, 2026

Glance at the passengers riding the morning subway across Seoul, and you will notice a subtle shift in the air. Not long ago, almost every glowing smartphone screen displayed endless feeds of short-form videos or social media updates. Today, an increasing number of thumbs are typing detailed prompts into AI chat interfaces, scanning bullet points, and refining generated drafts before stepping off at their stations. For many professionals in Korea, opening an AI assistant has quietly replaced checking the morning news as the very first ritual of the workday.

Just two years ago, generative AI was largely viewed as an entertaining novelty in South Korea—a clever trick that could write a funny poem or help students cheat on basic homework. Today, it has transformed into critical digital infrastructure. According to recent mobile market data, monthly active users of ChatGPT in South Korea have crossed the 18 million mark, with some industry estimates placing the figure as high as 18.76 million. In a country of roughly 51 million people, that means more than one out of every three citizens—and the vast majority of the active workforce—now carries an artificial intelligence assistant in their pocket.

Beyond 18 Million: The Shift from Casual Curiosity to Workplace Survival

A 44 percent year-over-year surge in mobile app users is impressive by any standard, but in South Korea’s hyper-connected market, it signals something deeper: a complete shift in workplace standards. The current migration mirrors the historic rollout of KakaoTalk, South Korea’s dominant messaging app, a decade ago. Back then, holding out against downloading KakaoTalk eventually became practically impossible if you wanted to maintain a social or professional life. Today, refusing to use generative AI in Korean office environments increasingly feels like running a race with a self-imposed handicap.

Across Korean tech hubs, finance districts, and creative industries, employees are relying on AI to draft marketing copy, generate complex Excel formulas, summarize multi-page government reports, and flag risky clauses in legal contracts. In South Korea’s notoriously intense corporate culture, where long working hours and fierce competition remain the norm, workers are turning to AI out of pure necessity. The tool is no longer an optional luxury; it is a mechanism for survival in a squeezed labor market.

Even more revealing than the total user headcount is how long Koreans are staying inside these apps. Data shows that average user duration on ChatGPT has doubled compared to the previous year. People are no longer using AI for single, casual queries like checking the weather or asking trivia questions. Instead, they are engaging in prolonged, multi-turn dialogues:

  • Debugging complex code: Programmers spend hours feeding error logs back and forth into the chat window to isolate bugs.
  • Deep document analysis: Researchers and corporate strategists upload entire academic papers, corporate disclosures, and policy briefs to extract core arguments and edge cases.
  • Iterative drafting: Workers refine campaign strategies and client presentations through continuous feedback loops.

This doubling of time spent highlights a poignant reality of Korean working life. Under heavy workloads and relentless deadlines, employees are aggressively leveraging AI to squeeze every possible drop of efficiency out of their day, hoping to save precious minutes in a culture that still struggles with excessive overtime.

Claude’s 15-Fold Surge Meets OpenAI’s 91% Fortress

While OpenAI continues to lead the market, the landscape is far from static. The most striking dynamic over the past year has been the rapid rise of Anthropic’s Claude, which recorded an extraordinary 15-fold growth in South Korea. Though its absolute user count remains far smaller than OpenAI’s, its trajectory has sent ripples through the local tech community.

Claude has carved out a dedicated following among software engineers, research analysts, and professional writers. Many power users praise Claude for its superior coding assistance, handling of long context windows, and nuanced, human-like writing tone—a welcome alternative for those fatigued by ChatGPT’s occasionally formulaic responses. In specialized developer circles, Claude is increasingly viewed not just as an alternative, but as the primary tool of choice for heavy-duty technical tasks.

Yet, looking at the broader market metrics reveals the stark reality of platform dominance. Despite Claude’s 15-fold expansion, ChatGPT maintains a staggering market share of roughly 91 percent among mobile AI service users in South Korea. The network effects and brand recognition built by OpenAI have created a formidable moat:

  • Brand identity: For the general public, "ChatGPT" has become a generic trademark synonymous with artificial intelligence itself.
  • Ecosystem lock-in: Features like Custom GPTs, integrated voice modes, and extensive plugin ecosystems anchor users firmly within OpenAI’s environment.
  • Search dominance: Casual users downloading an AI app for the first time inevitably tap the top result in the app stores, reinforcing OpenAI's lead.

This dynamic has created a dual-track market in South Korea: an overwhelmingly dominant mass-market platform led by OpenAI, paired with specialized alternative tools adopted by tech-savvy power users. However, such heavy market concentration carries clear risks, raising concerns over potential price hikes, service lock-in, and technological dependency on a single foreign tech giant.

Leaked Keys and Corporate Secrets: The Hidden Risks of AI Reliance

As AI adoption accelerates across Korean society, security experts are raising urgent flags about the unintended consequences of unvetted usage. A particularly alarming trend recently surfaced in the cryptocurrency community, where several users accidentally exposed sensitive financial access keys through ChatGPT’s public "shared link" feature.

In their eagerness to seek technical advice, users copied and pasted their private cryptocurrency wallet seed phrases directly into the chat prompt, asking the AI how to store them securely. When these users subsequently generated public links to share their conversation threads online, their private seed phrases were indexed and exposed to potential theft. It stands as a textbook example of how sudden technical convenience can dull basic security reflexes.

Beyond individual financial missteps, corporate data leakage has emerged as a major institutional headache. Office workers routinely paste internal confidential memos, customer databases, and unreleased source code into prompts to meet tight deadlines, unaware that default settings may feed that data back into global training models.

While major Korean conglomerates and government agencies have rushed to issue generative AI compliance guidelines, enforcing them in real-time has proven nearly impossible. When an overworked employee is faced with a choice between following cumbersome corporate protocols or completing a report in five minutes using AI, convenience frequently wins out. Without automated safeguards and rigorous security literacy, South Korea risks suffering severe corporate espionage and data sovereignty breaches.

Digital Sharecropping vs. Sovereign AI: What Lies Ahead

As American tech giants tighten their grip on South Korea’s software landscape, domestic tech leaders—including traditional internet giants that once dominated local search and portal services—are finding themselves pushed onto the defensive. Building foundational frontier models requires astronomical capital investments and compute infrastructure that local firms struggle to match against Silicon Valley's balance sheets.

The gap in foundational AI capabilities was further widened by past domestic policy choices, notably the drastic R&D budget cuts enacted during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, which disrupted academic research pipelines and domestic tech initiatives. Today, the administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is mounting a policy drive to regain technical self-reliance and protect national data sovereignty. Yet closing the technological divide remains an uphill battle.

Without competitive domestic alternatives or robust local ecosystems, Korean enterprise and consumer knowledge workers risk becoming "digital tenant farmers"—paying perpetual monthly subscription fees to foreign tech monopolies while exporting vital national data abroad. Furthermore, total reliance on AI tools creates a subtle cognitive risk: as workers place uncritical trust in polished AI outputs, their capacity to spot hallucinations, logical flaws, and subtle biases inevitably erodes.

What to Watch Next:

  • Policy and Regulatory Frameworks: Watch how President Lee Jae-myung’s administration balances support for domestic "Sovereign AI" initiatives with strict security regulations on corporate data transfers.
  • Domestic Tech Response: Keep an eye on whether Korean tech leaders can successfully deploy specialized, domain-specific AI models tailored to local business contexts to challenge American dominance.
  • Enterprise Security Infrastructure: Track the adoption of private enterprise AI deployments and local data-loss prevention (DLP) solutions as Korean companies scramble to block sensitive IP leaks.

Surpassing 18 million users is a landmark achievement that confirms South Korea’s position at the forefront of global tech adoption. But as AI transitions from a novelty into the fundamental fabric of work and life, the central question for Korea is no longer how fast citizens can adopt these tools, but whether the nation can maintain its technological independence and critical oversight in an era built on foreign algorithms.
