South Korea’s Legal Battlefield: Special Counsel Clashes, Impeachment Threats, and an Economic Divide

October 17, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, the microphones at committee hearings rarely cool down. In South Korea, politics has long been fought not merely through policy debates, but through the prosecutorial machinery: court dockets, search warrants, and special prosecutor mandates. That dynamic has reached a fever pitch under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, where the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the conservative opposition People Power Party find themselves locked in an unforgiving war of attrition.

Every major faction is currently fighting a two-front battle. On one side, the political establishment is aggressively litigating the legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the ousted president who was impeached and now sits in prison. On the other side, rival politicians are weaponizing allegations of familial privilege and judicial bias to reshape the playing field ahead of the next legislative elections. Behind the fiery rhetoric of press briefings and parliamentary audits lies a fundamental fight over who commands the moral high ground—and who will survive the next cycle of voter reckoning.

The Special Prosecutor Wars: Family Resumes and Relocation Scandals

The sharpest personal offensive comes from Cho Kuk, the leader of the progressive minority Rebuilding Korea Party. Cho has demanded an immediate special counsel investigation targeting Han Dong-hoon, leader of the main opposition People Power Party, and his immediate family. Cho singled out Han’s daughter, alleging irregularities in the construction of her academic portfolio—specifically questioning reported volunteer credentials that supposedly totaled an impossible 20,000 hours.

For international observers, Cho’s target is not coincidental. Cho himself served briefly as justice minister years ago before being derailed and seeing his own family relentlessly prosecuted over academic credentials—a scandal that ultimately led to the criminal conviction of his daughter, Cho Min. Cho’s argument is straightforward and emotionally charged: apply the exact same uncompromising legal standard to Han Dong-hoon’s family that was applied to his own.

"Han Dong-hoon's innocence and his daughter's supposed brilliance can only be objectively verified through an independent special prosecutor," Cho argued, challenging the ruling Democratic Party to pass the investigative bill during the current regular parliamentary session.

Han and the People Power Party immediately hit back, dismissing the accusations as baseless disinformation designed to distract the public and pledging criminal defamation suits. But the call for an outside prosecutor resonates with a public weary of allegations that law enforcement treats political elites with a double standard.

At the same time, the special prosecutor tasked with examining past corruption delivered a stark reminder that political power in South Korea is fleeting. In final arguments regarding the controversial relocation of the presidential office and official residence to Hannam-dong during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, prosecutors demanded heavy prison terms for key figures: four years for Kim Dae-ki, Mr. Yoon’s former presidential chief of staff, and three and a half years for Lee Sang-min, the former minister of the interior and safety.

The indictment centers on allegations that lucrative construction contracts were illicitly handed to favored contractors without proper bids, wasting vast amounts of state funds during the rushed move. With Mr. Yoon already serving time following his historic impeachment and removal from office, the judicial reckoning is rapidly closing in on the top aides who executed his contentious executive orders.

Governance in the Open: Lee Jae-myung’s Cabinet and the Shifting Polls

Across town at the Yongsan presidential office, President Lee Jae-myung is pushing his own distinct governing doctrine: complete administrative transparency. Lee has stood firm on his decision to open Cabinet meetings to live broadcasts and public viewing, rejecting complaints from conservative critics who claim that turning the cameras on forces state ministers into timid performers who cannot speak candidly.

Lee dismissed those claims as deliberate distortions, maintaining that returning to closed-door sessions would merely revive the opaque backroom decision-making that facilitated past presidential abuses. For Lee, forcing ministers to defend their policies publicly is a tool of accountability. The People Power Party, however, brands it as pure public-relations theater that cripples genuine internal policy debate.

The political dividends of this posture appear modest but stabilizing. According to the latest regular survey by pollster Gallup Korea:

  • President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval: Rebounded by 2 percentage points to 39%, halting a downward slide, with negative appraisals sitting at 52%.
  • Party Support: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea held first place at 36%, widening its lead over the conservative People Power Party, which stalled at 27%.
  • Election Sentiment: Despite the ruling party's lead, 47% of respondents stated that opposition candidates should win the next general election to keep the administration in check, while only 34% believed the ruling party should win to support the government.
  • Potential Leadership Favorability: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon led individual favorability ratings at 30%, followed by Han Dong-hoon at 23% and Democratic Party Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok at 21%.

The numbers deliver a cautionary message to the administration: while President Lee has stabilized his core base near the 40% threshold, an undercurrent of public wariness persists. Voters may favor the Democratic Party over a splintered conservative opposition, but they remain anxious about unchecked executive authority.

Escalation in the Courts: The Threat to Impeach the Chief Justice

The confrontation between the branches of government reached a dangerous boiling point when Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the Democratic Party’s Supreme Council, openly raised the prospect of impeaching Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Moving beyond earlier polite calls for the chief justice to voluntarily step down over alleged institutional bias and delays in judicial reform, Kim signaled that the legislative majority is prepared to explore constitutional removal.

The ruling party’s frustration stems from what it views as a conservative judicial establishment dragging its feet on progressive legal reforms and protecting entrenched interests. Kim argued that an uncooperative judiciary that functions as a shield for specific political factions forfeits its claim to independence and must be checked by the legislature.

The People Power Party reacted with fury, accusing the majority of staging an unconstitutional assault on the separation of powers. Conservative leadership warned that attempting to subordinate the highest court in the land through legislative force would backfire, turning the Lee administration into a prematurely paralyzed "dead duck." The conflict spilled directly into the parliamentary audit of the Seoul High Court, where judicial efficiency took a back seat to hours of shouting and accusations designed for viral video clips rather than institutional reform.

Booming Equities and the Small-Business Squeeze

Beyond the legal warfare, the parliamentary audit exposed a glaring duality in the South Korean economy. On the trading floors, sentiment has been remarkably bullish: the benchmark KOSPI index recently breached the historic 5,500 milestone. The milestone should have been an unalloyed triumph for the administration's market-reform agenda.

Instead, it ignited a new political firestorm. Lawmakers from the People Power Party accused the government of pressuring the National Pension Service—one of the world's largest institutional investors—to artificially adjust its portfolio rebalancing rules to pump domestic shares for political gain. The opposition went so far as to demand yet another special prosecutor to investigate whether state pension funds were exposed to reckless risk just to inflate market figures.

Yet on the ground, away from the stock market tickers, the data tells a far more sobering story:

  • Credit Guarantee Fund Distress: In the National Assembly’s Political Affairs Committee, data revealed that subrogation claims at the Korea Credit Guarantee Fund (a state agency that guarantees bank loans for small enterprises) reached an alarming 8.9 trillion won (approximately $6.5 billion).
  • Insolvency Pressures: The surge in claims indicates that a historic number of marginalized small-business owners and mom-and-pop shops are defaulting on their commercial loans, threatening the stability of the public lending backstop.
  • Executive Field Operations: In response to mounting domestic stress, Prime Minister Han Seong-sook was dispatched to manage emergency responses to an industrial factory blaze in Bucheon and oversee regional tech investments in Daejeon, while the transport ministry pledged weekly construction site safety inspections.

The contrast is striking. While macro indicators and stock valuations break records, real-economy small enterprises are buckling under prolonged debt strains and high operating costs.

What to Watch Next

As the legislative session proceeds, South Korean politics is poised to accelerate along several specific fault lines:

  • The Han Dong-hoon Probe Vote: Watch whether the Democratic Party officially adopts Cho Kuk’s special counsel bill targeting Han Dong-hoon's daughter. Bringing the measure to the floor will force the People Power Party into an all-out procedural resistance.
  • Sentencing for the Hannam-dong Relocation: The Seoul Central District Court’s upcoming rulings on Kim Dae-ki and Lee Sang-min will provide a judicial verdict on the administrative conduct of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's inner circle, potentially delivering a fresh blow to conservative credibility.
  • The Chief Justice Standoff: Whether the ruling party formally tables an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae will signal whether Yeouido is prepared to enter an unprecedented constitutional showdown with the judicial branch.
  • Credit Defaults and Fiscal Support: With the Korea Credit Guarantee Fund facing $6.5 billion in bad debts, look for whether the National Assembly can pivot from partisan warfare to pass targeted credit relief for the country's struggling merchant class.
