Seoul’s 90-Week Housing Streak Masks a Widening Fracture Across the Capital

October 30, 2026

Step into a real estate brokerage in Apgujeong, the affluent heart of Seoul’s Gangnam district, and the mood is frozen. Phones rarely ring, sellers are grudgingly paring down asking prices on older luxury apartments that once commanded bidding wars, and prospective buyers are waiting on the sidelines. Yet take an hour-long subway ride south to Gunpo or Suwon in Gyeonggi Province, and realtors tell an entirely different story: listings are snapped up within days, asking prices jump tens of thousands of dollars over a single weekend, and young couples scramble to close deals before they are priced out of the capital region entirely.

On paper, South Korea’s real estate headlines still project unrelenting momentum. According to weekly market monitoring by the Korea Real Estate Board and private portal KB Real Estate, apartment purchase prices in Seoul have logged their 90th consecutive week of gains—the longest uninterrupted rally in modern tracking. But beneath this milestone lies a market splitting at the seams. The weekly pace of gains has slowed to 0.09 percent, marking two straight weeks of deceleration, while the forces propelling and suppressing prices have decoupled in dramatic fashion.

Understanding this shift requires peering past aggregate indexes. What is unfolding across the greater Seoul metropolitan area is not a broad-based boom, but a structural squeeze driven by stricter borrowing caps, high interest rates, and an escalating supply crunch in the rental market. As top-tier enclaves stall under debt curbs, panic-driven demand is spilling into suburban commuter belts and driving up rental costs for ordinary working families.

The Illusion of a 90-Week Bull Run and the Gangnam Crack

The headline figure—90 uninterrupted weeks of price appreciation—gives the impression of a market running on unshakeable optimism. In reality, that statistic is an aggregate artifact hiding a distinct loss of momentum. A weekly expansion of 0.09 percent points to buyer exhaustion. With the total volume of completed transactions drying up, nominal price levels are being sustained by a dwindling number of high-priced sales rather than robust, widespread market participation.

The clearest crack in the facade has appeared in the "Gangnam 3-gu"—the three high-value southeastern districts of Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa that historically dictate sentiment across the nation. For years considered bulletproof against downturns, the district’s premier assets are now faltering. In Gangnam and Seocho, apartment values have slipped for 12 consecutive weeks, and the margin of decline across all three districts widened in late October.

The catalyst is credit. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term, extensive financial deregulation and permissive lending policies fostered aggressive, debt-fueled acquisitions in Seoul's redevelopment complexes. Buyers piled onto multi-million-dollar mortgages, betting that capital appreciation would easily outpace borrowing costs. Today, with the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung aggressively clamping down on systemic household debt through rigid Debt Service Ratio (DSR) caps and stricter loan ceilings, that leverage mechanism has snapped.

Affluent homeowners and multiple-property owners who took on excessive leverage now face steep monthly interest repayments alongside heavy property holding taxes. Because new buyers cannot secure the oversized loans needed to absorb multi-billion-won listings, urgent sales (known locally as geummaemul) are trickling onto the market at discounted rates. With high-end purchases out of reach for all but all-cash buyers, the narrative of Gangnam's invincibility has ground to a halt.

Pushed North and South: The Suburban "Gap-Closing" Frenzy

While the luxury core cools, price pressures have simply been displaced. Shut out of the southern powerhouse districts by stringent loan caps, buyers hunting for homes have initiated an aggressive round of "gap-closing" (kki-macheugi)—bidding up relatively affordable properties across northern Seoul and the outer commuter rings.

In northern Seoul, the trend has broken historical records. Propelled by sustained buying in working- and middle-class districts like Seongbuk and Jongno, the average apartment transaction price in the Gangbuk region (north of the Han River) crossed the 1.2 billion won threshold (about $880,000) for the first time. For ordinary salaried households, standard 84-square-meter three-bedroom units—often referred to as the "national size"—have moved definitively out of reach within city boundaries.

The most acute fallout, however, is taking place across the southern rim of Gyeonggi Province, the suburban belt encircling Seoul. Blocked from central districts, buyers in their 30s and 40s have triggered a powerful "balloon effect," where squeezing credit in one area causes speculative pressure to bulge out in another:

  • Gunpo and Suwon (Yeongtong-gu): Apartment prices in both municipalities surged by 0.58 percent in a single week—more than six times the broader Seoul growth rate. Local brokerages recorded instances where asking prices climbed by 120 million won (about $88,000) in just four days.
  • Hwaseong (Dongtan New Town): Capitalizing on anticipated regional rail improvements like the GTX express lines, apartment prices in this planned hub rose nearly 1.0 percent in seven days.
  • Broad Regional Disparity: Overall apartment price growth across Gyeonggi Province clocked in at double the rate recorded inside Seoul proper.

This suburban surge reflects desperation rather than boundless consumer confidence. It is defensive buying, fueled by the mounting anxiety among younger households that if they do not purchase a suburban home immediately, the entry ladder to property ownership will disappear forever.

A Fractured Market: Falling Purchase Prices Meet Surging Rents

The broader distortion in South Korean housing becomes painfully evident when comparing purchase transactions to the rental sector. In the third quarter, the average purchase price for Seoul's standard 84-square-meter apartments fell by 2.5 percent compared to the same period last year. Yet over that exact same timeframe, average deposit costs for jeonse—South Korea’s unique lease system where tenants hand over a massive lump-sum deposit instead of paying monthly rent—surged by 3.8 percent.

This divergence exposes the core dysfunction of the current market. Prospective homebuyers, frightened by high mortgage rates and the prospect of falling asset prices in the capital, have chosen not to buy. Instead, they are remaining in the rental pool. This sudden flood of rental demand has collided with a severe drought of available jeonse listings, driving rental costs upward. In late October, weekly jeonse gains in Seoul hit 0.10 percent, comfortably outpacing the 0.07 percent uptick in underlying sale values.

The resulting squeeze creates a vicious cycle: as rental deposits creep closer to parity with total purchase prices, the financial barrier between renting and owning narrows, yet the cash required simply to maintain a tenancy grows more onerous each month.

For working-class families, the housing ladder is cracking from both ends. They are boxed out of purchasing homes by rigorous debt criteria, yet simultaneously punished in the leasing market by soaring deposits. The Lee Jae-myung administration has maintained a firm stance on curbing macroprudential risk and reining in speculative liquidity, viewing credit discipline as non-negotiable after the loose borrowing environment of prior years. Yet without dedicated measures to unlock rental supply and stabilize lease deposits, working families are bearing the immediate brunt of the adjustment.

What to Watch Next

As the market heads into the winter months, the current fragmentation points toward several concrete developments that will shape the capital region's property trajectory:

  • The Durability of the Gyeonggi Rally: Watch whether the sharp gains in southern Gyeonggi cities like Gunpo, Suwon, and Hwaseong can persist once buyers encounter the exact same DSR debt ceilings that halted Gangnam. If local household incomes cannot sustain recent price spikes, the suburban rally could abruptly stall.
  • The Widening Jeonse-to-Price Ratio: As purchase prices soften while lease deposits climb, the ratio of rental deposits to home values will rise. Historically, this dynamic encourages "gap investing"—speculators buying homes using the tenant’s deposit to fund the bulk of the purchase price. Observers should track whether tighter financing prevents this speculative channel from reopening, or if it sparks a new wave of disputes over unreturned tenant deposits.
  • Central Regulatory Balancing: Pay close attention to whether the Lee Jae-myung administration supplements its strict mortgage restrictions with targeted rental-market stabilization policies. While the clampdown on high-end leverage is deflating the Gangnam bubble, the growing shortage of urban rental housing poses an urgent policy challenge that credit restrictions alone cannot resolve.
