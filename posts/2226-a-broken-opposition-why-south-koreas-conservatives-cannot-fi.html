A Broken Opposition: Why South Korea’s Conservatives Cannot Find Their Footing

November 11, 2026

Behind the heavy doors of the National Assembly on November 10, the mood within South Korea’s conservative establishment swung between defensive rage and sheer exasperation. In private conference rooms, lawmakers traded biting insults over legislative bills. In public broadcast studios, elder statesmen delivered blistering verdicts on the party’s surviving figureheads. And across the river at the presidential office, the party’s floor leader warned the administration of President Lee Jae-myung that any hope for bipartisan cooperation was running on empty.

For the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative party, the descent into open factional warfare is not merely an ordinary mid-term squabble. It is an existential reckoning. Following the catastrophic December 3 martial law incident, the subsequent impeachment and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who now sits behind bars—the PPP was stripped of the presidency and shoved into the cold benches of the opposition. Instead of coalescing into a disciplined counterweight to the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the conservatives are fracturing into bitter ideological camps, unsure whether to purge their past or double down on street agitation.

Cho Gab-je’s Warning: Purging the Extremists or Courting Oblivion

The day’s most bruising public assessment did not come from the political left, but from the spiritual heart of conservative commentary. Cho Gab-je, a veteran conservative journalist and long-time opinion maker whose writings have shaped right-wing discourse for decades, gave an interview that sent shockwaves through the party ranks. His diagnosis was unsparing: if Korean conservatism has any hope of surviving, former interim leader Han Dong-hoon is completely indispensable, while current party leader Jang Dong-hyeok must be thoroughly excised.

Cho’s argument cuts straight to the strategic trap that has bedeviled conservative parties worldwide. Drawing an explicit comparison to the United States Republican Party, Cho warned that conservative movements that allow themselves to be hijacked by fringe conspiracy theories and hardline grievance inevitably lose elections. For South Korean conservatives, attempting to navigate the post-impeachment political landscape without shedding their most recalcitrant elements is, in Cho’s view, a guaranteed path to defeat in upcoming parliamentary and local races.

Cho’s intervention matters because it strips away the polite fiction that the party’s troubles are merely skin-deep. By openly urging the expulsion of sitting party leader Jang Dong-hyeok while championing Han Dong-hoon, Cho gave voice to a growing, anxious segment of the conservative electorate: those who realize that without a credible, modern face capable of winning back moderate urban voters, the right faces long-term electoral irrelevance.

Open Warfare Between Jang Dong-hyeok and the Pro-Han Faction

Inside the party apparatus, the tensions Cho described erupted into raw acrimony. Party leader Jang Dong-hyeok did not absorb the criticism quietly; instead, he lashed out directly at the so-called "pro-Han" (loyal to Han Dong-hoon) faction within the party assembly. Jang issued a blunt, public warning to his internal critics: "If you do not intend to fight together, keep your light mouths shut."

Such vitriol between a party chairman and his own lawmakers illustrates how brittle the PPP’s chain of command has become. The party is no longer functioning as a unified legislative bloc. When conservative lawmakers recently attempted to sponsor a bill introducing electronic voting for overseas nationals, members from their own benches publicly revolted, lambasting the initiative as an unforced blunder that risks undermining party integrity.

The discord spread into core economic legislation as well. In a closed-door general meeting of lawmakers, the party ground to a halt over whether to support a mandatory tender offer rule—a corporate governance measure designed to protect minority shareholders during corporate acquisitions. Lawmakers bickered without reaching a consensus. Caught between traditional pro-business orthodoxies and the urgent political need to appeal to retail investors, the PPP ended up looking paralyzed, unable to present a clear ideological stance on fundamental governance issues.

Street Protests and Clashes with the Presidential Office

While the party tore at itself from within, its parliamentary leadership attempted to project strength toward the government. People Power Party floor leader Jeong Jeom-sig met with Choi In-ho, the newly appointed Senior Presidential Secretary for Political Affairs under President Lee Jae-myung. In South Korean governance, the senior political secretary serves as the chief liaison between the executive branch and rival parties, tasked with smoothing the path for legislative compromises and budget approvals.

The encounter began with the conventional diplomatic pleasantries, with Jeong remarking that the presidential secretary should "wear down the doorstep" of the opposition through frequent visits and open dialogue. Yet the veneer of civility evaporated almost immediately. Jeong laid down strict red lines, warning the presidential office that the opposition will vigorously cut proposed funding for the administration's Future Response Fund and will flatly refuse to tolerate any special counsel legislation targeting the dismissal of past indictments.

Jeong justified the hardline stance by arguing that respecting the main opposition is equivalent to respecting the half of the public that did not vote for the current government. But having set those battle lines inside the National Assembly, the party leadership is choosing to take the fight to the pavement:

  • Nationwide Rallies: The PPP resolved to escalate its demands for presidential vetoes on special prosecutor bills by launching an aggressive public campaign across the country.
  • The Starting Point: The street mobilization is scheduled to begin on November 14 in the traditional conservative bastion of Busan, before expanding to other metropolitan hubs.
  • The Two-Track Paradox: While the floor leadership attempts to negotiate policy in legislative committees, the party apparatus is rallying conservative foot soldiers outside, leaving little room for actual legislative compromise.

This dual-track approach—boycotting negotiations inside parliament while staging rallies on the streets—is a familiar tactic in South Korea’s polarized democracy. Yet for a party that recently lost executive power following a disastrous constitutional crisis, returning to the streets risks alienating moderate voters who crave governance and stability rather than perpetual partisan warfare.

The Dec. 3 Shadow and the Illusion of Unity

Against this turbulent backdrop, whispers of a broad "conservative grand coalition" have once again begun circulating in Seoul political circles. Pundits and operatives frequently mention four key figures when discussing how the right might consolidate power: party leader Jang Dong-hyeok, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, former interim leader Han Dong-hoon, and reformist lawmaker Lee Jun-seok. The argument holds that only by uniting these competing power centers can the opposition mount a serious challenge against the ruling Democratic Party.

Yet this arithmetic looks increasingly disconnected from reality. Each of these four figures possesses radically different constituencies, competing presidential ambitions, and irreconcilable views on party reform. The acrimonious debates over whether to readmit Lee Jun-seok or how to respond to caustic critiques from public intellectuals reveal an opposition driven by individual survival rather than shared vision.

True conservative rebuilding cannot begin with backroom political alliances or tactical attacks on the government. It requires an honest, unvarnished reckoning with the December 3 martial law declaration. Without a sincere apology and deep introspection regarding the events that led to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's fall, conservative promises of renewal will remain empty slogans.

By obsessing over tactical friction with President Lee Jae-myung’s administration while failing to clearly disavow the authoritarian excesses of its recent past, the People Power Party remains trapped. Street rallies and aggressive rhetoric may energize an increasingly agitated core base, but they do nothing to answer the public's fundamental question: why should voters trust this party with state power ever again?

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether the People Power Party can establish a stable footing or if it will fracture even further:

  • The November 14 Busan Rally: Turnout and messaging at the upcoming street protest in Busan will reveal whether the party’s grassroots base is still motivated, or if public fatigue is setting in even in the conservative heartland.
  • Budget Standoffs: Watch how floor leader Jeong Jeom-sig navigates parliamentary committee reviews for the upcoming fiscal budget, particularly regarding the administration's Future Response Fund, to see if the party's threats result in government concessions or legislative deadlock.
  • Factional Retaliation: Following Jang Dong-hyeok’s blunt public warning to the pro-Han faction, monitor whether Han Dong-hoon’s allies escalate their public criticism or begin organizing an organized push to challenge Jang’s leadership directly.
