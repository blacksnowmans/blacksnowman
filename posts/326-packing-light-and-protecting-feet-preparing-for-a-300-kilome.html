Packing Light and Protecting Feet: Preparing for a 300-Kilometer Pilgrimage on the Camino de Portugal

September 07, 2026

The first time you walk the Camino de Santiago, it has a way of rewriting your life's map. Back in 2019, carrying a 20-kilogram (about 44 lbs) backpack across 800 kilometers of the French Way was an exercise in both blessing and agony. By the end, wrecked by lost toenails and a blanket of blisters, those hiking shoes were thrown away in favor of a pair of Crocs just to cross the finish line. Yet, the experience pulls you back. This time, the destination is the 300-kilometer (about 186 miles) Portuguese Way. Armed with the painful lessons of the past, the singular focus is shedding unnecessary weight and keeping feet intact.

The War on Weight: Streamlining the Backpack

The 2019 backpack was a portable department store of unneeded items. This time, the rule is simple: go as light as possible. Using a backpack bought from a market in Vietnam—suspected to be a knock-off—the initial scale reading came out at 7 kilograms (about 15.5 lbs). While slightly over the initial 5-kilogram goal, the plan is to shed items along the trail as needed. Hiking poles were left behind entirely; past experience proved they only caused shoulder strain rather than relief.

Packing strategy matters as much as the gear itself. Items rarely needed go to the bottom, while the sleeping bag is packed first. A Naturehike 420-gram ultra-light camping sleeping bag was chosen for this exact reason. Tech gear like GoPros, power banks, and charging cables has been strictly minimized. After all, a lighter bag leaves room for the actual landscape rather than just the burden of carrying it.

Editor's Note: Many pilgrims fixate on the official completion certificate waiting at the cathedral, but the real measure of the journey is how you treat yourself along the way. In 2019, the plaza outside the cathedral was choked with people desperate for their certificates. Too exhausted to stand in the endless lines, walking away without one felt completely fine—the ink-stamped passport pages were proof enough. This time isn't about performing for a crowd or forcing a grueling 25-kilometer day just to meet a quota. Pushing through the pain of plantar fasciitis or knee injuries only leads to an early ticket home. Emptying the pack is ultimately an exercise in emptying the mind of needless worry.

Logistics and the Art of Local Procurement

Financial prep was kept straightforward with just €450 (about $490 USD) in cash. Operating on a rough budget of €1 per kilometer, this is more than enough for a 300-kilometer trek, especially when relying on local ATMs rather than hauling heavy stacks of physical currency. For connectivity, a 40GB mobile data plan was secured through Movistar for about 19,800 won ($15 USD). Navigating via smartphone apps to track water stations and distances between villages has become an absolute necessity.

The midday Spanish sun can be punishing. The operational strategy is to start early around 7:00 AM while the air is cool, knocking out 70% of the day’s distance before noon. For stretches crossing expansive plateaus, extra water is carried beyond the usual 1-liter baseline, but heavy food supplies are kept to a minimum—relying on local bars and grocery shops along the route helps keep the backpack manageable.

Blister Prevention as a Survival Strategy

Blisters are the ultimate villain of the pilgrimage, capable of friction-inflicting torment that eventually destroys toenails. To combat them, a meticulous multi-sock strategy was deployed: toe socks, hiking socks, and a base liner sock worn underneath. Footwear is handled by Nepa Caneto Vibram boots, bought a size larger than normal to accommodate the thick layers and give the toes breathing room.

Rain gear purchased from Decathlon, including a poncho and short gaiters, forms the frontline defense against wet weather, as damp socks are the primary catalyst for blisters. A hip pack permanently houses blister pads, sewing needles, and thread, allowing for immediate draining and treatment before an irritation can fester into an infection. A headlamp is packed just for peace of mind, even if early mornings aren't always on the schedule.

The Rhythm of the Albergues and the Road Beyond

Accommodation along the Camino revolves around albergues—traditional pilgrim hostels. While travelers often stress over booking ahead, the reality of the trail dictates that accommodations are best decided day-by-day based on how far the feet can actually carry you. Forcing a rigid schedule to reach a pre-booked village can backfire when physical fatigue sets in. Along the broader Iberian journey, opportunities to absorb historical architecture add deeper meaning to the steps.

Ultimately, the pilgrimage is both a solitary endeavor and a shared social fabric. Walking 20 to 25 kilometers a day naturally draws fellow travelers close, turning strangers into companions as you greet each other with a cheerful "Buen Camino!" Crossing mountain passes and finding profound joy in a simple piece of bread reminds you how quickly extraneous worries fall away.

What to Watch Next

  • Backpack Weight: Keep the total load under 7 kilograms (about 15.5 lbs) and maintain the discipline to discard unneeded items while walking.
  • Foot Care: Trust the multi-sock system, keep gaiters ready for rain, and carry sterile needles for immediate blister management.
  • Budgeting: Anchor finances around a €1-per-kilometer guideline, utilizing a hybrid of cash and local ATM withdrawals.
  • Pacing: Ditch rigid itineraries; listen to the body and accept that walking and resting on your own terms is the only winning strategy.

What will this journey yield at its conclusion? How much has changed between the memories of 2019 and the reality of today? With a scallop shell tied firmly to the backpack, the road forward leads back to Portugal. More than any certificate of completion, the true reward is simply the warmth of the path beneath your feet.
