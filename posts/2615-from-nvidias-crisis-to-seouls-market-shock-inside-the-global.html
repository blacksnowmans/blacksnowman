From Nvidia’s Crisis to Seoul’s Market Shock: Inside the Global Chip Sell-Off

November 22, 2026

When trading screens turned deep red across Wall Street on November 21, the tremor did not stay in New York. Nvidia, the undisputed flagship of the artificial intelligence boom, plunged 14% in a single session. That drop dragged the Nasdaq down 5.3%—wiping out the tech-heavy index’s gains for the year—and knocked roughly 10% off the Nasdaq semiconductor index. For South Korea, the timing could hardly have been worse. The global rout erupted just one day before SK Hynix, one of the world’s dominant memory chipmakers, was scheduled to debut its American Depositary Receipts (ADRs) in the United States.

To understand why a rough day in Silicon Valley hits Seoul like a systemic shock, it helps to look at how South Korea’s market is built. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are not merely large technology companies; they are the twin pillars of the benchmark KOSPI stock index and the backbone of South Korea’s export economy. On top of that domestic concentration, an army of South Korean individual investors—known locally as "seohak gaemi," or Western-bound retail investors—spent the AI rally pouring money directly into US tech stocks and leveraged chip funds. When the AI trade cracked on November 21, South Korea took the hit twice: once in New York, and once at home.

Nvidia’s Unraveling and the AI Bubble Reckoning

Nvidia’s 14% plunge was triggered by a one-two punch of regulatory scrutiny and sudden executive turmoil. First came news that the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) had opened an accounting investigation into the chipmaker. Regulators announced no confirmed violations, yet the mere launch of the probe forced investors to confront uncomfortable questions they had mostly ignored on the way up. Chief among them is "circular financing"—the practice of Nvidia investing in AI startups that then use that same capital to buy Nvidia chips. If those revenue loops unwind, growth that looked unstoppable suddenly looks fragile.

Almost simultaneously, Nvidia was rocked at the top. Chief executive Jensen Huang, who had led the company for 33 years, abruptly resigned amid an affair scandal. Former US President Donald Trump publicly floated the idea of a return, only for Nvidia’s board to reportedly rule it out. Naming Colette Kress as interim CEO did little to calm nerves over a leadership vacuum. With technical support levels broken, some analysts began warning that Nvidia’s shares could fall below $100.

What began as a single-stock crisis quickly turned into a broader referendum on whether Big Tech’s massive AI data-center spending can actually pay for itself in time. An AI forecasting model cited by market observers put the probability of an AI bubble burst at 58%. Across the AI infrastructure trade, selling became indiscriminate:

  • CoreWeave, an AI cloud infrastructure player, plunged 26% in a single week.
  • Broadcom tumbled 28% over a week, brushing aside market expectations that its AI revenue would top $21.7 billion.
  • TSMC, the Taiwanese contract manufacturing giant that produces advanced chips for Nvidia and others, sank nearly 20% in a week, hit by both Nvidia jitters and Trump’s warning that China should only be allowed older-generation semiconductors.
  • Smaller chip ecosystem stocks—including AMD, Onto Innovation, Semtech, and Applied Optoelectronics—sold off alongside them.

Even major firms trying to stay in the data-center race face tightening financial math. Companies such as Qualcomm are contending with heavy debt loads or softer Apple-related sales, while market watchers are flagging an "IPO paradox": even if major AI startups go public to raise cash, much of that money may have to pay down existing debt rather than fund fresh semiconductor orders.

A Cracked Memory Narrative: Oversupply and Legal Risk

If Nvidia supplied the spark, the memory chip sector—South Korea’s core industrial strength—was already sitting on dry tinder. The clearest warning sign came from Micron Technology, the leading US memory maker. Down 46% from its June peak, Micron slid between 21.1% and 24% over a single week, logging seven consecutive trading days of losses.

Micron tried to arrest the slide by unveiling a massive share buyback worth about $35 billion (49 trillion won), while emphasizing full order books and construction of a new plant in Singapore. Management insisted that modern memory chips are no longer the boom-and-bust commodity they once were. Wall Street treated the stock like a classic cyclical downturn anyway.

Analysts remain bitterly divided on what comes next for memory profitability. Optimists argue Micron sits at a historic bargain with as much as 169% upside. Skeptics counter that Nvidia’s next-generation "Rubin Ultra" architecture could reduce the number of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacks and overall memory capacity used per chip, squeezing the exact high-margin AI segment that memory makers have relied on.

Meanwhile, the non-AI side of the memory market is flashing red. Market tracker TrendForce warned of further price drops in NAND flash memory, sending SanDisk down 12.5% in a single day. While AI servers still command premium HBM, ordinary memory chips used in smartphones and PCs remain stuck in severe oversupply with sluggish consumer demand. Adding to the strain, Micron, Samsung Electronics, and SK Hynix failed to block a class-action lawsuit alleging DRAM price fixing, saddling all three producers with fresh legal costs and potential damages just as pricing power weakens.

Seoul’s Double Exposure: Downgrades and Retail Losses

Inside South Korea, the global sell-off collided with a sharp turn in domestic brokerage sentiment. BNK Investment & Securities, a Korean brokerage, shocked Seoul’s market by slashing its target price for SK Hynix to about $750 (1.05 million won)—a level below where the stock was trading at the time—and warning of an imminent memory price crash. Foreign and institutional investors rushed to sell, pushing SK Hynix below roughly $800 (1.12 million won) and igniting talk of a halving in value right before its US ADR listing.

At the same time, Korean retail investors who chased the AI boom abroad suffered staggering paper losses. Having poured roughly $570 million (800 billion won) into buying the dip on Nvidia, South Korea’s overseas retail traders saw estimated paper losses swell to about $4.3 billion (6 trillion won). Heavy bets on double-leveraged semiconductor exchange-traded funds such as SOXL turned a routine market correction into a severe wealth shock for individual households.

In Seoul, fear is no longer just about missing the AI rally—it is about how quickly leverage can turn paper wealth into deep losses on both sides of the Pacific.

The rout also revived the gloomiest voices in South Korea’s domestic market, with some bears warning of an 82% wipeout that could shrink a $70,000 investment (100 million won) to roughly $13,000 (18 million won). Retail traders began fretting that Samsung and SK Hynix could retreat toward long-standing psychological defense lines known in Korean market slang as "70,000-won Samsung and 200,000-won Hynix." Not everyone shares that panic: global credit agency Moody’s maintained that the long-term financial strength, technological lead, and market dominance of both South Korean chip giants remain intact despite the short-term rout. In a crisis, however, balance sheets rarely calm a frightened trading floor overnight.

What to Watch When Markets Reopen

The era in which any company with an AI label could rise on narrative alone is over. From here, markets will demand hard earnings, cleaner financing, and clearer visibility on demand. Four concrete tests will show whether this sell-off stabilizes or deepens:

  • Monday’s KOSPI test: Watch whether South Korea’s benchmark index can hold the 5,000 and 4,000 support zones, and whether forced selling or further drops in 2x leveraged semiconductor ETFs accelerate outflows.
  • SK Hynix’s US ADR debut: With the listing arriving immediately after the sell-off, the key indicator will not be first-day hype, but how price discovery between New York and Seoul shapes sentiment toward Korean memory shares.
  • Nvidia’s governance and SEC findings: Any concrete details from the SEC accounting probe—and whether interim CEO Colette Kress can stabilize confidence after Jensen Huang’s exit—will set the tone for global tech risk appetite.
  • China restrictions and real AI profits: Investors will watch how strictly Washington enforces limits on chip exports to China, whether PC and smartphone demand recovers enough to clear legacy memory oversupply, and whether major AI firms such as Anthropic can turn public listings into profitable, cash-backed chip demand.
