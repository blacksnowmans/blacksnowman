Behind the Resignation: How Factional Infighting and the "Seongnam Line" Are Shaking the Blue House

September 24, 2026

When Kang Hoon-sik, the presidential chief of staff, abruptly announced his resignation, it sent a shockwave through Seoul’s political district of Yeouido. On paper, the departure is being framed as an honorable exit — a collective taking of responsibility for a string of failed ministerial nominations and a plunging approval rating. But step behind the official press releases, and a very different story emerges from insiders within the presidential office and the ruling Democratic Party. Kang’s resignation is not merely a personal decision born of exhaustion; it is the opening salvo in a bitter, long-simmering power struggle that has finally broken through its tipping point, exposing deep fractures over personnel vetting and the dominant influence of the so-called "Seongnam Line."

The Invisible Factions Shaping the Blue House

When an administration is riding high, everyone claims to be part of a single, cohesive team. But the moment power begins to fray, factional lines that appear nowhere on the official organizational chart suddenly snap into sharp relief. The swirling rumors of a turf war between Kang Hoon-sik and Kim Hyun-ji, the first presidential secretary for general affairs, are a glaring symptom of an administration running on political fumes. When a governing apparatus is so structurally broken that it cannot absorb minor shocks and instead turns inward to hunt for internal enemies, the system is failing.

Publicly, the Blue House has dismissed these reports as "groundless and malicious speculation," even threatening legal action. Yet reporters covering the ruling bloc paint a different picture. They describe an invisible but impenetrable wall between Kang’s camp and the loyalists surrounding Kim Hyun-ji—figures tied to President Lee Jae-myung’s earlier days in Seongnam politics. While policy disagreements are normal in any administration, treating fellow aides as adversaries and leaking accusations to the press over who holds real administrative sway is unprecedented.

The core grievance echoing through the ruling party is simple yet toxic: "The Seongnam Line recommends and vets the candidates, so why is Kang Hoon-sik taking the fall?" As chief of staff, Kang chairs the personnel committee, meaning he cannot escape formal accountability. But suspicions are rampant that the actual vetting and alignment of nominees are heavily dictated by the Seongnam Line, leaving Kang to shoulder the blame for choices he may not have fully controlled. Naturally, the conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has seized on the opening, demanding that Kim Hyun-ji and former party policy coordinator Jeong Jin-sang be called as witnesses in the upcoming parliamentary audits. It is the classic script of a presidential office trying to prune away a scapegoat to save the trunk, forcing observers to ask: why does this administration constantly exhaust its energy fighting over personnel rather than governance?

The Erosion of the Core Electorate and the Politics of Subtraction

A falling approval rating is never just a numbers game. When a government is healthy, its core supporters treat policy missteps or personnel blunders as fixable errors, acting as a political shock absorber. Today, that cushion is gone. With the administration's political capital depleted, minor grievances are metastasizing into full-scale power struggles. A government's core supporters are not a blind fan club meant to applaud no matter what the president says or does; they are the bedrock political foundation that shields a regime in times of crisis. When that foundation begins to hollow out, blaming the voters for a stalled engine is like removing the battery and then wondering why the car won't start.

Prosecution reform has long served as a litmus test for this trust. For the progressive base, reform of the prosecution service was never just another item on a policy checklist; it was a symbolic pillar inherited from the liberal administration of late President Roh Moo-hyun. When the government's path diverged from what citizens had long demanded, that pillar cracked. Now, regardless of the merits of any given policy, a fundamental suspicion lingers: Is this government even looking in the same direction we are?

Compounding this drift is what critics call the "politics of subtraction"—an environment where dissent is treated as a hostile act. Rather than engaging with critical voices from veteran figures like progressive commentator Rhyu Si-min or dissenting lawmakers, the establishment labels them as splitters. Narrowing the circle until only absolute loyalists remain is a recipe for total isolation. Finding the root cause of a failure lets you fix a system; hunting for a scapegoat just changes the face in the chair. Swapping out Kang Hoon-sik or Kim Hyun-ji changes nothing if the engine itself is broken. Appointments are supposed to signal to the public, "We hear your anger." If the gap between the administration and the public grows so wide that even basic public trust feels out of reach, musical chairs in the staff quarters will achieve nothing.

Progressive Values Outlast Transient Power

The progressive political movement in South Korea was not built by any single president or party leader. It is a legacy forged over decades by ordinary citizens in the streets, at the ballot boxes, and through fierce public debate. A single struggling administration will not erase it. When politics veers off course, citizens fight back, criticize, and deliver sharp rebukes. That is the proof that democracy is working.

Politicians must never mistake their supporters for property. Voters walk alongside an administration only as long as it genuinely represents their shared values. If the compass is spinning wildly, the first task for any leader is to figure out why they became estranged from the public. The turf wars and elbowing happening behind the scenes of this latest resignation saga only breed public fatigue. What citizens want to know is not who lost the internal knife-fight, but simply: how did things get this bad?

The path back to regaining the trust of the base is surprisingly straightforward, though it requires humility. It starts with acknowledging why people walked away, treating criticism as counsel rather than an assault, and restoring absolute transparency to personnel vetting and policy execution. Swapping out the engine parts while ignoring a seized motor is a fool's errand. The Blue House must stop hunting for internal traitors and open its windows to the public.

What we are witnessing right now may be far more dangerous than a simple shift in power; it is the erosion of trust itself. Until those in power can answer the public's honest questions with equal honesty, the fractured pieces of that trust will remain impossible to put back together.
