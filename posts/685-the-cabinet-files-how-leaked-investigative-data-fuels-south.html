The "Cabinet Files": How Leaked Investigative Data Fuels South Korea’s Toxic Political Culture

September 11, 2026

In the high-stakes arena of South Korean politics, there is a recurring spectacle that feels as inevitable as a seasonal change. Whenever the political climate turns volatile, sensitive documents—often referred to as "Cabinet Files"—mysteriously emerge from the depths of investigation agencies. Like clockwork, these files find their way into the hands of select journalists, leading to sensational headlines that seem perfectly timed to dismantle a political opponent or a high-ranking official.

For decades, the South Korean public has watched this grim theater repeat itself. Despite changes in government and the rapid evolution of digital media, the fundamental mechanics of this "gossip politics" remain stubbornly unchanged. It is a cynical, weaponized use of information that leaves little room for due process, prompting a critical question: Why does the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the country’s main liberal opposition—continually fall victim to these leaks without developing an effective counter-strategy? Journalist Jang In-soo recently shed light on this issue, challenging us to look past the headlines and confront the institutional rot at the heart of Korean public discourse.

The Anatomy of an Unequal Information War

The core of the issue, as highlighted by Jang, is the illicit flow of investigative materials from the prosecution to specific political figures and, subsequently, the media. A recent example involves Han Dong-hoon—the leader of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative party—who has been frequently observed introducing specific, damning details regarding Kim Seung-won, a recent nominee for Minister of Justice. These pieces of information are clearly sourced from confidential investigative files, yet they are presented in a surgically selective manner designed to inflict maximum political damage.

For over a decade, the DPK has been caught in this trap repeatedly. The list of those targeted is a roll call of major political figures, from current party leader Lee Jae-myung to former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, and even high-profile cases like the trial of former Prime Minister Han Myeong-sook. The script is always the same: The prosecution leaks, the conservative press amplifies, and the target is left fighting a defensive battle against fragments of data stripped of their context.

The tragedy here is the complete lack of a defensive shield. In a functioning democracy, investigative files should only be released through strict, official, and transparent procedures. Yet, in South Korea, the mechanism for punishing the unauthorized release of "facts of suspected crime" (the official term for these leaks) is functionally dead. Official data from the National Assembly reveals that over the past decade, hundreds of complaints regarding the illegal publication of such facts were filed with prosecutors. Out of 289 cases, not a single one resulted in an indictment. The prosecution, holding the monopoly on investigative power, has effectively shielded itself from accountability. Laws meant to protect the integrity of investigations have become nothing more than paper tigers, used as tools by those in power to manipulate public opinion.

The Asymmetry of "Explosion" versus "Explanation"

Media dynamics play a significant role in this disparity. As Jang aptly puts it, "In a battle between those who leak allegations and those who attempt to defend against them, the leakers always win." The nature of a "leak" is inherently sensational and provocative—it feeds the public’s appetite for drama. Conversely, the defense—logic, nuance, and the demand for due process—is often complex, tedious, and unappealing to a public already distracted by the "spiciest" soundbites.

The DPK’s failure to adapt to this reality is a source of deep frustration. They continue to use outdated communication strategies, seemingly unable to bridge the gap between their commitment to the truth and the reality of a media environment that prioritizes speed and sensation. When you bring a scalpel to a machine-gun fight, the result is predictable.

The Delicate Balance Between Transparency and Confidentiality

This does not mean that every investigation should be shielded from public view. The public has a legitimate right to know about major financial crimes, stock manipulation, or high-level corruption. However, the current system fails on both counts: it allows for "leaked" information to be used as a political weapon, while simultaneously keeping the public in the dark about the official progress of cases.

The solution requires a dual-track approach:

  • Strict Confidentiality During Investigation: Investigative agencies must maintain absolute silence while a case is active to protect the presumption of innocence.
  • Transparent Disclosure After Conclusion: Once an investigation is complete, the results should be presented to the public through official, comprehensive briefings.

If these two principles were enforced—with severe, actual penalties for anyone leaking information before the conclusion of a case—the current cycle of manipulation could be broken. We need only look at countries like the United States or Germany to see that strict controls on pre-trial publicity are not barriers to freedom of the press, but essential safeguards for the fairness of the judicial process. In Korea, the "right to know" has become a convenient cover for a press that acts less like a watchdog and more like a megaphone for power.

Beyond Political Maneuvering: A Call for Authenticity

Ultimately, the most potent weapon in politics is not sophisticated maneuvering or clever spin—it is sincerity. Lee Jae-myung has built his political identity on the philosophy that "politics is a tool for the citizens." Yet, when the DPK finds itself in positions of power or influence, that message often clashes with the reality of their defensive, often clumsy, political gaming.

The public is far more perceptive than politicians give them credit for. When leaders try to dodge crises with half-truths or selective statistics, they may survive the news cycle, but they erode the foundation of public trust. The path forward for the Democratic Party and, indeed, for all of Korean politics, lies in a fundamental change of attitude: moving away from the "cabinet file" culture and toward a standard of unwavering honesty.

The barrier of distrust that exists today was not built in a day, and it will not be torn down with more political theater. It requires the courage to admit when a plan has failed, the humility to apologize for shortcomings, and a steadfast commitment to transparent communication. The era of the "Cabinet File" must end, not through cleverer political tricks, but through the heavy, undeniable weight of truth.

What to watch next

  • Legislative Reform: Watch for potential bills in the National Assembly aimed at institutionalizing strict penalties for the illegal leakage of investigative files. Whether these gain actual traction will indicate if the DPK is serious about reform or merely complaining.
  • Media Conduct: Observe how major South Korean news outlets cover future investigations. Are they continuing to rely on "anonymous leaks" from the prosecution, or are they shifting toward reporting based on official, transparent, and verified sources?
  • Internal DPK Strategy: Watch for changes in how the opposition party handles its messaging. Will they continue to play the "victim" in a defensive, reactive way, or will they develop a proactive strategy that centers on accountability and clear communication?
