The Global Far-Right Surge: Why the Fragmentation of the West Matters for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

November 12, 2026

Imagine standing in the center of Warsaw on a cold November afternoon. The air is thick with the smell of red flares and the rhythmic chanting of 250,000 people. This is not a fringe gathering; it is the largest march in Poland's recent history, a sea of white and red flags marking Independence Day. But underneath the veneer of patriotism lies something sharper: a exclusionary nationalism that is increasingly defining the political landscape not just in Eastern Europe, but across the globe. From the digital echo chambers of Silicon Valley to the parliamentary floors of London and Berlin, the "center" of global politics is holding with less and less conviction. For observers in Seoul, where the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—the current leader from the Democratic Party of Korea—is navigating a complex web of international alliances, these tremors in the West are more than just foreign news. They represent a fundamental shift in the democratic order that South Korea has relied upon for decades.

The news reaching us on November 11th paints a stark picture of a world in transition. We are no longer seeing isolated incidents of political upheaval; rather, we are witnessing a coordinated, infectious spread of populism fueled by economic inequality, anxieties over immigration, and a burgeoning online culture of grievance. This movement is reshaping the traditional conservative landscape, forcing established parties to either radicalize or face irrelevance. As South Korea grapples with its own demographic challenges and the need for a stable global trade environment, the rise of "My Country First" movements in the UK, France, Germany, and even Japan suggests that the era of predictable, liberal-democratic cooperation may be drawing to a close.

The Erosion of the European Center and the Rise of Radical Alternatives

In the United Kingdom, the political gravity is shifting toward Reform UK, the populist party led by Nigel Farage. Recent polling suggests that Reform UK has effectively caught up with the Conservative Party—the traditional pillar of the British right. Farage, a seasoned provocateur, is currently challenging the legitimacy of mainstream polling while demanding a larger share of the political spotlight. This is a significant development for the Lee Jae-myung administration's trade diplomats: a Britain led or heavily influenced by Reform UK would likely be a much more protectionist and unpredictable partner than the one that signed the original KORUS-style agreements.

However, this surge is not without its internal rot. Reform UK is currently navigating a series of scandals that highlight the volatility of "personality-led" parties. Nigel Farage is reportedly awaiting a court ruling regarding a high-value donation of approximately 5 million GBP (about $6.4 million), a cloud that has hung over his leadership for six months. Furthermore, the party’s internal management is in disarray; its General Secretary recently resigned after only 16 months following failures in candidate vetting. Despite these organizational failures, the party’s rhetoric remains potent. By pushing the "Great Replacement" theory—a conspiracy suggesting white populations are being intentionally replaced by immigrants—and linking every crime to migration, Reform UK is successfully poisoning the well of social cohesion to gain short-term electoral points.

Meanwhile, across the English Channel, France is bracing for a premature start to its 2027 presidential race. Éric Zemmour, a far-right pundit turned politician, has officially declared his candidacy. His platform is even more radical than that of Marine Le Pen, focusing on the immediate deportation of unemployed foreigners. By launching his campaign years in advance, Zemmour is attempting to capture the growing "anti-immigrant" sentiment before it can be moderated by mainstream parties. This early mobilization is a warning to the world: the far right is no longer content to be a protest vote. They are building a shadow government, preparing for the day they hold the levers of power in one of Europe’s most influential nuclear states.

Key Global Far-Right Developments:
  • United Kingdom: Reform UK reaches parity with Conservatives; Farage faces $6.4 million donation probe.
  • France: Éric Zemmour declares 2027 presidential bid with radical anti-migrant platform.
  • Germany: AfD maintains support despite pro-Russia stances and internal misogyny scandals.
  • Poland: 250,000 march in Warsaw, signaling a massive surge in exclusionary nationalism.

The Weaponization of Crisis in Germany and Spain

In Germany, the Alternative for Germany (AfD), the country's most prominent far-right party, is increasingly acting as a bridge for Russian influence within Europe. In a move that has alarmed the Lee Jae-myung administration’s security analysts, the AfD recently joined forces with the Left Party to support Russia’s return to international ice hockey competitions. While seemingly trivial, this is a calculated attempt to normalize relations with Moscow amidst the ongoing war in Ukraine. By leveraging concerns over energy costs and economic stability, the AfD is successfully chipping away at the Western alliance’s resolve to sanction Russia.

Yet, the AfD’s rise is plagued by the same hypocrisy seen in the UK. Alice Weidel, the party’s co-leader, recently faced a scandal when leaked emails revealed she had previously criticized the very culture of misogyny that now defines her party’s base. It is a striking irony: a woman leading a movement that frequently disparages women’s rights in favor of "traditional values." This opportunism is a hallmark of the modern far right. They are less about a consistent ideology and more about harvesting whatever resentment is available—whether it is against immigrants, women, or the "elites" in Brussels. Coupled with a massive data leak involving millions of euros in donations, the AfD is a party under legal and ethical fire, yet its popularity remains resilient because it provides a simple target for complex frustrations.

In Spain, the Vox party is demonstrating how to weaponize the "kitchen table" issues that also resonate in South Korea, such as the housing crisis. Vox has proposed a "Nationals First" policy for housing subsidies and is pushing to restrict foreigners from buying property in tourist hubs like Mallorca. As housing prices skyrocket globally, this brand of populism is incredibly effective. Instead of addressing the structural causes of property inflation—low supply, interest rates, or urban density—Vox offers a scapegoat: the outsider. This tactic is causing significant friction within Spain, as the party uses its media influence to attack the independence of the press and redirect the anger of nationwide strikes toward immigrant communities. It is a blueprint for political survival in the 21st century: find a genuine grievance, then blame a minority.

The Eastern Front: From Polish Marches to Japanese Classrooms

The scale of the Polish march—250,000 people—is a sobering reminder of how far these sentiments have moved into the mainstream. This is not just a crowd; it is a mobilized political force. The tension in Warsaw is compounded by the shadow of the Kraków shooting, where a gunman who killed nine people was found to be a follower of Anders Breivik, the Norwegian mass murderer. The link between online radicalization and offline violence is becoming more direct. Poland finds itself in a precarious position, squeezed between the external threat of a revisionist Russia and the internal threat of a radicalized citizenry that views liberal democracy as an enemy.

Perhaps most surprising to readers in the West is that these trends are not confined to the Atlantic world. In Japan, recent surveys of middle school students reveal a growing hostility toward immigration. A significant portion of Japanese youth expressed that an increase in immigration would be "disadvantageous" to their society. This is a critical data point for the Lee Jae-myung government, which is currently weighing immigration as a solution to Korea’s own shrinking workforce. If the next generation in Japan—a culture often viewed as a mirror to Korea’s demographic path—is turning toward exclusion, it suggests that "multiculturalism" is failing to take root in East Asia’s youth.

This sentiment in Japan is often nurtured by the "single-race myth" and a media environment that emphasizes social harmony over diversity. Even as the country faces a dire labor shortage, the emotional desire for a closed society is overriding economic logic. For Korea, this is a warning. The Democratic Party under President Lee Jae-myung has often championed a more inclusive social safety net, but if the "exclusionary energy" seen in Japanese middle schools crosses the sea, it could derail Korea’s efforts to modernize its own demographic policies. Hate, it seems, is an export that travels easily across borders.

The Transatlantic Alliance of Grievance and the Crisis of Truth

The most dangerous development is the emerging synergy between the American MAGA (Make America Great Again) movement and European populists. We are seeing a "Transatlantic Far-Right Alliance" where figures like Donald Trump, Elon Musk, and Nigel Farage synchronize their messaging. They use platforms like X (formerly Twitter) to bypass traditional media, creating a feedback loop of misinformation that frames billionaires as the "true representatives" of the working class. This is a profound paradox: the world’s wealthiest men are convincing voters that they are the victims of the "elites."

This alliance is systematically weakening the ties that bind democratic nations. When Elon Musk uses his platform to amplify misogyny or anti-immigrant rhetoric, he provides the digital infrastructure for parties like Vox or the AfD to thrive. The result is a global environment where "good populism" is being rebranded as a legitimate alternative to the rule of law. As South Korea looks toward its future, it must recognize that the stability of the international order—which protects Korean trade and security—is being hollowed out from within by these actors.

What to Watch Next:

  • The Farage Investigation: Whether the $6.4 million donation probe leads to criminal charges will determine if Reform UK can survive its own growth.
  • French Presidential Polling: Watch if Zemmour’s radicalism forces Marine Le Pen to move even further right to protect her base, effectively ending the "centrist" era of French politics.
  • The AfD Data Leak: German authorities are scrutinizing the party's finances; any evidence of Russian funding could lead to a constitutional crisis or a party ban.
  • Korean Immigration Policy: How the Lee Jae-myung administration responds to the "Japanese Warning" will be crucial. Will Korea double down on multicultural education, or will it see a similar rise in youth-led exclusion?

The rise of the global far right is not a series of unfortunate coincidences. It is a systematic reaction to a world where many feel left behind. For the Lee Jae-myung administration and the people of South Korea, these trends are a mirror. They show us that democracy is not a permanent state, but a fragile agreement that must be defended against the seductive simplicity of hate. As we watch the flares burn in Warsaw and the polls shift in London, the question is no longer *if* the world is changing, but whether the foundations of our own democracy are strong enough to withstand the coming storm.

Editor's Note on Korean Politics

The current administration is led by President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea. Following the impeachment and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative party, currently serves as the primary opposition. This domestic stability stands in contrast to the volatility currently observed in the European and North American right-wing movements discussed above.
