Cracks in the Coalition: The Battle for the Soul of South Korea’s Progressive Ruling Bloc

November 09, 2026

For an outside observer looking at South Korean politics today, the progressive movement appears to have secured decisive institutional dominance. Following the impeachment and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, President Lee Jae-myung assumed the presidency, returning the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) to executive power while the conservative People Power Party scrambled to regroup from opposition benches. Yet inside the liberal grassroots, the prevailing mood is not celebratory triumph, but a claustrophobic tension. Korean political discourse often describes this suffocating feeling as having eaten "hundreds of dry sweet potatoes without water."

Despite holding the executive branch, liberal organizers and commentators find themselves locked in bitter factional infighting. A fierce debate is underway between those who view the presidency as the culmination of a decades-long mainstream liberal tradition and insurgent hardliners attempting to purge past icons in favor of ideological purity. Understanding this internal friction provides critical insight into how President Lee’s administration governs, how its grassroots base remains mobilized, and why the progressive bloc believes its current political unease is merely a preparatory stage before a broader reckoning with entrenched establishment forces.

The Rift Within: Factional Purity and the "New Lee Jae-myung" Phenomenon

At the center of the current friction is a vocal faction that labels itself "New Lee Jae-myung." Rather than seeking broad coalition-building to consolidate governing power, elements within this tendency have adopted an exclusionary stance that alarms traditional party loyalists. Grassroots anger has focused heavily on figures such as political commentator and broadcaster Oh Yoon-hye, who sparked fierce controversy by demanding that supporters who advocate for institutional solidarity or eventual merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party—the progressive minor party established by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk—pack up and leave the movement altogether.

Critics within the progressive base characterize such rhetoric as political gaslighting. By drawing rigid boundaries and demanding absolute fealty to an isolated brand of personal allegiance, these sectarian influencers risk isolating President Lee rather than safeguarding his administration. In the eyes of mainstream strategists, building defensive fortresses that declare anyone not entirely pure an enemy does not protect the ruling party; it merely benefits conservative remnants still aligned with the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and entrenched interests seeking to exploit divisions on the left.

Compounding this factional strain is a perceived deficit in intellectual leadership. Robust political movements typically rely not merely on passionate digital fandoms, but on respected public intellectuals who can articulate coherent governing philosophy—a role previously played by figures such as author and former health minister Rhyu Si-min. Within the "New Lee Jae-myung" circle, theoretical legitimacy has instead been spearheaded by figures like Ham Don-gyun and Kim Gap-soo. Mainstream critics argue that their commentaries prioritize emotional vitriol and divisive sorting over analytical depth, leaving the faction vulnerable to charges of posturing without institutional or popular breadth.

Defending Party Lineage: From Roh Moo-hyun to Lee Jae-myung

The substantive fight inside the ruling bloc is fundamentally about historical legitimacy. The Democratic Party of Korea is not a political vehicle born yesterday; its modern identity rests on a continuous, hard-fought progression. It traces its ideological soul to the participatory democracy championed by the late President Roh Moo-hyun, through the diplomatic and institutional dignity of former President Moon Jae-in, the strategic stewardship of party elder and former Prime Minister Lee Hae-chan, and finally into the policy-driven executive drive of President Lee Jae-myung.

Attempts by hardline purists to erase Roh, Moon, and Lee Hae-chan from the Democratic Party's core identity in order to elevate an isolated cult of personality are not just ahistorical; they threaten to tear apart the coalition that brought this administration to power.

Mainstream organizers maintain that over 90 percent of the progressive base still instinctively rallies around this four-part lineage. The loud demands to discard previous party titans represent a fringe single-digit percentage of the camp whose volume is amplified primarily by digital algorithms. When the legacy of Roh’s civic passion, Moon’s institutional steady hand, Lee Hae-chan’s political discipline, and Lee Jae-myung’s transformative agenda operate as an integrated whole, the ruling coalition enjoys an overwhelming numerical and cultural majority.

This commitment to solidarity was brought to a flashpoint by recent investigative actions directed against Lee Jong-won, the prominent head of the progressive political commentary channel Sisa Tapa. The execution of search-and-seizure operations against Lee sparked outrage among progressive street activists. Lee had spent months organizing direct street resistance during the harshest periods of state pressure under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure. For the base, the use of state apparatuses and investigative pressure against prominent progressive media figures evokes dark memories of past authoritarian crackdowns. Grassroots activists argue that attempting to intimidate movement veterans who openly stood their ground against Mr. Yoon's administration will only consolidate resistance and accelerate public pushback.

The Politics of Timing: Why Strategic Patience Trumps Premature Action

Faced with internal provocations and the lingering footprint of conservative institutional power, many liberal supporters are demanding immediate, dramatic mobilization. However, veteran organizers are counseling strict strategic discipline, turning to an unvarnished medical metaphor: you cannot pop a pimple until it turns yellow.

To strike at an inflamed situation before it is ripe yields only blood, scarring, and strategic failure. Politics, progressive strategists stress, is an art of structural timing. Forcing premature confrontations before political contradictions fully expose themselves hands opponents convenient justifications for counteroffensives. Instead, the current moment calls for energy conservation, quiet consolidation, and waiting for opposing factions to overplay their hands.

This perspective views the intense hostility from opposition elements not as a demonstration of strength, but as the desperate thrashing of an establishment backed into a corner. Having lost executive authority with the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, conservative hardliners find themselves politically depleted. Like cornered animals, their sudden spikes in aggression reflect vulnerability rather than a sustainable resurgence. Consequently, the ruling camp's tactical playbook relies on internal calm: rather than wasting political capital responding to daily skirmishes, the base is urged to remain patient until structural conditions make civic mobilization undeniably overwhelming.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will test whether South Korea's governing progressive bloc can maintain discipline while managing internal factional skirmishes. Observers monitoring the political landscape should track several concrete dynamics:

  • Coalition Dynamics with the Rebuilding Korea Party: Watch how DPK leadership responds to hardliners seeking to ostracize Cho Kuk’s party. Whether the two parties maintain strategic alignment or fall victim to territorial sniping will signal how securely President Lee holds his parliamentary flanks.
  • Investigative Pressure on Progressive Broadcasters: The fallout from the raid on Sisa Tapa’s Lee Jong-won will serve as a key barometer. If state investigative bodies expand scrutiny toward progressive civil society and independent media, expect grassroots outrage to transition rapidly from online forums into physical street demonstrations.
  • The Shift from "Dynamic Stillness" to Mobilization: The progressive strategy currently hinges on jeong-jung-dong (maintaining outward calm while gathering inner momentum). Watch for the specific political trigger—whether a legislative logjam, a judicial ruling concerning the remnants of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's faction, or a major cabinet reform push—that prompts organizers to call their disciplined majority back into public squares.

South Korea's ruling progressives are navigating the difficult transition from insurgent opposition to governing authority. While internal factions argue over ownership of the movement and purists try to prune the party's roots, the core constituency is choosing to bide its time, confident that its strategic depth will prevail once the decisive moment arrives.
