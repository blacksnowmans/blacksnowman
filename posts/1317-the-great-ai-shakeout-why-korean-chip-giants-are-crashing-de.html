The Great AI Shakeout: Why Korean Chip Giants Are Crashing Despite Record Profits

October 19, 2026

For traders watching the screens at Yeouido, Seoul’s financial district, the past few sessions have felt less like an orderly market retreat and more like panic in dense fog. South Korea’s benchmark tech powerhouses—Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, which together anchor the country’s export-driven economy and dominate global memory supply—have suffered punishing price swings. International investors have watched Seoul’s semiconductor sector absorb massive sell orders, mirroring a steep sell-off across New York and Taipei. Yet looking beneath the panic reveals an unmistakable paradox: while stock prices crumble under speculative short selling and macro anxiety, the physical memory chip market is entering one of its tightest supply squeezes in years.

To understand what is happening on the Korea Exchange (KRX), international investors must look beyond simple market sentiment. The current drop is not driven by a collapse in operational fundamentals. Instead, it is being shaped by cross-border liquidity distortions, aggressive short-selling by global macro hedge funds, and an intensifying debate over whether the hundreds of billions of dollars poured into artificial intelligence infrastructure will yield real corporate earnings.

The Wall Street Spark and the 'Neo-Cloud' Credit Scare

The immediate catalyst for the downturn did not originate in Suwon or Icheon, where Korea’s fabrication lines run around the clock, but on Wall Street. Tech-heavy benchmarks took a severe beating as the Nasdaq slid 2.14% and the S&P 500 lost 1.09%. At the heart of the drop was a sudden crack in the so-called "neo-cloud" sector—specialized cloud infrastructure providers that leveraged billions of dollars in private credit to stockpile Nvidia graphic processing units (GPUs).

High-flying names like CoreWeave tumbled 11.91%, while Nebius plunged 12.18%. For months, these specialized GPU cloud operators stood as the standard-bearers of unabashed AI optimism. When their shares cratered, the market jumped to a grim conclusion: if GPU renters cannot fill their capacity profitably, the entire AI capital expenditure cycle could stall. Compounding the anxiety, the US 10-year Treasury yield spiked to 5.061%, West Texas Intermediate crude surged into the $98-per-barrel range, and the Korean won weakened to roughly 1,335 won per US dollar. In a high-interest environment, market participants are suddenly laser-focused on 2026, when vast volumes of corporate debt issued during the low-rate era must be rolled over at punishing yields. If AI revenues fail to materialize, can debt-laden infrastructure operators service their loans?

Yet this narrative overlooks a critical sense of proportion. These specialized neo-cloud firms account for less than 10% of total AI data center capacity; the overwhelming bulk remains anchored by deep-pocketed hyperscalers such as Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta. Furthermore, concrete monetization is beginning to emerge. Anthropic recently delivered preliminary second-quarter revenue of $11.5 billion—a more than fourteen-fold increase year-over-year—marking its first turn to operating profitability on surging enterprise adoption of its Claude models and coding application programming interfaces (APIs).

While cautious studies from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and Boston Consulting Group (BCG) observe that 95% of organizations investing in generative AI have yet to turn a clear profit, historical transitions of this scale rarely monetize instantaneously. Long-range industry projections still indicate worldwide AI-related spending could surge 47% year-over-year to $2.6 trillion by 2026. The infrastructure buildout is not an illusion; rather, markets are stumbling over the mismatch between short-term payback expectations and multi-year architectural buildouts.

Seoul’s Chip Giants Caught in the Flow: Samsung vs. SK Hynix

When global sentiment curdles, South Korea’s equity market often serves as an international automated teller machine. Foreign funds dumped roughly 6.2 trillion won (about $4.64 billion) across the broader market over two consecutive sessions, directing an astonishing 87% of that selling volume straight into the semiconductor sector. Yet a closer look at the order book reveals a stark, telling divergence between South Korea's two memory champions.

During the rout, foreign institutional investors were net buyers of 181.8 billion won (around $136 million) worth of Samsung Electronics shares, even as they aggressively offloaded 74.3 billion won (approximately $55.6 million) of SK Hynix. This divergence highlights how institutional desks are navigating valuation floors:

  • Samsung Electronics: The stock slid toward 202,000 won before clawing its way back above 204,000 won. It remains above its late-July low of 189,200 won and trades at approximately 1.0 times its price-to-book ratio (PBR). For long-term capital, buying Samsung at book value has historically represented one of the most reliable asymmetric risk-reward entries on the Korean exchange. Foreign institutions appear to be hedging via index futures while quietly scooping up Samsung's physical common shares at rock-bottom valuations.
  • SK Hynix: As the undisputed leader in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) supply to top-tier AI accelerators, SK Hynix bore the brunt of rapid profit-taking. Measured from its recent cyclical highs, the stock experienced a Fibonacci retracement exceeding 68%. This severe technical drawdown occurred despite projections that the firm is on track to post record-breaking third-quarter operating profits.

This decoupling of stock valuations from physical operations has left equity analysts baffled. Industry data shows third-quarter fixed contract prices for conventional DRAM rising between 18% and 22% quarter-on-quarter, while NAND flash memory benchmark prices jumped more than 15%. Projected combined annual operating profit for Samsung and SK Hynix approaches an eye-watering 190 trillion won (about $142 billion). Enterprise server memory shortages are looming, not easing. The plunge on the KRX is therefore not an industrial forecast of falling chip orders, but a liquidity squeeze amplified by short sellers.

This distortion mirrors the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX), which sank 3.83% to 11,137.3, marking a seven-day losing streak that pushed the index down 24% from its 52-week peak. Heavily weighted components like Micron (-5.63%), AMD (-5.95%), and Intel (-5.79%) were dismantled, even while Nvidia held comparatively resilient at -1.43%. Global short hedge funds—which have reportedly extracted $24 billion in profits from software and tech shorts this year alone—are executing the fastest reduction in aggregate tech exposure recorded in a decade. When institutional players dump index baskets indiscriminately, high-beta Korean tech inevitably suffers collateral damage.

Defensive Havens and Mid-Cap Decoupling on the Korean Bourse

While large-cap tech suffered under foreign institutional outflows, smart money on the local bourse shifted into distinct counter-trend plays. Rather than abandoning Korean equities altogether, domestic institutions and savvy retail capital rotated toward either ultra-defensive cash cows or mid-caps backed by tangible idiosyncratic catalysts.

Traditional defensive counters like tobacco giant KT&G and beverage brewer HiteJinro saw stabilizing interest, but the most striking moves emerged among deeply undervalued industrial suppliers and specialty healthcare players:

  • PHA (043730): The automotive door module and parts supplier demonstrated remarkable downside resilience, backed by an astonishingly cheap valuation of 0.27 times book value and a price-to-earnings ratio (PER) of just 5.4. A recent board resolution authorizing share repurchases and cancellations provided a structural backstop. It reflects a wider, unappreciated pocket of the Korean market: automotive component makers like SJC Sejong (Sejong Industrial) trading at price-to-earnings multiples as compressed as 1.7.
  • L&C Bio (290650): The regenerative biomaterials company bucked the market trend on concrete progress regarding its MegaCartilage product lines and entry into the United States market through its Lituo joint initiatives, validating its aggressive facility expansions and fundraising maneuvers.
  • Curiox Biosystems: Defying the market rout entirely, this automated bio-cell washing and analysis equipment maker surged over 5%. Having transitioned from speculative promise to actual product placements across tier-one multinational pharmaceutical corporations, Curiox attracted institutional accumulation based on visible top-line revenue acceleration, despite current net losses.

The Real Hurdle: Shareholder Value and the Solidigm Factor

There is, however, an internal corporate governance issue weighing on local investor sentiment, particularly regarding SK Hynix: the handling of its US subsidiary, Solidigm (formerly Intel’s NAND memory business). Speculation surrounding a potential US listing of Solidigm has triggered sharp backlash among domestic minority shareholders. While investors understand the need to navigate geopolitical tensions and US industrial mandates, there is legitimate concern that an offshore listing risks double-counting corporate assets while diluting the parent company’s enterprise value.

Without a transparent commitment outlining how listing windfalls or capital raised will flow back to SK Hynix shareholders—via stepped-up dividends or stock cancellations—retail and domestic institutional investors remain wary of potential holding-company discounts. Korea’s investment landscape is changing; domestic shareholders are far more financially sophisticated than in past cycles and are demanding that capital allocation policies respect minority interests.

"Technological leadership in memory silicon means little if capital allocation and shareholder governance remain stuck in the past. To dismantle the chronic 'Korea Discount,' management must reward the capital that sustained them through brutal downcycles."

Korean market participants often point to reforms in neighboring Tokyo, where listed firms have expanded total shareholder return programs, introducing flexible dividend mechanics and aggressive share retirement programs. In South Korea, where the domestic economy continues to navigate stabilization efforts under the Lee Jae-myung administration following the turbulence and economic mismanagement of Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, corporate governance reform has taken center stage. If champions like SK Hynix wish to trade at the high multiples enjoyed by their Silicon Valley peers, resolving questions around Solidigm's roadmap through decisive shareholder-friendly measures will be critical.

What to Watch Next

For international investors navigating this volatility, several concrete inflection points will signal whether the semiconductor correction has reached its bottom:

  • US Hyperscaler Earnings Guidance: Imminent updates from major cloud hyperscalers—most notably Meta—will reveal whether real capital expenditure allocations are holding steady or softening for 2025 and 2026.
  • DRAM Contract Price Finalization: Investors should track the finalized fourth-quarter fixed transaction prices for enterprise server DRAM and high-density NAND. Continued price increases in the high single to double digits will definitively expose the equity market sell-off as a sentiment-driven divergence.
  • South Korean Margin Debt Cleanout: A noticeable contraction in retail margin debt balances (credit transaction balances) on the KRX will confirm that forced liquidations have subsided, historically marking cyclical entry points.
  • Samsung’s Technical PBR Support: Watch whether Samsung Electronics maintains solid support above its 1.0x book valuation floor (around 190,000 to 200,000 won), coupled with sustained stabilization on the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index.

The current turbulence across Korea’s semiconductor complex is steep and psychologically grueling, but it is driven by algorithmic positioning and hedge fund risk reductions rather than broken assembly lines. As long as the AI data center buildout continues to demand record volumes of advanced silicon, the operational foundation of Korea's memory champions remains firmly intact.
