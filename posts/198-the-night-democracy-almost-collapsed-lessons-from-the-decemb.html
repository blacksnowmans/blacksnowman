The Night Democracy Almost Collapsed: Lessons from the December 3 Martial Law Hearings

September 05, 2026

Watching the recent court proceedings, one cannot help but echo the sentiment that became a national cry of disbelief: "Is this even a country anymore?" As testimony unfolds regarding the events of December 3, 2024, the details emerging feel less like governance and more like a grim piece of black comedy. The former president’s references to "internal enemies" laid bare a superficial and dangerous understanding of the state—not as a constitutional democracy to be protected, but as an instrument to be wielded against political opponents. What we are witnessing in these courtrooms is not a record of calculated strategy, but a chilling, amateurish documentation of institutional insanity.

The False Pretext of a "National Crisis"

The witnesses in court have repeatedly cited a "national crisis" as the justification for the attempted martial law declaration. To hear them claim that the opposition party’s attempt to impeach the head of the Board of Audit and Inspection—South Korea’s independent state watchdog—constituted a national collapse is as absurd as it is offensive.

Under Article 77 of the South Korean Constitution, a president may declare martial law only during a state of extreme national emergency. However, such a declaration carries legitimacy only when it adheres to strict constitutional requirements and procedural checks. Using the term "internal enemy" to describe political opposition is the hallmark of a military coup, not a democratic exercise. As the Constitutional Court of South Korea confirmed in its April 2025 ruling that removed the president from office, the declaration failed to meet even the most basic substantive requirements, rendering it a clear act of insurrection against the constitutional order.

Who Authored the Decree?

Perhaps the most baffling element is the origin of "Martial Law Decree No. 1." The former president reportedly accepted documents from the Minister of National Defense—the head of South Korea’s military apparatus—without proper vetting or inter-agency consultation. The admission that the country's fate was placed in the hands of a military-background minister without discussion with the Minister of Justice or the Senior Secretary for Civil Affairs is evidence of a state being run like a private fiefdom.

The Constitutional Court’s 2025 ruling explicitly pointed out that declaring martial law and appointing a martial law commander require deliberation by the Cabinet. By bypassing these essential democratic guardrails, the administration effectively turned the military into an instrument for political suppression. It raises a haunting question: can an action driven by such impulsive, unchecked power truly be called a presidential decision?

🔥 The Scars of an Unconstitutional Gamble

Those who hold power should fear one thing above all: the legal and moral bankruptcy they create for themselves. The attempt to place civilians under the jurisdiction of military courts was a desperate, transparent strategy to neutralize the National Assembly. It remains a stain on the nation's history that such a plan was ever considered.

The Shadows of Power: First Lady Involvement

Testimony surrounding the role of First Lady Kim Keon-hee adds another layer of intrigue. Witnesses have attempted to deflect questions about her presence during key meetings, dismissing them as mere administrative formalities. Yet, when confronted with leaked Telegram messages, witnesses cryptically noted that the content was "not something a typical housewife would write"—a phrasing that only deepens the suspicion of shadow influence.

This pattern—where the core of state power operates through non-official, opaque communication channels rather than established systems—has been an exhausting, recurring theme in Korean politics over the past few years. Furthermore, the testimony from officials like Minister of the Interior and Safety Park Sung-jae, who claimed he did not recall attempts to dissuade the president, reads like a cowardly evasion of responsibility. When Cabinet members are treated as mere window dressing for a leader's arbitrary decisions, the democratic decision-making process is not just strained; it is obliterated.

The "I Don't Remember" Defense

In a courtroom, "I don't remember" is the universal shorthand for "I don't want to take responsibility." While the architects of the December 3 incident try to dress their actions in the language of concern for the economy and foreign policy, they conveniently ignore how their attempted coup nearly triggered an absolute economic and diplomatic catastrophe.

They claim they did not order investigations into the National Election Commission, yet they simultaneously justify the mobilization of the Defense Security Command and the Cyber Operations Command as "security inspections." This is deception at its peak. We remember the chill of that night in December—when tanks were reportedly seen near the National Assembly. It was a visceral reminder of how fragile our democracy remains, even in a highly developed, modern society.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The declaration of emergency martial law was a legally void, unconstitutional act of power.
  • The failure to follow legal procedures in issuing the martial law decree proves the collapse of internal governance.
  • Suspicions regarding the influence of unelected figures in the highest echelons of power remain unresolved.

Final Thoughts: Why We Must Remember

As a citizen, it is deeply bitter to watch these figures package their actions as "resolute decisions." We expected a functioning, common-sense society, but instead, we saw the highest authority in the land attempt to use military force to bypass the legislature—a scene reminiscent of the tragic, authoritarian eras of the 20th century. The fact that the procedural democracy and the spirit of dialogue, which so many fought to establish, were treated with such flippancy is a tragedy.

History is often said to be written by those who remember, but the process of remembering here is painful and humiliating. We must keep this record not because it is pleasant, but because power is fleeting, yet the scars left by the abuse of power last for generations. The most concrete thing to watch moving forward is how the judicial system handles the remaining trials of those involved. Will they be held accountable for the institutional trauma they inflicted, or will the "I don't remember" defense continue to hollow out our justice system?
