When a High-Ranking Official Peddles Conspiracy Theories: The Kim Jin-ae Yellow Controversy

September 16, 2026

Language carries weight, especially when it comes from someone holding high public office rather than an anonymous online commentator or a casual political pundit. South Korea's political discourse has recently been shaken by remarks from Kim Jin-ae, head of the Presidential Commission on Architecture Policy. Spotting citizens wearing yellow shirts—a color historically freighted with deep political meaning in the progressive camp—she casually dismissed them not as fellow citizens, but as "Shincheonji yellow," referencing a notorious religious cult. Later, when a prominent progressive politician's approval ratings outpaced her expectations, she wrote it off not as shifting voter sentiment, but as "organized manipulation" or an "abnormal phenomenon."

This incident exposes something much darker than a mere foot-in-mouth moment. It reveals how baseless conspiracy theories and lazy identity politics have penetrated South Korea's public sphere, replacing reasoned debate with paranoia. When figures tasked with advising the state on urban planning and public space start treating their fellow citizens like subversives based purely on their choice of clothing, it is time to ask what has happened to the basic tenets of democratic coexistence.

How Yellow Became a Suspect Color

To understand the absurdity of Kim’s remark, one has to understand the symbolic landscape of South Korean progressive politics. For decades, yellow has been inextricably linked to Roh Moo-hyun, the late, revered progressive president and the original base of grassroots supporters known as *Nosamo*. To those who cherish Roh’s legacy, wearing yellow is an act of solidarity and historical pride. Yet, in a broadcast appearance, Kim claimed, "That yellow... it's not the yellow of Nosamo. It's the yellow of Shincheonji."

This is a calculated act of ideological branding. Without a shred of evidence—no church membership rolls, no internal party registry leaks—Kim weaponized the name of a fringe religious sect against ordinary citizens simply because their political enthusiasm made her uneasy. When a high-ranking official labels everyday citizens as potential cult members just because they wear a certain color, it is an extraordinary display of institutional arrogance. It sets a dangerous precedent where anyone displaying the "wrong" color in public can be quietly marked as a threat to the social order.

Worse still is the cowardice of the retraction dance. When public backlash inevitably arrived, Kim reportedly retreated behind the classic rumor-mongering shield: "Well, that's just what people are saying." This is the ultimate form of political straw-manning. You drop a definitive smear to your audience, and when questioned, you hide behind anonymous hearsay. Public servants are meant to anchor public discussions in facts, not act as amplifiers for neighborhood gossip and groundless panic.

The Trap of the "Normal" Approval Rating

The second pillar of this controversy centers on how Kim viewed approval numbers for Rep. Chung Cheong-rae, a polarizing figure in the liberal Democratic Party. Commenting on the numbers during a media appearance, Kim asserted that "for someone like Chung Cheong-rae, getting around 20% is normal."

There is a terrifying authoritarian streak hidden inside that word: "normal." In Kim's view, reality has a pre-approved quota. If a politician polls at 20%, the universe is in balance. If that same politician hits 40%, the universe is broken, and dark forces must be at work. Instead of treating an unexpected surge as a signal to re-examine her own assumptions about the electorate, she chose to invalidate the voters themselves. The logic is chillingly circular: my prediction is infallible, therefore any reality that contradicts it must be an engineered anomaly.

A serious political analyst looks at a surprise result and asks, "Where did I misread the room?" An ideologue looks at a surprise result and declares the voters illegitimate. By suggesting that a higher-than-expected approval rating must be the product of "organized manipulation," Kim effectively stripped thousands of citizens of their political agency. Democracy does not require voters to conform to elite expectations; it requires elites to respect the unpredictable, messy reality of the ballot box.

Spatial Democracy and the Poverty of Empathy

What makes Kim’s remarks particularly jarring is her background. As an urban architect and the head of the presidential architecture commission, Kim has long championed the concept of "spatial democracy"—the idea that physical environments should foster openness, community, and authenticity. She has famously critiqued high-profile public structures for lacking "authenticity" and failing to serve the public good.

Yet her political utterances betray a profound lack of spatial and social empathy. True spatial democracy is about learning to coexist with strangers in shared environments—whether they wear yellow, blue, or no political colors at all. If an architect of public policy views the public square as a battlefield infested with hidden enemies, no amount of structural design can save the civic fabric. You cannot build inclusive cities while harboring exclusive, paranoid attitudes toward your fellow citizens.

The lesson here extends far beyond one official's misstep. Across South Korea's political spectrum, factions increasingly demand ideological purity, turning anyone who steps outside narrow internal boundaries into a traitor, an outsider, or a cultist. This exclusionary tribalism poisons internal party debate, replacing policy discussions with mutual suspicion.

What to Watch Next

    Establish accountability standards: Watch whether public officials face actual consequences for peddling unsubstantiated smears, or if institutional insulation protects them from retracting and moving on. The tone of progressive factions: Observe whether mainstream liberal politicians distance themselves from exclusionary "purity tests" or continue to embrace conspiracy-driven rhetoric ahead of upcoming political cycles. Civil society pushback: Look for how civic groups and everyday voters respond when officials attempt to criminalize or pathologize basic forms of political expression, such as clothing choices or unexpected poll numbers.

Democracy survives not because everyone agrees, but because enough people maintain a stubborn willingness to admit they might be wrong. Until South Korea's political class learns to treat differing voters as legitimate fellow citizens rather than covert threats, the public square will remain a place of noise, suspicion, and manufactured fear.
