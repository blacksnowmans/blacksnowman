South Korea's Ruling Party Fights Falling Approval and Distracting Scandals

October 12, 2026

Morning routines in Seoul often begin with a glance at the news feed, where the political landscape currently resembles a turbulent storm. South Korea's annual parliamentary audit season is in full swing, turning clashes between rival political parties increasingly bitter. At the epicenter of this political turbulence is the Democratic Party, the liberal ruling party currently backing the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. For the party's leadership, day-to-day governance has become an uphill battle.

Recent headlines paint a stark picture: a sliding public approval rating that the leadership is desperately trying to reverse, paired with a relentless barrage of political crossfire between rival camps. To understand why this matters, it helps to look past the superficial noise of Seoul's political arena and examine the structural pressures facing South Korea's ruling bloc.

The Battle for Public Trust Amid Slipping Poll Numbers

The Democratic Party leadership, led by Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok, is facing fierce political headwinds. The party's public approval rating has tumbled to its lowest level since the current administration took office, sending shockwaves through party headquarters. Kim had previously set an ambitious target of boosting public support by 10 percentage points within 100 days, but reality has proven stubbornly difficult to bend.

Recent polling indicators hover stubbornly around the 35% mark, showing little sign of a breakout. In response, party strategists have pivoted back to the basics: grassroots engagement. Stepping away from grand political rhetoric in Seoul, leadership figures have fanned out into regional districts like Iksan and Jeonju in North Jeolla Province, holding local town halls to pitch key regional energy infrastructure and mega-projects.

Yet, the core question remains whether the drop in popularity stems merely from poor public relations. Voters desperately want the ruling party to focus on livelihood legislation and social harmony, but daily political detours consistently block the path.

Beneath the central leadership's struggles, regional party chapters are scrambling to prepare for upcoming local elections and handle pressing local issues. Jeonju organizers have overhauled local leadership committees, while Gyeonggi Province youth representatives have fanned out to engage young voters. Yet these localized efforts are frequently overshadowed by internal friction. In North Chungcheong Province, local party members have engaged in public legal disputes against one another, deepening internal factionalism. Meanwhile, subtle rifts have emerged between the mainstream leadership and party hardliners—most notably when lawmaker Kim Yong-min publicly pushed back against President Lee Jae-myung's messaging on social media, exposing palpable tension within the ruling camp.

Tit-for-Tat Scandals and the Politics of Distraction

If policy debates are the lifeblood of a healthy democracy, modern South Korean politics has lately survived on junk food. The political arena recently erupted into a full-scale "children's proxy warfare," with rival parties trading blows over the alleged academic credentials of their political offspring.

The Democratic Party trained its guns on Han Dong-hoon, the former leader of the conservative People Power Party, demanding an immediate re-investigation into allegations that a ghostwriter penned an academic paper for Han's daughter. Han, refusing to yield, quickly fired back by dragging the children of Democratic lawmakers Kim Min-seok and Choo Mi-ae into the spotlight.

This descent into vetting each other's family trees and dredging up historical controversies generates deep fatigue among the public. While the Democratic Party frames these investigations as a form of accountable politics, public sentiment is far from forgiving. While politicians spend their time digging for dirt on rivals, crucial livelihood legislation slated for the parliamentary audit is consistently shoved to the back burner.

Diplomatic and security fronts have proven equally toxic to bipartisan cooperation. A delay in the U.S. visa issuance for senior ruling-party lawmaker Jung Chung-rae triggered a fierce offensive from the People Power Party, which branded the incident a fracture in the South Korea-U.S. alliance and proof of diplomatic incompetence by the government. The ruling party countered by defending Jung and arguing that a key ally ought to respect parliamentary duties.

Simultaneously, conservative lawmaker Na Kyung-won faced heavy fire from the ruling bloc—with critics comparing her actions to historical collaborators and labeling them diplomatic self-harm—after sending a letter of appreciation to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Turning foreign policy into domestic political ammunition during a time of rapidly shifting geopolitical risks is a dangerous game. The old adage that foreign policy and national security should transcend partisan politics has long become an unaffordable luxury in Seoul.

Judicial Reform Battles and the Livelihood Legislation Pushed Aside

As the parliamentary audit entered its second week, the Legislation and Judiciary Committee turned into a pressure cooker. Elements of the Democratic Party and progressive circles stepped up their campaign against the judiciary, demanding the voluntary resignation or immediate impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Reform-minded lawmakers like Kim Yong-min made it clear they would push judicial reform forward, even at the cost of their own political seats.

This push for institutional overhaul—spanning prosecution reform and the reappointment of Supreme Court justices—is a core pillar of the Democratic Party's political identity. The conservative opposition has framed these moves as part of a catastrophic policy failure designed to undermine the current administration, while the ruling party insists that reforming historical prosecutorial overreach is non-negotiable. Walking this tightrope requires immense political dexterity to minimize destructive institutional collisions.

Yet, hidden beneath the noise of high-stakes political warfare, practical legislative discussions affecting ordinary citizens' daily lives continue to grind forward. Momentum is building inside the National Assembly for regulations on delivery app commission fees before the end of the year, offering a potential lifeline for struggling small business owners and self-employed merchants. Additional bills aimed at easing the tax burden on everyday workers have also surfaced.

Summary of Current Political Dynamics

  • Approval Slump: The Democratic Party is grappling with post-inauguration lows in public support, forcing the Kim Min-seok leadership to chase grassroots solutions.
  • Scandal Wars: Aggressive cross-examinations over family credentials between conservative figures and liberal lawmakers have paralyzed bipartisan cooperation.
  • Institutional Overhauls: Parliamentary audits are heavily focused on judicial and prosecutorial reforms, while delivery app regulations and livelihood bills move quietly through committees.

No matter how noisy politics gets, the public ultimately measures a government by whether their wallets and daily lives improve. To prove its identity as a "competent party of the people," the Democratic Party must deliver tangible results on livelihood legislation despite the distracting political crossfire. Bouncing back from an approval rating stuck near 35% requires practical policymaking that scratches where the public actually itches.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on whether the ruling party can successfully pass delivery app fee regulations before the legislative deadline, and watch how the Supreme Court audit unfolds as opposition parties continue their push for judicial restructuring.
