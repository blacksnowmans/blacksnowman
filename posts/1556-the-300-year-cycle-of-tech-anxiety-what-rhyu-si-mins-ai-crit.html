The 300-Year Cycle of Tech Anxiety: What Rhyu Si-min’s AI Critique Reveals About Korea’s Labor Future

October 27, 2026

Inside a South Korean logistics warehouse, a sleek humanoid robot lifts heavy shipping crates with effortless precision, while in corporate suites across Seoul, major conglomerates quietly trim their entry-level white-collar hiring. For decades, young Koreans followed a well-trodden path: graduate from a top university, pass the grueling open-recruitment examinations known as gongchae, and secure a desk job for life. Today, that social ladder is splintering. Generative artificial intelligence is summarizing legal contracts in seconds, writing code, and analyzing market trends faster than any junior associate. The sudden dread that machines will render human labor obsolete is palpable across the country.

Yet, according to Rhyu Si-min—one of South Korea’s most prominent liberal public intellectuals, a best-selling author, and former Minister of Health and Welfare—this panic misses the core issue. In recent public lectures analyzing the intersection of artificial intelligence and the job market, Rhyu argues that our current anxiety is not an unprecedented catastrophe but the latest chapter in a 300-year history of technological upheaval. The fundamental threat is not the code itself, but the mismatch between hyper-accelerated corporate technology and the outdated political structures of the modern nation-state.

The Phantom of Machine Replacement: From 19th-Century Weavers to Humanoid Factory Floors

The terror of being displaced by an algorithm feels terrifyingly modern, but its origins stretch back to the dawn of industrial capitalism. Three centuries ago, English textile workers smashed automated looms during the Luddite uprisings, desperate to protect their livelihoods from steam-powered machinery. Parliamentary reports from 19th-century London depict a harrowing picture of early industrialization: children as young as seven worked grueling shifts, and exhausted laborers slept suspended on ropes near factory floors just to return to the looms before dawn. Human beings were treated as disposable components in the drive for capital efficiency.

Societies did not survive that era by destroying machines, but by inventing new political and legal institutions. Laws restricting working hours, outlawing child labor, and establishing social safety nets gradually transformed industrial horrors into modern prosperity. Every major technological shift under capitalism has aimed to cut labor costs. When agricultural technology advanced, the global farming workforce plummeted from over 70 percent to less than 5 percent in developed economies today. Rather than creating permanent mass unemployment, that displacement created entirely new sectors—manufacturing, services, information technology, and creative industries.

Today, the scale of automation has shifted from muscle to mind. Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates recently made headlines by suggesting that societies might eventually need to designate 40 percent of jobs as "human-only" to preserve employment stability. Meanwhile, humanoid robots are moving out of research labs and onto factory floors and loading docks. For workers watching automated limbs balance heavy cargo, the threat is concrete. Yet history suggests that demonizing technology confuses the tool with the system. The critical question remains how society redistributes the immense wealth generated by automated productivity.

Emergence and the Binary Brain: How AI Redefined Intellectual Monopoly

To grasp why generative AI feels so uniquely threatening, one must look at how it mirrors—and bypasses—the human brain. Natural human intelligence operates primarily through intuitive pattern recognition. A child does not calculate biological coordinates to distinguish a dog from a cat; after a few visual experiences, the brain’s neural network recognizes the pattern subconsciously. Seven decades ago, British mathematician Alan Turing theorized that a machine could replicate human-level intelligence if given just two components: an extraordinarily fast processing unit and a massive storage capacity capable of instant data retrieval.

Today’s large language models and deep-learning architectures are the realization of Turing’s prediction. By running complex algorithms across vast binary systems, AI simulates human neural connections. More importantly, when data accumulation reaches a critical mass, it triggers a phenomenon known in biology and system theory as "emergence"—a qualitative leap where entirely new, unprogrammed capabilities suddenly appear. Modern AI does not merely follow strict pre-programmed rules; it infers, synthesizes, drafts creative prose, and analyzes complex legal briefs without explicit human instruction.

Nowhere was this emergent power demonstrated more dramatically than on the traditional Go board. For centuries, professional Go players viewed early play in the "three-three" corner point as a fundamental mistake. When Google’s AlphaGo shattered human champions, it routinely opened with that exact move, proving it to be mathematically optimal. Today, human grandmasters no longer rely solely on centuries of refined intuition; they evaluate moves by checking AI win-rate graphs displayed on computer screens. The intellectual domain long considered the exclusive preserve of human intuition was restructured overnight by machine calculation.

The Vulnerable Professional Class and the Rise of Unchecked Tech Titans

The current wave of AI automation diverges sharply from past industrial revolutions in who it targets first. Research from the Bank of Korea—South Korea's central bank—estimates that approximately 3.41 million domestic jobs, representing 12 percent of the nation's total workforce, face high exposure to AI replacement. Globally, investment bank Goldman Sachs estimates that AI could automate the equivalent of 300 million full-time jobs. Crucially, these high-risk positions are concentrated among high-earning, highly educated professionals—data analysts, lawyers, accountants, and administrative specialists—who previously believed their expertise rendered them immune.

While white-collar desk jobs face rapid automation, physical hazard remains a stark reality in heavy industry. In South Korea, despite its status as a global tech powerhouse, approximately 2,000 workers die each year from industrial accidents, including machinery entrapment, falls, and electrocution, with total annual workplace injuries nearing 100,000. Logic dictates that humanoid robots and AI should be deployed first to dangerous dockyards and construction sites to save human lives. In practice, however, corporate incentives often prioritize eliminating white-collar entry-level positions to achieve immediate payroll reductions.

Compounding this imbalance is the concentration of power among non-elected tech leaders. A small elite of Silicon Valley executives and engineers now shapes the trajectory of global artificial intelligence, often guided by esoteric personal ideologies like "longtermism" or "effective altruism." Over 120 years ago, Czech playwright Karel Čapek coined the word "robot" in his play R.U.R. (Rossum’s Universal Robots), warning of artificial workers created for efficiency that ultimately escaped human ethical control. Allowing billionaire tech executives to dictate the future of human labor without democratic accountability risks repeating that literary warning in real time.

Outdated Nation-States and the Personal Blueprint for the AI Era

The broader structural challenge lies in the limits of our political governance. During the ancient Peloponnesian War, the Mediterranean world possessed the maritime technology and trade networks to build an integrated regional economy. Instead, rival city-states like Athens and Sparta dragged the region into destructive conflicts because their political imagination remained trapped inside local walls. Today, humanity faces global challenges—from artificial intelligence disruption to climate change—yet our decision-making remains confined to 19th-century nation-state frameworks.

In South Korea, public debate often fixates nervously on demographic projections showing the national population could halve over the next half-century. While low birth rates present genuine economic hurdles, obsessing over national headcounts ignores the much larger planetary and technological transformations underway. Borderless AI algorithms and automated supply chains do not respect national boundaries. Attempting to solve 21st-century technological shifts using outdated, insular national policy levers is a political mismatch.

For individuals navigating this transition, survival requires neither blind techno-optimism nor fatalistic panic. Outsourcing all intellectual effort to AI strips away the very process of learning and critical thought. Understanding complex geopolitical events or masterly texts requires the uncomfortable labor of reading, synthesizing, and writing—a process that builds human agency. As the labor market reshapes itself, individuals must maintain their role as original thinkers and designers rather than passive consumers of algorithmic output. Embracing authentic curiosity, cultivating uncopyable intellectual habits, and demanding democratic oversight of tech monopolies offer the clearest path forward in an automated world.
