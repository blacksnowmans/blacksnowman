Warning Lights in Seoul: Why President Lee Jae-myung Is Losing His Base

October 26, 2026

For months, the conventional wisdom inside South Korea's ruling camp was that its electoral foundation remained solid enough to weather economic headwinds and persistent political friction. That assumption evaporated in the final week of October. A series of weekly tracking polls delivered what political insiders in Seoul are describing as an unmistakable alarm: public disapproval of President Lee Jae-myung has decisively breached the 50 percent threshold, while the main conservative opposition has pulled off a dramatic polling upset against the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

The numbers represent far more than a routine polling fluctuation. The deterioration spans every critical pillar of the ruling coalition’s electoral coalition. Disapproval has crossed into majority territory not just nationwide, but in Honam—the southwestern Jeolla provinces long considered the emotional and electoral bedrock of South Korean liberalism. At the same time, centrist voters are turning away in droves, and progressive voters are defecting to smaller allied alternatives. Beneath the surface lies a compounding mix of frozen asset markets, paralysis inside the ruling party, and an unforced public relations debacle involving presidential aides and loose talk of martial law.

The Numbers Behind the Crack in the Progressive Stronghold

To grasp why these poll results have triggered such anxiety across the presidential office and the Democratic Party, one has to examine both the methodology and the demographic breakdown. In South Korea, polling agencies frequently deploy two distinct sampling methods: automated response system (ARS) robocalls, which are cheaper and capture highly partisan, highly motivated voters; and live telephone interviews, which cost more but typically reflect a broader, more nuanced cross-section of the public.

In the late October surveys, President Lee’s positive job evaluation dropped to 46.5 percent in ARS polling and sank even further to 41.2 percent in live telephone interviews. Meanwhile, negative evaluations surged to 52.8 percent in the ARS survey and a staggering 57.7 percent among live respondents. For a president who had previously maintained a defensive floor in the mid-to-late 40s, having nearly six in ten interview respondents rate his performance negatively signals that skepticism has hardened into consensus.

The demographic fissures are even more revealing:

  • The unraveling of Honam: In the southwestern Jeolla region, an area where Democratic candidates routinely expect overwhelming dominance, negative ratings of the president’s performance have now surpassed positive marks outside the statistical margin of error.
  • A fracturing progressive core: Among self-described liberal voters—a group historically viewed as "concrete" supporters—only 57.4 percent reported that President Lee is handling state affairs well. Fully 42.6 percent expressed negative views, meaning more than four in ten self-identified progressives have broken ranks with the administration.
  • The centrist flight: Among moderate and independent voters, negative evaluations now comfortably lead positive ones by 54 percent to 44 percent, mirroring the classic pattern of a mid-tenure governance crisis.

Historically, when South Korean voters abandon an incumbent administration, they often cite fatigue with tribal party warfare or cast a cynical plague on both major houses. This time, the drivers are far more tangible and personal.

Economic Stagnation and Self-Inflicted Wounds in the Presidential Office

At the root of the public's growing frustration is the freezing of South Korea’s asset markets. With both the housing sector and the domestic equity markets mired in a prolonged slump, household wealth has taken a direct hit. For middle-class and working-class families who expected tangible relief, the administration’s perceived inability to present effective macroeconomic solutions has transformed initial optimism into quiet resentment.

Compounding this economic malaise is an atmosphere of political amateurism emanating directly from the presidential office. The most damaging controversy in recent weeks erupted when members of the president's advisory team and close associates publicly floated rhetoric regarding "martial law" (gye-eom). In a country that endured decades of military authoritarianism before democratizing in the late 1980s, the mere mention of martial law invokes deep national trauma.

Whether those remarks reflected genuine deliberations within the inner circle or reckless, off-the-cuff political maneuvering remains unclear. However, the political fallout has been disastrous. Rather than swiftly containing the fallout, the presidential staff allowed the issue to metastasize across the news cycle, dominating domestic headlines and reinforcing the perception of a government that has lost its message discipline.

The contrast with past administrations has become an acute point of critique among political commentators. During the previous progressive administration of Moon Jae-in, the Office of the Senior Secretary for Civil Affairs exercised formidable, centralized control over the political and judicial agenda. Even during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure—prior to his historic impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment—figures like former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon exerted rigorous, top-down discipline across the executive branch. By comparison, President Lee’s current team is widely seen as lacking the political acumen and legal coordination required to shield the president from self-inflicted controversies.

The Realmeter Reversal and the Rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party

The erosion of presidential approval has now translated directly into a shift in partisan allegiances. In a late October joint survey conducted by the business daily Asia Economy and the polling firm Realmeter, party support figures diverged sharply depending on the polling technique, but pointed to a common trend.

In a standalone ARS robocall survey, the ruling Democratic Party still held a lead at 41.9 percent, compared to 36.7 percent for the conservative People Power Party and 6.7 percent for the Rebuilding Korea Party. However, in Realmeter's primary mixed-method poll—which combines automated calls with live interviewer data—the conservative People Power Party, the country's primary opposition force, surged to 42.6 percent. The ruling Democratic Party fell to 36.0 percent, handing the conservatives a lead well outside the margin of error, while the Rebuilding Korea Party registered 6.9 percent.

For a ruling party holding the presidency to surrender its lead to an opposition party still rebuilding after Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s catastrophic fall from power is an extraordinary setback. Yet the Democratic Party's losses have not simply flowed to the conservatives; they are also bleeding toward their own ideological flank.

The Rebuilding Korea Party, led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, has consistently captured between 8 and 9 percent in weekly surveys conducted by polling houses such as Poll Kkot and News Tomato. The party has effectively positioned itself as an outlet for disappointed progressive voters who view the Democratic Party leadership as tepid, overly cautious, and lacking the appetite for uncompromising political combat. By acting as a haven for alienated progressives, the Rebuilding Korea Party is exposing the ruling party's inability to keep its own coalition united.

Internal Fractures and What to Watch Next

Inside the Democratic Party, the reaction to these numbers has been marked by internal finger-pointing and strategic drift. Amid escalating legal risks surrounding key figures and intense friction over legislative priorities, veteran lawmaker Park Hong-geun recently floated the idea of launching a formal parliamentary investigation (gukjeong josa). The proposal is designed to allow the ruling party to tackle thorny political disputes frontally through the National Assembly while avoiding the dangerous escalation of an all-out impeachment clash.

Yet without a unified leadership cadre, tactical gambits like parliamentary probes risk falling flat. Party insiders have begun openly lamenting the lack of direction, with some privately describing the party's current posture as uncoordinated and chaotic. Caught between managing presidential controversies and fighting a resurging conservative opposition, the party has struggled to present a coherent narrative on the economy or governance.

Over the coming weeks, several concrete developments will determine whether this polling slump becomes a permanent crisis or a manageable mid-term correction:

  • A shakeup of the presidential staff: Observers will be watching closely to see if President Lee replaces key aides in the political affairs and communications offices to restore message discipline and purge the fallout from the martial law controversy.
  • Movement on the parliamentary investigation: Whether the Democratic Party can successfully rally its caucus behind Rep. Park Hong-geun’s proposed probe will show whether the party can seize the legislative initiative or remain trapped in defensive skirmishes.
  • Asset market interventions: With public discontent fundamentally anchored in economic anxiety, any fresh policy packages targeting real estate transactions and stock market liquidity will serve as the administration's primary tool to win back disaffected middle-class and centrist voters.
  • The progressive tug-of-war: Polling trends in Honam and the steady 8-to-9 percent baseline of the Rebuilding Korea Party will indicate whether the Democratic Party can reabsorb its disaffected base, or whether progressive fragmentation is becoming a permanent fixture of this administration.

In South Korea’s volatile democracy, public mandate is notoriously fluid. The electorate that lifted President Lee and the Democratic Party to power has delivered a decisive signal: without tangible economic results and steady political stewardship, even the most loyal voter strongholds will not wait indefinitely.
