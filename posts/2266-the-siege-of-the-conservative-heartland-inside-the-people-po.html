The Siege of the Conservative Heartland: Inside the People Power Party’s Budget Battle

November 12, 2026

On a chilly November afternoon in Daegu, the atmosphere inside the city hall was anything but cold. High-ranking officials from the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition party, had descended upon the city and its surrounding province, Gyeongsangbuk-do, for a high-stakes "Budget-Policy Consultation." To a casual observer, it might have looked like a routine administrative meeting. But for those following the shifting tectonic plates of Korean politics, it was a declaration of war—a "front-line struggle," as some insiders called it, to protect the financial lifeblood of the conservative heartland against the budget-tightening measures of President Lee Jae-myung’s administration.

The region, collectively known as "TK" (an abbreviation for Taegu and Kyongsang), has long been the spiritual and political fortress of the conservative movement. However, with the central government now firmly in the hands of the liberal Democratic Party, leaders in TK feel increasingly like they are living on a political island. On November 11, the People Power Party (PPP) leadership made it clear that they would not let their stronghold be starved of resources. From ambitious new airports to a massive administrative merger, the stakes involve more than just accounting; they involve the very survival of regional autonomy in an era of hyper-centralized power.

The Strategic Retreat to the TK Stronghold

The visit by the PPP leadership was a calculated move to consolidate their "home turf" ahead of upcoming local elections. Leading the delegation were Representative Jang Dong-hyeok, the party leader, and Jeong Jeom-sig, the party’s floor leader. Their mission was simple but daunting: ensure that the TK region remains a priority in the National Assembly’s upcoming budget review, despite facing a hostile executive branch.

The sense of urgency was most palpable in Gyeongsangbuk-do (often shortened to Gyeongbuk). During the meeting at 3:13 PM, Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sig listened as provincial officials proposed an unusual strategy: the creation of a dedicated "Budget Camp" specifically aimed at securing state funds for 2027. It is rare for a local government to start a formal, joint task force with an opposition party three years in advance. This move reflects a deep-seated fear that under President Lee Jae-myung, regional projects in conservative areas will be pushed to the bottom of the pile.

Governor Lee Cheol-woo of Gyeongbuk was blunt in his assessment. He didn't just ask for cooperation; he demanded a "one-team" spirit. For the PPP, acting as a "megaphone" in the National Assembly’s Special Committee on Budget and Accounts is the only way to counteract the central government's control over the purse strings. In the current political climate, where the ruling Democratic Party holds the presidency and a significant presence in the legislature, the PPP is positioning itself as the last line of defense for regional interests that do not align with the Blue House’s agenda.

Infrastructure as Survival: The New Airport and Regional Integration

At the center of this financial tug-of-war are two massive projects that TK leaders believe will determine the region’s fate for the next half-century: the Daegu-Gyeongbuk (TK) New Airport and the Administrative Integration Law. These aren't just construction projects; they are attempts to create a "second capital" region to rival the overcrowded Seoul metropolitan area.

The TK New Airport is a multi-billion dollar endeavor intended to replace the existing dual-use military and civilian airport in Daegu. During the consultations, the focus shifted toward making this a "state-led" project. Essentially, Daegu and Gyeongbuk are admitting that the scale of the project is too large for local governments to handle alone. They are calling for the central government to take full responsibility for its construction and operation. While the PPP leadership signaled their support, the path forward is fraught with obstacles. President Lee’s administration has emphasized fiscal responsibility and "balanced development," which often translates to skepticism toward massive infrastructure projects in opposition-held territories.

Then there is the issue of the "Administrative Integration Law." This proposed legislation would merge Daegu and Gyeongbuk into a single administrative entity, granting it unprecedented local authority. Governor Lee Cheol-woo argued that this is the "only alternative" to prevent regional extinction as populations migrate to Seoul. However, this requires the central government to voluntarily give up power—an unlikely prospect in the current zero-sum game of Korean politics. The PPP has promised to push for this in the National Assembly, but without the president’s signature, the dream of a "Mega-TK" remains on paper.

The "Lifeblood" Controversy: Local Taxes and Central Control

If the airport is the region's future, the "Local Allocation Tax" is its present. This tax is a mechanism where the central government collects revenue and redistributes it to local governments to bridge the gap between wealthy and poor regions. For provinces like Gyeongbuk, which have lower local tax bases, these transfers are essential for basic services—healthcare, road maintenance, and social welfare.

The Lee Jae-myung administration, citing a shortfall in national tax revenue and the need to manage national debt, has moved to cut these local allocations. The reaction in TK has been nothing short of explosive. PPP officials and local leaders have characterized these cuts as "an act of cutting the region’s lifeblood." This is where the budget battle becomes deeply personal for voters. When a rural bus route is canceled or a community center loses funding, the PPP points the finger directly at the president in Seoul.

"The government's wrongheaded budget must be corrected during the National Assembly's review process," Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sig declared. "We will not stand by while the future of our regions is stolen."

The PPP’s strategy is to use the budget review process as a "microscopic audit." They intend to identify and cut funding for the president’s signature policies—such as his "Universal Basic Income" initiatives or other programs they deem "populist"—and redirect those funds back to local governments. This sets the stage for a grueling winter in the National Assembly, where the national budget may become a hostage to these regional demands.

Political Symbolism and the Road to 2027

Beyond the spreadsheets and project lists, the visit to Daegu was about optics. By standing shoulder-to-shoulder with local leaders, the PPP leadership is trying to prove that they are still relevant. This is particularly important given the "marginalization" narrative that has taken hold in the region. Choo Kyung-ho, the Mayor of Daegu (per the current administrative lineup), used a striking metaphor during the meetings: he said Daegu feels like it is "outside the parentheses."

In Korean bureaucratic language, being "inside the parentheses" means you are included in the main text of a policy or budget; being outside means you are an afterthought or a footnote. Choo argued that projects that were gaining momentum during the previous era of Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi have lost all energy or seen their budgets slashed under the current administration. By framing the budget battle as a fight against "TK Passing" (the perceived intentional ignoring of the region), the PPP is tapping into a powerful sense of regional grievance.

However, this strategy carries risks. By focusing so heavily on their conservative base, the PPP risks being seen as a "regional party" rather than a national one. Voters in Seoul or the liberal Jeolla provinces might view the "budget camp" for Gyeongbuk as an unfair demand for special treatment. Party Leader Jang Dong-hyeok had to navigate these waters carefully during a 4:09 PM press briefing, insisting that the survival of TK is a matter of national survival, not just regional favoritism.

What to Watch Next

As the winter budget season begins, the rhetoric will likely sharpen. The People Power Party has made its priorities clear, but their ability to deliver on these promises is limited by their status as the minority in the executive branch. Here are the key milestones to watch for in the coming months:

  • The National Assembly Budget Deadlock: The legal deadline for the budget is early December. Expect the PPP to use every procedural tool available to stall the president’s "Basic Income" funds in exchange for TK infrastructure and local allocation tax restorations.
  • The Fate of the Administrative Integration Law: Watch for whether this bill even makes it to the floor. If the Democratic Party blocks it, the PPP will use it as a primary talking point for the next election cycle, painting the government as "anti-regional."
  • The New Airport Tussle: Keep an eye on the "state-led" designation. If the central government refuses to take the lead, the project could stall, leading to a massive political backlash in Daegu.
  • The Fiscal Data War: Both sides will soon release competing data on whether TK is actually being "shortchanged" or if the cuts are truly proportional across the country. The truth likely lies somewhere in the middle, but in the court of public opinion, the narrative of "neglect" is already winning in the southeast.

The "Budget-Policy Consultation" on November 11 was more than just a meeting; it was the opening bell of a long, expensive, and deeply partisan fight for the soul of the Korean provinces. For the people of Daegu and Gyeongbuk, the result of this struggle will determine whether their ambitious plans for the future take flight or remain stuck on the drawing board.
