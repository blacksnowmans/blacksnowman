A New White Paper Challenges the Official Orthodoxy on the Cheonan Sinking

November 10, 2026

In South Korean public life, few subjects carry as much political third-rail voltage as the sinking of the ROKS Cheonan. On a cold March night in 2010, the 1,200-ton Pohang-class corvette broke in two and sank near Baengnyeong Island in the Yellow Sea, claiming the lives of 46 South Korean sailors. The official multinational military investigation concluded that a North Korean midget submarine fired an acoustic homing torpedo, causing a non-contact underwater bubble-jet explosion that snapped the warship's hull. In Seoul's political mainstream, that finding became an article of faith. To question it was not simply to offer an alternative forensic hypothesis; it was treated as an act of ideological subversion or an apology for Pyongyang.

Sixteen years after the disaster, that orthodoxy faces an unusual challenge from paper and ink. Author Chae Soo-beom has released the Cheonan Citizens' White Paper, published by the independent press Ingan & Jayeonsa (Human & Natural History). More than 30 books have examined the sinking over the past decade and a half, but Chae's effort sets itself apart by its methodology. Rather than advancing speculative theories or polemics, the volume reconstructs the disaster almost entirely from thousands of pages of official trial records, witness depositions, and sworn testimonies generated through South Korea’s judicial system.

When an Open Inquiry Turned Into an Article of Faith

In his preface to the volume, Chae offers a bleak assessment: the Cheonan tragedy long ago ceased to be treated as a matter of science and evidence and instead "became a religion." In South Korea's intensely polarized political ecosystem, rational inquiry was supplanted by an ideological litmus test. Public debate collapsed into a binary trap where citizens were judged not by their command of facts, but by whether they "believed" or "did not believe" the government narrative.

Those who raised scientific anomalies or pressed for independent forensic reviews were routinely dismissed as heretics and smeared across the conservative press. Chae writes that he felt compelled to take up his pen out of an obligation to the next generation, arguing that a democratic society cannot allow state-sponsored conclusions to become inviolable dogmas immune to common-sense cross-examination.

Bringing the manuscript to light was itself an ordeal. Major South Korean publishing houses repeatedly turned the project down, citing fears of public backlash, conservative protests, and potential commercial repercussions. The manuscript was ultimately picked up by Ingan & Jayeonsa, an independent press willing to brave legal exposure and political hostility. Without that decision, the extensive trial transcripts and survivor depositions collected in the book would likely have remained buried in inaccessible court archives.

Court Records, Unanswered Questions, and Backchannel Diplomacy

The substantive heart of the white paper draws directly on courtroom testimony, piecing together a timeline that frequently diverges from the crisp narrative presented by the Ministry of National Defense (MND) at the time.

Among the most politically striking sections is the book's documentation of backchannel diplomacy. Even as the administration then in power adopted an unyielding public posture—declaring a virtual state of crisis and blaming Pyongyang—it was simultaneously attempting behind-the-scenes negotiations. The white paper details how South Korean officials reportedly attempted to offer envelopes of cash to North Korean counterparts during secret meetings to broker high-level talks, only to be rejected and publicly exposed by Pyongyang. For Chae, this episode illustrates how national security rhetoric was exploited for domestic political survival, masking deep institutional dysfunction behind a veneer of bellicose patriotism.

The trial transcripts also expose baffling operational choices in the immediate aftermath of the sinking:

  • Naval search anomalies and U.S. Navy drills: The white paper cites testimony and contemporaneous press accounts showing that during the critical "golden hour" when surviving crew members might have been rescued, the South Korean Navy's rescue coordination fell into confusion. Meanwhile, deployed U.S. Navy assets in the area engaged in their own rescue drills rather than being seamlessly integrated into the emergency survivor search.
  • The unidentified two-meter object: The volume revisits widely broadcast television news footage showing a military helicopter airlifting an unidentified, roughly two-meter-long object from the sea using a cable hoist. Despite being aired on national networks such as public broadcaster KBS, the object was omitted from the defense ministry's official inventory of salvaged debris, leaving open basic questions about what it was and why it was transported with such urgency.

The High Cost of Scrutiny and Administrative Retaliation

The book’s third major section charts what Chae describes as a painful history of institutional suppression against those who dared to probe the disaster. State institutions did not merely disagree with dissenters; they actively mobilized state machinery to silence them.

A prime example documented in the white paper is the fate of KBS's flagship investigative program, Tracking 60 Minutes (Choo-jeok 60-bun). When the program broadcast an in-depth investigation highlighting scientific discrepancies between on-site physical evidence and the defense ministry's simulation models, the Korea Communications Commission (KCC)—South Korea's broadcast regulator—swiftly handed down severe disciplinary penalties against the production staff. Although the courts eventually overturned the KCC’s sanctions as unlawful after years of litigation, the chilling effect on investigative journalism had already done its damage.

"The Ministry of National Defense was offered a completely open forum: any location, any format of its choosing, with civil society and independent scientists participating. Their response was silence, refusal, and a flurry of criminal complaints."

Chae himself made a direct offer to the defense ministry: he proposed a public, televised verification session where the military could choose the venue, the testing parameters, and the scientific protocols, so long as independent experts and civil society observers were permitted to examine the evidence together. If the official report was scientifically airtight, an open demonstration would have permanently quelled public skepticism and united a fractured nation. Instead, the ministry rejected the offer and pursued legal complaints against critics, choosing the criminal justice system over transparent public debate.

What to Watch Next

The publication of the Cheonan Citizens' White Paper arrives at a fraught cultural moment. A generation has grown up since the hull of the corvette cracked off Baengnyeong Island; children born in 2010 are now entering adulthood. Whether South Korea can look back on one of its greatest naval tragedies through the lens of archival evidence rather than tribal politics remains an open test of its democratic maturity.

Readers monitoring South Korean civil liberties and media policy should watch three concrete developments in the wake of this release:

  • Legal and institutional reaction: Observers should track whether the Ministry of National Defense or conservative civic groups seek legal injunctions, defamation suits, or distribution bans against Chae and his publisher, Ingan & Jayeonsa.
  • Media re-engagement: Watch whether mainstream South Korean broadcasters and major daily newspapers engage with the courtroom transcripts compiled in the white paper, or whether self-censorship around national security taboos continues to keep the book relegated to the margins.
  • Archival access: Pay attention to whether academic researchers and civic organizations use this release to demand the declassification of remaining unreleased operational logs, sonar recordings, and salvage records from March 2010.
