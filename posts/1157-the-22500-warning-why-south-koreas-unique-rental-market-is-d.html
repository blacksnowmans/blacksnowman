The $22,500 Warning: Why South Korea’s Unique Rental Market Is Descending Into an Era of Lawsuits

October 14, 2026

Imagine living in a comfortable apartment in Seoul for two years, only to receive a cold notice from your landlord: "I’m moving in myself. You need to pack your bags." Under South Korean law, this is one of the few ways a landlord can legally refuse to renew a lease. You move your children to a new school, pay for expensive movers, and struggle to find a new place in a skyrocketing market. Then, a year later, you discover the truth. The landlord didn't stay. They moved out after 14 months and brought in a new tenant for a much higher price.

This exact scenario recently led to a landmark Supreme Court ruling that has sent shockwaves through the Korean real estate market. The court ordered the landlord to pay 30 million won (about $22,500) in damages to the former tenant. While the sum might seem modest to some, the legal precedent is a massive blow to the traditional trust-based "Jeonse" system. It marks the beginning of what many are calling the "Era of Great Litigation," where every rental dispute is moving from the living room to the courtroom.

The $500,000 Deposit and the Domino Effect of Financial Ruin

To understand why this case matters, one must understand Jeonse. Unique to South Korea, Jeonse is a system where a tenant hands over a massive lump-sum deposit—often 60% to 80% of the home's value—instead of paying monthly rent. The landlord keeps this money interest-free for two years and returns it at the end of the lease. For decades, this was the "housing ladder" for young Koreans, allowing them to save for their own homes while landlords used the deposits to invest in more property.

In this specific case, the conflict began in July 2020. A tenant signed a Jeonse contract for 660 million won (about $495,000). As the two-year mark approached, the landlord demanded an increase to 1 billion won (about $750,000), citing rising market prices. When the tenant hesitated, the landlord played their final card: the "self-occupancy" clause. Under the Housing Lease Protection Act, a landlord can kick a tenant out if they or their immediate family intend to live in the unit themselves.

The tenant moved out in September 2022, but the landlord’s victory was short-lived. By early 2023, the landlord was back on the market, looking for a new tenant. They had only lived in the unit for 14 months. The law, however, requires a landlord who evicts a tenant for self-occupancy to reside there for at least 24 months. If they leave early without a "justifiable reason," they are liable for damages.

The landlord’s defense was a window into the current fragility of the Korean market. They owned another building—a dagagu (a multi-family house where one person owns the entire building and rents out individual units). Amidst a national "Jeonse fraud" panic, six of their other tenants suddenly demanded their deposits back. To prevent a total financial collapse and pay back those tenants, the landlord had to rent out their primary apartment to raise cash. They argued that avoiding bankruptcy was a "justifiable reason." The Supreme Court disagreed.

When 'Personal Circumstances' Fail in the Eyes of the Law

The core of the legal battle rested on what constitutes a "justifiable reason" for breaking the 24-month residency rule. The lower courts were initially sympathetic. The first trial ruled in favor of the landlord, acknowledging that the sudden "run" on deposits at their other property was an unpredictable financial emergency. In a market where liquidity can vanish overnight, the judge saw the landlord’s actions as a desperate survival tactic.

However, the High Court and eventually the Supreme Court overturned this. Their reasoning was cold and strictly commercial: a landlord’s overall portfolio risk is their own responsibility. The court ruled that since the landlord knew they owned a multi-family building with six expiring leases, they should have prepared for the possibility of having to return those deposits. Using one tenant’s eviction to solve a liquidity crisis at another property was deemed a violation of the first tenant's right to lease renewal.

This ruling highlights a growing tension in Korean society. For years, the law has moved toward protecting the "housing rights" of tenants as a fundamental social good. The "Three Lease Acts"—a controversial set of laws passed during the previous Moon Jae-in administration and often criticized for being poorly managed during the subsequent Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi era—gave tenants the right to a "2+2" year stay. This means a tenant can unilaterally extend a two-year lease for another two years unless the landlord moves in.

Critics argue that the law has turned tenants into "landlord investigators." Since the burden of proof for a "justifiable reason" lies entirely on the landlord, former tenants are now incentivized to monitor their old homes. They check mailboxes to see whose name is on the utility bills or browse real estate apps to see if their old unit has been re-listed. A single slip-up by a landlord can result in a windfall for the former tenant, often equaling several months' worth of rent.

The Ghost of the Yoon Suk-yeol Era and the Current Crisis

The current Lee Jae-myung administration, representing the main liberal Democratic Party (DPK), is now tasked with cleaning up the legislative and economic mess left behind. During the time Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi held office (before his impeachment and subsequent imprisonment), the rental market was characterized by extreme volatility. High interest rates made Jeonse deposits a burden for tenants, while a lack of clear regulatory guidance left landlords and tenants in a state of constant legal friction.

The "Jeonse fraud" (Jeonse-sagi) crisis, where unscrupulous developers "flipped" buildings and vanished with thousands of deposits, created a culture of fear. This fear is what drove the six tenants in the Supreme Court case to demand their money back simultaneously. While the current government is attempting to stabilize the market through expanded public housing and stricter oversight of HUG (the Korea Housing & Urban Guarantee Corporation), the damage to the social contract between landlord and tenant may be permanent.

The People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, continues to argue that these restrictive lease laws are actually hurting the very people they were meant to protect. Their logic is simple: if being a landlord becomes too legally risky and expensive, landlords will simply stop offering Jeonse. They will switch to monthly rent (wolse), which is significantly more expensive for the average Korean worker who used to rely on the "forced savings" aspect of the lump-sum deposit.

The Rise of Corporate Landlords and the Death of 'Small Owners'

What comes next? Many market analysts see this Supreme Court ruling as the final nail in the coffin for the "mom-and-pop" landlord. In the past, an average middle-class family might own one extra apartment as a retirement fund. Now, with the threat of $22,500 lawsuits and the complexity of proving "justifiable" residency, the risk is becoming too high for individuals.

This creates a vacuum that is quickly being filled by corporate and institutional landlords. We are beginning to see the "Westernization" of the Korean rental market. Instead of negotiating with a person, future tenants will likely sign contracts with massive property management firms. These firms have the legal teams to navigate the 24-month residency rules and the capital to survive a "run" on deposits. While this might bring more professional management, it also means the end of the flexible, human element of Korean housing.

Furthermore, the "Jeonse-to-Monthly" transition is accelerating. According to recent data, monthly rent contracts have surpassed Jeonse in many parts of Seoul for the first time in history. For a generation of young Koreans already struggling with stagnant wages and high inflation, the loss of the Jeonse system means the primary "housing ladder" is being pulled up. Without a large deposit to get back at the end of a lease, saving for a down payment on a permanent home becomes nearly impossible.

What to Watch Next

The real estate market in Korea is at a crossroads, and the following developments will determine if the "Era of Litigation" can be de-escalated:

  • Legislative Amendments: Watch for the Democratic Party-led National Assembly to potentially introduce a "White List" of justifiable reasons for moving out of a property early (such as sudden job transfers or medical emergencies). This would provide the legal certainty that the Supreme Court's current case-by-case approach lacks.
  • The HUG Deficit: The Korea Housing & Urban Guarantee Corporation (HUG) is under massive financial strain as it pays out insurance for failed Jeonse deposits. If HUG requires a massive taxpayer bailout, it could become a major political flashpoint for the Lee Jae-myung government.
  • The 2026 'Renewal Peak': A massive wave of "2+2" year contracts signed during the height of the previous market cycle are set to expire in 2026. This will be the ultimate test of whether the market can handle a large-scale return of deposits without triggering more lawsuits.
  • Shift in Investment Trends: As individual landlords exit the market, watch for whether the government introduces tax incentives for "Corporate Rental Housing" to fill the supply gap, a move that could fundamentally change the urban landscape of Korean cities.

The 30 million won fine recently upheld by the Supreme Court is more than just a settlement; it is a signal that the era of "handshake deals" in Korean real estate is over. As the law tightens its grip on the relationship between those who own homes and those who live in them, the very nature of what it means to be a "neighbor" in Korea is being redefined by the gavel.
