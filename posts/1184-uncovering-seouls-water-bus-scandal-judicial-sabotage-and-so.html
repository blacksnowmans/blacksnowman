Uncovering Seoul’s Water-Bus Scandal, Judicial Sabotage, and South Korea’s Big Power Shift

October 15, 2026

Inside a heated parliamentary audit room in Seoul, a stunning whistleblower revelation brought South Korea’s political center to a standstill. An official from the Seoul Metropolitan Government’s internal audit committee had uncovered an unaccounted sum of 1.5 billion won (about $1.1 million) linked to Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s signature "Han River Bus" project—a high-profile water transit initiative aimed at turning the capital’s central waterway into a major public transport artery. But what shocked lawmakers wasn't just the alleged kickback chain leading to senior executives at the state-backed Seoul Housing & Communities Corporation (SH). It was what happened after auditors drafted an official request for a police investigation: superiors gave a silent, chilling directive to destroy the documents.

Fearful of criminal liability for destroying public records, the whistleblower locked the file behind a password on a desktop computer, preserving a paper trail that now threatens to dismantle the political credibility of Mayor Oh. This local municipal scandal is not happening in a vacuum. It erupts at a volatile moment in South Korean politics, defined by a bitter constitutional standoff at the Supreme Court, the rollout of a landmark overhaul of the nation's prosecutorial system under President Lee Jae-myung, and deep anxiety over economic inequality.

A Hidden Audit and an Alleged Kickbacks Scheme in Seoul City Hall

The controversy surrounding the Han River Bus centers on Oh Se-hoon, the mayor of Seoul and a prominent figure in the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP). Oh has long staked his political legacy on flashy urban development projects. However, during a parliamentary audit, Representative Park Joo-min of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) presented evidence that a internal review had flagged major financial irregularities in the city's deals.

According to the audit findings, Gadeok Heavy Industries—the shipbuilder awarded a non-competitive contract for manufacturing the water buses—routed 1.5 billion won ($1.1 million) out of its accounts for purposes completely unrelated to vessel construction. Investigators suspect a significant portion of these funds was funneled through brokers as kickbacks to senior officials at SH Corporation, the public enterprise overseeing the operation of Seoul's commuter water buses. When auditors realized crime had likely taken place, they prepared formal documents requesting a law enforcement investigation.

Instead of sending the dossier to the police, City Hall leadership attempted to reroute the case to the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI)—a move widely viewed by critics as a stalling tactic. Even worse, internal whistleblowers testified that higher-ups exerted pressure to delete the investigation request altogether. When questioned at the National Assembly audit, Seoul’s acting Director General of Administrative Affairs, Jeong Sang-hoon, repeatedly claimed he "could not recall" the crucial meetings of early September. For Mayor Oh, who is already awaiting a second-instance court verdict in an unrelated legal case, the combination of potential bribery, document destruction, and public cover-up poses an existential threat to his political future.

Judicial Power Plays and Gridlock at the Supreme Court

While municipal corruption dominates municipal news, a dangerous institutional crisis is unfolding at the highest levels of the judiciary. Under Chief Justice Jo Hee-de—who was appointed during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former leader who was impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—the Supreme Court faces mounting accusations of deliberate judicial delay and constitutional overreach.

At the heart of the outcry is the Supreme Court’s handling of high-profile criminal cases involving figures from the previous conservative government, including the bribery charges against former First Lady Kim Keon-hee. Despite strict legal time limits governing how long suspects can be held in pre-trial detention, the Grand Bench of the Supreme Court slowed its review process to a crawl, dragging the proceedings toward the mandatory detention expiration date of October 31. Legal observers note that by setting hearing dates just days before the deadline, the court effectively forced special prosecutors led by Min Joong-ki to scramble for additional arrest warrants simply to prevent suspects from walking free before a verdict was rendered.

Simultaneously, Chief Justice Jo triggered an unprecedented political confrontation by abruptly refusing the presidential office’s request to re-nominate a successor for outgoing Supreme Court Justice Noh Tae-ak. This refusal has left a seat on the 14-member court vacant for over 220 days. As Minister of Government Legislation Cho Won-chul pointed out during parliamentary hearings, the power to appoint Supreme Court justices constitutionally belongs to the democratically elected president, while the Chief Justice’s role is to submit recommendations to complete the constitutional bench. By weaponizing his recommendation rights and bypassing internal court consultation mechanisms, Jo stands accused by the ruling party of staging a judicial blockade against the Lee Jae-myung administration to preserve conservative entrenchment within the courts.

Prosecution Reform and President Lee’s Economic Pragmatism

This judicial friction comes as South Korea takes its biggest step in decades toward curbing the historically unchecked power of its prosecutors. Following 11 hours of intense questioning, the National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee adopted the confirmation report for Kim Ji-yong, nominated to serve as the inaugural head of the newly established Serious Crime Investigation Agency (SCIA).

The creation of the SCIA marks the formal execution of South Korea's "prosecutorial reform" campaign, designed to permanently strip the State Prosecution Service of its direct investigative authority and restrict prosecutors solely to indictment and trial proceedings. While opposition lawmakers from the PPP and minority progressive parties like the Rebuilding Korea Party raised concerns over Kim's past background as a career prosecutor, the administration argued that a seasoned administrator was necessary to build a brand-new agency capable of tackling complex financial and corporate crimes without creating a policing void.

Alongside this structural reform, President Lee Jae-myung has signaled a sharp pivot toward domestic economic stability. In a surprise cabinet reshuffle, President Lee appointed Lim Kwang-hyun, a former commissioner of the National Tax Service, as his new Presidential Chief of Staff. Replacing the political operatives who traditionally hold the post with a veteran tax administrator underscores the administration’s focus on economic execution and public livelihoods (*minsaeng*).

Concurrently, President Lee publicly put an end to rumors stirred up by opposition commentators regarding potential constitutional amendments. Taking to social media, the president explicitly rejected speculative claims that he sought to modify the constitution to alter term limits or introduce a parliamentary system. By firmly closing the door on constitutional maneuvering, Lee reassured his base that the administration remains focused strictly on economic recovery, lowering cost-of-living burdens, and dismantling systemic corruption.

Independent Journalism and Changing Cultural Landscapes

Beyond high-level politics, South Korea’s public sphere is witnessing subtle but meaningful shifts in how citizens consume information and build community. As legacy media outlets face declining public trust due to partisan bias, independent investigative outlets are stepping up to fill the void. One notable example is Newshada, a regional investigative news nonprofit operating in the Incheon and Gyeonggi regions. Founded through a grassroots independent media incubator initiative, the outlet relies entirely on small donor contributions to uncover corruption among local municipal councils and regional business cartels that mainstream outlets frequently ignore.

At the same time, broader social attitudes among younger South Koreans are undergoing a quiet transformation. Amid persistent economic pressures, young adults are increasingly turning away from traditional dating apps like Tinder and Bumble, citing exhaustion over superficial profiles, commercialized interactions, and the pressure of endless swiping. In their place, a trend known as "soft socializing" has gained momentum.

Gen Z and millennial South Koreans are flocking to low-pressure, interest-based gatherings—such as birdwatching groups, pottery workshops, book clubs, and plant swaps. These low-stakes environments allow people to build genuine human connections around shared passions without the performative anxieties of modern dating. Whether in politics, journalism, or social life, there is a visible move away from top-down structures toward transparency, accountability, and authentic engagement.

What to Watch Next

As these interconnected political and legal battles move into their next phase, several key developments will determine the country's momentum:

  • The Han River Bus Criminal Probe: Watch whether law enforcement formally opens an investigation into Seoul City Hall officials and Mayor Oh Se-hoon regarding the destruction of audit documents and the suspected $1.1 million kickback scheme.
  • Supreme Court Appointments and High-Profile Verdicts: Key dates loom as the Supreme Court faces intense pressure to resolve its bench vacancy standoff with the president and deliver delayed rulings on high-profile detention cases.
  • The Launch of the SCIA: Following Kim Ji-yong’s confirmation process, the practical transition of major investigative powers from the Prosecution Service to the newly minted Serious Crime Investigation Agency will serve as a critical test for President Lee’s institutional reform agenda.
  • Economic Performance under the New Chief of Staff: With former tax chief Lim Kwang-hyun steering the presidential staff, look for concrete fiscal measures aimed at addressing income polarization and regional economic disparities.
