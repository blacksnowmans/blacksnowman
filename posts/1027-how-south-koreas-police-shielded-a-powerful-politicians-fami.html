How South Korea's Police Shielded a Powerful Politician's Family

October 12, 2026

Every society claims to value fairness, yet the true test of a legal system is how it handles the powerful when they are accused of bending the rules. In South Korea, few issues ignite public fury faster than allegations of elite college admissions fraud and faked résumés—a sore spot in a hyper-competitive educational culture where ordinary families sacrifice everything to give their children an edge. Against this backdrop, a lingering controversy over the police's decision to drop an investigation into the daughter of Han Dong-hoon—a prominent conservative politician and former Justice Minister—has resurfaced, exposing deep-seated cynicism about whether the rule of law applies equally to everyone.

To understand why this case has sparked such profound anger, one has to look past the dry legal terminology and examine a timeline that reads less like an investigation and more like a masterclass in bureaucratic stalling. The handling of the allegations against Han’s daughter reveals a pattern of institutional reluctance, shining a harsh light on how South Korean law enforcement agencies operate when powerful figures are in the crosshairs.

A 20-Month Investigation That Went Nowhere

When the police take nearly a year and a half to investigate a high-profile resume-padding and plagiarism allegation, alarm bells should ring. In a standard criminal inquiry, investigators quickly follow up initial complaints by summoning the accused for intensive questioning. Yet in this case, the investigation dragged on for an extraordinary 1 year and 8 months. During that entire stretch, the police hauled the original complainant in for questioning a staggering six times, while the primary subject of the investigation was subjected to just a single written questionnaire.

It defies basic logic. Law enforcement agencies do not relentlessly summon the accuser while treating the accused with such gentle detachment unless there is an underlying reluctance to uncover the truth—or a foregone conclusion that the case will be quietly shelved. Dragging out an investigation for 20 months ensures that evidence scatters and public interest wanes. Instead of resolving doubts through objective fact-finding, the police effectively parked the case, eroding public trust in the impartiality of law enforcement.

Compounding this passive approach was a glaring absence of coercive investigative tactics. The allegations—involving suspected ghost-written academic papers and copyright violations—generated massive public outcry at the time. Yet police never sought a search warrant or attempted a single raid to secure digital records or physical documents. At the time the allegations surfaced, Han Dong-hoon was serving as Minister of Justice, holding immense sway over the nation's legal apparatus. It is difficult to divorce the police's timidity from the formidable weight of his office. If an ordinary citizen faced similar accusations, a search warrant would likely have been secured within days. Treating a high-ranking official's family with kid gloves makes a mockery of the principle that justice is blind.

Blocked International Cooperation and Selective Justice

The obstruction went beyond local foot-dragging. Securing overseas digital evidence often requires an official request for international criminal judicial assistance. In this case, however, the prosecution took the extraordinary step of unilaterally blocking the police's request for international cooperation, refusing even to forward it to the Ministry of Justice. Bypassing both the Supreme Prosecutors' Office and the Ministry broke standard protocol, heavily suggesting a deliberate effort to shield Han from oversight.

The justifications offered for shelving the cooperation request—arguing that the procedures were too complex or of low utility—were little more than excuses designed to let investigators avoid doing their jobs. Meanwhile, the police meekly accepted the block without lodging a formal protest or demanding a review, hiding behind convenient interpretations of internal regulations.

The numbers tell a stark story of selective law enforcement: while the investigation into Han Dong-hoon's daughter logged zero search warrants and a single written questionnaire over 20 months, a separate high-profile investigation into Lee Jae-myung’s wife, Kim Hye-kyung, involved 131 search warrants and numerous in-person summonses executed within just six months.

This stark numerical contrast strips away any plausible deniability from the police. If investigators had applied even a fraction of the tenacity seen in other politically sensitive cases to Han's family, the public outcry might have been avoided. Instead, the clear discrepancy reinforces the public perception of a two-tiered justice system.

Official Deception at the Highest Levels

Perhaps the most shocking twist in the saga unfolded when the head of the National Office of Investigation, Woo Jong-su, stood before reporters at a press briefing and confidently claimed that investigators had interviewed numerous witnesses in the case. Subsequent checks revealed a stunning reality: the number of witness interviews conducted was zero.

When a top law enforcement official publicly feeds falsehoods to the press and the public, it signals a complete collapse of institutional integrity. A zero-witness interview count is definitive proof that the investigation was little more than theater—an effort to create the illusion of due diligence while letting a powerful figure off the hook. When the head of the national investigative body actively distorts reality, it ceases to be an isolated miscommunication and becomes an organized cover-up.

This kind of brazen deception cannot be swept away by internal reprimands of low-level staff. It demands an immediate, thorough inspection by higher authorities and a comprehensive reinvestigation from the ground up. Sweeping past malpractice under the rug—echoing the systemic biases that flourished during the administration of jailed former leader Yoon Seok-yeol—only paves the way for future abuses.

What to Watch Next

  • Internal Disciplinary Actions: Watch for whether the National Police Agency initiates a formal internal audit into the National Office of Investigation regarding the false briefings and prolonged inactivity.
  • Parliamentary Pressure: Look to the National Assembly, where opposition lawmakers are likely to demand hearings and subpoena records concerning the unusual block on international legal cooperation.
  • Calls for a Special Prosecutor or Reinvestigation: Monitor civil society groups and political parties for formal petitions calling to reopen the case under an independent framework to ensure a truly impartial review.

The ultimate verdict on this case will not be written by the police files that quietly closed the matter, but by how the public and future historians evaluate a system stretched to its breaking point by elite privilege. Until law enforcement demonstrates that titles and political connections offer no shelter from accountability, the shadow of suspicion will remain.
