Inside the Democratic Party’s Civil War: How Internal Factions Threaten South Korea’s Main Opposition

September 15, 2026

For anyone watching South Korean politics closely, the current climate within the country's progressive camp often induces a strange mix of frustration and dark humor. The Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s primary liberal opposition, finds itself bogged down by internal friction at a time when unity should be its greatest asset. Recent maneuvers by senior party figures have left core supporters feeling alienated, watching anxiously as political maneuvering supersedes genuine policy debate. To understand why the party's current trajectory feels so self-defeating, one has to examine the mechanics of its internal power struggles and the heavy-handed appointments that are currently dividing its ranks.

The Taboo Word: Why Raising the Specter of Impeachment Backfired

The trouble began in earnest when Kim Min-seok, a senior Supreme Council member of the Democratic Party, took direct aim at Rep. Choo Mi-ae. Kim publicly compared Choo’s national political maneuvering as the governor of Gyeonggi Province to the pre-conditions that led to the historic impeachment of former President Roh Moo-hyun. In the high-stakes world of Korean politics, words are weapons, but careless phrasing often turns into a blade that cuts the user.

By invoking the "I-word," Kim effectively dragged the dark prospect of impeaching party leader Lee Jae-myung into the public consciousness. It is a psychological trap akin to being told not to think of an elephant; the harder party leadership insists that impeachment is not on the table, the more prominent the idea becomes in the minds of voters and supporters.

While the comment may have been engineered to reassure Lee’s inner circle, the public perception was one of petty factionalism. Invoking the deep-seated trauma of the Roh Moo-hyun impeachment—a catastrophic fracture in South Korea’s progressive history—merely to police a high-profile party member felt regressive. Instead of enforcing discipline, the remark magnified voter fatigue and handed critics an easy narrative about an insular leadership obsessed with internal enemies rather than national governance.

How Attacks on Choo Mi-ae Accidentally Forged a New Contender

Paradoxically, the coordinated pushback against Choo Mi-ae by the party's leadership apparatus has only burnished her credentials as a heavyweight presidential hopeful. Choo is a seasoned, tough-nosed political operator. Despite facing intense crossfire from hardline factions within the party's base, she has continued to champion sensitive issues like prosecutorial reform, using the incoming fire to solidify her own political capital.

Recent public opinion polls underscore her underlying strength. Surveys from outlets like OhmyNews and Realmeter have shown Choo securing the top spot among Democratic Party supporters. Her strategy of treating Gyeonggi Province governance as a springboard for national policy debates has struck a chord. While the party apparatus attempts to box her in, these attacks have cast her in the role of a persecuted champion of justice. In politics, narrative is power, and the leadership’s attempts to sideline her have inadvertently gifted her one of the most compelling narratives in modern Korean politics.

Privatizing the Party’s Brain Trust: The Controversial Appointment of Kang Duk-ku

The friction extends far beyond personality clashes into the structural core of the party. The Democracy Institute serves as the intellectual engine of the Democratic Party, consuming roughly 30% of the party’s operational budget to shape policy and strategy. It is supposed to be staffed by the most capable minds available. Thus, the appointment of Rep. Kang Duk-ku—a figure previously tied to intense internal factional squabbles—as the institute's new head triggered widespread dismay.

Critics, including mainstream conservative outlets, pointed out the obvious: the move looks less like an effort toward party-wide integration and more like an attempt to turn the party’s primary think tank into a personal political campaign vehicle. Placing a close loyalist at the helm of the party’s intellectual core sends a chilling message to internal critics that loyalty matters more than competence. It revives memories of the arrogance that plagued the progressive camp during previous election cycles.

There is a stark difference between academic pedigree and political acumen. Steering a major political movement requires the ability to bridge divides and gather disparate voices, not merely surrounding oneself with sycophants. When a party's think tank is perceived as being privatized for factional survival, its capacity to produce broad-based public policy inevitably withers.

What to Watch Next

As South Korean politics moves deeper into the current electoral cycle, several critical markers will determine whether the Democratic Party can recover its footing or continue its descent into factional infighting:

  • Gyeonggi Province Polling Trends: Watch how Choo Mi-ae's approval ratings hold up against rival contenders. Continued dominance in regional surveys will signal whether the public appetite for alternative leadership is impervious to party pressure.
  • Internal Pushback: Monitor whether other non-aligned lawmakers begin openly challenging leadership appointments, particularly regarding the operation of key party organs like the Democracy Institute.
  • Shifts in Core Voter Fatigue: Keep an eye on regular tracking polls for signs that internal bickering is translating into a measurable drop in progressive voter turnout or enthusiasm.
  • The Handling of Dissent: Observe how the leadership responds to upcoming legislative showdowns—whether they double down on internal purges or pivot toward broad-tent coalition building.

South Korean voters are remarkably discerning. They watch how parties handle power long before they step into the voting booth. If the progressive camp continues to prioritize the elimination of internal rivals over the presentation of a compelling national vision, the cost will be paid at the ballot box. The coming months will test whether the party can step back from the brink of factional self-destruction and return to the foundational principles that brought it to power.
