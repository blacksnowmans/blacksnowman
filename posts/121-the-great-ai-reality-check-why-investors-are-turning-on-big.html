The Great AI Reality Check: Why Investors Are Turning on Big Tech

September 03, 2026

Walking through the current stock market feels like treading on thin ice. The days of blind enthusiasm, where simply shouting "AI is the future!" was enough to fuel a rally, are firmly behind us. The market has grown cold and analytical, asking a blunt, uncomfortable question: "You are pouring hundreds of billions of dollars into this—what are the actual returns?"

This is the essence of the "AI bubble" debate we face today. While major technology companies are funneling astronomical sums—often exceeding $700 billion (1,000 trillion won) annually—into capital expenditures (CAPEX), real-world consumer adoption of AI remains stuck at roughly 10%. This widening gap between infrastructure spending and tangible utility is unsustainable. If the market is to avoid a correction, investors need to look past the "rosy outlooks" and identify the survival strategies hidden behind the cold, hard data.

From Earnings per Share to Cash Flow

There was a time when a company’s worth was determined by simple metrics: high earnings per share (EPS) and consistent dividends. Those days are over. In an era where Big Tech firms are aggressively borrowing to build data centers and AI infrastructure, traditional profit metrics are losing their luster.

The new benchmark is the efficiency of cash flow. Investors are beginning to realize that the profits reported on financial statements can be deceptive, often inflated by the valuation of AI-related assets rather than actual operational success. The market is now separating "real" income—generated from core business operations—from the "paper gains" of the AI hype cycle. We must look at how these companies reinvest their earnings and whether that spending leads to genuine growth or just accounting "number games."

Furthermore, the "safety net" that supported the U.S. stock market over the last decade—aggressive share buybacks—is fraying. Companies are diverting capital away from returning value to shareholders and into massive AI infrastructure projects. Without those buybacks, the floor under stock prices is thinner, meaning investors are no longer satisfied with the promise of future glory; they demand proof of growth.

  • EPS and dividends are no longer the ultimate defensive weapons.
  • Survival depends on "actual cash flow" capable of absorbing massive infrastructure costs.
  • If share buybacks disappear, growth must be proven, or the market will punish the stock.

Beyond Broadcom: Sorting the Gold from the Dross

As the market fixates on macroeconomic data, such as employment reports, we must keep our eyes on the hardware giants, like Broadcom (a major global designer and supplier of semiconductor and infrastructure software products). The key is not just whether they sold more chips, but the quality of their guidance.

The real focus should be on the Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) market and networking technology. An ASIC is a custom-designed semiconductor tailored for specific tasks rather than general use. Companies that can secure high-value clients for these chips while using networking tech to maximize data center efficiency are the ones that will endure. The AI frenzy sparked by Nvidia (the leading designer of AI graphics processing units) was monumental, but we are entering a phase of industrial evolution where not all hardware will survive. The winners will be those that minimize CAPEX burdens while maintaining profitability.

AI is currently locked in an intense "cost struggle." As the cost of manufacturing and development climbs, companies that fail to convert this into a viable, profitable commercial product will soon find themselves shut out by financial institutions. Do not fear companies that scale back their infrastructure spending; be wary of those that recklessly take on debt to simply grow the size of their investment.

The Second Half: Breaking the Traditional Cycles

Historically, there was a predictable cycle: software leads, and hardware follows. This time, the cycle is broken. The entire AI value chain is under a microscope, and the sector leaders for the second half of the year will not be determined by traditional categorizations, but by individual "profit momentum."

Financial institutions are no longer optimistic by default. The moment any signs of an AI bubble emerge, they are prepared to pivot toward extreme conservatism. In this environment, capital will flee to sectors with undeniable growth and abandon those that rely on vague expectations. If the AI adoption rate does not scale from 10% toward 50%, much of the current investment is destined to be written off as sunk costs.

Looking Ahead: A Time for Cold Calculation

We are standing in the largest capital experiment in history. Whether this leads to a new industrial revolution or a burst bubble remains an open question, but the market will no longer be lenient toward the unprepared.

In my view, the current market skepticism—driven by macroeconomic fears—is actually the quietest, best time to find real value. When the hype dies down and the "madness" of the crowd subsides, we are left with the only question that matters: "Who is the real player?"

Investors should not fear the coming volatility. Instead, use this period to verify the health of the companies in your portfolio. Are they chasing trends to burn cash, or are they carefully planning for a future they will eventually dominate? The difference between a failed experiment and a new industrial revolution lies in the balance sheets of the companies we choose to hold today. The time for blind faith is over; the time for forensic analysis has begun.
