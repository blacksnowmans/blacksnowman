The Weaponization of Law: Cho Kuk on South Korea's Judicial Crisis

September 10, 2026

The voice coming through the YouTube broadcast was calm, perhaps surprisingly so, given the weight of the grievances being aired. Cho Kuk, the former Minister of Justice and current head of the Rebuilding Korea Party’s Innovation Policy Institute (the policy think tank for the Rebuilding Korea Party, a progressive splinter party that emerged as a significant force in the recent general election), was dissecting the state of South Korean judicial integrity. What began as a personal check-in about his daughter’s life quickly shifted into a sharp, uncompromising critique of how the law is being used as a political weapon.

To an outside observer, the back-and-forth between Korea’s courts and its National Assembly—the country’s unicameral legislature—might seem like standard political theater. However, the issues Cho raised suggest something far more structural: a transformation of legal interpretation into a tool for political suppression. For those watching the trajectory of South Korean democracy, the question is no longer just about who is right or wrong, but whether the judiciary has abandoned its role as a neutral arbiter.

The Illusion of Judicial Independence

A recurring topic in Korean political discourse is the "withdrawal of prosecution," a legal mechanism that allows the state to drop charges. Cho clarified a crucial point that is often missed in the media frenzy: under the current legal framework, withdrawing prosecution is only possible for cases in the first or second trial phase. Once a case reaches the Supreme Court, it is structurally impossible to drop. Even if the legislature were to pass a special law forcing a withdrawal for cases still in lower courts, it would almost certainly be struck down as unconstitutional.

The problem, according to Cho, lies in the Supreme Court’s recent behavior. He points to cases where the Court has overturned established legal precedents to remand cases back to lower courts with instructions that essentially mandate a guilty verdict. When a case returns to a lower court with a "guilty" directive from the highest bench, it is effectively a procedural formality—the lower court is highly unlikely to defy the Supreme Court. This was most visible in recent election law cases, where the Supreme Court, under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, issued a reversal within just nine days. Critics argue this wasn't a pursuit of legal logic, but a deliberate move to disqualify a key political figure.

This creates a dangerous feedback loop where the judiciary acts as a partisan agent. When a court flips its own established legal interpretations within days to target specific individuals, it erodes the very foundation of judicial independence. It signals to the public that the law is not a static set of rules, but a variable to be adjusted by those in power.

The 2000 Legislative Trap

At the center of this legal firestorm is the Public Official Election Act, specifically regarding the "dissemination of false information." Cho identifies a critical flaw in the law, dating back to a 2000 legislative amendment that introduced the term "act" (행위) into the statute.

The law is intended to punish clear-cut lies, such as faking academic credentials or concealing assets. However, by including the vague term "act," the law now allows prosecutors and judges to label any subjective recollection of an event as a "false act." Because human memory is fallible and context-dependent, this amendment has effectively turned the law into a trap. By broadening the definition of what constitutes a "false statement," the judiciary can now criminalize political speech that would have been considered protected opinion in the past. Cho argues that this isn't just a minor technical issue; it is the mechanism by which the law has been weaponized to create "crimes" out of thin air.

Beyond Prosecution: The Path to Reform

So, if "withdrawing prosecution" is legally impossible for cases already in the upper courts, how does the system reset? Cho argues that the solution lies not in the hands of the prosecutors, but in the National Assembly.

There is currently a legislative amendment sitting in the National Assembly that would remove the problematic word "act" from the election law. This bill has already passed the Legislation and Judiciary Committee and has been ready for a floor vote for over a year and a half. Members of the Rebuilding Korea Party, alongside members of the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea's main liberal opposition party), have expressed support for the measure.

Cho’s argument is stark: the National Assembly needs to stop watching the political winds and simply hold the vote. If the law is amended to remove the vague "act" clause, the legal basis for these contentious cases disappears. This wouldn't be a political favor or a "get out of jail free" card; it would be a necessary legislative correction to fix a law that has been twisted to facilitate judicial overreach. The fact that the Assembly has hesitated to act despite having the authority to do so highlights a broader paralysis in the political class.

What to Watch Next

The push for systemic reform is moving toward two specific fronts:

  • Legislative Action: Keep a close eye on the floor agenda of the National Assembly. Whether the amendment to the Public Official Election Act comes to a vote will be the ultimate test of the opposition's political will to check the current judicial trend.
  • Institutional Oversight: Cho has called for an audit by the Ministry of Justice and the activation of the "Prosecution Future Committee," a body designed to oversee investigative conduct. Establishing a formal record of alleged prosecutorial misconduct—such as the coercion of witnesses or evidence fabrication—is essential for moving beyond individual grievances and toward a systemic reckoning.

The situation in South Korea underscores a sobering truth: when the machinery of the law is turned against political opponents, the resulting damage isn't just to the individuals involved, but to the public's faith in the state itself. As Cho suggests, the way out is not through more political maneuvering, but through the hard, often tedious work of fixing broken laws and enforcing accountability within the institutions that claim to uphold them.
