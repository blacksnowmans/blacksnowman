Rocket Thrusters, Upside-Down Stunts, and Broken Promises: Inside the Reality of Tesla's Next Roadster

October 17, 2026

Picture an electric sports car hurtling toward a massive ramp, launching into an airborne roll, righting itself in midair using bursts of compressed gas, and coming to a standstill completely inverted—hanging from the ceiling as if gravity had simply been switched off. For anyone watching recent demonstration footage of the second-generation Tesla Roadster, the sight feels less like automotive engineering and more like a scene lifted straight from science fiction. Driven by roughly ten cold-gas thrusters and an underbody fan that generates vacuum-like suction, the vehicle claims a mind-bending zero-to-sixty sprint of just 0.81 seconds.

Yet for thousands of reservation holders across the world—including early adopters in South Korea who wired over hefty deposits nearly a decade ago—the spectacle provokes as much exhaustion as excitement. Nine years after its surprise unveiling in late 2017, the promised successor to Tesla's original halo car remains commercially elusive. Behind the dizzying physics of rocket engineering lies an uncomfortable reality: hundreds of millions of dollars in customer deposits locked up, shifting product specifications, and regulatory hurdles so steep that driving this machine legally on public streets may never happen.

From a 2020 Promised Delivery to a Nine-Year Mirage

When Elon Musk rolled out the second-generation Roadster at the close of the Semi truck unveiling in November 2017, the targets seemed otherworldly. Tesla pledged a 0-to-60 mph acceleration in 1.9 seconds, a top speed surpassing 250 mph, a four-seat configuration, and a range of 1,000 kilometers (around 620 miles) on a single charge. Customers were promised delivery by 2020. Enraptured by the vision, prospective buyers lined up to wire cash. In South Korea, reservation holders put down 55 million won (roughly $40,000) to secure a place in line for a vehicle carrying a base price tag of around $200,000 (roughly 270 million won).

What followed was an almost surreal corporate delay. Year after year, the Roadster lingered quietly in the fine print of Tesla's quarterly earnings decks, perennially classified as being in "design development." Worldwide, customer deposits accumulated to an estimated $250 million (roughly 330 billion won). Because these funds were not placed into protected escrow accounts and yield zero interest to buyers, critics have increasingly pointed out that early reservation holders effectively handed the company an immense, decade-long, interest-free operating loan.

The frustration is compounded by how radically the vehicle's parameters have drifted. The promised 1,000-kilometer range has quietly shrunk to around 480 kilometers in updated estimates, and the nominal four-passenger cabin has effectively contracted into a two-seater. While Musk previously quipped on social media that the "SpaceX option package" would feature rocket thrusters arranged seamlessly around the chassis, few took the claim seriously at the time. The recent unveilings show the company actually pursued that technical madness, but using customer patience as an open-ended credit line has strained consumer trust to the breaking point.

Rocket Engineering on Wheels: COPVs, 10,000 PSI, and Ground-Effect Suction

The mechanical underpinnings of the updated Roadster demonstrate a direct transplant of aerospace hardware into an automotive chassis. Rather than relying solely on tire tread—which reaches an absolute physical ceiling governed by friction coefficient and tire compound limits—the vehicle bypasses conventional traction through cold-gas propulsion and active aerodynamic suction.

  • Cold-gas thrusters: Borrowing attitude-control technology directly from SpaceX's Falcon 9 booster rockets, the Roadster incorporates approximately ten thruster nozzles around its body. Instead of igniting liquid fuels, the system violently vents highly compressed gas mixtures (primarily nitrogen) through high-speed electric actuators. This delivers thrust independent of road contact, enabling external forces that can launch the chassis forward at rates exceeding 3.4G, resulting in the reported 0.81-second sprint.
  • High-pressure storage: To fuel these bursts, the car houses a 600-liter Carbon Overwrap Pressure Vessel (COPV) pressurized to an astonishing 10,000 psi (roughly 680 atmospheres)—a pressure equivalent to ocean depths of 7,000 meters. Regulated via ultra-fast electronic valves operating within fractions of a second, the system can dynamically correct vehicle pitch, yaw, and roll midair to ensure the car lands wheels-down.
  • Active suction fans: To pull off the stunt of remaining stationary while hanging inverted, Tesla installed an active high-powered underbody fan system. By evacuating the air beneath the car, the system creates a low-pressure vacuum zone. With atmospheric air exerting roughly 10 metric tons of pressure per square meter from above, the net downforce generated reaches up to twice the vehicle's curb weight.

The aerodynamic trickery is not entirely novel. In 1978, Formula One designer Gordon Murray introduced the Brabham BT46B "fan car," which deployed a massive rear extraction fan to generate ungodly levels of mechanical grip. The machine crushed its debut race so thoroughly that it was promptly withdrawn and outlawed over unfair advantage and safety concerns—namely that the vacuum fan acted like a shotgun, hurling track gravel and debris directly into the faces of trailing drivers. Tesla has revived this long-banned concept five decades later, replacing mechanical gear drives with digital fly-by-wire software control.

The Stone Wall of Road Safety Regulation

Engineering a machine that can flip upside down on an enclosed stunt course is a triumph of robotics; getting certified to share an intersection with school buses and sedans is an entirely different matter. Tesla has already hinted that the rocket-equipped variant is not road-legal, and automotive safety bodies have little reason to grant exceptions.

In the United States, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) is already probing Tesla's autonomous Cybercab concept over the removal of steering wheels and pedals. The prospect of approving a consumer passenger car that carries a 10,000-psi pressure vessel and expels high-velocity gas blasts at curb level borders on the impossible. In real-world driving conditions, a burst of compressed nitrogen could blind trailing motorists, destabilize nearby cyclists, or injure pedestrians. Moreover, COPVs carry their own notorious engineering baggage: in September 2016, a Falcon 9 rocket suffered a catastrophic launchpad explosion traced back to a COPV structural failure.

In South Korea, certification hurdles are even more insurmountable. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) oversees vehicle safety through stringent technical roadworthiness standards that leave zero leeway for external propulsion nozzles. This dynamic touches directly on national policy debates surrounding automotive standards.

Unlike the chaotic deregulation pushes witnessed during Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office, where safety considerations often took a backseat to performative announcements, South Korea's regulatory bodies under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung have taken a far more deliberate stance: championing pragmatic technological innovation while strictly enforcing non-negotiable public safety guardrails.

South Korean transport authorities are fundamentally tasked with shielding everyday motorists and pedestrians from hazardous vehicular anomalies. Without road-legal certification, any Roadster delivered to domestic buyers would be permanently relegated to private tracks or private display garages.

A Shifting Corporate Identity: The Last Human-Driven Machine?

Beyond regulatory barriers, the Roadster's rebirth raises fundamental questions about Tesla's strategic trajectory. Tesla has spent recent years pivoting aggressively away from driver-centric enthusiast vehicles and toward full autonomy, robotaxis, and humanoid robotics. The company has wound down production priorities for flagship models like the Model S and Model X while converting factory floor space toward the Optimus humanoid robot line.

In this context, Musk's characterization of the Roadster as potentially Tesla's "final manual-driving vehicle" is revealing. The car is no longer designed as a practical transport platform for mass electrification. Planned for a boutique run of roughly 2,000 units per year with a newly floated target date of 2028, it functions primarily as an ultra-expensive technological halo—a plaything designed to prove what extreme engineering can pull off when unbound by conventional commercial viability.

While the trickle-down benefits of this research may eventually enhance emergency stabilization systems—similar to early automotive research conducted by firms like Germany's Bosch—the narrative feels hollow to the people who funded it. For reservation holders who have watched 55 million won sit dormant for nine years while specifications shifted and deadlines evaporated, an inverted stunt car feels less like the democratization of green transport and more like an elaborate, self-indulgent diversion.

What to Watch Next

As Tesla looks toward its latest target of rolling out production units by 2028, several concrete milestones will reveal whether this rocket car can become a deliverable product or remain an eternal prototype:

  • Production facility tooling: Watch whether Tesla officially allocates floor space, capital expenditures, and custom assembly lines for COPV and thruster integration during upcoming quarterly investor filings, or if the vehicle remains relegated to R&D status.
  • Track-only vs. street-legal variants: Look for whether Tesla formally splits the Roadster lineup into an uncertified, track-only "SpaceX Package" and a stripped-down, road-homologated standard trim to satisfy basic transportation rules.
  • Regulatory engagement with NHTSA and MOLIT: Monitor whether Tesla formally files safety exemption petitions or crash-test validation filings for the 10,000-psi pressure containment vessels with transport authorities in the US and South Korea.
  • Customer deposit attrition: Pay close attention to refund rates among early reservation holders, particularly in overseas markets where frustration over non-interest-bearing escrow conditions continues to mount.
