The Erosion of Accountability: Inside South Korea’s Political and Judicial Scandals

September 05, 2026

Walking through Yeouido—South Korea’s equivalent of Capitol Hill—these days feels like a déjà vu of the country’s worst political habits. As a casual observer, it is easy to become cynical: politicians recycle outdated practices, while others engage in self-serving power plays, all while draping themselves in the rhetoric of "fairness." We live in an era where how power is used matters far more than the acquisition of it, yet the current landscape leaves one feeling exhausted. The powerful are rewriting the rules to suit their own appetites, and without constant public scrutiny, accountability seems like a fading dream. Behind the facade of “serving the people,” the gears of Korean politics are grinding away, focused almost entirely on self-preservation.

The Weight of Power: Legislative Independence vs. Personal Ambition

The recent controversy surrounding Yong Hye-in, a nominee for a cabinet-level position, is a perfect case study in the tension between moral integrity and political survival. Yong, a lawmaker who secured her seat through the proportional representation system, has expressed a desire to keep her legislative seat while simultaneously serving in the executive branch. Her defense is rooted in pragmatic necessity: if she resigns from the legislature, her minor party loses government subsidies, which would cripple their operations.

While the financial struggle of small parties is a real issue, the optics are disastrous. Proportional representatives are meant to act as the conscience of the legislature—they are the direct representatives of specialized interests and minorities. To hold an executive role while keeping a legislative badge is a direct affront to the separation of powers. Article 43 of the South Korean Constitution prohibits lawmakers from holding concurrent positions in the executive branch. While cabinet posts are a legal exception, historical precedent dictates that those chosen for such roles resign their legislative seats. This isn't just a matter of etiquette; it is a fundamental democratic safeguard. When a politician claiming to represent the marginalized adopts the most classic "old-school" political loophole, it leaves the public feeling deeply betrayed.

The conflict of interest is obvious. If a minister is also a lawmaker, the executive branch effectively gains influence over the legislature, and the essential function of the parliamentary audit—where lawmakers hold the government accountable—is neutralized. How can a lawmaker objectively audit a government department if the person running that department is their colleague? When power concentrates in one corner, the eyes of the watchdogs go dim, and the public inevitably pays the price.

  • The Core Issue: Concurrent service risks crippling the separation of powers.
  • The Financial Defense: The loss of party subsidies does not justify bypassing constitutional norms.
  • The Result: Concentrated power prevents the legislature from performing its primary duty: oversight.

The Judiciary’s Secret Bonuses and Hidden Records

If the legislature is struggling with ethics, the judiciary is facing a crisis of transparency. News recently broke that Supreme Court justices distributed nearly $1 million (1.3 billion won) in "encouragement bonuses" among themselves without any legal basis. In a nation where inflation and high interest rates are squeezing the average household, the image of top judges awarding themselves a "self-bonus" of roughly $1,500 (2 million won) per month is nothing short of scandalous. It highlights a dangerous detachment from the reality of the people they are supposed to serve.

Even more concerning is the Court Administration’s refusal to make lower-court rulings transparent. Despite calls from the President to open these records, the judiciary has remained obstinately silent, acting as if they are above public demand. In many other democracies, particularly the U.S., the evolution of "legal tech" has made court records more accessible, thereby increasing public trust. In South Korea, however, the judiciary treats rulings like state secrets. One has to ask: why should the documents that define the rule of law be kept hidden from the very people living under those laws? Transparency is the only foundation upon which judicial authority can legitimately rest. When a court hides its reasoning, it loses its right to public trust.

The Lesson of Transparency: The judiciary fears that if its rulings are analyzed and criticized by the public, its aura of infallibility will vanish. But true authority doesn't come from hiding; it comes from standing up to public scrutiny.

Internal Conflicts and the Ethics Oversight Failure

Within the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the main liberal opposition party, internal friction has reached a boiling point. The recent dysfunction of the party’s Ethics Oversight Committee has sparked concerns that the body is being used as a weapon for internal factional warfare rather than a tool for moral accountability. Leaked messages involving committee leadership suggest that the organization is being manipulated to protect certain cliques while targeting rivals.

The value of the progressive movement in Korea has always been its claim to moral superiority. When an organization meant to enforce ethics acts with double standards, it renders their cries for external justice hollow. If a party cannot cleanse its own house, its pursuit of broader political reform will never be taken seriously by the electorate.

Looking Ahead: What Should We Watch?

As we move forward, there are three critical areas that demand our continued attention:

  • Constitutional Integrity: Whether the trend of "legislator-ministers" continues, or if public outcry forces a return to the standard of resigning one's seat upon cabinet appointment.
  • Judicial Transparency: Watch for potential legislative pushes to mandate the disclosure of all lower-court rulings. The battle between the public’s "right to know" and the judiciary's "right to privacy" will be a defining legal issue of the year.
  • Party Reform: Observe whether the opposition party can reform its internal ethics committees to be truly independent, or if they continue to devolve into tools for factional fighting.

Ultimately, politics should be a battle of values, not a fight for survival. The next generation of Korean voters is no longer satisfied with politicians who simply claim they were "wronged" or "misunderstood." We are looking for integrity in both the process and the result. It is time for those in power to stop looking at their own plates and start looking at the public they were elected to serve.
