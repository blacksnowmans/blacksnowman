Seoul’s Ferry Scandal, Presidential Jet-Setting, and South Korea’s Fractured Trust in Power

November 02, 2026

Along the banks of Seoul’s Han River, Mayor Oh Se-hoon envisioned a sleek network of water buses transforming the capital’s commuter grid. But what was sold as a modern municipal achievement has quickly degenerated into a high-profile political scandal, complete with shadow contracts, missing funds, and alleged orders to shred evidence. The controversy is just one spark in a volatile political landscape where public cynicism toward institutional power in South Korea is reaching a boiling point.

From suspicious municipal procurement in Seoul to fierce battles over President Lee Jae-myung’s extensive overseas summitry, South Korea’s political arena is caught in a web of competing narratives. Beneath the daily headlines of poll numbers and parliamentary posturing lies a deeper debate among South Korean voters over administrative ethics, judicial impartiality, and the practical value of national leadership on the world stage.

The Han River Bus Scandal: Kickback Suspicions and Shredded Evidence

The trouble for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent member of the conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), began with a choice that raised eyebrows across the maritime industry. To construct the vessels for his ambitious "Han River Bus" water transport project, the Seoul Metropolitan Government awarded a contract to Gadok Heavy Industries—a firm with precisely zero prior ship-building experience.

Questions raised by National Assembly member Park Joo-min of the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and investigative journalist Hong Sa-hoon soon escalated into systemic concerns. An internal review conducted by Seoul’s own audit committee uncovered red flags pointing to an alleged kickback scheme valued at roughly 320 million won (about $240,000 USD). For a project touted as a major public transit upgrade, assigning contract fundamentals to an inexperienced builder while hundreds of thousands of dollars unaccounted for circulated in the background raised immediate alarm.

The scandal deepened during the subsequent administrative cover-up attempts. According to whistleblower reports within the city government, internal auditors initially prepared to hand their findings over to law enforcement for a formal criminal investigation while simultaneously requesting an audit from the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), South Korea’s top government audit agency. However, senior leadership allegedly stepped in, ordering staff to destroy all documents related to the requested criminal referral.

When the matter reached the national audit agency, the BAI summarily dismissed the case, claiming the municipality should handle the matter internally. This decision drew intense criticism, with observers accusing the auditing body of acting as a political shield for the city’s leadership. With municipal opposition lawmakers demanding answers and city hall remaining largely silent, calls are growing for prosecutors to launch a full-scale criminal investigation into potential evidence tampering and official corruption.

President Lee Jae-myung’s Jet-Set Diplomacy Under Fire

While Mayor Oh faces mounting domestic trouble in Seoul, President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea is waging a different war on the diplomatic front. Opposition lawmakers have launched a concerted attack against the president’s frequent overseas travel, accusing the administration of ignoring domestic economic hardships to focus on international vanity trips.

Representative Lee Jin-sook of the opposition People Power Party led the charge, highlighting that President Lee had undertaken 14 overseas tours visiting 23 countries since taking office. Framing the travel budget as a waste of taxpayer money at a time when ordinary citizens are struggling with living costs, the opposition sought to pin a "neglecting public livelihood" label on the presidential office.

President Lee directly confronted the criticism during a meeting with overseas Korean residents in Brazil. Addressing the audience, Lee argued that face-to-face summit diplomacy is not a luxury, but an essential tool for an export-dependent economy facing complex global supply chain realignments. Restoring bilateral relations, securing energy security, and opening foreign market doors for South Korean corporations require direct presidential engagement, his administration contends, emphasizing that initial diplomatic investments yield long-term national dividends.

The battle of policy vision also extended to domestic social affairs. When PPP Representative Na Kyung-won sarcastically offered praise for President Lee’s stance on multicultural policies, Lee used social media to draw a sharp line between inclusion and fairness. While affirming the state’s duty to support multicultural households, President Lee explicitly rejected any specific policies that create irrational reverse discrimination against South Korean citizens. By framing his approach around pragmatic fairness rather than partisan rhetoric, the administration sought to disarm opposition attempts to leverage cultural grievances.

Polling Fractures: Economic Friction and the Cho Kuk Factor

Despite diplomatic visibility—including a high-profile summit in Germany and positive current account balance figures—President Lee’s approval ratings remain largely locked in a narrow band. Recent Realmeter survey data showed executive approval ticking upward by 1.6 percentage points, but gains were tempered by volatility in domestic equity markets, including sharp drops in the benchmark KOSPI index. Poll methodology continues to reveal a sharp divide: President Lee scored 45.6% approval in telephone interview surveys, compared to 39.4% in automated response system (ARS) polling, reflecting varying enthusiasm between moderate voters and partisan bases.

In party support metrics, competition between the ruling DPK and the main opposition PPP remains fiercely contested. Polls conducted by Flower Research placed DPK support at 41.2% in phone interviews, but the party dipped to 38.1% in ARS surveys—where the opposition PPP surged to 41.9%.

A central variable in this numerical deadlock is the performance of the minor left-leaning Rebuilding Korea Party, led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. The party captured 9.1% in ARS polls and passed the 10% threshold among voters in their 60s. By siphoning off a notable segment of progressive and center-left voters, the Rebuilding Korea Party has effectively capped the ruling DPK's poll ceiling. Political analysts note that unless the DPK establishes a clear strategy regarding coalition building or unity with Cho Kuk’s outfit, the consolidated conservative base of the People Power Party will continue to pose a formidable challenge in parliamentary math.

Eroding Faith in the Bench: Judicial Distrust and Elite Privilege

Beyond party politics, public trust in South Korea’s legal institutions faces a profound crisis. A high-court acquittal of Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon on political finance charges met widespread public skepticism. Surveys indicated that 48% of respondents believed a guilty verdict would have been more appropriate, compared to 42% who agreed with the acquittal. Disapproval of the ruling was particularly high among young women in their 20s and 30s, highlighting growing generational dissatisfaction with how legal standards are applied to powerful politicians.

This cynicism has fueled radical shifts in public opinion regarding the judiciary:

  • 58% of respondents favored introducing artificial intelligence (AI) judges to replace human magistrates, citing concerns over bias and political influence.
  • 60% of respondents supported a parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de over administrative judicial concerns.
  • Public sentiment regarding the impeachment of the Chief Justice remains split at 47.9% in favor and 47.9% opposed, reflecting caution over extreme constitutional remedies despite deep frustration with court delays.

Simultaneously, scandals involving public figures have reinforced perceptions of elite double standards. Prominent journalist Joo Jin-woo has drawn intense public scrutiny following allegations surrounding an exclusive "emperor golf" outing. Despite denials from professional golfer Lydia Ko, 64% of surveyed citizens voiced support for an official investigation into the matter. Joo’s failure to offer a clear public explanation has damaged his standing as a champion of public ethics.

Compounding the sense of systemic uncertainty, ruling party Representative Jin Sung-jun was recently denied an entry visa to the United States. While some foreign policy analysts attribute the decision to routine administrative procedures under strict American immigration rules, others view the denial as an intentional political signal from Washington regarding the current administration’s strategic direction. The incident underscores how domestic political friction in Seoul routinely bleeds into delicate foreign relationships.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters the next legislative cycle, several key developments will signal whether institutional credibility can be restored or if political gridlock will deepen:

  • The Han River Bus Prosecutorial Shift: Watch whether law enforcement steps in to initiate a mandatory criminal investigation into Seoul City officials over the contract award to Gadok Heavy Industries and the alleged destruction of internal audit records.
  • Progressive Alignment: Track whether the ruling Democratic Party attempts a formal legislative alliance or electoral pact with the Rebuilding Korea Party to reclaim dominance in national polling.
  • Judicial Reform Legislation: Monitor whether the National Assembly pushes forward with parliamentary audits targeting the Supreme Court leadership, or if proposals for automated judicial processes gain actual legislative traction.
  • U.S.-Korea Visa Clarifications: Pay attention to official responses from both Washington and Seoul regarding Rep. Jin Sung-jun’s visa status to gauge potential diplomatic friction points under the Lee administration.
