The Wine Scandal: South Korea’s Judiciary Faces a Crisis of Credibility

October 07, 2026

Watching South Korean politics can often feel like witnessing a surreal performance, but the recent National Assembly audit has pushed even the most seasoned observers to sigh in exasperation. In a scene that captures the current degradation of the country's political landscape, the head of the judiciary—the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court—found himself grilled not on constitutional principles, but on the itemized count of wine bottles purchased with taxpayer money. This spectacle, involving Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative opposition party), reveals a disturbing nexus of personal scandal and institutional overreach.

The Scandal of the Judiciary’s "Wine Budget"

The details emerging from the recent government audit are nothing short of farcical. Records submitted to the National Assembly for a two-day period reveal that a staggering 26 bottles of wine were purchased using official business expenses. When questioned about this, the Chief Justice’s response was, at best, evasive. He insisted he does not drink alcohol and claimed the bottles were not gifts, essentially playing dumb by asserting he "never even looked at the credit card."

This "I wasn't the one holding the card" defense has infuriated the public. While it is likely that aides handled the actual transactions, the head of the nation's highest court claiming total ignorance of such significant expenditures—funded by the public—suggests either gross negligence or blatant dishonesty. When pressed by former prosecutors-turned-lawmakers like Lee Sung-yoon and Kim Ki-pyo, the Chief Justice struggled to provide coherent explanations, frequently growing visibly agitated or offering "selective" answers to evade scrutiny. His behavior during the audit, marked by defensive grimaces and dismissive remarks, has undermined the very authority he is sworn to uphold.

The issue goes beyond just wine. Frequent reports of the judiciary using these funds for high-end "omakase" (expensive chef’s-choice sushi) meals have solidified public perception that the judiciary views tax revenue as a private slush fund. This is not merely a bureaucratic mishap; it is a profound ethical failing that demands a full investigation and accountability.

Political Ambitions and the Shadow of Executive Overreach

The tension during the audit hinted at a deeper, more dangerous undercurrent: the judiciary’s perceived desire to challenge the authority of the democratically elected government of President Lee Jae-myung. When asked by Progressive Party lawmaker Son Sol if he harbored ambitions to be a "second Yoon Suk-yeol"—referring to the disgraced, impeached, and incarcerated former president—the Chief Justice reacted with extreme sensitivity, calling the question "insulting." His retort, "I do not plan to declare martial law," was a chilling admission that the prospect of his own political ascension is clearly on his mind.

There is a growing concern that members of the judiciary are positioning themselves as the new standard-bearers for the conservative bloc, which currently lacks a viable path to the presidency. By resisting the administration’s input on judicial appointments and hiding behind vague claims of "customary practice" to justify their autonomy, these judges are effectively attempting to govern from the bench. This is a direct challenge to the constitutional order where the source of power is the mandate given by the people to the President.

A Judiciary Defined by Resentment and Cowardice

Many observers trace this institutional rot back to 2018, following the "judicial scandal" involving former Chief Justice Yang Sung-tae, and the 2022 rise of a prosecutor-turned-president (Yoon Suk-yeol). This shift left many career judges feeling insulted and insecure, leading to a "what do we have that the prosecutors don't?" mentality. This misplaced inferiority complex has turned the judiciary into a hotbed for political maneuvering, with figures like the Chief Justice being propped up as a potential political alternative.

The professional connection between the Chief Justice and current People Power Party leadership, such as Chang Dong-hyuk, further complicates the picture. This isn't just about judicial independence; it is about back-room political alignments. Meanwhile, the silence from the broader legal community on the Supreme Court’s bulletin boards speaks volumes. The cowardice of rank-and-file judges, who choose to stay silent rather than hold their leader accountable for his "wine-and-omakase" habits, is perhaps the clearest sign that the institution has lost its moral compass.

What to Watch Next: The Kim Keon-hee Warrant

The most critical point to watch in the coming weeks is the potential issuance of an arrest warrant for Kim Keon-hee (wife of the incarcerated former president Yoon Suk-yeol). During the audit, the head of the National Court Administration hinted that the Supreme Court could issue a warrant sua sponte—on its own authority—even without a formal request from the prosecution. This suggests that even within the judiciary, the pressure to stop shielding the former First Lady is mounting.

If the Supreme Court continues to play political games, using its power to protect figures associated with the previous, failed administration rather than upholding the law, the backlash will be catastrophic. The judiciary risks not just a loss of credibility, but a complete institutional overhaul. For the supporters of the current administration, the task ahead is clear: the era of "judicial autocracy" must end. Whether through impeachment proceedings or aggressive reform of the National Court Administration, the democratic forces in South Korea are now at a crossroads. The upcoming decisions regarding the arrest warrant and the accountability for the judiciary’s budget will determine whether the institution can be salvaged or if it must be dismantled to protect the nation's democracy.
