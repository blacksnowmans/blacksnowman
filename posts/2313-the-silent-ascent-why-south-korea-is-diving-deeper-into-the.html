The Silent Ascent: Why South Korea is Diving Deeper into the Freediving Industry

November 14, 2026

Imagine the weight of ninety meters of seawater pressing against your chest. At that depth, the lungs are compressed to the size of oranges, the heart rate slows to a crawl, and the world is a silent, crushing blue. For Kateryna Sadurska, a diver from war-torn Ukraine, this was the scene last week as she reached a new world record in the Constant Weight No Fins (CNF) category. Using only her own muscle power and a single breath, she descended and ascended 90 meters (295 feet)—a feat that took three minutes and 33 seconds of absolute physical and mental control.

While Sadurska was redefining the limits of human physiology on the global stage, a different kind of movement was surging thousands of miles away in South Korea. In the bath-warm training pools of Gyeonggi Province and the choppy waters of the Yellow Sea, a burgeoning community of Korean divers is transforming freediving from a niche extreme sport into a mainstream lifestyle and a lucrative market. From the 10th anniversary of the country’s first major competition to the launch of specialized gear by domestic sportswear giants, the "freediving fever" in Korea is signaling a shift in how the nation views leisure, risk, and the pursuit of the "purest" form of sport.

The Purity of the Breath-Hold: Breaking Records and Barriers

To understand why a world record in Ukraine matters to a reader in Seoul or San Francisco, one must understand the discipline of Constant Weight No Fins (CNF). In the world of freediving, this is often considered the "purest" and most difficult discipline. Divers carry no weights to help them sink and wear no fins to help them swim. It is a raw test of buoyancy control and stroke efficiency. Sadurska’s achievement—her tenth world record—was not just a sporting milestone; it was a psychological one. She spoke of bringing hope to her home country, proving that even under the most immense pressure, one can find the strength to return to the surface.

This narrative of resilience resonates deeply in South Korea, where the "hell-Joseon" culture of high-stress work and social competition has driven many young professionals toward solitary, meditative sports. Freediving is unique because you cannot succeed while stressed. If your heart rate is high, you burn oxygen too fast. If your mind is racing, you cannot equalize the pressure in your ears. For many Koreans, the sport serves as a form of "forced meditation."

The global headlines generated by Sadurska were mirrored by domestic celebrations of the Korea Cup, South Korea’s inaugural freediving competition, which recently marked its 10th anniversary. A decade ago, the Korea Cup was a small gathering of enthusiasts in a country that barely understood what freediving was. Today, it is the cornerstone of a professionalizing scene. The longevity of the Korea Cup reflects the maturation of the local community, which has moved past the "fad" stage and into a period of sustained growth. Korean divers are no longer just hobbyists; they are becoming serious contenders on the international circuit, supported by a domestic infrastructure that is rapidly catching up to world standards.

A Decade of Growth: How Korea Built its Own Diving Culture

For years, freediving in Korea was hampered by geography. The peninsula is surrounded by water, but the conditions are often difficult—low visibility, strong currents, and seasonal cold. This led to a unique development: the rise of the "indoor diver." Facilities like DeepStation in Yongin (near Seoul) or K26 in Gapyeong—a 26-meter deep pool that was once the deepest in Asia—provided the perfect, controlled environment for thousands of office workers to earn their certifications.

However, the sport is now moving out of the pools and back into the ocean, and the government is taking notice. In Boseong County, a coastal region in the southern province of South Jeolla, construction is well underway on the Yulpo Marine Leisure Complex. Currently 60% complete and scheduled to open in late 2025, this facility is designed to decentralize the sport. By providing high-tech deep pools and training centers in the south, the government hopes to create a regional hub for marine tourism.

This infrastructure matters because it lowers the barrier to entry. In the past, if you lived in the southern provinces, you either had to drive four hours to the outskirts of Seoul or fly to the Philippines or Egypt to train. The Yulpo complex represents a multi-million dollar bet that freediving and its cousin, scuba diving, are the future of Korean domestic tourism. It is a transition from the traditional "sightseeing" model of tourism to an "experience-based" model, where visitors spend days or weeks mastering a skill.

Gear and Innovation: The Mainstreaming of the Deep

As the community grows, so does the appetite for specialized equipment. This week, Barrel—South Korea’s dominant sportswear brand, which gained fame for its "rash guard" swim shirts—announced the pre-order for its new "Genius Swell" short fins. While professional divers use long, carbon-fiber fins that can cost upwards of $600, Barrel is targeting the entry-level and intermediate market with open-heel short fins.

The strategic move by a major brand like Barrel is telling. It signifies that freediving has moved from the "pro-only" category into the "general consumer" category. The "Genius Swell" fins are designed for pool training and light ocean use, appealing to the "casual diver" who wants better performance than a standard snorkel fin but isn't ready to lug a one-meter-long carbon fin on a plane. By offering a 20% discount (bringing prices down to a more accessible range for hobbyists), Barrel is aiming to lock in the thousands of new divers certifying each year.

The tech side of the market is also seeing "K-innovation." DIVEROID, a Korean startup, is preparing to launch WatchDive on Kickstarter this December. Their premise is simple: why spend $1,000 on a dedicated dive computer when you can turn your existing smartwatch into one? As Apple and Garmin move into the dive space, Korean companies are looking for ways to make the data—depth, dive time, and safety stops—more accessible and social. For a generation of Korean divers who document every "PB" (personal best) on Instagram, the ability to automatically sync dive logs to a smartphone is a major selling point.

The Growing Pains of a High-Risk Sport

With rapid growth comes increased risk. On November 11th, the dangers of the sport were highlighted by a chilling rescue operation near Eueondol Port in Taean, a popular coastal spot on Korea’s west coast. A male diver in his 40s was swept away by a sudden swell during a late-night dive. He was eventually rescued by the Taean Coast Guard, but the incident served as a stark reminder of the "cowboy" culture that can sometimes emerge in booming hobbyist scenes.

The Taean incident involved several critical errors: diving at night in unfavorable weather and, crucially, a failure of the "buddy system." In freediving, the buddy system isn't just a suggestion—it is the only thing that prevents a "blackout" (loss of consciousness due to oxygen deprivation) from becoming a fatality. As more people take up the sport through YouTube tutorials or hurried certification courses, the safety culture is struggling to keep pace with the equipment sales.

International communities, such as those on Reddit, have been debating these very issues. The technical discussions often center on "CO2 Tables"—a method of training the body to tolerate carbon dioxide—and "Lung Stretching." While these are vital for reaching depths like Sadurska’s 90 meters, they can be dangerous if practiced alone or without proper physiological understanding. The Korean scene is currently at a crossroads: it must decide if it will prioritize "faster, deeper" records or "slower, safer" education.

What to Watch Next

The next few months will be pivotal for the Korean freediving market and its global standing. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The "Smartwatch Wars": Watch for the launch of DIVEROID’s WatchDive in December. If it succeeds, it could disrupt the pricing power of traditional dive computer brands like Suunto and Shearwater, making the sport even more accessible to the masses.
  • Regional Expansion: As the Yulpo Marine Leisure Complex nears completion in 2025, look for a surge in diving schools relocating to South Jeolla Province. This could shift the "gravity" of the Korean diving scene away from the Seoul metropolitan area.
  • Safety Regulation: Following the Taean rescue, there is likely to be increased pressure on the Korea Coast Guard and local municipalities to regulate "unorganized" diving. We may see stricter enforcement of buddy system rules or mandatory check-ins at popular entry points.
  • The 100-Meter Barrier: With Sadurska hitting 90m in the no-fins category, the eyes of the diving world are on who will be the first woman to hit 100m. Given the competitive nature of Korean sports, don't be surprised to see a Korean name climbing the world rankings in the coming years.

Freediving in Korea is no longer just about holding one's breath. It has become a complex ecosystem of technology, real estate, and cultural identity. As the nation dives deeper, the challenge will be ensuring that every diver who descends into the silent blue also has the training and the support to find their way back to the surface.
