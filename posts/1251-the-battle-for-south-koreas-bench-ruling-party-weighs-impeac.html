The Battle for South Korea's Bench: Ruling Party Weighs Impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

October 17, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly on October 16, South Korea's ongoing power struggle shifted abruptly toward the apex of the judicial branch. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) escalated its confrontational posture by formally bringing the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae onto the party's official agenda. For months, rank-and-file progressives and civic groups had grumbled about chronic trial delays and perceived partisan bias within the courts. Now, the ruling establishment has turned that simmering frustration into an overt weapon of institutional pressure.

The move comes at a sensitive moment in South Korean politics. With the administration of President Lee Jae-myung advancing its agenda while the primary conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), fights to hold its ground, the judiciary has become the ultimate battleground. By targeting the nation's top judge alongside aggressive parliamentary audits of conservative strongholds like the Seoul Metropolitan Government, the Democratic Party is betting that an uncompromising offensive can overcome public ambivalence and secure political momentum.

Targeting the Top Judge: The Drive to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

The tone from the ruling party leadership hardened noticeably when Kim Min-seok, the DPK leader, announced in an official party meeting that formal internal deliberations over impeaching the Chief Justice had begun. While emphasizing that the party leadership had previously avoided even mentioning the word "impeachment," Kim made clear that the threshold had been crossed. The leadership, he argued, can no longer ignore widespread calls from the party's base to reform a court system they view as compromised.

To understand why a ruling party would contemplate the extraordinary step of impeaching the head of the judiciary, foreign observers must look at the lingering friction over judicial appointments and procedural backlogs. The current judicial hierarchy took shape during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in power, an era that ended with his dramatic impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment. The progressive camp has long asserted that the courts adopted a double standard during that turbulent period, dragging out critical cases while offering a protective shield to conservative figures. For the DPK, moving against Cho is framed not merely as a political skirmish, but as an overdue reckoning with what it characterizes as entrenched judicial privileges.

The conservative opposition reacted with unbridled fury. Representative Jeong Jeom-sik of the People Power Party warned that the moment the DPK formally tables an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho, public sentiment will turn fiercely against the ruling camp, predicting severe political blowback and an early lame-duck phase for the Lee Jae-myung presidency. Conservative figures across the aisle condemned the maneuver with heated rhetoric, labeling it an unconstitutional overreach that borders on insurrection against the separation of powers. The PPP maintains that impeaching the Chief Justice is a blunt instrument designed to subordinate the legal system to executive and legislative majorities.

Auditing the Capital: The Battle Over Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon's Ferry Project

While testing the waters on judicial impeachment, the DPK simultaneously turned its guns on local governance, zeroing in on Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, one of the conservative party's most prominent surviving figures. Operating through the ongoing 2026 parliamentary audit—the annual, sweeping legislative investigation into state and local affairs—the DPK floor leadership launched a coordinated offensive against Oh's signature municipal initiative: the Hangang River Bus project.

Han Byung-do, the DPK floor leader, and lawmakers on the parliamentary Land, Infrastructure and Transport Committee publicly alleged serious irregularities, including kickback schemes and coordinated attempts to destroy internal records within the city administration. DPK lawmakers demanded to know whether Mayor Oh was briefed on these alleged illicit kickbacks and whether City Hall issued directives to purge documentation before auditors could review it. By pressing for key witnesses to be summoned and demanding unredacted files, the ruling party is attempting to strike directly at Oh's administrative credibility.

The clash over Seoul's river transit system is part of a broader tactical confrontation running through the second half of the audit season. The friction spilled over into other legislative bodies as well; in the Culture, Sports and Tourism Committee, disputes over the screening of the documentary film Assassins derailed a scheduled inspection visit to the Busan International Film Festival. DPK floor leader Han denounced the opposition's obstructive maneuvers, insisting that partisan rancor should not be allowed to stifle artistic freedom or derail legislative oversight.

Courting Busan: Constitutional Memory and Economic Blueprints

Beyond the legal and municipal crossfire, the DPK leadership traveled south to the southeastern port city of Busan to hold an on-site Supreme Council meeting, marking their second visit to the region in two weeks. Busan, together with South Gyeongsang Province (collectively known as the PK region), has historically leaned conservative, making it a critical swing battleground for any national governing coalition.

The leadership's visit coincided with the 47th anniversary of the Buma Democratic Uprising—the historic 1979 pro-democracy protests in Busan and Masan that precipitated the collapse of the military dictatorship of Park Chung-hee. Kim Min-seok declared that Busan, alongside the southwestern democracy stronghold of Gwangju, forms one of the two foundational pillars of South Korea's democratic history. He reaffirmed the DPK's commitment to amending the Constitution to explicitly enshrine the spirit of the Buma Uprising into its preamble, directly challenging the conservative People Power Party to move beyond lip service and vote in favor of the revision.

Alongside the appeals to democratic heritage, the party paired its pitch with significant regional economic promises:

  • Maritime Capital Status: Pledging centralized legislative and fiscal support to establish Busan as the nation's undisputed maritime hub.
  • Bukhang Waterfront Development: Supporting the construction of a state-of-the-art dome baseball stadium in the North Port redevelopment district.
  • Balanced Regional Development: Exploring actionable measures to decentralize economic power from the Seoul capital area, including advancing debates over relocating the state-run Korea Development Bank to Busan.

This dual approach—appealing to local democratic pride while offering major infrastructure commitments—demonstrates the DPK's ambition to chip away at conservative dominance in the Yeongnam region well ahead of future electoral cycles.

Grassroots Lawmaking Amid High-Stakes Friction

Beneath the national headlines dominated by high-stakes constitutional battles and partisan confrontations, lawmakers also advanced a slate of practical bills aimed at everyday grievances. For the ruling party, showcasing pragmatic competence is vital to insulating itself from voter fatigue.

A primary legislative push came from Representative Jeon Jin-sook, who demanded the swift passage of legislation granting special judicial police powers to the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). The measure targets so-called samujang hospitals—illicit, corporate-run medical facilities set up by non-licensed operators who exploit regulatory loopholes, defraud patients, and pocket billions of won (millions of dollars) in public insurance subsidies before closing down overnight. Currently, prosecuting these shadowy operations often drags on for over a year due to limited investigative authority, bleeding the national health insurance fund. Granting NHIS investigators direct subpoena and raid powers aims to choke off these financial leaks.

Other lawmakers rolled out targeted bills touching consumer and daily life concerns:

  • Wildlife Protection: Representative Park Hae-cheol sponsored revisions to the Wildlife Protection Act and the Zoo and Aquarium Act to establish strict legal liability for the abandonment or neglect of wild animals.
  • Narcotics Oversight: Representative Nam In-soon introduced a measure requiring mandatory identity verification before physicians can prescribe controlled narcotic medications, plugging holes in the medical monitoring network.
  • Crypto Policy: Representative Park Min-gyu spearheaded efforts to extend the moratorium on taxing virtual assets, a move intended to ease anxiety among the country's vocal demographic of young retail crypto investors.
  • Platform Ticket Scalping: Representative Kim Hyun-jung drafted rules targeting digital ticket brokers who use automated software and online platforms to corner entertainment tickets for extortionate resale.

What to Watch Next

Despite the DPK's flurry of policy initiatives, public sentiment remains complex and volatile. Recent polling data from Gallup Korea illustrates the precarious ground on which the ruling coalition stands. While the Democratic Party's support rating of 36% remains comfortably ahead of the People Power Party's 27%, broader sentiment regarding the next general election paints a different picture: 47% of respondents favored an opposition victory to check the government, compared to just 34% who favored a ruling-party win. Voters may favor the DPK over a weakened conservative opposition, but their appetite for unchecked executive and legislative dominance remains decidedly limited.

In the coming weeks, the decisive questions will center on three fronts:

  • The Impeachment Motion: Observers must watch whether Kim Min-seok and the DPK leadership formally submit an impeachment resolution against Chief Justice Cho to the National Assembly plenary floor, or whether the current rhetoric serves primarily as an escalating pressure tactic to compel administrative concessions from the court.
  • Audit Referrals on City Hall: Watch whether the parliamentary Land Committee refers the Hangang River Bus findings to state prosecutors or investigative authorities, which would pull Mayor Oh Se-hoon into a direct legal defense during his second half in office.
  • The Battle for the Swing Regions: Track whether the DPK's focus on Busan's constitutional heritage and port infrastructure can move the needle in public polling across the conservative-leaning southeast, especially as the debate over amending the national Constitution gathers steam.
