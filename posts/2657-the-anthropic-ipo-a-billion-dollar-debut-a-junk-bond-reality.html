The Anthropic IPO: A Billion-Dollar Debut, A Junk Bond Reality Check

November 24, 2026

For a brief moment, the New York Stock Exchange felt the tremor of a seismic shift. Anthropic, the AI powerhouse, touched down on the public markets with a staggering $110 billion (about 143 trillion won) in fresh capital, setting a record that made even the legendary debuts of giants like SpaceX look modest. But as the ticker settled, the euphoria that greeted the opening price of $83 per share rapidly gave way to a cold, hard morning-after for investors—both in Manhattan and across the Pacific in Seoul.

The story of Anthropic’s public entry is not just a tale of Silicon Valley excess; it is a case study in the widening chasm between the speculative frenzy of the stock market and the skeptical, bottom-line reality of the debt markets. For South Korea, a nation heavily invested in the global AI narrative, this volatility has had immediate and painful consequences.

The $83 Mirage: High Hopes and Market Whiplash

When Anthropic priced its shares at $83, the market was quick to crown it a trillion-dollar company—a valuation that pushed it into the rarified air of the world's most elite tech firms. Early trading saw a 17% surge, a burst of optimism that suggested the "AI Gold Rush" was far from over. However, the momentum evaporated as quickly as it had appeared. A wave of profit-taking, fueled by doubts about the sustainability of current AI business models, sent the stock tumbling back toward its launch price.

The volatility was so intense that market analysts pointed to an "exit probability" spike—a metric suggesting that as the hype cooled, the conviction of early buyers was rapidly fraying. Even the intervention of Cathie Wood’s ARK Invest, which snapped up 935,000 shares at the eleventh hour, served only as a temporary floor rather than a turning point. For the average retail investor, Anthropic’s debut became a dizzying lesson in how quickly the "Next Big Thing" can turn into a battle of attrition between long-term believers and short-term opportunists.

The Debt Market’s Cold Shower: Why "Junk" Labels Matter

While equity investors were busy debating price-to-earnings multiples, the bond market was delivering a much harsher verdict. Despite the massive cash infusion, major financial observers, including the Wall Street Journal, have signaled that Anthropic’s corporate debt is likely to be viewed as "junk" (speculative grade).

The contradiction is stark: in the stock market, Anthropic is a visionary leader; in the bond market, it is a high-risk borrower. This divide stems from the company's promise to pour $900 billion into infrastructure—an eye-watering commitment that requires consistent, massive capital expenditure. When your computing costs are rising by 58%—even as you scale—the bond market begins to worry less about your potential to change the world and more about your ability to pay interest on your debt.

The friction is exacerbated by the leadership of the Amodei siblings, Dario and Daniela. Their reputation for a rigid, sometimes combative approach toward investment banks—reportedly demanding that underwriters drop rivals like OpenAI—has created a frosty relationship with the very institutions needed to manage their ongoing financial needs. It is a bold, uncompromising strategy, but one that raises questions about whether their governance can survive the grueling demands of public markets.

Shockwaves in Seoul: The SK Telecom Paradox

The ripples of this volatility traveled fast, hitting South Korea’s tech sector with surprising force. SK Telecom, the country’s largest telecommunications provider and a major strategic partner of Anthropic, found itself in an ironic trap. Having been marketed to investors as a primary beneficiary of the AI revolution, SK Telecom saw its stock price crater by 20% in just two trading days following the IPO.

For many domestic investors, the Anthropic IPO was supposed to be the validation of their thesis on SK Telecom. Instead, it became a "sell the news" event. As Anthropic’s stock wavered, the "AI premium" baked into Korean shares evaporated, leaving local investors to grapple with the reality that global AI market instability is now a domestic concern. Further complicating the landscape is the launch of new "LLM (Large Language Model) ETFs" by firms like Mirae Asset. While these funds provide long-awaited access to global AI players, they also threaten to pass the high-stakes volatility of the US market directly onto South Korean pension funds and passive retail investors.

What Comes Next?

Despite the market turbulence, the technological ambition remains undeterred. Politicians and local governments in Korea are already vying for a piece of the action. Figures like legislator Lim Moon-young have begun formal discussions with Anthropic leadership, proposing the establishment of AI research centers in Gwangju, specifically aiming to integrate Anthropic’s models with South Korea’s proprietary NPU (Neural Processing Unit) chips. It is a strategic move to hedge against total reliance on foreign hardware.

Looking ahead, there are three things to watch:

  • The "Junk" Reality: Watch for Anthropic’s next debt issuance. If they are forced to borrow at prohibitive interest rates, it will confirm the bond market’s fears and likely trigger another sell-off in their stock.
  • SK Telecom’s Pivot: Whether the company can decouple its share price from the daily volatility of Anthropic by showing concrete revenue growth from its own internal AI applications.
  • Infrastructure Synergy: Keep an eye on whether the proposed Gwangju research collaboration moves beyond a "love call" into a signed, operational partnership. If successful, it could provide the stable, tangible use-case the market currently doubts Anthropic possesses.

The AI revolution is entering a phase of financial maturity. The days of blind, hype-fueled investing are ending, replaced by a much more rigorous—and unforgiving—scrutiny. For companies like Anthropic, the test is no longer just about building the smartest model; it is about proving they can survive the cold logic of the balance sheet.
