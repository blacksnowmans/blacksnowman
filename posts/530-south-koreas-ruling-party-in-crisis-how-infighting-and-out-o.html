South Korea’s Ruling Party in Crisis: How Infighting and Out-of-Touch Strategies Are Derailing the Progressives

September 10, 2026

Walk the streets of any South Korean neighborhood right now, and you will hear a consistent, exhausting refrain at dinner tables and cafes: people are struggling to pay rent, everyday goods cost more than they did last year, and ordinary households feel entirely squeezed. Yet, if you turn on the television or look at the political headlines coming out of Seoul, the ruling camp seems to inhabit a completely different reality. Instead of addressing the biting economic anxiety felt nationwide, the country’s progressive establishment is tied up in internal backstabbing, sliding approval ratings, and bafflingly tone-deaf policy debates. How did South Korea’s ruling party manage to plunge into such a deep political crisis, and who are these politicians actually serving?

Kim Min-seok and the Politics of Self-Preservation

At the center of the current storm is Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea's main liberal party. Once heralded as a senior figure capable of steadying the ship, Kim has instead become a lightning rod for controversy, with critics both inside and outside the party accusing him of opportunistic maneuvering. Severe criticism has been leveled against his leadership, with detractors labeling him a political turncoat more focused on his own survival than the collective wellbeing of the progressive movement. Observers note uncomfortable parallels between his current trajectory and past internal fractures within the party.

Rather than projecting authority, Kim has increasingly looked like a political liability. His attempts to build internal media outlets to counter conservative commentary largely fizzled into embarrassing spectacles, and attempts to muzzle critical online YouTubers through legal threats backfired, leading to mounting countersuits against him. Reports that tens of thousands of party members contested the legitimacy of his initial election as leader underscore a profound crisis of internal authority. Whether it was shifting his proposed election run from Busan to Pyeongtaek and parachuting favored associates into local races, his moves have alienated the grassroots base. In South Korean politics, where loyalty and perceived authenticity matter deeply, Kim's shifting stances look to many like the worst kind of political opportunism.

To compound the damage, when crises erupt, party loyalists rush to deploy a defensive "both-sides-ism," attempting to dilute blame by claiming that everyone is equally at fault. When Senior Presidential Secretary for Political Affairs Hong Eui-pyo went on a popular political podcast to claim shared blame, it did little to soothe an angry base. True democratic accountability requires acknowledging failure and taking responsibility; instead, party insiders have relied on defensive wordplay and superficial calls for a "horizontal party-presidency relationship" that ultimately mean very little to voters watching their purchasing power evaporate.

The Blue House Slump and Blundering Communications

The situation inside the Blue House—the executive office of South Korea's presidency—is equally grim. As presidential approval ratings crater against a backdrop of soaring living costs and stagnant consumer spending, the administration’s attempts at damage control have repeatedly backfired. The decision to dispatch Secretary Hong onto a major political broadcast to defend the administration resulted in a cascade of factual errors and public incredulity. When a senior official’s defense amounts to procedural excuses rather than substantive answers, the public is left wondering whether the executive branch understands the severity of the crisis at all.

Voters increasingly feel that the presidential office is either completely out of touch or willfully ignoring the economic pain on the ground. The president has remained largely behind the scenes, leaving aides to test the waters with half-baked media appearances that satisfy no one. This communication vacuum has created a vicious cycle: falling poll numbers are treated merely as PR problems rather than clear warning signs from a public whose patience has run thin. Without decisive leadership and frank admissions of missteps, executive attempts to manage the narrative simply alienate voters further.

Constitutional Reform Out of Nowhere and Superficial Stunts

Compounding these communication blunders is a staggering lack of political timing. With small businesses struggling and household debt reaching critical thresholds, DPK leader Kim Min-seok chose this exact moment to suddenly push for major constitutional reform. While debates over revising the nation's power structure or enshrining historical democratic milestones are important under the right conditions, introducing them while citizens are worried about putting food on the table strikes many as profoundly tone-deaf. Critics smell the familiar scent of political maneuvering aimed at securing a parliamentary cabinet system rather than delivering tangible economic relief.

If the constitutional push felt disconnected, Kim’s proposal to rearrange the National Assembly’s plenary chamber seating alphabetically pushed absurdity into the mainstream. The idea that mixing lawmakers by their last names would suddenly foster bipartisan harmony or bridge deep ideological divides was met with widespread mockery. Voters are not exhausted because politicians sit too far apart from each other; they are exhausted because the political class refuses to engage in serious, substantive competition over policies that actually affect daily life.

Police Reform and the Self-Inflicted Wounds of the Progressive Camp

Beyond institutional grandstanding, the ruling party's legislative priorities are raising alarm bells in the security sector. While the DPK has long championed structural overhauls—shifting its focus from prosecuting institutional corruption to restructuring the national police force—critics argue these efforts ignore the realities on the ground. South Korea boasts world-class public safety largely due to the grueling work of frontline officers. Yet, proposed reforms risk loading police forces with heavier responsibilities without providing the necessary budgetary support or manpower increases, treating law enforcement as a target for blame rather than an essential public service.

Perhaps the most concerning aspect of the current crisis for the progressives is how effectively they are dismantling their own support networks. Unlike past administrations that maintained tight discipline during difficult stretches, the current ruling camp is fracturing from within. Supportive media commentators and prominent progressive YouTubers have found themselves sidelined or disillusioned, unable to mount the robust defenses seen in previous political battles. With friendly media firewalls crumbling, the administration finds itself completely exposed to relentless external attacks.

As South Korea looks toward upcoming local and parliamentary battlegrounds, the DPK faces a stark choice. If it continues to indulge in internal feuds, out-of-touch constitutional debates, and superficial seating arrangements while ignoring the economic distress of ordinary citizens, it risks squandering the political capital that brought it to power. Whether the party can rebuild its fractured defenses and reconnect with the public before it is too late remains the defining question of Korean politics this season.
