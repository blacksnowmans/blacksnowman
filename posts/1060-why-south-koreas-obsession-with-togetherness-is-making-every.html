Why South Korea's Obsession with Togetherness is Making Everyone Miserable

October 13, 2026

We live our lives surrounded by an unspoken mandate: be happy, be social, and never, under any circumstances, be alone. From the moment we are old enough to understand schoolyard hierarchies, spending time by oneself is treated as a social failure—a sign of awkwardness, a symptom of isolation, or worse, a rejection of the group.

Yet, for many, the quietest moments of the day—reading a book alone in a study, putting thoughts down on paper without an audience—offer a profound sense of relief. It raises a quiet, nagging question: What if the pressure to constantly mingle is entirely wrong for us?

This tension was recently put into sharp focus by a prominent public intellectual in South Korea, author and former politician You Si-min, during an appearance on a web talk show. Rather than treating loneliness as a pathological condition to be cured, his reflections cut straight to the core of why modern society views solitude with such deep-seated terror.

The Evolutionary Trap of Social Isolation

To understand why South Koreans—and modern humans generally—feel such acute anxiety about being alone, we have to look back further than the smartphone, the 9-to-5 grind, or modern urbanization. We have to look back hundreds of thousands of years.

Humans are fundamentally eusocial animals, much like bees or ants. For our ancestors roaming the prehistoric wilderness, being cast out of the tribe was not an awkward social faux pas; it was a death sentence. Survival required cooperation, shared labor, and collective defense. Over millennia, evolution hardwired a biological alarm system directly into our subconscious. When we see someone living life entirely on their own, a quiet alarm bell goes off in our heads: What if that happens to me?

This ancestral survival mechanism has warped into a modern psychological straitjacket. Today, as South Korea wrestles with a surge in single-person households and rising rates of social isolation—often discussed alongside the tragic phenomenon of solitary deaths—the fear of being alone is no longer about physical survival. It has transformed into a profound cultural anxiety.

Society still views the person who quietly keeps to themselves with a sideways glance, assuming a lack of social skills. We mistake a deliberate choice for solitude with forced exile. But as You noted, there is a world of difference between being pushed away by others and closing your own door by choice. The former is devastating loneliness; the latter is freedom.

Defying the "Selfish Gene" and the Myth of Happiness

Modern life compounds this by wrapping our existence in the relentless tyranny of mandatory happiness. We are fed the cultural narrative that every moment should be filled with joy, productivity, and connection. If you aren't smiling, posting, or socializing, something must be broken.

This is where human consciousness performs its most remarkable trick: rebelling against our own biology. Richard Dawkins popularized the concept of humans as biological machines driven entirely by "selfish genes" programmed exclusively for survival and reproduction. Yet, Homo sapiens did something entirely unexpected with the brains built for survival—we started asking abstract, unhelpful philosophical questions like Who am I? and Why do I exist?

When individuals make the conscious choice to embrace solitude, they are actively overriding the genetic imperative of collective tribal survival. It is a high-level cognitive evolution—a declaration that one will not live purely as a slave to biological propagation or societal peer pressure.

This same critical lens applies to how South Korean society views its plunging birth rate—which hit a devastating low of 0.75 children per woman, prompting no shortage of apocalyptic prognostications from foreign observers. While outside commentators frequently look at the numbers and declare the country doomed, You argues that the future is unwritten; society may simply undergo a massive structural transformation as the population adjusts.

The real trap here is what philosophers call the "naturalistic fallacy"—the lazy assumption that whatever exists naturally must be right or good. Society often looks at the crushing burdens placed on young people, where child-rearing and domestic labor fall disproportionately on women, and treats it as an immutable law of nature. In reality, young people are opting out of marriage and children not because they lack patriotism, but because surviving in a hyper-competitive economy leaves them with zero margin for error. When simply keeping oneself alive feels like an extreme sport, demanding that individuals sacrifice their lives for the abstract concept of national survival is a non-starter.

Redefining a Life Well-Lived

So, if the pursuit of relentless happiness and compliance with tribal expectations are both traps, what should a well-rounded human life actually look like?

You suggests that a balanced life rests on four core pillars: primary work, play, love, and solidarity with others. Many people manage to make a good living and indulge in lavish hobbies, but a life built solely on work and play remains hollow. It is only when self-centered pursuits are balanced with a genuine commitment to helping and connecting with others that life acquires real depth.

Crucially, this balance cannot be forced through rigid societal timelines. Societies that demand twenty-year-olds instantly find their "passion" or map out a grand ambition are inflicting unnecessary violence on the young mind. Life is an ongoing process of wandering, fumbling, and stumbling into self-awareness. Discovering what you actually care about—whether it is a career in writing or simply finding peace in daily routines—takes time.

The same goes for intellectual pursuits. Generations of students have been bullied by cultural expectations into grinding through dense, intimidating classics just to check a box for cultural literacy. But books, much like people, require chemistry. If a universally praised masterpiece leaves you cold and bored, putting it down isn't a failure of intellect; it's simply acknowledging that the text doesn't speak to who you are *right now*.

What to Watch Next

  • Policy shifts regarding single-person households: Watch how South Korean municipal and national agencies adapt social welfare infrastructure to accommodate a population where solo living is fast becoming the default rather than the exception.
  • Economic pressure on the younger demographic: Keep an eye on ongoing labor and housing reforms under the current administration, which will dictate whether young adults ever feel secure enough to step off the hyper-competitive treadmill.
  • Cultural shifts in media narratives: Notice the growing popularity of essays, television shows, and creator content that normalize solitude, slow living, and the rejection of traditional milestone pressures.

Ultimately, life is inherently solitary, and the color you choose to paint that solitude is entirely up to you. Letting go of the scripts written by evolutionary instincts and societal scolding is the first real step toward claiming your own time.
