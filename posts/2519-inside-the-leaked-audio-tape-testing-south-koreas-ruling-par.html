Inside the Leaked Audio Tape Testing South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 19, 2026

On October 16, inside a closed-door gathering of local party activists in Namyangju, a commuter city northeast of Seoul, Senior Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee spoke candidly for 70 minutes. Nearly a month later, on November 12 and 13, 2026, excerpts from a recording of that meeting surfaced in South Korean outlets Sisa Journal and Hankook Ilbo, shattering the fragile calm inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). Within 40 minutes of the presidential office dismissing Choi’s remarks as groundless, DPK leader Kim Min-seok ordered an internal ethics investigation.

Yet as follow-up reporting by JoongAng Ilbo and broadcaster JTBC—alongside legal analysis on journalist Hong Sa-hoon’s YouTube program on November 19—has peeled back the layers of the episode, the crisis has moved well beyond one lawmaker’s loose tongue. What began as a dispute over historical claims has turned into a fierce battle over Kim Min-seok’s leadership, internal party discipline, and a puzzling month-long gap between when top officials first received the audio and when they chose to act.

Seventy Minutes in Namyangju and an Explosive Apology

To understand why a local meeting triggered such panic in Seoul, it helps to grasp the political backdrop. South Korea is currently led by President Lee Jae-myung of the DPK, following the impeachment, removal from office, and imprisonment of his predecessor, Yoon Suk-yeol. Because the DPK’s governing mandate is tied closely to the events surrounding that impeachment, any suggestion that Lee wavered at the decisive moment strikes at the heart of his administration’s legitimacy.

According to the leaked audio reported by Sisa Journal and Hankook Ilbo, Choi told Namyangju party members that Lee—then the opposition leader—had initially opposed impeaching Yoon Suk-yeol and instead leaned toward pushing for Yoon’s resignation. She further claimed Lee had considered partnering with then-Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, alleging that veteran DPK lawmaker Park Ji-won supplied the political rationale for that approach.

The Blue House, South Korea’s presidential office, immediately rejected the account as baseless. Park Ji-won also pushed back on Facebook on November 13, stating he had never discussed such matters with Choi. According to Park, after the impeachment bill passed, lawmakers merely pressured Han Duck-soo—who held authority to appoint Constitutional Court justices—and impeached Han only after he refused, while Lee worked relentlessly to seat a full nine-justice bench.

Facing mounting backlash, Choi apologized publicly at a National Assembly press conference on November 13 and again at a party Supreme Council meeting on November 16, blaming a "memory lapse" and confusion. Still, fellow Supreme Council members Seo Mi-hwa and Park Sun-won rejected that explanation, with Park pointing to a social media phrase recently used by President Lee: "arrows flying from behind one’s back."

The rest of the 70-minute tape revealed that Choi’s grievances ran far deeper than a single historical anecdote. She openly questioned Kim Min-seok’s grip on the party, noting he had pledged to raise approval ratings by 10 percentage points within 100 days of taking office.

"At 100 days, the approval rating is still 30 percent. Then that is a very serious matter."

Saying she wished support would at least reach 40 percent, Choi told local members that lawmakers were staying quiet not out of fear over Kim’s power to hand out election nominations—questioning whether he could even oversee nominations under such numbers—but to avoid destabilizing a leader before his 100-day mark in late November. She also broke with Kim’s call for "grand integration," arguing instead for an immediate merger with the minor Rebuilding Korea Party and lamenting that the only rising political demographic she saw was young men in their 20s and 30s, whom she dismissed as aligned with "Ilbe," a notorious far-right South Korean online forum.

Choi spared few figures in the current administration either. On the tape, she called Presidential Chief of Staff Lim Kwang-hyun—appointed just two days earlier—a "yes-man," labeled Serious Crimes Investigation Agency chief Kim Ji-yong an "opportunist," and blamed Minister Song Mi-ryeong over a farmland inspection controversy that she claimed cost five percentage points in approval. She also mentioned receiving unverified tips and recordings alleging that the Unification Church and Shincheonji—a controversial South Korean religious sect frequently scrutinized over mass political sign-ups—had backed Kim Min-seok in Gwangju, though she said she did not release them because their credibility could not be confirmed. At the same time, she criticized Supreme Council member Park Sun-won’s cross-checking of a 100,000-name membership list for Shincheonji infiltration as illegal. Underscoring how tangled the religious accusations have become, reporters separately quoted a Gwangju Shincheonji member saying sect members planned to vote for Kim’s rival, former party leader Jung Chung-rae, because Kim had harassed them.

The Missing Month: What Did Party Leaders Know and When?

As the shock of Choi’s remarks settled, attention shifted to a glaring timeline gap. According to accounts compiled by JoongAng Ilbo, JTBC, and party officials, a whistleblower sent the audio file on October 19—just three days after the Namyangju meeting—to Kim Min-seok’s aide, his chief of staff Kim Tae-sun, and DPK lawmaker Kim Woo-young, who had also met local Namyangju members on October 16 and obtained portions of the tape. Yet for 24 days, the leadership took no official action, moving only after the Sisa Journal report and the Blue House’s denial on November 12.

Kim Min-seok responded on Facebook that Kim Woo-young had sent him only the excerpts concerning Kim Min-seok personally in mid-October, and that he had chosen to overlook them—arguing the full recording revealed in November was completely different in character. Allies of the president and the party leadership maintain that Kim Min-seok exercised restraint to avoid internal strife until the president’s own legitimacy came under fire.

On the November 19 broadcast of the Hong Sa-hoon Show, however, attorneys Jung Pil-seung and Kim Kwang-min raised sharp doubts about that explanation. Either Kim Woo-young somehow stripped out every explosive comment about the president and government from a 70-minute meeting and reported only four items concerning Kim Min-seok, they noted, or Kim Min-seok knew the broader contents all along and waited for a politically opportune moment.

Accounts of how the file moved through party offices have only deepened those questions. According to JoongAng Ilbo and JTBC, Kim Min-seok’s aide claimed he received only photos, while Chief of Staff Kim Tae-sun said the email went straight to his spam folder—an excuse Jung Pil-seung dismissed as disqualifying for a senior aide. Meanwhile, Kim Woo-young stated on November 14 that a shocked local party member had tipped off a youth political panelist, who passed word to him. Yet that panelist claimed he had left the message unread amid a flood of tips until the controversy erupted in November, when he thought, "Oh, I have that," and opened it without ever forwarding it. Journalist Hong Sa-hoon and the two attorneys questioned how anyone could locate an unopened file in a crowded inbox on cue once news broke, suggesting that the allegation linking Kim Min-seok to Shincheonji was what initially alarmed insiders before pro-Lee lawmakers saw the tape and news spread rapidly.

Former DPK leader Jung Chung-rae seized on the timeline on November 18, breaking his silence on Facebook to oppose disciplining Choi.

"The confession that the recording was already obtained last month is shocking. Was it really only part of it, as leader Kim Min-seok says? And was the timing deliberately managed? These questions arise."

Jung Pil-seung and Kim Kwang-min also pointed out that just two weeks earlier, Kim Min-seok had publicly brushed off Shincheonji allegations as baseless—fueling speculation that he downplayed personal warnings until the leaked presidential remarks gave him cover to launch an ethics probe.

Loyalty Contests and the Legal Risk of Factional Discipline

Inside the DPK’s Supreme Council, the ethics investigation has split leadership along factional lines. Hardliners such as lawmakers Choi Hyun-il and Kim Moon-soo are demanding heavy sanctions—comparing the situation to former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook’s one-year party suspension over controversial remarks—and warning that a light penalty would destroy party credibility. By contrast, Supreme Council members Han Min-soo and Lee Sung-yoon boycotted meetings to protest that Kim ordered the investigation without consulting Choi Min-hee, demanding that a separate disciplinary petition filed against Kim Min-seok himself be treated with equal rigor.

Attorney Kim Kwang-min diagnosed the push for harsh punishment as a classic symptom of weak leadership. Leaders unsure of their authority, he argued, constantly test subordinates’ loyalty, prompting allies to prove their devotion through aggressive, over-the-top demands. Analysts pointed to September’s one-year suspension of Busan city councilor Cho Yong-ho for harshly criticizing Kim Min-seok online, as well as Kim’s threat to expel anyone leaking closed-door meetings. Even with Blue House backing and a joint statement of support from 55 freshman lawmakers, Kim—who won the party leadership campaigning on a "pro-Jung, pro-Lee" platform rather than building his own independent base—has struggled to subdue the pro-Jung Chung-rae wing.

Recent court rulings involving South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), offer a stark legal warning for the DPK. The Seoul Southern District Court has already granted injunctions suspending a one-year party penalty against PPP lawmaker Bae Hyun-jin and disciplinary measures against former PPP Supreme Council member Kim Jong-hyuk, ruling that while political parties enjoy autonomy, internal punishments remain subject to judicial review if they violate constitutional or legal due process. PPP lawmaker Seo Beom-soo has likewise sought an injunction against a 10-month suspension, citing procedural flaws such as a concealed ethics committee roster, amid escalating infighting involving PPP leader Jang Dong-hyuk’s camp, ethics chair Yoon Min-woo, and allies of independent lawmaker Han Dong-hoon. If the DPK’s Ethics Tribunal appears to act as a factional tool rather than an impartial body, the ruling party risks dragging its internal feuds straight into the courtroom.

What to Watch Next

As the DPK tries to contain the fallout from 70 minutes in Namyangju, several concrete developments in the coming weeks will determine whether Kim Min-seok consolidates his authority or faces a wider rebellion:

  • The 100-day polling test: As Kim Min-seok reaches 100 days in office in late November, watch whether party approval ratings move off the 30 percent mark Choi cited—or stagnate, giving critics fresh ammunition to demand a leadership shake-up.
  • The Ethics Tribunal’s ruling and symmetry: Observe how the party’s ethics body rules on Choi Min-hee and whether it handles the pending disciplinary petition against Kim Min-seok by the same standard. A heavy penalty perceived as procedurally flawed could trigger court injunctions mirroring the PPP’s legal battles.
  • The Shincheonji cross-check report: Attorneys Jung Pil-seung and Kim Kwang-min have urged the DPK to release the full Shincheonji membership cross-check report that Kim Min-seok allegedly obtained during his leadership campaign, arguing that keeping it secret only fuels conspiracy theories.
  • External institutional pressures: All of this internal turbulence unfolds alongside major external tests for the ruling party, including former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo’s upcoming Supreme Court appeal and how the DPK navigates its tense confrontation with Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s judiciary.

Whether the ethics probe restores discipline or deepens the divide between South Korea’s ruling party factions will depend on whether party leaders prioritize transparent procedure over short-term political survival.
