The Myth of the Savior: Why South Korea’s Search for a 'Great Leader' is Failing

September 30, 2026

We often dream of a figure appearing like a comet, ready to slice through the gordian knots of our societal problems with a single stroke of genius. It is a form of "Messiah syndrome"—a persistent, seductive hope that has long haunted the South Korean political landscape. This collective yearning for a perfect leader is not just a passing phase; it is a fundamental challenge to the health of the nation’s democracy. The time has come to ask a difficult, foundational question: Is the "great leader" we crave a reality, or is it a phantom we have invented to excuse ourselves from our own civic responsibilities?

The Weight of Sovereignty in a Republic

In South Korea, democracy is frequently misunderstood as a mere mechanical exercise—a tool utilized once every few years when we head to the polling station. However, the true essence of a citizen in a democratic republic goes far beyond casting a ballot. As the prominent South Korean intellectual and former politician Rhyu Si-min has argued, a true citizen is someone who feels a sense of pride and weight equivalent to that of the President. This is not arrogance; it is the somber realization that the fate of the community rests, in part, in one’s own hands.

A society is only as capable and just as its citizens are "awake." When people view themselves as mere cogs in a machine or helpless subjects, they become prime targets for manipulation by those in power. If the majority of citizens disengage, the political space inevitably turns into a playground for a small elite looking to secure their own private interests. In this sense, the absence of civic pride is a direct precursor to democratic decay.

The dilemma of modern democracy is that it does not guarantee competence. We often look to "meritocracy"—the idea that those with the most virtue and skill should lead. Yet, as the philosopher Michael Sandel has noted, when we reduce success to a mere product of individual effort, society splits into two dangerous extremes: the arrogance of the winners and the humiliation of the losers. True meritocracy cannot be measured by a market price tag; it must be found in the harmony between genuine virtue and the ability to serve the common good.

Reflecting on a Decade of Democratic Governance

South Korea’s modern political history, spanning the progressive administrations of the early 2000s—often referred to as the "Participatory Government" and the "Government of the People"—was marked by a desperate, sincere effort to uphold democratic principles and the rule of law. Yet, critics rightly point out that while these governments succeeded in formal democratic structures, they often lacked the "competence" to tangibly improve the daily lives of citizens. Economic disparities widened, and a reliance on neoliberal globalization—often pursued in the name of efficiency—left many behind.

The populism we see today is, in many ways, a visceral reaction to that brand of elite-led governance. When a society uses meritocracy as a cudgel, labeling those who fail as simply "not trying hard enough," democracy loses its moral authority. The failure of these past governments suggests that we must move away from the charisma of individual leaders and toward structural reform. We must identify why systemic economic structures remained so rigid despite political shifts, and why public discourse was so often outsourced to technocrats rather than debated by the people.

The "Everyday Fascism" in Our Daily Lives

Democratic reform is not just about changing laws in the National Assembly; it is about confronting the "micro-fascism" embedded in our daily lives. From the authoritarian drinking cultures forced upon university freshmen to the rigid, top-down hierarchies in the workplace and the often-violent methods of discipline in some households, the lack of democracy in our private spaces reflects a deeper national malaise. If we cannot respect others in our daily lives, we cannot expect our political life to be anything other than a cesspool of intolerance.

True civic duty is a defensive mechanism against those who seek to manipulate the public for profit. It requires the courage to say "no" to undemocratic practices every day—not just on election day. Democracy is not a gift dropped from the heavens; it is a process won through the constant, exhausting struggle to treat one another with dignity.

The Urgent Tasks Ahead

To move forward, South Korean society faces two primary, non-negotiable tasks.

  • Establishing Fair Rules and the Rule of Law: The law must be a shield for the weak, not a sword for the strong. Currently, there is a pervasive perception that those close to power receive "indulgences" for their actions, while ordinary citizens are held to an impossibly harsh standard. Correcting this "tilted playing field" is the most urgent duty of a sovereign citizen. We must demand that the law be applied with equal rigor to everyone, regardless of their status.
  • Bridging the Economic Gap: A democracy that fails to answer the question of "how to survive" is a failed democracy. When the top 1% controls a vast share of the nation's wealth and the bottom 50% sees no path to a better future, social cohesion becomes impossible. The state must prioritize a safety net that protects the human dignity of the irregular worker, the small-business owner, and the laborer.

The cold reality is that in many developed nations, the myth of the "meritocratic ladder" has already statistically collapsed. Statistics often show that the vast majority of students at elite institutions come from the top 20% of income brackets, proving that the belief that "anyone can make it if they try hard enough" is increasingly a fiction. In a society where the ladder of social mobility has been kicked away, politics can no longer be a source of hope unless it addresses these structural inequalities.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward the future of South Korea, watch how the citizenry engages with the upcoming legislative and political cycles. Are we still looking for a "savior," or are we beginning to demand concrete, systemic solutions to inequality? The shift from a passive, waiting public to an active, designing public is the only trend that matters.

The goal is not to find a hero who will fix everything for us; it is to create a society where such a hero is unnecessary—a society where the dignity of every individual is protected by the rules we collectively uphold. The change starts with the refusal to be a bystander. If we want a warmer, fairer Korea tomorrow, we must shed our collective passivity today.
