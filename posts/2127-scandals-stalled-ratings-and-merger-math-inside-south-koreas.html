Scandals, Stalled Ratings, and Merger Math: Inside South Korea's High-Stakes Political Realignment

November 09, 2026

Inside South Korea’s political sphere, public sentiment rarely grants a grace period to missteps or administrative opacity. Only months into the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the political mood in Seoul is shifting from reformist expectation to sharp, impatient scrutiny. The public is demanding two things at once: decisive structural reforms to clear away past abuses, and immediate stability in their daily economic lives.

Yet a wave of recent polling numbers and party infighting is flashing warning lights across the political spectrum. On one side, the conservative opposition is rallying behind former Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon despite a mounting scandal over his flagship Han River ferry project and a ticking clock on potential criminal charges. On the other side, President Lee’s governing coalition faces stagnating approval ratings, financial market turmoil, and a fractious debate over whether to merge the ruling Democratic Party of Korea with its smaller progressive ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party. How these intersecting crises play out will shape South Korea’s political trajectory for years to come.

The "Hangang Bus" Audit Scandal and Conservative Shielding

A One-Page Audit Document Exposes Administrative Misdirection

The controversy surrounding former Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a leading figure in the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP), has taken a serious legal turn. At the center of the storm is the "Hangang Bus" project—an ambitious river bus service introduced during Oh's tenure in City Hall. Recent testimony from individuals questioned by police reveals a striking gap between official administrative records and the former mayor's public claims.

When citizens filed a formal request with the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI)—South Korea's top government oversight agency—demanding an inspection of the Seoul Metropolitan Government's handling of the project, the BAI issued a straightforward administrative dismissal (*gak-ha*). The reason stated in the official one-page document was purely procedural: because the Seoul Metropolitan Government already possesses its own internal audit body, external audit requests must first be handled internally unless specific exceptions apply. The BAI simply directed Seoul City Hall to conduct its own self-audit.

However, when speaking before the Seoul Metropolitan Council, Mayor Oh presented a starkly different narrative. He claimed on the council floor that the audit could not proceed because the BAI was busy handling separate audit requests submitted by the National Assembly. Nothing in the BAI’s written response supported this claim. Later, on the day he registered his candidacy for the March local elections, Oh declared before news cameras that "the BAI audit cleared all suspicions" regarding the Hangang Bus. That video recording has now been submitted to police as key evidence of alleged false statements under the Public Official Election Act.

The central issue now is time. Under South Korean election law, the statute of limitations for charges of spreading false information during an election campaign expires in less than 30 days. Investigators are facing intense pressure to determine whether Oh deliberately mischaracterized an administrative dismissal as a clean bill of health to deceive voters before the clock runs out.

A Controversial Residence Dinner Raises Interference Concerns

Compounding the controversy is a revelation that has drawn heavy criticism from civil society. Shortly after police conducted interviews with the whistleblowers who filed the complaints against Oh, a group of People Power Party lawmakers serving on the National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee attended a private dinner at Oh’s official residence.

This parliamentary committee holds direct budgetary and oversight authority over the National Police Agency. For committee members from the suspect's own party to gather for a closed-door dinner with a high-profile target of an active police investigation—with the statute of limitations under a month away—has raised immediate suspicions of political pressure.

Key Takeaway: The Board of Audit and Inspection's procedural dismissal was not an exoneration. Attempting to frame internal audit referrals as official clearance—while key parliamentary members meet privately with the suspect—threatens to undermine basic standards of executive accountability and legal justice.

President Lee's Stagnating Polling and Systemic Distrust

Critical Signals from Realmeter and Opinion Poll Flower

While the opposition battles legal risks, President Lee Jae-myung and the governing Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) are confronting their own troubling signals from public opinion polls. Data from Realmeter shows President Lee’s positive job performance rating dropping to 35.9%, down 1.0 percentage point from the previous week. After briefly approaching the 40% threshold mid-week, his approval trended downward in the latter half of the polling period.

Party support figures reflect a similarly tight landscape. Approval for the governing Democratic Party fell 1.8 percentage points to 36.5%, while the opposition People Power Party stood at 39.7%, maintaining a narrow lead within the margin of error for a second consecutive week. Given that the conservative opposition was brought to the brink of collapse following the impeachment and imprisonment of former President Yoon Suk-yeol (who remains incarcerated as Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol), the ruling party's inability to break out of the mid-30% range represents a serious strategic vulnerability.

Further breakdowns from Opinion Poll Flower (Yeoron Josa Kkot) highlight a growing alienation among moderate voters, with negative assessments of the administration outnumbering positive ones across almost every geographic region outside the traditional progressive stronghold of the Honam region (the Jeolla provinces).

This slump was driven by a combination of political friction and economic distress. On the political front, DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok was forced to issue a public apology over accusations regarding controversial Shincheonji church members joining party ranks, sparking internal dissent. On the economic front, public anxiety spiked as South Korea's benchmark stock indexes, the KOSPI and KOSDAQ, plummeted below the 5,000 mark, triggering automated circuit breakers. When retail trading accounts shrink and household living costs rise, voters quickly question the governing party's competence, regardless of its reform rhetoric.

Deep Distrust in the Post-Impeachment Judiciary

Adding to the political headwinds is a profound erosion of public faith in South Korea’s legal and investigative institutions. A recent survey by Gallup Korea paints a grim picture of institutional trust across the justice system:

  • Constitutional Court: 49% public trust
  • Regular Courts (Judiciary): 41% public trust
  • Investigative Agencies: High-ranking Officials Corruption Investigation Office (CIO), National Police Agency, Serious Crimes Investigation Office, and Prosecution Service all languish in the 20% range.

This widespread cynicism stems from what many citizens perceive as hesitation and soft rulings by senior judicial leadership—including Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—regarding figures implicated in the illegal martial law declaration during the crisis under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Despite the massive public mobilization that led to Yoon's impeachment, voters feel the core legal establishment remains largely untouched. When seven out of ten citizens distrust the agencies tasked with upholding the rule of law, the governing party loses a vital anchor for social stability.

The Early 2028 Presidential Field and Deepening Social Fractures

Conservative Consolidation vs. Progressive Fragmentation

Although the next presidential election is years away, early polling for potential candidates reveals starkly different dynamics inside the two main political camps.

Within the conservative opposition, former Mayor Oh Se-hoon has rapidly established himself as the sole frontrunner ("one-top"). His primary intra-party rival, Representative Han Dong-hoon, has seen his standing eroded by ongoing trial risks and an overly aggressive rhetoric style that alienates moderate voters. Conservative voters appear to be making a pragmatic choice, coalescing around Oh as a more stable, administrative figure despite his current legal troubles.

Conversely, the progressive camp is entering a chaotic "Warring States" period with no clear frontrunner. Prominent figures—including Cho Kuk (head of the Rebuilding Korea Party), Kim Min-seok (DPK Chairman), Kang Hoon-sik (former Presidential Chief of Staff), and Jung Chung-rae (former DPK leader)—are clustered together in low single- to double-digit figures. Kim Min-seok's recent apology over party enrollment controversies checked his momentum, allowing Kang Hoon-sik to absorb some moderate support. Without a commanding figure to rally around, the progressive base remains divided and vulnerable to fatigue.

Consensus on Tech Regulation, Division over Insurrection

Public opinion polls also highlight sharp social divides on policy and historical justice:

  • Youth Social Media Restrictions: Public sentiment is overwhelmingly in favor of imposing strict statutory limits on social media platforms (such as Instagram and Facebook) for children under 14, reflecting urgent parental concern over cyberbullying and digital addiction.
  • Sentencing for Insurrection Crimes: When asked about the judicial prosecution of officials involved in Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's failed martial law attempt, 58.5% of telephone interview respondents demanded maximum criminal punishment. However, polling reveals a severe demographic split: a majority of men in their 20s advocated for leniency or reduced sentences. This gender and generational divide highlights how historical memory and democratic norms are interpreted differently by younger demographics.

The Strategic Math Behind a Progressive Merger

Five months after local elections, the proposed organizational merger between the governing Democratic Party of Korea and Cho Kuk's smaller Rebuilding Korea Party (RKP) remains stuck in political gridlock. While both sides share fundamental policy goals—such as the DPK's structural reform agenda and Cho Kuk’s "Social Rights" welfare vision—translating ideological alignment into a single political apparatus has proven immensely complex.

Key negotiators, including DPK Representative Park Beom-kye and RKP Secretary General Jung Choon-saeng, continue to keep channels open. However, structural hurdles remain high. DPK Representative Lee Yong-woo pointed out that under the DPK's strict and entrenched primary nomination system, candidates from the smaller Rebuilding Korea Party would stand little chance of winning local nominations against established DPK organizers without explicit structural quotas.

Timeline disagreements further complicate the process. Former DPK leader Jung Chung-rae has advocated for a firm January deadline to give prospective merged candidates at least six months to build local precinct networks. Conversely, Park Beom-kye argues that formal integration discussions could extend into mid-2028 without damaging electoral prospects. For Cho Kuk's party, delaying the timeline risks absorption or marginalization; for the ruling DPK, rushing it risks alienating grass-roots party members who oppose top-down candidate deals.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's political landscape recalibrates, three concrete markers will determine the immediate direction of momentum:

  • The Statute of Limitations Deadline (<30 Days): Watch whether police prosecutors move to formally indict former Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon for alleged false election statements regarding the BAI audit before the legal window closes.
  • Financial Stabilization Measures: Track how President Lee Jae-myung's economic team responds to index crashes below the 5,000 level on the KOSPI/KOSDAQ, as market stabilization will be critical to arresting the administration's polling decline.
  • The DPK-RKP January Merger Benchmark: Observe whether the Democratic Party of Korea and the Rebuilding Korea Party establish a formal joint committee by January, or if primary system disagreements force them to run on separate tracks.
