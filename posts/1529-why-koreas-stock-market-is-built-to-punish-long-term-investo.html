Why Korea’s Stock Market Is Built to Punish Long-Term Investors

October 27, 2026

For international investors looking at global equities over the past few years, the charts of the benchmark S&P 500 or Tokyo’s Nikkei have painted a picture of structural rallies and historic highs. Then there is South Korea. The Korea Composite Stock Price Index, widely known as the KOSPI, remains stuck in a persistent malaise, hovering stubbornly around the 2,500-point mark. While the country produces world-class memory chips, cutting-edge electric vehicle batteries, and dominant automobiles, its benchmark index behaves like an exhausted regional exchange unable to break free of its own gravity.

To many casual observers, the problem is labeled the "Korea Discount"—a vague catch-all phrase often blamed on geopolitical friction with North Korea or the cyclical swings of export-dependent industries. But the domestic consensus among seasoned market watchers is far more scathing. Korea’s markets are not suffering because their flagship corporations do not make money; they are languishing because those profits are systematically hoarded, diluted, and steered away from ordinary shareholders to protect the dynastic control of founding chaebol families.

Until this structural contradiction is dismantled, domestic retail traders—affectionately termed gaemi, or "ants"—will continue to view their own stock market not as an engine of wealth accumulation, but as a treacherous casino designed to extract their capital.

The Anatomy of the Discount: Cheap Versus Discounted

There is a fundamental difference between an asset that is "cheap" and an asset that is "discounted." A cheap stock trades below its intrinsic value because the market has temporarily mispriced its cash flows; an investor can buy it with the reasonable expectation that the gap will eventually close. A discounted market, by contrast, is one where the entire institutional framework has lost the trust of investors. In that environment, market participants actively penalize asset prices because they know the cash on the balance sheet will never reach their pockets.

Recent public dialogues between prominent Korean market analysts, including real estate and financial commentator Lee Kwang-soo and economic analyst Park Si-dong, have laid this distinction bare. South Korean balance sheets look extraordinarily cheap on paper, often trading at price-to-book ratios (PBR) well below 1.0. Yet global and domestic investors refuse to re-rate them. The market participants have reached a rational, cynical conclusion: corporate earnings are an internal piggy bank for controlling shareholders, not a pool of capital to be distributed proportionately.

This dynamic was captured in miniature during a recent earnings report from memory chip titan SK Hynix. On paper, the company posted stellar operating numbers. The stock initially jumped, only to reverse course violently and tumble within an hour. The trigger for the sell-off was telling: the corporate briefing contained no meaningful commitment to cancel treasury shares or expand shareholder returns. The collective response of the trading floor was brutal and immediate. If record operating profits translate into neither dividend growth nor supply reduction, the earnings belong solely to corporate management. Investors treated the good news not as a catalyst to hold, but as an urgent liquidity window to exit.

This reality has bred a hyper-short-term day-trading culture. Foreign observers often marvel at the frenetic, speculative nature of retail trading in Seoul, where individual investors hop aggressively between thematic stocks, quick momentum scalps, and leveraged bets. But retail traders did not adopt this behavior out of sheer impatience. They adapted to a predatory ecosystem. In an environment where management routinely floods the market with rights offerings or spins off core units the moment valuations rise, anyone holding a Korean equity for the long term is effectively volunteering to have their value diluted.

The Chaebol Playbook: How Treasury Shares Became Corporate Shields

To understand why the Korea Discount persists, one must examine the corporate tool most abused by Korean conglomerates: treasury stock. In standard corporate finance theory, when a public company buys back its own stock, it does so to return excess capital to shareholders. Retiring those shares reduces the total share count, boosts earnings per share (EPS), and makes every remaining share more valuable.

Consider the stark benchmark set by Wall Street. Over the past decade, Apple has spent more than 800 trillion won (approximately $600 billion) buying back and extinguishing its own shares. By actively eliminating supply, Apple orchestrated a massive surge in EPS, helping drive its share price up nearly ninefold. That systemic, binding promise that cash will flow back to capital providers is the underlying reason why global capital concentrates in New York.

In South Korea, treasury share buybacks are frequently co-opted into a mechanism that subverts the very shareholders who financed them. Under domestic corporate practice, treasury shares carry no voting rights while held on the company’s books. However, those voting rights can be miraculously revived if the shares are transferred to a third party. When a chaebol patriarch faces a governance challenge, an activist shareholder dispute, or an internal succession struggle, the playbook is well-rehearsed: the company executes a "friendly share swap" with an allied conglomerate.

"If I go to an allied corporate head and say, 'Let's swap our treasury shares,' my dormant treasury shares suddenly transform into common voting shares in their hands," analyst Lee Kwang-soo noted in his critique of the practice. "The company uses cash generated by all shareholders to purchase its own stock, and then uses that stock as an entrenched bunker to protect the controlling family's private grip on power."

This sleight of hand effectively turns the company’s capital against its minority owners. Even worse are the predatory financial maneuvers observed on the darker edges of the market, where companies issue private bonds or construct questionable corporate debt against treasury holdings, eventually transferring equity into the hands of hand-picked creditors while stripping cash out of the public firm. The public company is run not as a joint-stock enterprise, but as the personal fiefdom of an insider clan.

Political Battle Lines: Yoon’s Laissez-Faire Era Versus Lee’s Governance Push

This corporate behavior did not emerge in a vacuum; it flourished under regulatory forbearance. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, corporate reform took a back seat to sweeping deregulation. Under the banner of establishing a "business-friendly environment," the previous administration largely tolerated governance abuses, relying on voluntary corporate initiatives that did little to rein in the traditional chaebol defense mechanisms. The consequence was predictable: market skepticism deepened, capital flight toward foreign exchanges accelerated, and the KOSPI sank into a prolonged slumber.

Now, the political ground has shifted dramatically. Under President Lee Jae-myung, the current administration and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea have placed systemic capital market reform near the center of their economic agenda. The government’s legislative push focuses directly on corporate governance reform, with lawmakers demanding strict legal mandates requiring companies to cancel treasury shares within a set period after acquisition, closing the share-swap loophole once and for all.

Proposals under discussion also borrow from international playbooks. Analysts point to neighboring Japan, where the Tokyo Stock Exchange successfully pressured underperforming firms by demanding explicit turnaround roadmaps from companies trading below a price-to-book ratio of 1.0. Korean reform advocates are going a step further, debating whether the state should levy taxes on excessive corporate cash reserves to discourage companies from sitting on massive capital piles while leaving public shareholders empty-handed.

The pushback, however, is fierce. The primary opposition, the conservative People Power Party (PPP), has voiced sharp resistance, framing structural corporate governance mandates and retained-earnings taxes as heavy-handed state interventions that undermine corporate autonomy and weaken domestic industry competitiveness against global rivals. For the opposition, corporate cash reserves are a vital rainy-day cushion for research and capital expenditure, not an idle hoard to be regulated away.

Bringing the Fight into the Open: The 'Stock Hearings'

Tired of waiting for traditional financial press outlets—which frequently rely on chaebol advertising budgets—to confront these issues, progressive media voices and financial commentators have floated an aggressive alternative: public "Stock Hearings."

Originally proposed by prominent independent broadcast host "Gongjangjang" alongside analysts Lee Kwang-soo and Park Si-dong, the concept seeks to haul market dysfunction into the public eye. Produced with coordination from figures like producer Choi Seo-young, the hearings aim to convene panels of sharp, independent financial experts to cross-examine why specific blue-chip equities collapse on positive earnings, how corporate splits dilute minority stakes, and which corporate boards are weaponizing treasury shares against public investors.

The logic is simple: sunshine is the best disinfectant. Even if sweeping legislative reform faces fierce battles in the National Assembly against the People Power Party, hauling corporate decision-making into the spotlight creates reputational friction that corporate boards cannot easily ignore. It reframes corporate governance not as a dry, technical accounting dispute, but as an issue of economic justice for millions of retail investors whose retirement savings are tied to domestic equities.

What to Watch Next

If South Korea ever hopes to break out of its 2,500-point trap and usher in a genuine "KOSPI 3,000" era, macroeconomic stimulus will not be enough. The domestic market does not lack liquidity; it lacks basic trust. Over the coming months, several key milestones will signal whether real structural change is underway:

  • Treasury Stock Cancellation Legislation: Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party pushes through a binding legal mandate requiring firms to cancel repurchased treasury stock, effectively outlawing the friendly-share-swap defense tactic.
  • Fiduciary Duty Expansions: Keep an eye on ongoing legislative efforts to amend commercial law, explicitly defining a director’s fiduciary duty as owed to "all shareholders," rather than solely to "the company"—a legal distinction that has historically protected management from lawsuits over value-destroying spin-offs.
  • Corporate Retained Cash Tax Debates: Monitor parliamentary discussions on penalizing excessive corporate hoarding, a policy metric that could force cash-rich conglomerates to choose between aggressive dividend payouts or punitive tax bills.
  • The Impact of the Stock Hearings: Observe whether public, expert-led hearings succeed in turning esoteric corporate governance flaws into mainstream political issues that pressure the People Power Party and conservative business lobbies to negotiate compromise reforms.

The Korean stock market is at a crossroads. For decades, the implicit contract of the KOSPI was that public investors provided cheap capital while the benefits of industrial expansion accrued to an entrenched corporate elite. That era is no longer sustainable. If the Lee Jae-myung administration and corporate reform advocates cannot align the financial interests of corporate boards with their minority shareholders, the "ants" will simply take their capital elsewhere—leaving Korea's premier exchanges permanently discounted.
