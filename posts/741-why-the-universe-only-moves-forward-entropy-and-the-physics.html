Why the Universe Only Moves Forward: Entropy and the Physics of Our Existence

October 05, 2026

There is a specific, dizzying moment that often hits when watching science content online. It happens when you realize that a simple mathematical concept—the logarithm—is not just a tool for complex equations, but a compass that points to the very direction our universe is traveling.

For many of us, the logarithm was a source of classroom frustration, buried in the back of textbooks. Yet, as we grow older, these fundamental scientific principles begin to speak a different language. By exploring everything from the nature of money to the tragic life of Ludwig Boltzmann, we begin to realize that our very existence is a miraculous product of probability in a universe governed by heat and randomness.

The First Law: Energy is Never Lost, Only Exchanged

We live in a world governed by energy, but we rarely stop to consider how it moves. The First Law of Thermodynamics is simple: the total amount of energy in the universe is constant. Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it merely changes form or moves from one place to another.

To visualize this, consider an analogy often used by Professor Kim Beom-jun, a prominent South Korean statistical physicist: imagine him handing $7 (10,000 won) to Defconn, a well-known South Korean television personality. If Kim loses $7 and Defconn gains $7, the total sum of money between them remains identical. In physics, energy is the same. You can pass it back and forth, but the total pool remains fixed.

This provides a sense of comfort, but it leaves a gaping hole in our understanding. If energy is always conserved, why does the universe only move in one direction? Why can’t we "un-mix" a cup of coffee or watch a shattered glass reassemble itself? The First Law of Thermodynamics tells us what is possible, but it does not tell us why time has an arrow.

The Entropy Compass: Clausius and the Arrow of Time

To understand the direction of time, we turn to the Second Law of Thermodynamics, which introduces the concept of "entropy." The term was coined by the German physicist Rudolf Clausius, who derived it from the Greek word trope, meaning "change." He proposed that in any natural process, a specific quantity—entropy—always increases.

Clausius defined this change as the heat energy transferred divided by the absolute temperature (ΔS = Q / T). When heat moves from a hot object to a cold one, the total entropy of the system increases. The reverse—heat spontaneously moving from cold to hot—would result in a decrease in entropy, which we never observe in reality.

This reveals the "arrow of time." Because entropy naturally increases, we experience a future that is distinct from the past. However, while Clausius was brilliant, he could only describe the phenomenon from a macroscopic view. He saw the "forest," but he could not explain why the trees behaved this way at the atomic level.

Boltzmann’s Gamble: Probability Governs All

The bridge between the microscopic world of atoms and the macroscopic world of heat was built by Ludwig Boltzmann, an Austrian physicist. Boltzmann realized that the increase of entropy was not a rigid, mystical rule, but a matter of probability.

Consider 100 coins. If you toss them all, the chance of them all landing as "heads" is astronomically low—there is only one way that state can occur. However, the chance of a 50/50 mix is overwhelming because there are countless ways to arrive at that outcome. Boltzmann realized that particles, like coins, naturally drift toward the state that has the highest number of possible configurations.

He immortalized this in the equation S = k_B log W, where S is entropy and W is the number of possible states. It is a formula so foundational to his worldview that it is carved onto his tombstone. To Boltzmann, entropy isn't just "disorder"—it is the universe settling into its most statistically probable state.

The Tragedy of a Genius and the "Heat Death"

Boltzmann’s ideas were revolutionary, but they were met with fierce hostility from his contemporaries. In the late 19th century, scientists like Ernst Mach mocked him for proposing that atoms—which had never been seen—were the basis of physical reality. The intellectual isolation and vicious criticism drove Boltzmann to take his own life in 1906.

It is one of the great ironies of science that just months before his death, Albert Einstein published his work on Brownian motion, providing the experimental proof for the existence of atoms that Boltzmann needed to be vindicated. He died just before the world caught up to his brilliance.

Boltzmann also introduced us to the concept of "Heat Death." If entropy continues to increase, the universe will eventually reach a state of equilibrium where energy differences disappear. In this future, nothing moves, nothing changes, and no work can be performed. It is the cold, silent destination toward which our dynamic universe is slowly drifting.

Finding Comfort in the Chaos

When life feels chaotic or unfair, it is easy to succumb to the idea that we are being punished by fate. But viewed through the lens of entropy, our lives are not the result of moral judgment; they are simply the way the universe functions.

The fact that we are here—that matter organized itself into biological life—is a miracle of probability. We exist in a brief, fleeting moment of low entropy within a vast, cooling universe. Instead of blaming ourselves for the random outcomes of life, we can find comfort in the fact that our very existence is a statistically impossible, beautiful exception to the rule.
