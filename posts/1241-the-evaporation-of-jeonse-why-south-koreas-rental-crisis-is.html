The Evaporation of Jeonse: Why South Korea’s Rental Crisis Is Forcing Tenants to Buy

October 16, 2026

For decades, South Korea’s unique jeonse system functioned as the country’s ultimate residential stepping stone. Instead of paying monthly rent, a tenant hands over a massive lump-sum deposit—often 60 to 80 percent of the home's market value—to the landlord for a two-year contract. At the end of the term, the landlord returns the deposit in full, having earned interest or invested the principal in the interim, while the tenant walks away with their savings intact to eventually purchase a home. Today, that time-tested housing ladder is turning into a financial trap.

Across Seoul and the surrounding metropolitan region, rental inventory is vanishing at an unprecedented pace. Over the past year, nationwide jeonse listings have plunged between 40 and 50 percent, and in several high-demand working-class districts of the capital, more than 70 percent of available rental stock has simply evaporated. For middle-class renters staring down lease renewals, the calculus has suddenly shifted from patiently building equity to a stark matter of residential survival.

The Evaporation of Jeonse: A Rental Squeeze Pushing Tenants to the Brink

Recent real estate data paints a grim picture for anyone looking to sign or renew a lease. As of early September, North Chungcheong Province saw its available jeonse listings drop by 49.9 percent year-over-year. The southeastern industrial hub of Daegu recorded a 45.7 percent decline, while Gyeonggi Province—the sprawling suburban belt girding Seoul—contracted by 42.3 percent. Nearly half of the nation's traditional deposit-lease inventory has disappeared in twelve months.

Inside Seoul proper, the shortage borders on systemic paralysis. In northeastern Jungnang District (Jungnang-gu), rental listings collapsed by 74.3 percent over the year. In practical terms, where ten rental units once sat on the market, barely three remain today. Neighboring Dongdaemun-gu, along with southwestern industrial-residential hubs like Geumcheon-gu and Guro-gu, face nearly identical inventory shocks. Just across the city boundary in Gwangmyeong, Gyeonggi Province, roughly 10,000 newly built apartment units were delivered to the market last year. That surge initially offered breathing room, but as those units were quickly absorbed, available rental listings plunged by 84.4 percent, forcing dozens of prospective tenants to compete for the remaining 15 percent of stock.

This disappearance of stock has triggered violent price spikes. In Seoul’s eastern Gangdong District, jeonse deposit rates surged 28.79 percent over the standard two-year contract cycle. For a tenant who secured an apartment two years ago with a 500 million won deposit (roughly $370,000), signing a lease on an equivalent unit today requires an additional 150 million won (about $110,000). In northern Nowon-gu, rates climbed 20 percent. Across the border in Hanam, Gyeonggi Province, deposits jumped 26.57 percent, the fastest pace in the province, leaving tenants facing increases easily exceeding 100 million won ($74,000).

For salaried workers, saving an extra $100,000 in disposable cash over two years is nearly impossible. Faced with these numbers, property analysts who previously advocated conservative renting are now advising tenants to break their leases early to buy real estate. With property owners increasingly notifying tenants of their intention to move in themselves or seeking to re-lease at higher rates, landlords are often happy to let existing tenants leave ahead of schedule. The consensus among real estate strategists is clear: remaining on the rental sidelines risks being priced entirely out of transit-connected metropolitan apartment complexes.

The 30-Year Supply Cliff and the Policy Lag Facing the Lee Administration

The root engine driving this crisis is a historic constriction in physical housing construction. For the first time in thirty years, the number of new apartment units scheduled for delivery in Seoul is set to drop below 10,000 in an annual cycle. A major metropolis of nearly ten million people adding fewer than 10,000 new apartments in a year represents an unprecedented supply vacuum.

Real estate construction operates on multi-year lead times. Today's severe delivery drought traces back to the missteps and supply-chain breakdowns during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, poorly targeted deregulation and macroeconomic policy misjudgments stalled essential urban redevelopment projects while scattering speculative development plans that failed to break ground.

The current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, representing the progressive Democratic Party of Korea, has attempted to stabilize the sector through ambitious public-led housing initiatives—including the expansion of "basic housing" models—and by accelerating construction timetables for the "3rd Generation New Towns" around Seoul. However, physical concrete cannot be poured overnight. Between project permitting, groundbreaking, and final inspection, high-rise residential projects require three to four years minimum. While the government attempts to dampen short-term speculative fever through tax revisions and cautious messaging, the physical shortage of new homes will remain an unavoidable market reality for the immediate future.

The Selling Wave Paradox: Why Capital Gains Caps Are Unlocking Pockets of Inventory

While rental homes have all but vanished, a striking paradox has emerged in the purchase market: in certain neighborhoods, sales listings are abruptly surging. The most prominent example is northern Seoul’s Seongbuk District. Following government tax announcements on August 3, Seongbuk's inventory of homes listed for sale rose by 13.8 percent in twenty days, expanded to 29.4 percent by mid-September, and recently touched an extraordinary 37.7 percent increase.

This localized flood of inventory stems directly from tax reform and capital gains dynamics. During South Korea's preceding property boom, many residential complexes in Seongbuk-gu saw their asset values roughly double, creating capital gains exceeding 1 billion won (about $740,000) for ordinary homeowners who had stretched their personal finances to purchase with mortgages. Unlike ultra-wealthy owners in southern Gangnam, middle-income homeowners in Seongbuk view a $740,000 profit as life-changing wealth. Spooked by regulatory talk of a 1 billion won cap on preferential capital gains treatment, these owners are rushing to liquidate their holdings and lock in profits rather than risk future tax hits.

A similar impulse is rippling across Seoul’s coveted "Han River Belt," including Mapo-gu, as well as the affluent southern hubs of Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa. Rather than digging in to absorb escalating property holding taxes, long-time owners who believe the market has peaked are seeking an organized exit. This divergence has fractured the market: while rental availability remains near zero, prospective buyers armed with financing are finding sudden negotiating leverage in select districts where motivated sellers are competing for cash liquidity.

What to Watch Next: Navigating the Fragmented Commuter Belt

The inventory fracture is extending deeper into Gyeonggi Province’s prime commuter cities, offering a clear roadmap of where the market is bending:

  • The Split in Bundang: Bundang-gu in Seongnam, traditionally one of the wealthiest planned cities south of Seoul, recently led Gyeonggi Province with a 27.6 percent surge in sales listings, eclipsing Gwacheon (17.1 percent). Yet the internal split is stark: core neighborhoods such as Seohyeon, Sunae, and Jeongja have virtually no available listings, with asking prices jumping between 200 million and 300 million won ($150,000 to $220,000) following their designation as special urban renewal zones. Instead, the inventory surge is concentrated entirely in Bundang's peripheral neighborhoods as secondary owners seek an exit.
  • Secondary Commuter Hubs: Beyond Bundang and Gwacheon, listings are climbing steadily in Anyang’s Dongan District, Seongnam’s older urban quarters of Jungwon and Sujeong, and eastern Hanam. Because these regions offer direct subway access to Seoul’s key business districts, they serve as the primary landing zone for middle-class buyers fleeing the capital's rental crisis.
  • Permit and Groundbreaking Data: Watch actual construction commencement metrics rather than headline policy announcements. Until housing starts and municipal redevelopment permits turn upward decisively, the structural supply shortage will continue to underpin baseline valuations across the capital region.

South Korea’s metropolitan housing market has entered an unfamiliar phase where traditional historical patterns no longer apply. As the traditional rental safety net unravels, the gap between those who secure physical ownership and those caught in the shrinking jeonse pool is widening rapidly, making hyper-local inventory tracking the defining metric for surviving Seoul’s residential squeeze.
