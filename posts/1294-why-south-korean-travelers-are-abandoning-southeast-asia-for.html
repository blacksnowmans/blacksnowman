Why South Korean Travelers Are Abandoning Southeast Asia for Japan and China

October 19, 2026

For more than a decade, Friday night departure gates at Incheon International Airport—South Korea’s primary international hub west of Seoul—were filled with exhausted young office workers bound for Southeast Asia. A three- or four-day weekend in Bangkok, Da Nang, or Bali was the ultimate relief valve for South Korea’s hyper-stressed workforce. You could land, enjoy a $15 massage, eat cheap street food in a night market, and lounge at a five-star hotel for under $100 a night. For the budget-conscious South Korean consumer in their twenties and thirties, Southeast Asia represented an affordable paradise where hard-earned Won stretched remarkably far.

That long-standing social contract between South Korean vacationers and Southeast Asian tourism hotspots has officially broken down. Facing surging local inflation across Southeast Asia, aggressive new entry and exit fees, and a misguided attempt by local governments to transform budget paradises into luxury resorts, South Korean tourists are walking away in droves. Instead, they are redirecting their travel budgets toward Japan and China, while South Korea itself faces an unprecedented surge in incoming foreign tourists that is reshaping the streets of Seoul.

The Mass Exodus: What the Numbers Reveal About Southeast Asia's Decline

The shift is not a subtle trend; it is a statistical collapse. Between January and mid-July, overall foreign arrivals in Thailand nudged down by about 3 percent compared to the previous year. But looking specifically at South Korean visitors, the figure crashed by an astounding 25.3 percent. Compared to pre-pandemic levels, South Korean tourism to Thailand has effectively been cut in half. Where Suvarnabhumi Airport in Bangkok once swarmed with South Korean backpackers and families every winter and summer holiday, the crowds have visibly evaporated.

Thailand is not an isolated case. In Vietnam—long considered South Korea’s favorite resort playground, to the point where the coastal city of Da Nang was jokingly nicknamed "Da Nang County, Gangwon Province"—South Korean arrivals fell by 5.5 percent between January and September. While Russian tourist arrivals to Vietnam surged over 160 percent and Chinese visitors grew modestly, the decline in South Korean visitors represents a severe blow to local merchants. South Korean tourists are typically high-frequency spenders who generate fast turnover for local restaurants, cafes, and private tour operators.

For South Korean consumers, who are among the most price-sensitive and trend-discerning travelers in Asia, this exit serves as a canary in the coal mine. It signals that the underlying value proposition of Southeast Asian tourism—high efficiency and comfort at a low cost—has completely unraveled.

Bangkok Pricier Than Gangnam: The Coffee Index and Tax Avalanche

The primary driver behind this exodus is the dramatic surge in on-the-ground living costs across major Southeast Asian cities. A striking illustration of this distortion can be found in the "Starbucks Index." In Seoul’s upscale Gangnam district, a Tall Cafe Latte at Starbucks costs 5,200 Korean won (about $3.80). In downtown Bangkok, where Thailand's per capita GDP is less than a quarter of South Korea’s, the exact same coffee sells for 130 to 140 Thai baht—equivalent to roughly 5,230 to 5,630 won (around $3.80 to $4.10).

When a global franchise coffee costs more in a developing economy than in the heart of Seoul, local pricing structures have clearly detached from reality. It is not just coffee: mall restaurants, trendy cafes, rideshare fares, and spa services in Bangkok have all seen steep price increases. South Korean travelers who expect their money to go further find themselves paying Seoul prices while dealing with heavy traffic congestion, air pollution, and taxi markups.

Compounding this inflation shock is an aggressive wave of new levies proposed or implemented by Thai authorities:

  • Existing Airport Passenger Service Fee: 1,120 baht (about $32) embedded in airfares.
  • Proposed Foreign Land/Air Entry Fee: 450 baht (about $13).
  • Proposed International Departure Tax: 1,000 baht (about $29).

If fully implemented, these taxes would force every foreign visitor to pay a total of 2,570 baht (about $75 or 102,800 won) simply for crossing the border in both directions. For a South Korean family of four taking a five-day vacation to Bangkok, these non-negotiable fees instantly add over $300 to the trip budget before a single meal or activity is booked. For middle-class households operating on tight holiday budgets, that added friction is enough to scratch Thailand off the list entirely.

The "Louis Vuitton" Fallacy: Debt Crises and Misguided Elite Strategy

Why are local governments pushing these policies when tourists are fleeing? The answer lies in a combination of elite hubris and fiscal desperation. Back in 2022, Thailand’s former Deputy Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul publicly urged the tourism sector to adopt a luxury strategy akin to French fashion house Louis Vuitton, arguing that "the more expensive it is, the more customers there will be." This mindset morphed into the Tourism Authority of Thailand’s official slogan, "Value is the New Volume," prioritizing high-margin luxury travel over mass tourism.

"Louis Vuitton earns its price through decades of craftsmanship and brand heritage. Trying to slap a luxury price tag on an destination without fixing basic infrastructure or security is simply gouging travelers."

Behind this high-end pivot lies a troubling fiscal backdrop. Thailand’s public debt-to-GDP ratio climbed from 41 percent in 2019 to nearly 68 percent, while household debt sits at a staggering 85.2 percent of GDP. With local consumer spending constrained by debt and fiscal revenues stretched thin, political elites have turned to foreign tourists as an easy source of revenue to shore up public coffers. Rather than reforming tax structures or upgrading travel infrastructure, governments have resorted to predatory border fees.

The Great Travel Realignment: Japan, China, and Seoul’s Overtourism Shock

South Korean consumers have not stopped traveling abroad; overall outbound travel rose 2.7 percent in the first half of the year. Instead, they have redirected their capital toward cleaner, safer, and highly predictable destinations. Driven by a weak Japanese yen and reliable infrastructure, South Korean travel to Japan surged 18.6 percent, with 9.46 million South Koreans visiting Japan over a 12-month period—outpacing even China in total visitors to Japan. Travel to mainland China also jumped 16 percent following expanded visa-free entry policies.

Even as Japan raises its own international tourist taxes (tripling its departure tax to 3,000 yen, or about $20, alongside local hotel taxes), South Korean travelers remain willing to pay because the overall service quality, food hygiene, and safety are perceived as worth the expense.

Meanwhile, the global travel map has flipped inbound toward South Korea itself. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea has seen a massive influx of foreign visitors. Between January and August, South Korea welcomed a record 15.05 million foreign tourists—a 21.6 percent jump year-over-year. Chinese tourists accounted for 32 percent of all arrivals (4.82 million), followed by Japanese and Taiwanese travelers.

This inbound rush has transformed popular neighborhoods in Seoul like Myeongdong, Seongsu-dong, and Hongdae. While this boom boosts local retail and hospitality markets, it has also sparked complaints among Seoul residents regarding crowded public transport and rising neighborhood living costs—a stark reminder that travel realignments create domestic friction on both sides of the flight route.

What to Watch Next

As Asia's travel market reshapes itself, several key dynamics will determine where tourists move next:

  • Thailand’s Tax Decisions: Watch whether the Thai cabinet formally enacts the 450-baht entry fee and 1,000-baht departure tax, or rolls them back under pressure from local hotel and hospitality associations facing plummeting occupancy rates.
  • South Korea's Inbound Policy: How President Lee Jae-myung's administration balances aggressive inbound tourism targets with local neighborhood management in Seoul, particularly regarding short-term rental regulation and public transit congestion.
  • Currency Fluctuations: Any significant strengthening of the Japanese yen could alter the cost dynamic that currently makes Japan the top alternative for South Korean travelers, potentially shifting demand toward domestic travel or cheaper East Asian alternatives.
