South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Faces Record Disapproval as Public Support Fractures

September 16, 2026

The mood in Seoul's political district of Yeouido and the Blue House shifted abruptly last month when the latest survey results landed. For a administration that swept into power with high expectations for reform, the numbers from the 31st KSOI regular survey—conducted nationwide among 1,000 adults in mid-September 2026—felt like an electric shock. President Lee Jae-myung, who took office promising a new era of progressive governance, watched his disapproval rating surge to a record high of 59.4%, while his positive marks plummeted to 35.9%. These are not the gentle ripples of a routine midterm slump; they represent a deep, bitter fracture in the public mood that extends far beyond ordinary partisan grumbling.

Even citizens who defended the government through previous controversies are now pulling back, crossing their arms, and watching from a distance. To understand how South Korea's progressive leadership arrived at this precarious juncture, we have to look past the top-line percentages and examine the structural decay of the administration's political coalition.

The Anatomy of a Collapse: Demographics, Pessimism, and the Core Base

A closer look at the KSOI survey reveals that economic and peace pessimism have both surged past the 50% threshold, hitting 54.8% and 55.0% respectively. This indicates that the everyday working class—the bedrock of South Korean stability—has reached its breaking point. Most alarmingly for the Blue House, "strongly positive" responses cratered to just 19.2%, down sharply from the 42.8% highs recorded in April ahead of local elections. Conversely, "strongly negative" sentiment jumped to 44.9%, proof that the administration has not merely weakened its coalition; it has hardened an active, vocal opposition.

The erosion of support is remarkably uniform across geography and generation. Historically reliable progressive strongholds among voters in their 30s and 40s have turned cold, with disapproval ratings in those brackets hitting 64.1% and 51.9%. Regionally, aside from the traditional liberal bastion of the Jeolla provinces, negative public sentiment dominates everywhere. Seoul sits at 61.5% disapproval, Gyeonggi and Incheon at 61.3%, and the Chungcheong region at a national high of 67.3%. When asked to name a single policy area where the government is succeeding, 40.6% of respondents answered simply: "None."

This is no longer a temporary communications failure. When a progressive administration loses the enthusiastic backing of the voters who built its mandate, it signals that the fundamental social contract between the leader and his base has frayed. Historical precedents under past progressive and conservative administrations show that rhetorical appeals and superficial cabinet shuffles rarely halt such a slide. The public's fatigue is systemic, born from a widening gap between grand reformist rhetoric and the harsh economic realities felt on the ground.

Personnel as Policy: The Cost of Defiant Appointments

Nowhere is the disconnect between the Blue House and the public more evident than in recent personnel choices. The administration's handling of key cabinet and agency appointments has turned the confirmation process into a political minefield.

For the critical post of Minister of Justice, nominee Kim Seung-won faced a staggering 52.1% public opposition rate against just 25.1% support. Similarly, Kim Ji-yong, nominated to head the newly established Investigation Bureau, recorded 38.8% opposition versus 21.4% approval, with undecided voters hovering near 40%. Rather than projecting strength, these stubborn nominations reinforce the public perception that the administration is entirely tone-deaf to demands for basic competence and fairness.

Critics within the progressive camp itself have begun pointing out a deeper structural flaw: the absence of elite messaging directors capable of translating complex policy wins into the language of ordinary citizens—a deficit compared by insiders to the skilled public relations machinery of past administrations. When cabinet picks repeatedly trigger public backlash, pushing ahead regardless of the optics transforms noble talk of reform into an exercise in political arrogance. If the executive branch refuses to reset its standards for vetting officials, the remaining term risks descending into perpetual crisis management.

Friendly Fire and the Taboo Word: "Impeachment"

The political shockwaves reached a fever pitch when ruling party figures themselves began injecting radical terminology into the public discourse. Kim Min-seok, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the country's main liberal political force—stunned observers during a media interview by comparing the current crisis to the prelude leading up to the historic impeachment of former President Roh Moo-hyun. For a ruling party chief to invoke the word "impeachment" against a president of his own party is virtually unprecedented in South Korean political history.

The remarks provoked immediate fury within party ranks, with critics accusing the party leadership of stoking internal chaos to secure factional advantage rather than shielding the presidency. For progressive veterans and core supporters, the memory of Roh Moo-hyun's 2004 impeachment remains an open wound—a devastating trauma inflicted by political betrayal and conservative maneuvering. Hearing the leader of the Democratic Party casually invoke that historical ghost against a sitting liberal president felt to many supporters like a betrayal from within.

Prominent progressive commentators have echoed these warnings. Yoo Si-min, a respected progressive intellectual, recently complained of facing targeted attacks from within the presidential orbit, noting that harsh internal policing has alienated loyal defenders of the movement. Broadcaster Kim Eo-jun similarly diagnosed that the plunging numbers stem not merely from lagging economic metrics, but from the alienation of the "core"—the dedicated ideological base that brought the government to power and now sits watching with folded arms.

What to Watch Next: The September 18 Press Conference

Recognizing the existential gravity of the moment, the Blue House has announced that President Lee Jae-myung will hold a direct press conference on September 18. This scheduled appearance will serve as the ultimate test of the administration's willingness to pivot.

To watch moving forward, observe three concrete indicators during and after this critical turning point:

  • Substantive Personnel Reversals: Will the president formally withdraw or replace contested nominees like Kim Seung-won, or will the Blue House double down on defiant appointments under the banner of unyielding reform?
  • Tone of Communication: Does the president offer a genuine, unscripted admission of policy and communication failures, or does the address rely on defensive rhetoric blaming external factors and media bias?
  • Management of Party Factions: Will the leadership rein in internal infighting and mend fences with alienated progressive intellectuals and core grassroots organizers, or will factional struggles intensify ahead of upcoming legislative hurdles?

Poll numbers are ultimately just numbers, but in South Korean politics, they serve as the final warning lights before a government loses its moral authority. If the September 18 press conference devolves into another exercise in self-justification, the current administration may find that the distance between sitting in the Blue House and losing the public trust entirely is shorter than it appears.
