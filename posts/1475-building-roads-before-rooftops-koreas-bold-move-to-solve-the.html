Building Roads Before Rooftops: Korea’s Bold Move to Solve the New City Commuter Crisis

October 26, 2026

Imagine waking up at 5:30 a.m. in a pristine, high-tech apartment on the outskirts of Seoul. Your home is part of a "New City," a massive government-led housing development designed to offer a modern lifestyle at a fraction of Seoul’s skyrocketing prices. But as you step outside, the dream fades. The "metropolitan" roads promised in the brochure are still dirt paths or clogged two-lane country roads. The nearest subway station is a 30-minute bus ride away—if the bus even shows up. For tens of thousands of Koreans, this "island life" has been a grueling reality for decades.

South Korea’s "New City" (Sindosi) projects are central to the country's urban planning. They are meant to decentralize the population and cool the overheated real estate market in the Seoul Capital Area (SCA). However, a chronic failure to align housing construction with transportation infrastructure has turned these areas into "bed towns" where residents spend three to four hours a day trapped in transit. On October 25, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) and the Metropolitan Transport Commission (MTC) announced a major policy shift to break this cycle. By expanding "direct authorization" for critical road projects, the government is attempting to bypass the local bureaucratic infighting that has historically left residents stranded.

Bypassing the Bureaucratic Bottleneck

The core of the new measure is a strategic use of administrative power. Traditionally, building a major road that connects a new city to Seoul is an administrative nightmare. Because these roads typically cross the boundaries of multiple local municipalities—such as Seoul, Incheon, and various cities in Gyeonggi Province—every inch of the pavement requires separate negotiations. Local mayors often stall projects to demand more exits in their districts, or to avoid paying for maintenance costs on roads that "just pass through" their jurisdiction. This "NIMBYism" (Not In My Backyard) or local interest lobbying can delay a single road project for three to five years.

To solve this, the MTC—a specialized agency under the Ministry of Land—is taking the wheel. Under the "direct authorization" (Jikjeop Inheoga) system, the central government effectively strips local authorities of their power to block or delay these specific projects. The MTC will now directly approve and manage the entire process, from route selection to land compensation. This is a massive shift in Korea’s administrative culture, which usually emphasizes local autonomy. However, the government has reached a point of "desperate urgency," as one official noted, where the right to commute must take precedence over local political maneuvering.

The October 25 announcement specifically expanded this direct management from five key road projects to seven. The two new additions are critical arteries for the Incheon Gyeyang district, a flagship "3rd Generation New City" project. By taking direct control, the government expects to shave at least one to two years off the construction timeline, aiming to ensure that when the first residents turn their keys in their new apartments, the roads are actually ready to take them to work.

The Trauma of the Second Generation Failures

To understand why this matters so much to Koreans, one must look at the "scarring" left by the 2nd Generation New Cities. Projects like Wirye (southeast of Seoul) and Geomdan (west of Seoul) became cautionary tales. In Korea, home buyers in these new cities often pay a "transportation contribution fee" (Gwangyeok Gyotong Gaeseon Daechaek Bundamgeum) as part of their apartment purchase price. This fee can range from $15,000 to $30,000 per household, intended to fund the subways and highways that will serve the community.

"We paid for the roads with our own money before the first brick was even laid, yet we spent ten years waiting for a bus that never came."

This sentiment is common among residents of 2nd Generation cities. In many cases, the housing was completed in three years, but the subways took fifteen. This was largely due to the administrative paralysis that characterized previous eras of governance. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, infrastructure projects often drifted without a clear "control tower." His administration focused heavily on supply numbers—announcing thousands of new homes to satisfy the market—but neglected the grueling, granular work of mediating between local governments to get the infrastructure built. The result was a widening gap between where people lived and where they could realistically travel from, a failure that the current Lee Jae-myung administration is now tasked with rectifying.

The "Transport First, Housing Second" (Seon-gyotong Hu-ipju) principle is the Lee Jae-myung government's answer to this legacy. It is an admission that a house is not just a structure of concrete and steel; it is a node in a network. If the node is disconnected, the house loses its value as a home and becomes a cage. The current push for direct authorization is a move to restore public trust in the government's ability to deliver a functional city, not just a collection of apartment blocks.

Strategic Focus: Incheon Gyeyang and Hanam Gyosan

The expansion of the direct management list to include two roads in Incheon Gyeyang is particularly significant. Gyeyang is strategically located near Seoul's Magok and Sangam districts—high-tech hubs for IT and media. However, the existing road networks, such as Bongo-daero and Beolmal-ro, are already notorious for "parking lot" traffic levels during rush hour. Without the two new road expansions being fast-tracked, the influx of tens of thousands of new residents would not just cause a local headache; it would paralyze the entire western corridor of the Seoul metropolitan area.

On the other side of the capital, the Hanam Gyosan district in the southeast is facing similar pressures. Regarded as the "blue chip" of the 3rd Generation New Cities due to its proximity to the wealthy Gangnam district, Gyosan's success hinges entirely on its connectivity. Recently, local leaders like Jeong Byeong-yong, the Chairman of the Hanam City Council, have been vocal in demanding that the central government guarantee transportation infrastructure before move-in dates. These local leaders are under immense pressure from their constituents, who are wary of the "empty promises" of the past.

By focusing on these specific "choke points"—seven road projects worth billions of dollars—the government is trying to prove that it can manage the complex logistics of a modern megalopolis. For the 30-something office worker in Seoul looking to buy their first home, these administrative changes are more important than the interest rate. They determine whether they will see their children before they go to sleep, or whether they will spend those precious hours staring at the taillights of the car in front of them.

Restoring the Role of the Public Sector

The shift toward direct authorization also signals a broader political change in how Korea manages its public assets. Under the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, there was a tendency toward "market-first" approaches that often saw public infrastructure take a backseat to private development interests. The lack of a strong central arbiter led to the current backlog of delayed road and rail projects. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, currently imprisoned following his removal from office, left behind a bureaucracy that was often hesitant to exercise federal authority over local municipalities, leading to the "administrative nimbyism" we see today.

The current government, led by President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party (the current ruling party), is repositioning the state as the ultimate guarantor of urban livability. This "Administrative Fast-Track" is not just about speed; it is about accountability. When the MTC takes over a project, the responsibility for its success or failure rests squarely with the central government, leaving no room for the "blame game" between mayors and governors that has stalled progress for decades.

However, this centralized approach does carry risks. Bypassing local governments can lead to friction regarding environmental concerns or local noise complaints. If the central government moves too fast without consulting the people on the ground, it could face a different kind of backlash. The challenge for the Lee Jae-myung administration will be to maintain this "fast-track" momentum while ensuring that the roads they build actually serve the communities they pass through, rather than just cutting through them.

What to Watch Next

As these seven road projects move into the direct authorization phase, there are several key indicators of success to monitor in the coming months:

  • Construction Timelines: Watch for the "first shovel" on the Incheon Gyeyang road projects. If these begin within the next 12 months, it will be a sign that the administrative bypass is working.
  • Local Government Response: Observe whether the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition party) or local mayors from affected districts challenge the MTC’s authority in court. Any legal stay could derail the "Transport First" timeline.
  • The "Gyosan Subway" Decision: While the current focus is on roads, the Hanam Gyosan district’s subway extension remains a major point of contention. Whether the government applies similar "direct authorization" logic to rail projects will be a major signal for the market.
  • Market Confidence: Real estate analysts will be looking at subscription rates for 3rd Generation New City apartments. High demand will indicate that the public believes these transportation promises will finally be kept.

For the international observer or the prospective investor, Korea’s New City projects represent more than just a housing solution; they are a massive experiment in urban engineering and administrative reform. If the Lee Jae-myung government can successfully bridge the gap between housing and transport, it will set a new global standard for how to build sustainable, connected mega-cities. If it fails, it will simply be adding more "islands" to an already overcrowded sea.

The true value of a home is not found in its walls, but in the path that leads you back to it.
