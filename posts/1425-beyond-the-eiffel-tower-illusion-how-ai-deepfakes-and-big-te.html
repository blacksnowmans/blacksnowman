Beyond the Eiffel Tower Illusion: How AI Deepfakes and Big Tech Algorithms Threaten South Korea’s Democracy

October 24, 2026

Imagine scrolling through your phone and seeing a video of the Eiffel Tower draped in massive foreign flags. The camera sways naturally, the sun reflects off the iron girders, and the fabric billows convincingly in the Paris breeze. Even a skeptical viewer might pause for a second, wondering if a sudden geopolitical demonstration just erupted in France. It takes a trained eye—or specialized software—to realize that the scene never happened. It was generated entirely by artificial intelligence.

We have moved far beyond the era of crude Photoshop edits and uncanny facial swaps. Modern AI models calculate physics, lighting reflections, and subtle human movements with eerie precision. As visual trust crumbles, society faces a daunting reality: when seeing is no longer believing, the cost of verifying truth escalates dramatically. Nowhere is this threat more potent than in national politics, where a single hyper-realistic deepfake distributed hours before a vote can sway an election before anyone has time to debunk it.

For South Korea, a country with near-universal smartphone penetration and hyper-connected messaging networks, synthetic media and algorithmic manipulation represent a clear and present danger to democratic integrity. Understanding how deepfakes are evolving—and how global tech platforms exacerbate the problem—is crucial to seeing what lies ahead for Korean politics.

The Underground Pipeline: KakaoTalk, Telegram, and Low-Light Manipulation

To maximize their psychological impact, modern deepfake creators rarely set their fabrications in broad daylight. Instead, they increasingly favor low-light settings, deliberate camera shakes, or grainy aesthetics reminiscent of dashcams and security feeds. When a video looks like a covert, night-time recording leaked from a hidden camera, it triggers a powerful cognitive bias: viewers assume they are witnessing an unvarnished, clandestine truth. The artificial noise in a dark video acts as a cloak, masking subtle computational artifacts that might otherwise reveal the clip as a synthesis.

Once created, these viral landmines need a delivery pipeline. In South Korea, that pipeline operates largely out of public view. While public timelines on platforms like X (formerly Twitter) or Facebook allow for open debate and crowd-sourced fact-checking, fake news in Korea thrives inside closed mobile messaging environments.

Chief among these is KakaoTalk, South Korea’s ubiquitous messaging app used by virtually the entire population, alongside global encrypted networks like Telegram. In these private chat rooms and family group conversations, messages arrive backed by personal trust. When a user receives a secretive-sounding video from an acquaintance accompanied by a caption like "Watch this before it gets taken down," skepticism drops. The information circulates inside insulated echo chambers, reinforcing pre-existing biases completely out of sight from journalists, electoral monitors, or opposing voices.

By the time professional fact-checkers discover that a fabricated video of a candidate engaged in illegal activity or making inflammatory remarks has taken hold, it has already reached hundreds of thousands of voters. In a high-stakes campaign, post-election retractions or legal penalties cannot repair the damage inflicted on a compromised ballot.

When Big Tech Algorithms Distort Elections: The Brazilian Warning

Deepfakes represent the content side of technical manipulation, but the algorithms distributing that content pose an equally severe threat. The vulnerabilities inherent in modern digital campaign ecosystems were laid bare during a recent presidential election in Brazil—a case study that serves as a stark warning for South Korea and democracies worldwide.

During the Brazilian election, the campaign of progressive candidate Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva experienced sudden disruptions on platforms owned by Meta (the parent company of Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp). Paid promotional features for Lula’s campaign were abruptly disabled. More suspiciously, when users searched for candidates on WhatsApp and Instagram, the search algorithms systematically prioritized his far-right opponent, Jair Bolsonaro, while Lula’s official account was difficult to find even when typing his exact name.

The bias extended into generative AI tools. Meta’s AI image generator refused to create supportive images for Lula while willingly generating positive depictions of Bolsonaro. When challenged, Meta attributed the discrepancies to a neutral "technical glitch" in global view-counting and advertisement processing systems. Yet the fact that these "glitches" operated almost exclusively to the detriment of one candidate during a razor-thin runoff election raised profound questions about platform accountability and foreign tech influence in sovereign elections.

The Brazilian case is directly relevant to South Korea because of public media consumption habits. In Brazil, 97 percent of the population uses WhatsApp daily, and over 70 percent relies on Instagram for news. South Korea exhibits a remarkably similar structural vulnerability, with KakaoTalk dominating daily communication and YouTube serving as a primary news source for millions of citizens. When a single foreign tech platform holds a virtual monopoly over a nation's newsflow, invisible algorithmic tweaks—whether accidental or intentional—can quietly tilt an entire political landscape.

South Korea’s Democratic Stakes in the Current Era

South Korea is currently navigating a delicate political transition. Following the dramatic constitutional crisis that led to the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the nation elected President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea. Under the current administration, the state has been working to restore democratic stability, rebuild public trust, and address long-standing media polarizations that deepened during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term.

However, as upcoming elections approach, political strategists and legal experts warn that synthetic media could undermine these stabilizing efforts. The combination of hyper-realistic AI and targeted algorithmic feeds creates an environment where character assassination campaigns can be executed with unprecedented speed.

In response, South Korea's policy ecosystem is attempting to build technical and legal defenses. On the technological front, domestic firms are deploying detection software such as the DeepBrain AI AI Detector. These systems analyze video frames for sub-pixel inconsistencies, lighting anomalies, and synthetic voice patterns to flag deepfakes before they go viral.

Yet media scholars, including Professor Yun Seong-ok of Kyonggi University’s Department of Media & Communication, and legal commentators like Attorney Shin Yu-jin emphasize that software alone cannot solve a structural problem. Key figures in the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, such as Representative Park Joo-min, alongside independent investigative journalists, have consistently called for comprehensive legislative reforms to ensure platform transparency.

Experts argue that global tech giants can no longer hide behind the defense of being neutral intermediaries. Proposed measures include enforcing mandatory digital watermarks on all AI-generated media, imposing severe financial liabilities on platforms that fail to rectify algorithmic biases during official election periods, and establishing independent oversight bodies to audit recommendation systems.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea prepares for its next major electoral cycles, the battle against AI-driven disinformation will serve as a bellwether for digital democracies globally. Key developments to monitor include:

  • Legislative Reform on AI Watermarking: Watch for bills introduced in the National Assembly that would make visible and invisible digital watermarks mandatory for all commercially available AI generation tools in Korea.
  • Platform Liability Standards: Observe whether the Lee Jae-myung administration pushes for legislative frameworks that hold major platforms legally and financially accountable for systemic algorithmic biases during official campaign windows.
  • The Tech Arms Race: Track the adoption of real-time detection software like DeepBrain AI's detector by public election oversight commissions, news outlets, and messaging services to see if verification speed can keep up with generation speed.
  • Civic Media Literacy Initiatives: Monitor how educational institutions and public institutions roll out digital literacy programs aimed at teaching citizens—especially older demographics reliant on closed messaging apps—how to critically evaluate unverified visual content.

Technology continues to accelerate, but the ultimate defense of a democracy rests on transparency, regulatory oversight, and a vigilant public. Ensuring that code and synthetic pixels do not subvert the popular will remains one of the defining challenges of our time.
