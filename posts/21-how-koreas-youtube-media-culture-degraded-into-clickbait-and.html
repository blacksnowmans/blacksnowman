How Korea's YouTube Media Culture Degraded Into Clickbait and Tribal Warfare

September 24, 2026

Open YouTube in South Korea today, and you will be buried in an avalanche of current affairs commentary and political critique channels. Traditional news outlets—ranging from major terrestrial broadcasters to conservative legacy newspapers like Chosun Ilbo, JoongAng Ilbo, and DongA Ilbo, collectively known as the "Cho-Joong-Dong"—are no longer the sole gatekeepers. A vast ecosystem of digital start-ups and independent creators has crowded the microphone. Yet watching this frantic media circus elicits less admiration than a sense of utter bewilderment. Places that ought to maintain a dignified standard of media critique have somehow degenerated into mudslinging arenas of mockery and abuse.

A recent appearance by Rhyu Si-min—a prominent progressive public intellectual and former minister—on a live broadcast hosted by Media Today, a progressive media-watch outlet, cut straight to the heart of this chaotic ecosystem. Watching commentators surrender their dignity to YouTube view counts and algorithm slave-driving makes it agonizingly clear that our very relationship with news and critique has broken down. To understand how South Korea's public square reached this dismal state, we have to examine the anatomy of a system captured by capital, poisoned by tribalism, and stripped of basic journalistic integrity.

Capital Capture and the Erosion of Internal Critique

Rhyu’s observation about Media Today is both fascinating and melancholy. Noting that the outlet's largest shareholder is the National Union of Media Workers, Rhyu remarked, "It's a media outlet that fires shots at its own political camp. Frankly, it's sometimes the press our own stakeholders find most uncomfortable." That single sentence captures the predicament: an outlet established to foster self-criticism among media workers, backed by the union, has become an endangered species.

Whether in politics, society, or corporate life, the moment an organization loses its internal dissenters, it stagnates and rots. Complacency leads directly to corruption and inefficiency. Yet most legacy media and digital newcomers are far too busy covering up the flaws of their own groups while tearing into the faults of rivals. When Rhyu half-jokingly suggested that "society might become a bit healthier if Media Today reached a million subscribers," it was a damning reflection of how narrow and partisan South Korea's public discourse has become.

The structural reality behind media ownership is even bleaker. Moving past traditional family-dynasty media inheritances, it has become commonplace for construction conglomerates and private equity funds to swallow up news organizations. Statistics show that substantial stakes in major media outlets now rest in the hands of corporate entities whose sole purpose is profit extraction. When media is utilized as a tool to wring out maximum returns, the lofty fragrance of public-interest journalism vanishes entirely. Long ago, the press surrendered its traditional role as a social compass to the unyielding logic of capital.

The Rise of "Immune Critics" and the Death of Nuance

One of Rhyu's sharpest critiques targeted the mutation of media criticism itself. "They aren't critiquing the media; they're attacking specific people," he noted. If a legacy broadcaster's YouTube channel is placed under the microscope, the focus ought to be on the structural contradictions and directional biases of its reporting. Instead, these broadcasts spend hours obsessively fixating on individual panelists or guest commentators.

Placing sensational names on thumbnails while casts pile on to mock and belittle targets serves no constructive purpose. The reality that such lowbrow content racks up massive view counts exposes how deep our collective voyeurism and hostility run. Critique of media should always be welcomed, but what happens on the modern YouTube circuit resembles a low-grade hunt rather than analysis.

Rhyu coined a biting term for this phenomenon: "immunity critics" (or "legislative-immunity-style critics"). Like juveniles protected by legal immunity, these commentators hurl vicious personal attacks and engage in witch hunts without context, only to slip away with a nonchalant "so what if I was wrong?" attitude when debunked. They aim stones meant for the powerful straight at the foreheads of powerless private individuals, wrapping the whole cowardly display in the noble-sounding cloak of media critique.

The Collapse of Journalistic Norms and Partisan Warfare

Textbook journalism rests on three pillars: grounding reports in facts, respecting logical rules, and treating opposing factions fairly. According to Rhyu, while news producers generally try to uphold the first two rules—otherwise they lose their audience and perish—the third rule, "fairness," has historically been little more than a polite fiction that legacy media never truly honored. Under the guise of mechanical neutrality, the establishment press quietly served conservative interests for decades.

When digital media exploded, even that thin illusion was smashed. Today's digital media ecosystem is a no-holds-barred arena dominated by partisan combatants who do not even pretend to care about fairness. When traditional newspaper staff cross over to YouTube, their hosts often shed any pretense of balance, hurling inflammatory remarks to serve specific political factions. In a survival-of-the-fittest jungle dictated by subscriber counts and click-through rates, fairness is a dead word found only in old textbooks.

This relentless drive for partisan victory has made retractions and apologies exceptionally rare. While major Western outlets like the New York Times or the Guardian meticulously unearth and correct decades-old reporting errors, South Korean media outlets prefer to brush off mistakes with a shrug. Rhyu himself reflected on his past broadcasts—such as the controversial YouTube channel *Alileo*, where he propagated unverified suspicions regarding the Roh Moo-hyun Foundation's bank accounts—and offered a public, painful apology for falling victim to confirmation bias and emotional hostility. That rare capacity for self-correction stands in stark contrast to an industry hooked on demonizing opponents.

Finding a Path Forward Through the Digital Chaos

From a historical standpoint, the digital revolution and the rise of YouTube have liberated information. For generations, media was an instrument of suppression. Ordinary citizens had no voice unless they curried favor with newspaper owners or broadcast executives. Technology shattered that monopoly, granting anyone the ability to broadcast their thoughts without owning a printing press.

Yet this unprecedented freedom has also turned into a breeding ground for unbridled hatred and disinformation. Some panic at this chaos, arguing that a controlled, legacy-managed media order was preferable. But Rhyu’s perspective remains fundamentally macroeconomic and optimistic: public audiences are not fools. Even if viewers are occasionally drawn to sensational clickbait, they ultimately learn to reject content that offers no substance or constructive alternatives.

Just as the invention of the printing press initially unleashed a flood of conspiracy theories and fraudulent texts before society built institutional filters, today's digital media turmoil is part of a painful transitional phase. The democratization of speech cannot be undone, nor should we wish to return to past censorship.

What to Watch Next

  • Platform Accountability: Monitor whether YouTube and other digital platforms face stricter regulatory frameworks in South Korea regarding targeted harassment and unverified political exposés.
  • Legacy Media Shifts: Observe if major traditional newspapers and broadcasters pull back their shock-jock YouTube operations as audience fatigue with partisan screaming matches grows.
  • The Rise of Fact-Checking Collectives: Keep an eye on independent, data-driven startup media ventures attempting to fill the void left by partisan cheerleaders.
  • Political Digital Strategy: Watch how major political parties—particularly the governing People Power Party and the opposition Democratic Party of Korea—adjust their reliance on radical partisan YouTubers ahead of upcoming electoral cycles.

Ultimately, the health of a nation's public square reflects the habits of its citizens. As long as consumers continue to reward malicious mockery and hollow outrage with clicks, the market for counterfeit critique will thrive. Rebuilding basic journalistic norms—demanding adherence to facts and logical consistency while discarding tribal venom—is the only antidote to an information ecosystem slipping into a desert of distrust.
