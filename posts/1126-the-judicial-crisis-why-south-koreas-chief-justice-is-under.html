The Judicial Crisis: Why South Korea’s Chief Justice is Under Fire

October 14, 2026

Imagine the head of a nation’s highest court appearing before parliament, refusing to take a formal oath, and wielding his constitutional power to nominate judges as a weapon to challenge the President’s executive authority. This isn't a scene from a political thriller; it is the current reality in South Korea. The ongoing controversy surrounding Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae has transcended a mere internal administrative dispute within the courts; it has evolved into a full-blown constitutional collision that threatens the very foundation of South Korea's democracy.

From tense standoffs during parliamentary audits to a laundry list of criminal allegations brought forth by the Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive, reform-minded party), the judiciary’s current posture looks less like an impartial arbiter of justice and more like a fortress built to protect the establishment’s grip on power. To understand why this matters, we must look beyond the courtroom and examine the political calculations fueling what appears to be a deliberate, systemic delay in judicial proceedings.

The Chief Justice vs. The Constitution

The trouble began in the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, where the atmosphere was charged from the start. Lawmakers questioned the propriety of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s refusal to take an oath during a government audit—an act many critics view as a blatant display of arrogance. The confrontation deepened when Cho suggested that the constitutional requirement to "respect" the President’s input on judicial appointments was merely a political suggestion, not a legal obligation.

This claim has sent shockwaves through legal circles. The Chief Justice holds the power of "recommendation" for Supreme Court justices, which the President then formally appoints with parliamentary consent. By essentially telling the President, "I will pick the candidates, and you will accept them," Cho is positioning himself as an unelected gatekeeper of the judiciary. As constitutional scholar Kim Seon-taek of Korea University Law School aptly noted, this is a clear case of dereliction of duty. By holding the nomination process hostage to advance his own political leverage, Cho is paralyzing the judicial system and eroding public trust.

Allegations of Corruption at the Top

The controversy took a darker turn when the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Shin Jang-sik, leveled seven specific criminal allegations against the Chief Justice. These accusations go beyond political posturing; they allege systemic financial abuse at the highest levels of the judiciary.

The party claims that the Supreme Court improperly distributed over 600 million won (approximately $430,000) in "encouragement bonuses." Under South Korean law, the misappropriation of state funds of this magnitude can lead to severe criminal penalties. Furthermore, the allegations include the falsification of official documents and the misuse of "business promotion expenses"—effectively treating public funds as a private slush fund.

Details of these expenses are particularly damning. Reports indicate that meal receipts were fabricated to hide the true number of attendees at official dinners. In one instance, a dinner for three people was claimed as a meal for twelve, with the excess funds pocketed. When such blatant financial impropriety occurs in the institution tasked with upholding the rule of law, the public’s confidence is inevitably shattered. Critics argue that the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) has been slow to act, hampered by holdovers from the era of the ousted former leader, Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving time in prison.

Stalling Justice: The "Political Shield" Strategy

Perhaps the most alarming aspect of this crisis is the intentional delay of high-profile legal cases. A prime example is the case involving former government officials from the era of Yoon Suk-yeol, such as Han Duck-soo. Despite being indicted last December, the trial has been mired in procedural games, specifically the filing of "judge recusal applications"—tactics used to disqualify judges who are deemed unfavorable to the defense.

While standard procedural logic dictates that these applications should be resolved within months, the Supreme Court has remained silent for over ten months. Analysts suggest this is a coordinated effort to run out the clock. By keeping these cases in limbo, the court provides a "shield" for officials associated with the previous regime, preventing them from being held accountable for their actions during the Yoon Suk-yeol years.

The strategy is transparent: wait for the upcoming February judicial reshuffle. By stalling until the mandatory rotation of judges, the current leadership hopes to replace the presiding judges with others who may be more amenable to their interests, thereby breaking the momentum of ongoing trials.

What to Watch Next

The judiciary in South Korea is at a critical juncture. The days of expecting the court to "self-correct" through internal ethics are long gone. As the public witnesses the Chief Justice and his allies prioritize institutional self-preservation over the impartial application of the law, the demand for external pressure and reform is growing louder.

Looking ahead, keep an eye on these three developments:

  • The Supreme Court’s response to the recusal applications: Any further unexplained delays will likely spark massive public protests and intensify calls for parliamentary investigations into the individual justices involved.
  • The status of the criminal complaints: How the Corruption Investigation Office handles the seven allegations against Chief Justice Cho will determine whether the institution can regain any semblance of credibility.
  • Judicial Reform Legislation: Expect the ruling Democratic Party and other reform-minded groups to push for legislative changes to the judicial appointment and oversight processes to strip the Chief Justice of his ability to unilaterally block judicial transparency.

The judiciary is designed to be the ultimate check on power. When the judges themselves begin to act like a political cartel, the entire democratic system is at risk. For South Korea, the battle for a fair and accountable justice system has only just begun.
