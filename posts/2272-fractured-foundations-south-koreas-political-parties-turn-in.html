Fractured Foundations: South Korea’s Political Parties Turn Inward

November 13, 2026

On November 12, the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul were eerily quiet regarding legislative progress, but the political atmosphere was suffocatingly tense. Rather than debating the urgent economic issues facing South Korea, both the ruling and opposition parties were consumed by internal crises. It was a day defined not by policy, but by self-inflicted wounds, as both the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), scrambled to contain escalating internal fractures.

The DPK’s Internal Discipline and the "Presidential" Misstep

The ruling Democratic Party—the party of President Lee Jae-myung—found itself in damage-control mode following a series of controversial remarks by lawmaker Choi Min-hee. In a move that sent shockwaves through the party leadership, Choi publicly claimed that "President Lee Jae-myung himself opposes the impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol."

The comment caused immediate political whiplash. The impeachment of the disgraced former leader, currently imprisoned, is a sensitive topic that cuts to the core of the DPK’s base. By invoking the President's name to voice a stance that contradicted the party’s hardline approach, Choi not only confused the public but also drew a sharp rebuke from the Presidential office, which expressed "deep regret."

Party leader Kim Min-seok acted with uncharacteristic speed, ordering an immediate ethics inspection into Choi’s conduct. This was more than just a reaction to a single gaffe; it was a clear signal from the leadership that they are losing patience with rogue messaging. As the DPK struggles to maintain a unified front, Choi’s subsequent apology did little to stop the bleeding, especially after her disparaging remarks toward journalists further soured public sentiment.

The People Power Party’s "Civil War" Over an Online Bulletin Board

While the DPK dealt with internal discipline, the People Power Party (PPP) seemed to be nearing a state of total structural collapse. The conflict centers on the party’s own online bulletin board, where anonymous posts attacking party leadership have fueled a fierce, bitter feud between current party leader Han Dong-hoon and senior lawmaker Jang Dong-hyeok.

Jang has publicly accused Han’s leadership of overseeing a "political purge" and engaging in "immoral politics." The situation reached a breaking point when an ethics committee member admitted that disciplinary actions against Han's camp had been manipulated. This revelation has turned the party into a battlefield, with accusations of backroom deals and character assassination becoming the new normal.

For a conservative party currently relegated to the opposition, this infighting is disastrous. With the PPP’s approval ratings hovering in the mid-20% range—significantly lower than the DPK’s 35%—the party appears unable to function as a viable alternative to the current government. Instead of drafting policy, the PPP is obsessed with internal power dynamics and the fallout from the "bulletin board scandal," leaving their supporters disillusioned and the party’s future direction in total limbo.

The Judiciary Under Siege: A Dangerous Precedent

Amidst this political chaos, a shadow looms over the judiciary. With the legal cases surrounding former leader Yoon Suk-yeol reaching critical junctures—specifically the potential release of key aide Han Duck-soo upon the expiration of his detention—some in the DPK are ramping up pressure on the courts.

Calls for the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae have resurfaced among DPK hardliners. They argue that the judiciary is dragging its feet, creating an environment where high-profile figures associated with the previous administration might escape justice. However, this tactic is a dangerous gamble. By framing judicial delays as a reason to threaten the impeachment of the head of the judiciary, the DPK risks undermining the very independence of the courts they claim to protect. It is a cynical maneuver: the parties treat the judiciary as a weapon when it serves their interests and as a target of "manipulation" when it doesn't.

What to Watch Next

As the dust settles on November 12, two major indicators will determine whether these parties can pull themselves back from the brink:

  • The Outcome of the DPK Ethics Inspection: The investigation into Choi Min-hee will serve as a barometer for the DPK’s internal stability. If Kim Min-seok follows through with strict disciplinary measures, he may succeed in reining in the party's populist wings. If the process drags on, it will signal that the internal factionalism—compounded by ongoing debates over tax policies like the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax—remains unresolved.
  • The PPP’s Leadership Crisis: The bulletin board scandal is unlikely to fade. Watch for how the party handles the aftermath of the "manipulated discipline" scandal. If Han Dong-hoon cannot silence his critics, or if the party continues to prioritize personal vendettas over legislative strategy, the PPP may face a formal schism before the next regional elections.

For now, the Korean public remains the main victim of this political paralysis. While politicians in the National Assembly focus on internal "hyenas" and character attacks, the pressing realities of the economy and the public’s livelihoods continue to be pushed to the margins. The question remains whether either side is capable of putting the country before their own internal survival.
