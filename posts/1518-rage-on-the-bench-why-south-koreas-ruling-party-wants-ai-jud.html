Rage on the Bench: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Wants AI Judges After Seoul Mayor’s Acquittal

October 27, 2026

When the Seoul High Court cleared Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon of charges in his appellate trial, it was meant to conclude a contentious legal chapter for one of South Korea’s most prominent conservative figures. Instead, the verdict detonated an institutional crisis. Within hours, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea erupted in fury, accusing the judiciary of issuing a tailored “get-out-of-jail-free card” to a powerful political elite. The fallout has escalated far beyond routine partisan complaints: lawmakers are now openly preparing legislation to replace human judges with artificial intelligence in political trials, while demanding the impeachment of Chief Justice Jo Hee-de.

To understand the intensity of this reaction, one must look at the charged political landscape in Seoul. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the ruling Democratic Party has placed structural reform of the country’s legal and prosecutorial apparatus at the center of its agenda. Yet, senior progressives argue that the courts remain deeply compromised by ideological networks and institutional habits inherited from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was ousted via impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence. In the eyes of the ruling bloc, Mayor Oh’s acquittal is not an isolated legal dispute, but undeniable evidence of a judiciary systematically shielding conservative elites.

A High-Profile Acquittal and the Shadow of the Myung Scandal

Mayor Oh Se-hoon, currently serving as the head of the capital’s metropolitan government, represents the heavyweight political tier of the People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition. His legal entanglements have intersected with the broader political storm known as the Myung Tae-kyun scandal—a sweeping controversy involving a political fixer and pollster accused of covertly manipulating public opinion data and peddling influence across conservative political campaigns. During Oh’s appellate trial, prosecutors and political critics pushed heavily on circumstantial records and testimony pointing toward questionable campaign activities linked to this network.

The appellate court’s complete dismissal of the charges struck the ruling camp like an open challenge. The Democratic Party’s internal task force dedicated to the Myung Tae-kyun affair immediately condemned the verdict, arguing that the court had applied an impossibly lenient standard of evidence tailored specifically to clear a powerful politician. Democratic Party Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok openly lamented the “politicization of the courts,” arguing that the legal reasoning behind the written ruling was fractured and detached from common sense.

For ruling party strategists, the acquittal represents a double standard that ordinary citizens would never enjoy. When low-income defendants face trial, the legal system applies strict, unforgiving metrics; when a high-profile conservative mayor stands before the bench, the ruling camp argues, judges craft labyrinthine legal technicalities to ensure an exit. This perceived bias has convinced the Democratic Party that the traditional appeal to judicial independence has degenerated into an ironclad shield behind which judges make calculated political decisions.

"Cold Algorithms Over Biased Men": The Rise of the AI Judge Proposal

Faced with what they view as a fundamentally broken bench, several ruling party lawmakers have moved toward a radical, unprecedented alternative: taking human beings out of the courtroom altogether.

Leading this charge is Representative Choi Min-hee, an outspoken Democratic Party lawmaker who publicly proposed stripping human judges of their authority over politically sensitive trials. Her argument is simple: if subjective political alignments, ideological networks, and personal career calculations make human judges incapable of neutrality, justice is better served by cold, emotionless algorithms trained strictly on statutory texts and objective precedent.

"When human judges lose the trust of the public by repeatedly issuing incomprehensible rulings that bend toward power, data-driven machines become the fairer alternative."

Choi announced that she is preparing formal legislation to pilot artificial intelligence in judicial proceedings concerning political figures. While the proposal initially sounded like high-tech hyperbole to outside observers, party insiders confirm that it is being treated as a serious policy option. It signals a major doctrinal shift within South Korea’s progressive camp: rather than attempting to reform the judiciary through slow personnel appointments or procedural tweaks, they are contemplating automated systemic disruption.

The opposition People Power Party reacted with fierce indignation, dismissing the idea as an unhinged assault on the constitutional order. Conservative spokespersons accused the Democratic Party of acting like an authoritarian faction that demands algorithmic programming simply to produce guilty verdicts against its political rivals. However, the ruling bloc maintains that the AI initiative is merely an honest reflection of collapsed public faith in human judges who hide their partisan calculations behind their black robes.

Targeting the Top: The Momentum to Impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-de

The fury sparked by Mayor Oh’s acquittal has climbed straight to the pinnacle of South Korea’s judicial hierarchy: Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-de. Within the ruling party, the word “impeachment” has rapidly shifted from a fringe rhetorical threat into an active legislative strategy.

Senior figures including Representative Jung Chung-rae and former party leader Song Young-gil have openly championed an impeachment resolution against Chief Justice Jo, asserting that the judiciary under his tenure has subtly but unmistakably drifted back into conservative deference. Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok has put forward a structured plan: launch a sweeping parliamentary inquiry into judicial bias first, assemble the evidentiary record, and then initiate formal impeachment proceedings on the floor of the National Assembly.

This pressure is reinforced by the Rebuilding Korea Party, a staunch progressive ally led by Cho Kuk. Following Oh’s verdict, Cho urged the Democratic Party to take proactive, decisive measures rather than passive complaints, proposing an urgent summit among five progressive-leaning parliamentary party leaders to orchestrate collective action against what he termed "Jo Hee-de's politicized judiciary" before next summer.

The confrontation boiled over inside the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee during its recent audit sessions. Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Hyun-ji sparked fierce opposition walkouts and disruptions after projecting presentation slides directly dismantling the legal logic of the appellate court’s acquittal. When questioned, Court Administration Minister Cheon Dae-yeop offered a boilerplate defense, insisting that the Supreme Court leadership cannot and does not interfere with the decisions of lower-court judges, and warning that attacks on individual rulings undermine the rule of law. To ruling party lawmakers, however, that defense sounded like bureaucratic evasion, leaving the committee hearing paralyzed in bitter partisan gridlock.

What to Watch Next

South Korea’s political battlefield has now firmly shifted from the executive branch to the courtroom door. As the Democratic Party pairs its everyday legislative duties—designating November as a focused "Citizens' Safety Month"—with an aggressive judicial accountability campaign, several key fault lines will determine the outcome of this institutional showdown:

  • The Supreme Court's Appellate Review: Prosecutors are preparing to challenge Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s acquittal before the Supreme Court. The highest bench's handling of the evidence surrounding the Myung Tae-kyun network will serve as a definitive litmus test of whether the judiciary intends to de-escalate tensions or double down on its current trajectory.
  • Drafting of the AI Judiciary Bill: Track whether Representative Choi Min-hee’s draft bill garners official backing from the Democratic Party leadership. If introduced as a formal party-line bill, it will ignite an unprecedented global debate over the legality and ethics of delegating constitutional judicial powers to machine learning systems.
  • The Parliamentary Inquiry into the Courts: The immediate tactical move to monitor is Kim Min-seok's proposed National Assembly investigation into judicial bias. If the ruling coalition musters its legislative majority to pass the inquiry motion, it will haul senior judicial administrators before live broadcast hearings, laying the groundwork for a historic impeachment vote against Chief Justice Jo Hee-de.

Mayor Oh's legal victory was meant to steady his political footing. Instead, it has ignited a fierce, existential battle over who ultimately holds power over South Korean justice—the judges who interpret the statutes, or the lawmakers determined to rewrite the entire system.
