The Crumbling Alt-Right International: Why Global Populism is Stuttering and What it Means for Korea

November 06, 2026

For years, the political map of the West and parts of Asia seemed to be tilting toward a permanent populist right-wing shift. From the MAGA movement in the United States to the rise of the AfD in Germany and Reform UK in Britain, the playbook was consistent: weaponize economic frustration, stir resentment against immigrants, and align with "strongman" figures like Donald Trump or Vladimir Putin. But recent developments suggest that this global far-right network is beginning to fray at the seams, caught between a lack of viable economic solutions and a sudden, sharp distancing from its former American figureheads.

As news broke on November 5th, it became clear that the global far-right is facing a moment of profound upheaval. The catalyst was an unexpected Democratic landslide in the U.S. midterm elections, which saw the party maintain and expand its control of Congress. This shift has sent shockwaves across the Atlantic and toward the Pacific, forcing populist leaders in Europe and Japan to scramble for a new identity. For South Korea—a nation currently navigating the administration of President Lee Jae-myung while the main opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), struggles to find its footing after the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—these global trends offer a stark warning about the shelf life of "identity politics" in the face of real-world crises.

The Trump Divorce: Why European Populists are Scrubbing Their Social Media

For nearly a decade, being "Trump-adjacent" was a badge of honor for Europe’s nationalist leaders. Figures like Marine Le Pen in France and the leadership of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) viewed the MAGA movement as a blueprint for success. However, following the decisive victory of the Democrats in the U.S. midterms, that alliance has turned toxic almost overnight. European far-right parties are now engaging in a frantic process of "distancing," realizing that the Trumpian brand of populism may no longer be the electoral "cheat code" it once was.

This shift is not merely about optics; it is a cold calculation of survival. These parties, which built their platforms on "national interests" and "sovereignty," are proving to be remarkably opportunistic. The moment their ideological North Star in Washington began to dim, they moved to abandon him. This highlights a fundamental truth about global far-right alliances: they are built on a foundation of sand. Because these movements are rooted in national egoism, they lack the coherent, long-term solidarity found in traditional internationalist movements. When the tide turns, it is every leader for themselves.

This phenomenon carries a heavy lesson for South Korea’s conservative opposition. During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, certain factions within the People Power Party (PPP) attempted to mimic the aggressive, confrontational style of Western populism. Now, with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol in prison following his impeachment and the country under the leadership of President Lee Jae-myung, the PPP is witnessing the limits of that strategy. Relying on the "winds of populism" from abroad is a dangerous gamble; when those winds die down, leaders who have failed to cultivate a genuine, policy-driven connection with their own citizens find themselves exposed.

The Farage-Le Pen Snub and the Myth of Far-Right Unity

Nowhere was the internal friction of the far-right more visible than in the recent public spat between Nigel Farage, leader of Reform UK, and Marine Le Pen’s National Rally (RN) in France. Farage, a long-time architect of Brexit and a vocal Trump supporter, recently attempted to bolster his standing by announcing a high-profile meeting with Le Pen in Paris. He framed it as a grand summit of the "new European right."

The response from the French side was a humiliating rebuff. Officials from the National Rally flatly denied that any such meeting was on the schedule, effectively telling Farage that he was not welcome. For Le Pen, the calculation was simple: Farage’s close ties to a weakened Trump and his past praise for Vladimir Putin have become liabilities. Le Pen is currently attempting to "de-demonize" her party to win over mainstream French voters, and a photo-op with a British firebrand known for conspiracy theories was the last thing she needed.

"The sudden cold shoulder from Paris shows that even within the far-right, there is a hierarchy of survival. Alliances are discarded the moment they threaten a party’s path to domestic power."

Farage’s attempt to use "security and defense" as a new platform further illustrates this desperation. He recently appointed a former Royal Air Force (RAF) official with ties to the Ukraine conflict as his party's defense spokesperson, while simultaneously calling for a blanket pardon for veterans accused of crimes during past operations. It is a classic populist move: wrapping oneself in the flag to distract from past mistakes. However, critics have been quick to point out Farage’s own history, specifically his 2014 comments suggesting he "admired" Putin for his handling of the Crimea crisis. This hypocrisy—posing as a defender of national security while having historically sympathized with aggressors—is a recurring theme that is finally catching up with the global far-right.

The Economic Wall: When Hate Fails to Lower Gas Prices

The primary reason these movements are faltering is the "economic reality check." For years, far-right parties blamed every domestic problem on immigrants or "globalist" elites. But as the world grapples with the fallout of the war in Ukraine and rising energy costs, these simplistic slogans are failing to put food on the table. In Europe, the spike in inflation has been a disaster for populist credibility.

Initially, these parties blamed the sitting governments for the high cost of living. However, as the public began to realize that the global energy crisis was exacerbated by the very "hardline" foreign policies championed by the Trump-aligned wing of the right, the logic collapsed. Voters are discovering that while populists are excellent at identifying grievances, they are notoriously poor at managing economies. In France, this frustration recently boiled over into massive street protests, with 600,000 people marching across 220 locations to demand higher wages and better student rights. While the far-right tries to frame these protests as "chaos" that only they can fix, the protesters themselves are focused on the bread-and-butter issues that the far-right’s "identity war" ignores.

In South Korea, President Lee Jae-myung has focused his administration’s narrative on economic stability and social safety nets. This presents a challenge for the opposition PPP. If the conservative movement continues to focus on "anti-other" rhetoric—whether directed at labor unions or gender issues—they risk becoming as irrelevant as the European parties currently struggling to answer why their "nationalist" policies haven't lowered the price of a liter of petrol. As global trends show, when the bill comes due, voters prefer solutions over slogans.

The Rising Threat of Hybrid Warfare and Hate Speech

While the political wings of the far-right may be struggling, their "hybrid" tactics—using misinformation and hate speech to destabilize society—remain a potent threat. In Germany, the AfD was recently humiliated after its members shared a viral video of a "suffering child" that turned out to be a fabricated piece of Russian propaganda. This incident exposed how deeply the far-right has become a conduit for foreign disinformation, often without even performing basic fact-checking.

This trend is also visible in Japan, where the ruling Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) is exploring a coalition with the Democratic Party for the People to shore up its power. Amidst this political maneuvering, a wave of xenophobic sentiment has surged on Japanese social media. Fabricated stories and old photos are being used to incite anger against migrant workers. Activists like Shin Sugok have noted that while Korea is often seen as a potential future leader in human rights, the current climate in Japan shows how easily a democratic society can backslide when politicians flirt with fringe hate groups for electoral gain.

Perhaps most concerning is the emergence of "hybrid threats" involving extremist groups. Recently, a Russian-linked misogynistic group claimed responsibility for bomb threats against European airliners, showing that the line between political agitation and outright terrorism is blurring. These groups use "culture war" issues to recruit and then transition into activities designed to create widespread public fear.

What to Watch Next

The global far-right is currently in a state of "unstable retreat." They have lost their primary benefactor in the U.S. and are failing to provide answers to the global economic crisis. However, they are most dangerous when they are cornered. Here are the specific developments to monitor in the coming months:

  • The PPP's Identity Crisis: Watch how South Korea's People Power Party rebrands itself ahead of upcoming local elections. Will they double down on the populist tactics of the Yoon Suk-yeol era, or will they attempt a pivot toward the "rational conservatism" that some European parties are now desperately seeking?
  • European Decoupling: Look for whether Marine Le Pen and other European leaders continue to distance themselves from "MAGA" aesthetics. The success or failure of this "de-Trumpification" will determine if the far-right can survive as a mainstream force.
  • The Misinformation Battle: As seen in Germany and Japan, the use of Russian-style "fake news" is becoming a standard tool for the far-right. Watch for how governments, including the Lee Jae-myung administration, move to regulate online disinformation without infringing on free speech.
  • Economic Protests: If the massive strikes in France spread to other parts of Europe or Asia, it will signal a definitive shift in the public mood—away from "identity politics" and back toward a class-based demand for economic justice.

The "Alt-Right International" was always a marriage of convenience. Now that the convenience is gone, the world is watching to see what, if anything, will take its place.
