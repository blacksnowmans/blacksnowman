How South Korea's Justice Ministry Became a Prosecutors' Club Again

October 02, 2026

Walk into the Ministry of Justice in Gwacheon or Seoul today, and you will hear a familiar sound: the sharp click of prosecutors' shoes on the stone floors. Just a few years ago, "de-prosecutorialization"—the drive to strip career prosecutors of administrative posts and hand them to civilian legal experts—was touted as the cornerstone of South Korea's judicial reform. Today, looking at spreadsheet tracking data within the ministry, that grand vision looks less like a policy and more like a relic of the past.

To understand modern South Korean politics, one must understand the sprawling, often invisible web of institutional power. Under successive administrations, particularly during the liberal Moon Jae-in presidency (2017–2022), the government tried to break the prosecution service's legendary monopoly over investigation, indictment, and policy-making. But under the subsequent conservative administration of President Yoon Suk Yeol—himself a former Prosecutor General—the pendulum has swung back with astonishing force. The Ministry of Justice has not only returned to its old habits; it has locked the door behind it.

The Quiet Recapture of the Ministry of Justice

The evidence of what critics call "re-prosecutorialization" is laid bare in internal staffing charts. Within the Ministry of Justice's core Prosecution Bureau—tasked with overseeing prosecutorial policy—98 out of 127 total positions are held by active-duty prosecutors. In the Inspection Office, 15 of 32 slots belong to prosecutors. In the Legal Affairs Bureau, 67 out of 196 personnel are prosecutors, with key divisions like the Legal Affairs Division, Unification Legal Affairs, Commercial Legal Affairs, and even the North Korean Human Rights Records Preservation Center being headed exclusively by prosecutors.

This is not merely an issue of human resources; it is a structural takeover of policy-making power. The Ministry of Justice is formally designated as the senior administrative body meant to supervise and keep the prosecution service in check. When the ministry's own offices are staffed predominantly by prosecutors, the institutional watchdog effectively becomes the watchdog's master. The Ministry of Justice has transformed from an independent administrative agency into a commanding headquarters for the prosecutorial elite.

Even bureaus seemingly far removed from criminal investigation—such as the Human Rights Bureau and the International Legal Affairs Bureau—are heavily staffed by prosecutors. Key sections dealing with women's and children's human rights, crime victim support, and foreign investment disputes are routinely led by men and women seconded from the local prosecutor's office. The once-lauded goal of ensuring civilian expertise in legal administration has been thoroughly neutralized.

The Extended Network: Prosecutors Embedded Across Government

The influence of South Korea's prosecutors does not stop at the Ministry of Justice's gates. As of late 2026, dozens of active-duty prosecutors remain dispatched to outside government bodies and regulatory agencies—18 different institutions in total, accounting for at least 27 seconded prosecutors.

Chief among these is the Financial Services Commission (FSC), South Korea's top financial regulator, which routinely hosts several prosecutors. For an ambitious prosecutor, agencies dealing with corporate finance and chaebol (family-run conglomerate) accounting are high-value postings. They sit at the intersection of capital, regulatory oversight, and potential white-collar crime. While officially framed as "inter-agency cooperation," critics argue these secondments allow the prosecution to build an intelligence network across the government, utilizing special judicial police powers to peer into the inner workings of regulatory bodies.

Despite legal revisions intended to separate investigation from indictment—a core demand of progressive reformers—subordinate regulations have left wide loopholes. Special judicial police operating within regulatory agencies are still legally mandated to notify prosecutors immediately of major corporate, labor, or national security investigations. In practice, prosecutors retain informal "guidance and advice" powers, effectively steering investigations from behind the scenes.

The Media Landscape and the Crisis of Journalistic Neutrality

As state power consolidates around traditional legal elites, South Korea's broader public square is undergoing a separate, equally turbulent upheaval. The relationship between the media, politicians, and the public has broken away from the old twentieth-century model of high-cost, centralized broadcasting.

Veteran public figures and progressive commentators like Rhyu Si-min have long noted how elite print and broadcast media once acted as a restrictive gatekeeper. In the old days of expensive printing presses and state-licensed television networks, ordinary citizens had virtually no direct way to challenge the narrative set by major newspapers and broadcasters. The democratization of media via digital platforms, smartphones, and independent YouTube channels has shattered that monopoly.

Yet mainstream journalism in South Korea has responded to this shift not by adapting, but by retreating into defensive arrogance. Established reporters often label alternative digital media as biased or conspiratorial, assuming that institutional press badges equate to inherent objectivity. In reality, the public's trust in legacy media has plummeted. Major outlets frequently operate as political "players" wearing the mask of neutral referees, attacking opposing political factions while dismissing their own partisan leanings as objective reporting.

This hypocrisy extends to how the media handles criticism. Mainstream journalists have spent decades critiquing politicians, celebrities, and activists. Yet when independent commentators or alternative media turn the lens back on the press, legacy journalists react with indignation, insisting that their own work is beyond public accountability. Until South Korea's mainstream press acknowledges its own partisan alignment, public cynicism toward traditional journalism will only deepen.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates the remainder of the 2020s, several concrete developments will indicate whether these institutional trends will harden or face a renewed pushback:

  • Personnel Shifts in the Bureaucracy: Watch for upcoming ministerial appointments and internal reshufflings at the Ministry of Justice. Any future movement to replace bureau heads with civilian legal scholars will signal a genuine return to de-prosecutorialization.
  • Regulatory Agency Secondments: Keep an eye on legislative audits tracking the exact number of prosecutors dispatched to financial regulators like the FSC and the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS).
  • Media Reform Debates: Monitor upcoming parliamentary discussions regarding defamation laws, punitive damages for media outlets, and press arbitration rules, which remain a perennial battleground between conservative and progressive lawmakers.
  • Civil Society Pushback: Observe how civic groups and opposition parties challenge the remaining legal loopholes that allow prosecutors to supervise specialized investigative police units.

In an era where institutional guardrails are steadily being rewritten by the very actors they were designed to restrain, the future of South Korea's democracy may depend less on grand political rhetoric and more on the unglamorous, day-to-day work of public scrutiny and transparent record-keeping.
