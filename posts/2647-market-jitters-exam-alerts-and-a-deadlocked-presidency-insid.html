Market Jitters, Exam Alerts, and a Deadlocked Presidency: Inside a Week of Split South Korean Polls

November 23, 2026

Imagine sitting at a desk in Seoul during a week that refuses to stay in one lane. Before lunch, your news feed brings dispatches from President Lee Jae-myung's diplomatic swing through ASEAN and APEC summits. By the time the financial markets close, push notifications warn that South Korea's benchmark stock index has tumbled. Mixed into the same seven-day cycle are a major court ruling involving Han Duck-soo and the nationwide logistical undertaking of South Korea's annual college entrance exam.

On paper, a week packed with that many heavy headlines should send public opinion swinging sharply in one direction or another. Yet polling figures analyzed on the online program Humble Broadcast by Lee Taek-soo, CEO of the South Korean polling firm Realmeter, and political commentator Park Si-young of Park Si-young TV reveal a much more intricate picture. Across both live telephone interviews and automated response system (ARS) surveys, President Lee's job approval rating barely budged, shifting by roughly 0.1 percentage points. At the very same time, 65 percent of respondents gave positive marks to the government's coordinated handling of the national college entrance exam, known locally as Suneung.

Looking beneath the headline averages—from daily swings in the presidential numbers to sharp divergences between political parties—shows a public mood split between economic anxiety on one hand and appreciation for practical, everyday competence on the other.

Why a Flat Presidential Rating Hides a Fierce Tug-of-War

A 0.1 percentage-point shift over a single week might look like stability at first glance, but in polling terms, it often signals opposing forces canceling each other out. As Lee Taek-soo and Park Si-young traced the numbers, positive impressions from overseas diplomacy and smooth exam management pushed ratings upward, while market turbulence and tension surrounding the Han Duck-soo court ruling pulled them back down in equal measure.

The daily breakdown from Realmeter tells a far more dramatic story than the weekly average suggests. President Lee's approval rating had been fluctuating within a roughly one-percentage-point band before climbing to 38.4 percent on Thursday, sparking talk within political circles that his support might break through the 40 percent threshold. For a brief moment, diplomatic visibility and steady domestic administration appeared ready to give the presidential office real upward momentum.

Then came Friday, when the KOSPI—South Korea's primary benchmark stock index—fell below the 5,000 mark. That single session wiped out the gains accumulated through Thursday and knocked the weekly rating back to flat.

For international readers, it is hard to overstate how closely urban South Koreans, especially office workers in their 30s, track equity markets. Lunchtime conversations in Seoul's business districts routinely revolve around domestic stocks, exchange rates, and consumer prices. Diplomatic summits happen on television screens; a sudden drop in the stock market hits personal brokerage accounts and household budgets in real time. The sharp contrast between Thursday's 38.4 percent high and Friday's slump illustrates that in a stagnant polling environment, economic indicators—not political messaging—hold the steering wheel.

Polling Methods Expose a Divide Inside the Ruling Camp

While the president's overall rating stood still, party support moved in opposite directions depending on how pollsters asked the question. Understanding this split requires a brief note on South Korean polling methodology. Live telephone interviews conducted by human callers generally reach a broader, more representative cross-section of the public, including moderate and politically detached voters. By contrast, automated response system (ARS) surveys—which use pre-recorded voice prompts—tend to attract respondents who follow politics closely and hold firmer partisan views.

According to the data reviewed by Lee Taek-soo and Park Si-young, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea dropped by 4 percentage points in live telephone interviews while gaining 2 percentage points in ARS surveys. A six-point divergence in direction points to two simultaneous trends: hardcore, politically active supporters remain mobilized and vocal, while broader and more moderate voters are stepping back or showing signs of fatigue amid political and market uncertainty.

Complicating the picture further, the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller progressive party—crossed the 10 percent mark in an ARS survey for the first time, reaching 10.4 percent. Combined, the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party pushed their joint support back above 50 percent. Rather than shrinking, the broader progressive-leaning electorate appears to be dividing its backing across two parties, creating a balance in which voters support the wider camp while still expecting mutual checks and cooperation.

Even with that combined majority, internal leadership numbers inside the Democratic Party carry warning signs. The polling discussed on the broadcast showed that Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok's job performance rating fell compared to a month earlier. More concerning for the party is his standing among its own supporters, which sits noticeably lower than the 70 to 80 percent approval levels recorded under former party leaders Lee Jae-myung and Jung Chung-rae. Holding a party together requires energizing loyalists just as much as appealing to swing voters, and cooling enthusiasm within the base presents a tangible challenge for Kim's leadership.

How an Exam Won 65 Percent Approval—Especially From Thirty-Somethings

While political and economic indicators delivered mixed signals, everyday public administration produced a clear standout. Roughly 65 percent of respondents positively evaluated the government-wide handling of Suneung—nearly double President Lee's overall approval rating.

For readers outside South Korea, Suneung is not just another standardized test. Held once a year, this high-stakes university entrance exam shapes the academic and career trajectories of hundreds of thousands of students. On exam day, cities effectively reorganize around the schedule: authorities adjust morning work hours to ease congestion, manage traffic around testing centers, and enforce strict noise controls near school buildings.

In the days leading up to the exam, public commentary and online forums carried familiar anxiety over potential disruptions or administrative errors. When the day unfolded without major incidents, public relief translated directly into high approval ratings. Most citizens expect the government, above all else, to keep essential national schedules running smoothly, and the absence of chaos demonstrated basic institutional reliability.

Notably, respondents in their 30s expressed especially strong approval for the exam response. At first glance, thirty-somethings might seem removed from Suneung, as most are past their own university years and not yet parents of high school seniors. Yet they are the everyday commuters who directly experience morning traffic adjustments and noise restrictions on their way to work. Many also watch younger siblings or nieces and nephews prepare for the test, and still remember how a single administrative mistake on exam day can upend a student's future. For this generation, ideological arguments carry far less weight than watching public systems operate calmly, fairly, and predictably.

Approval was not just about avoiding accidents, however. Polling also reflected a widespread perception that authorities effectively detected cheating during the exam. In a fiercely competitive testing environment, fairness is non-negotiable; confirming that rule-breakers are actually caught reinforces public trust far more effectively than any promotional campaign.

The Third Text Alert and the Power of Good Timing

Perhaps the sharpest reversal in public sentiment during the Suneung operation involved the government's use of emergency disaster text alerts. South Koreans receive frequent cell-broadcast safety alerts on their phones for everything from heavy rain to missing-person notices, leading to widespread alert fatigue. When officials announced plans to deploy disaster text messages as part of the exam oversight system, early reactions were skeptical, with many questioning whether another alert would accomplish anything.

That skepticism faded quickly once actual results emerged. As Lee Taek-soo and Park Si-young highlighted on the broadcast, the decisive factor was timing—specifically, a third disaster text alert sent at a moment when test-takers and supervisors might have started letting their guard down after the initial tension of the morning had passed. Instead of functioning as a routine, mechanical reminder sent just to check a bureaucratic box, the third message arrived at a calculated psychological lull and contributed directly to uncovering cheating.

The turnaround offers a straightforward lesson in governance: voters routinely discount polished policy announcements that produce no visible effect, yet they will readily reward an initially unpopular method once it proves its value in the field.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves past a week defined by colliding headlines, four concrete indicators will show which way public sentiment breaks next:

  • The KOSPI and the 40 percent ceiling: Watch whether South Korea's stock index stabilizes after slipping below 5,000, and whether lingering financial jitters continue to block President Lee's approval rating from crossing the 40 percent mark he approached on Thursday.
  • The polling gap inside the Democratic Party: Track whether the divergence between live telephone interviews (down 4 percentage points) and ARS polls (up 2 percentage points) widens or narrows, which will signal whether fatigue among moderate voters is spreading.
  • The Rebuilding Korea Party's staying power: Monitor whether the party can hold above 10 percent in upcoming surveys and how a combined progressive-leaning share exceeding 50 percent reshapes legislative bargaining.
  • Kim Min-seok's standing with loyalists: Watch whether the Democratic Party leader can halt his one-month slide and rebuild internal confidence closer to the 70 to 80 percent benchmarks seen under his predecessors.
