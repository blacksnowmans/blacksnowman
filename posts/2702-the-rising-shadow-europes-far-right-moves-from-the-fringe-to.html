The Rising Shadow: Europe’s Far-Right Moves from the Fringe to the Mainstream

November 24, 2026

For years, political observers in Europe and North America dismissed far-right movements as noisy outliers—fringe groups relegated to the edges of the public square. They were often treated as social pariahs or the focus of occasional ridicule in mainstream media. But the landscape has shifted, and the reality is now impossible to ignore: these forces have moved beyond the periphery and are aggressively infiltrating the mainstream institutional power structure.

From the rise of the Vox party as a potential coalition kingmaker in Spain to the blatant hardline stance of the Alternative for Germany (AfD), the move toward the center is not a fleeting trend. It is a systematic restructuring of the European political map. As progressives and civil society groups scramble to organize digital counter-offensives, the rest of the world is left asking: How did these groups, once considered extremist curiosities, manage to capture enough voters to challenge the very foundations of the European Union?

Spain’s Snap Election and the Normalization of Vox

In Spain, the transformation of the far-right is perhaps most visible. The Vox party, which was once the target of scorn from the political establishment, has transformed into a central variable in the national government. The party’s growth is not merely a matter of improved polling numbers; it is a fundamental shift in political arithmetic. In a fragmented legislature where traditional conservative parties often fail to secure a majority, the far-right has become the necessary key to power.

For years, a "cordon sanitaire"—a political agreement to never partner with extremist groups—was the standard. That barrier is now crumbling. As the conservative People's Party (PP) looks to secure a path to government under leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo, they are increasingly forced to view Santiago Abascal’s Vox as a viable, if uncomfortable, coalition partner. This shift signals a structural crack in Western politics: the realization that seizing power is becoming more important than upholding long-standing democratic taboos.

Should a coalition between the mainstream right and the far-right materialize, the implications for Spanish policy are severe. Vox has campaigned on aggressive anti-immigration stances, the reduction of LGBTQ+ rights, and centralized state control. By welcoming them into the fold, the mainstream right risks legitimizing policies that were once considered beyond the pale, effectively opening the door for extremist agendas to be codified into national law.

Germany’s AfD and the Digital Resistance

In Germany, the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) is deploying a different, more confrontational strategy. The party has recently made headlines for extreme rhetoric, including calls for the repatriation of over 2.6 million immigrants. More than just populist posturing, this is viewed by many as a precursor to actual mass deportations. Furthermore, the party has openly celebrated the stalling of climate change negotiations, positioning themselves as a bulwark against "globalist" climate policies that they argue harm the national economy.

However, this radicalization has triggered a significant response. Recognizing that traditional party politics alone may not be enough, German progressives and civil society are taking cues from the U.S. political playbook. Movements are now adopting digital organizing techniques—similar to those used by the Bernie Sanders campaign—and utilizing "Super PAC"-style political fundraising to challenge the far-right’s dominance on social media.

This is not a temporary election-cycle effort. It is an evolving, permanent network of citizens dedicated to protecting democratic values. By running constant, real-time fact-checking campaigns and counter-messaging, these groups aim to puncture the digital echo chambers that the far-right has used to radicalize the 20-30 demographic. The stakes are clear: if they fail to stop the digital encroachment of extremist rhetoric, the future political landscape of Germany will be fundamentally altered.

The Collective Push to Reshape the European Union

The ambition of these groups extends far beyond national borders. European far-right factions are increasingly coordinating a long-term goal: to fundamentally restructure the European Union by 2029. Their objective is to dismantle the EU’s core pillars—multilateralism, climate action, and refugee integration—and replace them with a bloc focused on hardline national sovereignty.

There is also an active campaign to force the resignation of European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. By attacking the EU’s environmental and migration policies, these parties hope to weaken the leadership and consolidate their own influence across the continent. This is not just a call for new faces in Brussels; it is a challenge to the European project itself, replacing the spirit of international cooperation with a narrow, exclusionary nationalism.

The trend is not without its hurdles. In France, the government has begun legal procedures to dissolve far-right anti-immigration groups accused of inciting violence. Meanwhile, in Hungary, the Fidesz party—long the poster child for right-wing dominance—is reeling from a series of cash-for-influence scandals and internal fractures. These events highlight a critical vulnerability in the far-right model: the tendency for movements built on exclusionary rhetoric to eventually succumb to the very corruption they claim to despise.

Lessons for a Fragile Democracy

The global rise of far-right sentiment serves as a sobering warning. From the absence of a viable progressive alternative in Canada’s Alberta province, which has allowed right-wing dominance to go unchecked, to the rise of private "vigilante" groups filling the vacuum of perceived insecurity, the message is clear: when institutional politics fails to address the economic anxieties and social grievances of the working class, extremist forces are quick to fill the void with hatred and blame.

Democracy is not a static state; it is a process that requires constant vigilance. As we observe these shifts—whether in Europe, North America, or here in South Korea, where the political landscape remains highly polarized under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—the conclusion remains the same. The firewall protecting human rights and the rule of law is only as strong as the civil society that supports it.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the upcoming parliamentary debates in Spain. If a formal coalition between the People’s Party and Vox is finalized, it will likely serve as a catalyst for other mainstream conservative parties in Europe to abandon their own firewalls. Furthermore, monitor the effectiveness of the digital organizing networks in Germany; if they succeed in curbing the influence of the AfD, they may provide a replicable model for other nations currently facing similar extremist surges.
