Alienating the Base: President Lee Jae-myung’s Dangerous Electoral Gamble

October 22, 2026

When South Korean President Lee Jae-myung took to his official social media account to write a crisp declaration—"In South Korea, we follow the law. #AWorldWithoutUnfairPrivilege #NoExceptionsForLawbreakers"—it was supposed to sound like a resolute stance on public ethics. Instead, it lit a fuse inside his own political home, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP).

The outrage was not caused by the words themselves, but by what followed. In a bid to demonstrate open communication, President Lee personally reshared and interacted with several accounts on the platform. Sharp-eyed party loyalists quickly noticed that among these accounts were aggressive online commentators who had spent months leveling personal attacks against veteran progressive figures, including former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae and prominent progressive intellectual Yu Si-min. To the grassroots activists who had stood out in the freezing cold during candlelight rallies to bring the Democratic Party to power, seeing their president offer a public shout-out to these hostility-driven accounts felt like a betrayal.

South Korea’s ruling party is currently gripped by a bitter internal feud over what party strategists call "structural majority theory"—a grand strategy aimed at winning over moderate and center-right voters by tempering the party's traditional ideological edge. But in pursuing these prospective swing voters, President Lee and his close allies are running a high-stakes experiment: casting aside their loyal core base under the assumption that hardcore progressives have nowhere else to go. From mysterious police raids on friendly media to public denunciations from party intellectuals, the strategy is threatening to fracture the ruling bloc from within.

The Flawed 'Structural Majority' and Internal Party Warfare

To understand the depth of the current rift, one must look at the quiet chaos brewing within the Democratic Party leadership. South Korean political parties rely heavily on dedicated volunteer bases and enthusiastic online networks. For years, the DP's strength lay in its passionate grassroots supporters, who viewed themselves as guardians against conservative power structures.

However, party dynamics shifted following the rise of internal factional warfare. Lawmaker Kim Min-seok, a senior DP figure, recently raised explosive allegations that Shincheonji—a controversial fringe religious group frequently accused of attempting to infiltrate South Korean political organizations—had systematically intervened in the party’s internal selection processes. When factional rivals within the party called Kim’s bluff and demanded a formal criminal investigation to clear the air, Kim’s camp abruptly pivoted to a cautious, hesitant stance.

Party insiders have expressed deep frustration with this double-dealing. A senior DP leadership official acknowledged off the record that continually airing cult-intervention claims without pursuing actual legal complaints was damaging the party. "If you want to expose an illicit coalition, you ask for a formal police investigation," the official noted. "When asked if they actually want an investigation, they back off. It’s a political double game." Weaponizing dramatic conspiracy theories to sideline internal rivals while refusing to submit those claims to official scrutiny has exhausted long-time party members.

This internal friction has directly impacted President Lee’s public standing. Approval rating data over recent months shows extreme volatility—climbing to 84 percent in July, crashing to 37 percent in August amidst internal squabbling, bouncing erratically in September, and settling around 66 percent in October. While an absolute majority, such wildly swinging numbers signal a destabilized political foundation. The strategy championed by President Lee and Lawmaker Kim aims to construct an enduring majority by wooing moderate swing voters, but in practice, it is systematically alienating the very loyalists who build party momentum on the ground.

A Target on Friendly Media: The Raid on Sisa Tapa

The friction inside the ruling coalition escalated from online arguments to official state power on October 15. Just one day prior, Parliament held confirmation hearings for Kim Ji-yong to head the newly created Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA)—a key pillar of the government's law enforcement reform program. On October 15, President Lee formally signed off on Kim’s appointment.

On that exact same day, law enforcement officers executed a sweeping search-and-seizure raid against Lee Jong-won, head of the prominent progressive news and commentary channel Sisa Tapa. Police deployed riot units to raid Lee’s home, office, and personal belongings. To many observers, the overwhelming show of force against a prominent media figure aligned with the broader progressive movement looked less like a standard criminal inquiry and more like targeted intimidation.

In an early-morning broadcast following the raid, Lee Jong-won detailed the items seized by police: publicly accessible YouTube livestreams, browser history files, and public polling data. "They mobilized riot police to seize materials that anyone can download with a single click," Lee said. More controversially, Lee claimed that officers specifically targeted and confiscated whistleblower files stored in his office containing evidence of alleged illegal campaign activities by pro-faction figures within the Democratic Party itself.

A Troubling Precedent: While Korean law enforcement has previously raided political headquarters during campaign disputes—such as the 2020 raid on the DP’s Seongbuk-gap office during the 21st National Assembly election or regional investigations into mobile primary fraud—those actions followed formal criminal complaints. Raiding a friendly media personality to seize internal whistleblower tips raises severe questions about the neutral exercise of police power.

For a government that promised to reform state power and curb prosecutorial excess, using heavy-handed police tactics against internal whistleblowers and friendly commentators risks mirroring the very abuses of power progressives rallied against during the tenure of former President Yoon Suk-yeol (who was impeached, removed from office, and remains imprisoned). Instead of signaling fair law enforcement, the incident has left the progressive base feeling that state machinery is being weaponized to settle internal party accounts.

The Intellectual Revolt: Yu Si-min’s Scathing Indictment

As discontent mounted, one of the progressive movement’s most influential public intellectuals broke his silence. Yu Si-min, a former government minister and long-time opinion leader whose commentary holds immense weight among center-left voters, issued a sharp critique of President Lee’s governance style.

Yu took aim at the core premise of President Lee’s moderate pivot, stating bluntly: "It is desirable to be a president for everyone, but a president with 100 percent approval cannot exist." He argued that Lee’s attempt to appeal to everyone was eroding the administration's core identity. Yu went further, criticizing the president's retreat from his core campaign promises regarding law enforcement reform.

"The reform of the prosecution service that the president envisioned was completely different from what the public understood, what the Democratic Party stood for, and what was pledged during the election," Yu declared, adding harsh words rarely directed by progressive heavyweights at their own leader: "The president has, in effect, deceived the public."

Yu also pointed out a quiet contradiction in the presidential office’s behavior regarding the so-called "New Lee Jae-myung" faction—a group of newer, opportunistically aligned figures such as Lawmaker Lee Un-ju who have aggressively targeted legacy progressives. While President Lee quickly responds to minor online slights directed at himself, the presidential office has remained conspicuously silent as these new factions launch witch-hunts against long-standing party figures like former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae.

Rather than addressing Yu’s substantive arguments, Democratic Party Chairman Kim Min-seok responded defensively, urging supporters to refrain from "derogatory expressions" and threatening formal legal action against internal critics under party rules. Treating internal intellectual criticism with legal threats has only deepened the perception that the party leadership is growing dangerously insular.

Historical Lessons and the Danger of 'Silent Punishment'

Political history in South Korea offers stark warnings about the dangers of abandoning one's political base under the guise of moderate expansion.

Successful attempts to broaden political coalitions—such as President Kim Dae-jung’s landmark "DJP Alliance" in 1997 with conservative leader Kim Jong-pil, or Kim Young-sam’s 1990 three-party merger—succeeded precisely because the leaders kept their core geographic and ideological bases secure before reaching across the aisle. Conversely, the tragic collapse of the Uri Party in 2003–2004 demonstrates what happens when a ruling party wins a legislative majority but destroys itself through internal factional warfare and mutual distrust with its foundational voters.

The current "structural majority" strategy risks repeating the Uri Party mistake. In traditional Korean party politics, conservative voters often rally around their party during elections regardless of dissatisfaction ("voting with pinched noses"). Progressive voters, however, operate differently. When betrayed by their own leadership, progressive voters rarely defect to the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP). Instead, they resort to "silent punishment."

  • Refusing to turn out at the ballot box on election day.
  • Canceling monthly party membership dues and financial donations.
  • Ceasing voluntary advocacy in online spaces and personal networks.
  • Disengaging entirely from national political debates.

This silent withdrawal does not register clearly in standard polling until election night, when it manifests as unexpected, catastrophic turnout slumps. If the current trend continues, party insiders warn that the Democratic Party could face severe setbacks in upcoming legislative and presidential cycles, risking a return to a prolonged period in opposition.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will test whether President Lee Jae-myung’s push for a "structural majority" is a visionary realignment or a fatal political miscalculation. To gauge which direction the ruling party is heading, political analysts should monitor several concrete indicators:

  • Follow-up Actions on the Sisa Tapa Raid: Watch whether the police or the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency pursue actual charges against Lee Jong-won, or if the seized internal campaign files are leaked and weaponized in internal party disciplinary proceedings.
  • Party Dues and Membership Retention: Track reports on Democratic Party monthly fee-paying membership numbers. A sharp drop in paid memberships will be the first quantifiable sign of "silent punishment" taking hold.
  • Legislative Reform Votes: Pay close attention to how the National Assembly handles upcoming prosecutorial and judicial reform bills. Whether the DP passes compromised, diluted versions or pushes through complete structural separation will show whether Lee has permanently pivoted to the center.
  • Reactions from Factional Minority Figures: Observe whether moderate and reform-minded lawmakers within the DP (such as Lawmakers Woo Won-shik or Im Mi-ae) begin publicly organizing against the leadership's centralized control, or if party discipline forces total compliance ahead of future candidate nominations.
