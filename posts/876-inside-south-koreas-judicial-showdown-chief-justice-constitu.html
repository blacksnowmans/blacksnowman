Inside South Korea's Judicial Showdown: Chief Justice, Constitutional Clash, and the Legacy of Authoritarian Rule

October 09, 2026

Late one night past 10 PM, the atmosphere inside the National Assembly turned electric. Kim Kyung-ho, a lawyer and military academy professor, took the witness stand during a parliamentary audit of the Supreme Court and dropped a bombshell. What began as friction over conservative opposition tactics quickly escalated into a blistering critique of South Korea's highest legal authorities, exposing a deep institutional rot that has festered for decades.

At the center of the storm is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and his fiercely defended assertion that his power to nominate Supreme Court justices is absolute and untouchable by either the president or the parliament. But as critics point out, stretching this legal logic to its breaking point reveals how far South Korea's judicial elite has drifted from the basic principles of the separation of powers. To understand why this late-night hearing matters, we have to look past the courtroom doors and examine how authoritarian ghosts continue to haunt modern Korean democracy.

The Illusion of Absolute Authority: Challenging the Chief Justice's Power

When opposition lawmaker Seo Young-kyo of the Democratic Party of Korea—the country's ruling liberal party—invoked Article 104, Section 1 of the Constitution during the audit, Chief Justice Cho was ready with a prepared script. His argument was blunt: the Chief Justice's nomination power belongs to the judiciary alone, and any outside interference from the legislature or the executive branch is unconstitutional.

This stance relies on a quiet, arrogant premise: that the head of the judiciary stands on equal footing with the president. Yet nothing in South Korea's constitutional framework supports the idea that the chief jurist shares equal power with the head of the executive branch. The president, elected directly by the people following the blood-bought victories of the 1987 Democracy Movement, and the National Assembly, chosen by direct public vote, both hold direct democratic legitimacy. The Chief Justice, by contrast, is appointed by the president with parliamentary consent. His democratic mandate is inherently indirect.

Yet the National Court Administration and the Supreme Court often behave as though they exist above the Constitution. Warning directly elected leaders to stay out of judicial affairs reveals a troubling insular mindset, treating the judiciary not as a public institution of a democratic republic, but as a privileged sanctuary insulated from the public will.

Shadows of the Yushin Constitution: How Nomination Power Was Distorted

To understand why the nomination debate is so explosive, one must look at its history. Following the May 16 military coup in the 1960s, the authoritarian regime of Park Chung-hee superficially recognized the Chief Justice's nomination power while forcing oversight through an external committee. Nomination was treated as a binding constitutional duty rather than a unilateral weapon.

That changed drastically with the imposition of the draconian Yushin Constitution, designed to completely subjugate the judiciary. From that era onward, the nomination process was warped into a tool of control, allowing a succession of leaders to bend lower courts to their will through backroom deals with the presidential Blue House. For 50 years, this shadow practice persisted. Chief Justice Cho's insistence on an unrestrained nomination power relies on a legal tradition rooted squarely in military dictatorship.

Refusing Renominations: The Contradictions at the Top

The internal contradictions of the Chief Justice's position become glaringly obvious when looking at what happens after a nomination stalls. If the Chief Justice claims total autonomy in naming candidates, basic administrative logic dictates that if a nomination is rejected or deadlocked, the constitutional duty is to submit a fresh candidate.

Instead, Chief Justice Cho has refused to make renominations while offering no coherent legal justification. Treating nominations as a personal preference rather than a mandatory constitutional duty reduces a vital state process to a game of stubborn pride. Filling the highest court is an collaborative constitutional requirement involving the Chief Justice, parliament, and the president. Refusing to complete this loop is a dereliction of duty that directly threatens constitutional order.

To justify this maneuvering, the Chief Justice has attempted to draw parallels to the president's veto power over legislation. But comparing the two is fundamentally flawed. The National Assembly's lawmaking power is the core of representative democracy, making the executive's veto an essential check against overreach. Supreme Court nominations, by contrast, are not the fundamental essence of the judiciary's mandate—fair trials are. Nominations are simply an administrative mechanism designed to staff a constitutional body collaboratively, not a license for judicial gatekeeping.

Breaking the Cartel: What Comes Next

The stubborn defense of judicial prerogative stems from an elite legal cartel that protects its own. In a system dominated by ambitious judges who rose through elite academic and examination tracks, openly challenging the Chief Justice's administrative edicts remains rare. Kim Kyung-ho's solitary appearance at the audit stands out precisely because such institutional defiance is uncommon.

Solving this deadlock requires concrete legislative action:

  • Codify Nomination Rules: Enact clear statutory laws governing Supreme Court appointments, explicitly requiring the Chief Justice to submit a renominating candidate if an initial pick fails.
  • Parliamentary Countermeasures: Should the judiciary attempt to block reform through constitutional challenges, the National Assembly must be ready to deploy its constitutional oversight and impeachment tools.
  • Transparent Investigations: Thoroughly probe allegations surrounding administrative budget mismanagement and improper political contacts involving high-ranking judicial figures.

Judicial independence was never meant to be a license for judges to operate above the law; it is a constitutional guarantee designed to protect citizens' rights through fair trials. When that independence is weaponized to shield institutional privilege, public tolerance reaches its limit.

The late-night revelations at the National Assembly served as a sharp wake-up call. As South Korea navigates its democratic future under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—following the removal and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Suk Yeol—the country is learning that the rule of law must apply to the judges just as much as ordinary citizens. Until the judiciary steps out of its self-ordained sanctuary and answers to public accountability, lawmakers and citizens alike must keep a close watch on the courts.
