The Crisis of South Korea’s Progressive Movement: Inside the Democratic Party’s Civil War

October 13, 2026

For anyone watching South Korean politics closely these days, the overriding emotion is a weary sigh. After a hard-fought battle to retake the presidency, the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—led by the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's main liberal party—is governing in a way that feels utterly alien to the expectations of its base. Stock markets are flagging, the livelihoods of ordinary citizens are taking a beating, and yet those in positions of power seem more preoccupied with scrolling through social media and picking petty fights than addressing the real issues.

This deep sense of alienation has sparked a grim realization among longtime progressives: if things continue on this trajectory ahead of the upcoming legislative elections, the party faces absolute ruin. How did the Democratic Party—a historic institution once defined by the reformist ideals of former leaders like Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—reach such a fractured state? And more importantly, what path forward remains for those who still believe in those foundational values?

A Fractured Ruling Party and the Cost of Blind Loyalty

When an election loss or a sharp decline in public support hits, the natural instinct should be to reset the board, not double down on failure. In South Korean politics, history offers precedent: former conservative President Park Geun-hye once famously distanced herself from her own party’s preceding administration to survive a tough election cycle. Yet today, within the liberal camp, there is a paralyzed reluctance to confront the sitting president. Blindly shielding President Lee Jae-myung and his inner circle is a recipe for a sweeping defeat in the upcoming parliamentary elections.

There is still time to pivot, but it comes with a steep political price. The only viable survival strategy for the party’s reform-minded members is to clearly establish political independence from President Lee, demand structural accountability, and pivot toward a competitive stance. The party’s recent leadership conventions have laid bare deep internal fault lines. Figures like Jung Chung-rae, a prominent party stalwart, might have historically stepped up to absorb attacks and shield the leadership at all costs, but analysts argue that such unconditional defense only masks a deeper rot. The true nature of the current administration’s disconnect became impossible to ignore as the leadership races exposed factional infighting and a severe lack of governance competence.

Subsequent leadership changes have done little to heal the party. Instead of fostering broad competence, the party apparatus has descended into factional gatekeeping, while approval ratings plummet. Had this reality remained hidden a bit longer, the party might have been hollowed out entirely before anyone noticed. The awakening, painful as it is, has come just in time to force a critical choice.

The Rise of the 'New Lee' Faction and the Cuckoo Politics of Takeover

The internal political culture within South Korea's progressive camp has shifted dramatically. Where past party conventions—though contentious—typically ended with factions rallying back together under a shared banner, today's party members and even prominent public figures often avoid eye contact in the hallways. Communication has broken down completely.

Critics argue this insular, aggressive culture runs counter to the democratic DNA forged by former progressive icons Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in. Instead, a new political phenomenon has taken root. Much like a cuckoo laying its eggs in another bird's nest and pushing out the original chicks, a newly ascendant faction—often dubbed the "New Lee" movement—has occupied the Democratic Party apparatus and begun remodeling it to serve narrow, factional interests.

This dynamic reflects what political commentators have termed a "reconstruction theory" of the party: an effort by figures who were never fully comfortable with the political ethics of Roh and Moon to systematically dismantle and replace the party's traditional identity. Prominent commentators aligned with this new wave have openly stated they feel no sense of political debt or ideological continuity toward former Presidents Roh or Moon. By wearing the colors of the traditional Democratic Party while importing foreign political machinery, digital echo chambers, and top-down donor-driven operations, this faction has fundamentally altered the party's character. For traditionalists, purging this outside influence has become an existential necessity.

This ideological drift has also manifested in sensitive matters of foreign policy. Recent controversies surrounding the deployment of South Korea's Cheonghae anti-piracy naval unit have laid bare the administration's defensive postures. Critics point out the stark hypocrisy when compared to historical precedents—such as the massive anti-war and anti-deployment protests seen during Roh Moo-hyun's presidency. Under the current administration, allied political channels and digital influencers who once made careers out of opposing military deployments have fallen deafeningly silent. This quiet acquiescence suggests that crucial foreign policy decisions are being brokered behind closed doors, far from public scrutiny.

The Judiciary, the Law, and the Battle for the Soul of Progressivism

Beyond internal party strife, progressives face an external institutional battle: the ongoing legal challenges surrounding prominent political figures. Led by Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, recent efforts to push forward with stalled high-profile trials are viewed by party traditionalists not merely as legal proceedings for individuals, but as a systemic pressure point designed to keep the entire progressive movement on a tight leash.

The underlying fear is a repeat of a familiar political trap: opposition figures caught in prolonged legal limbo just ahead of major electoral cycles, allowing conservative opponents to frame the entire progressive camp as corrupt and legally compromised. For the party's base, protecting the future of the reform movement requires insulating the broader political ecosystem from judicial overreach while refusing to let factional survival define the progressive agenda.

When a ruling party of over 160 seats begins to fracture under the weight of its own internal contradictions, the question of ownership becomes paramount. For those who view their political identity as inextricably linked to the democratic traditions of Kim, Roh, and Moon, ceding the party entirely to a transient faction is out of the question.

"Being part of a smaller, principled minority is far more important than holding a majority built on compromised values."
If a hundred-plus lawmakers of the pro-Lee alignment wish to depart and chart their own course, traditionalists argue they should be free to do so—leaving a dedicated core of 30 to 40 lawmakers to rebuild a genuine, grassroots-oriented Democratic Party from the ground up.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea edges closer to its next major legislative showdown, the political landscape hangs in the balance. Several concrete developments will signal which direction the progressive camp ultimately takes:

    Internal Factional Splits: Watch for formal defections or structural realignments within the Democratic Party parliamentary group. A clean break between the traditional pro-Moon/Roh base and the newer factional leadership would signal a permanent fracture.
    Voter Turnout and Polling Trends: Pay close attention to demographic breakdowns in upcoming metropolitan and national polling. A continued desertion of moderate and young voters will force the party's hand on leadership reform.
    Judicial Developments: Monitor decisions from the Supreme Court regarding pending political trials. How the administration and the party leadership respond to legal pressures will test their institutional resilience and unity.

The road ahead for South Korea's progressives is perilous, fraught with internal backstabbing and external pressure. Yet history suggests that political movements survive not through the accumulation of unearned power, but through an unwavering commitment to core principles. Whether the traditional guard can reclaim their party—or whether they must forge a new path from the ground up—will define the next chapter of South Korean democracy.
