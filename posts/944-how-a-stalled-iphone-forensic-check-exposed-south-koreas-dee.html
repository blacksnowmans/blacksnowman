How a Stalled iPhone Forensic Check Exposed South Korea's Deepest Prosecutorial Cartels

September 22, 2026

Picture the scene in a subterranean archive room at South Korea's Supreme Prosecutors' Office in Seoul: an iPhone, seized as critical evidence in a high-stakes political scandal, sits untouched in a drawer for an entire year. Outside, the public is told that justice is grinding forward. Inside, a quiet, bureaucratic war is being waged to ensure that the phone's encrypted secrets never see the light of day. This was the reality of the 2020 "prosecutor-media collusion" scandal involving Han Dong-hoon—then a high-profile prosecutor and later a major political figure—and it remains the skeleton in the closet of a man now nominated to lead South Korea's newly proposed National Investigation Agency.

The nomination of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the National Investigation Agency has triggered a fierce backlash among reform advocates and legal watchdogs. To understand why this nomination has caused such a political earthquake, foreign observers must look past the dry language of bureaucratic appointments. What is unfolding is a rare, unfiltered look into the informal networks that have allowed South Korea's prosecutors to protect their own, frustrate investigations, and treat state power as private property. The allegations against Kim are not merely administrative hiccups; they expose the mechanics of how high-level institutional resistance has repeatedly crippled South Korea's attempts at prosecutorial reform.

The Anatomy of a Stalled Investigation

To grasp the gravity of the allegations against Kim Ji-yong, one must return to July 2020. At the height of the investigation into alleged collusion between prosecutors and journalists, investigators seized an iPhone belonging to Han Dong-hoon. At the time, Lee Jeong-hyun, then the senior prosecutor at the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office, was determined to crack the device. Lee went so far as to contact Cellebrite, the Israeli digital forensics firm whose technology is trusted by intelligence agencies worldwide, and secured the funds required to run the decryption process.

Yet, according to testimonies from former Ministry of Justice officials, those preparations were brought to a sudden halt by Kim Ji-yong, who was then serving as the head of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office's Criminal Department. When junior staff attempted to process the payment for the Cellebrite software, the expenditure was deliberately blocked. "They intentionally blocked the forensics by withholding the money," a former insider testified. While Kim has reportedly deflected responsibility by claiming he was not the ultimate decision-maker, the obstruction meant the phone sat gathering dust in a Supreme Prosecutors' Office warehouse for over a year before being returned to its owner with zero forensic results.

This selective application of investigative technology stands in sharp contrast to how the prosecution treated other cases during the same era—such as the swift and aggressive decryption of devices belonging to deceased investigators when it suited institutional priorities. The obstruction in the Han Dong-hoon case was not an administrative oversight; it was a demonstration of how internal hierarchy can be weaponized to suffocate an inquiry from the top down.

Opportunism and the Anatomy of Institutional Protection

Kim’s career trajectory offers a textbook study in bureaucratic survival and opportunistic shifting of allegiances. During the tenure of Choo Mi-ae—the fiercely reform-minded Justice Minister under the Moon Jae-in administration—Kim climbed rapidly through the ranks to become a chief prosecutor. Yet, when the conflict between Minister Choo and then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol reached a boiling point, Kim pivoted, playing a key role in resisting the disciplinary actions launched against Yoon.

The pattern of using procedural power to shield allies while punishing rivals appears repeatedly in Kim's record. When senior prosecutor Jeong Jin-ung attempted to physically secure evidence during the search of Han Dong-hoon and later found himself indicted on charges of professional abuse of physical force, the indictment was spearheaded by none other than Kim Ji-yong. Jeong faced intense internal pressure and public humiliation from conservative factions within the service. Throughout these episodes, Kim's signature was consistently found on the paperwork that advanced the punitive measures against reform-oriented prosecutors.

Critics argue that Kim's recent advocacy for maintaining broad "supplementary investigative powers" for prosecutors is part of the same playbook. Stripped of direct investigative authority under ongoing structural reforms, prosecutors have fiercely fought to retain loopholes. Opponents contend that Kim’s defense of these powers is a transparent bid to preserve the prosecution's institutional grip rather than a commitment to public safety or human rights.

Patronage Networks and the Limits of Reform

The surprise surrounding Kim's nomination for the National Investigation Agency is compounded by questions over how such a polarizing figure reached the shortlist in the first place. Political analysts point to entrenched informal networks within South Korea’s legal and political establishment, specifically tracing ties back through former Vice Justice Minister Ko Gi-young. With a working relationship dating back to their days at the Daejeon District Prosecutors' Office in 1999, Ko and his associates have long been accused of placing loyal subordinates in key institutional choke points.

Observers note that when high-level appointments bypass rigorous vetting to reward institutional insiders, the momentum behind state reforms stalls. President Lee Jae-min’s subsequent call for additional verification of Kim's candidacy has been widely interpreted as a strong signal for him to step aside voluntarily. However, the fact that the nomination occurred at all highlights a persistent blind spot in South Korea's political screening processes: a lack of institutional memory regarding how career prosecutors maneuvered through past political battles.

What to Watch Next

As the political fallout over Kim Ji-yong’s nomination continues to unfold, several key developments will signal the direction of South Korea's governance and judicial reform:

  • Whether Kim voluntarily withdraws: Pressure from both civil society and ruling party figures makes his survival as a viable candidate increasingly unlikely. Watch for an official statement regarding his status in the coming days.
  • Formal inquiries into the 2020 forensics obstruction: Opposition lawmakers and civic groups are pushing for a formal investigation into the decision-making process that kept Han Dong-hoon's phone locked away for a year.
  • Reforms to the National Investigation Agency appointment process: The controversy has exposed vulnerabilities in how leadership candidates are vetted, which may prompt legislative efforts to introduce stricter transparency and public oversight for top law enforcement posts.
  • The broader fate of investigative powers: How the administration handles this appointment will serve as a litmus test for its willingness to confront institutional resistance from the traditional legal establishment.

South Korea's ongoing journey toward judicial transparency has long been hampered by cozy cartels and institutional self-preservation. Until the individuals who built and maintained those barriers are held accountable, the promise of reform will remain out of reach.
