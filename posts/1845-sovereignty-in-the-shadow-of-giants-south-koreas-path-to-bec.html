Sovereignty in the Shadow of Giants: South Korea’s Path to Becoming a Global Peacemaker

November 02, 2026

Imagine standing on a narrow ridge while two massive tectonic plates begin to grind against one another. To the east, a resurgent and unpredictable "Trump 2.0" looms over the American political horizon; to the west, Xi Jinping secures an unprecedented fourth term, cementing a rigid, one-man rule over the world’s second-largest economy. For South Korea, this is not a hypothetical geopolitical exercise. It is the immediate reality facing the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the current leader from the Democratic Party of Korea. As the global order shifts toward a volatile era of "Strongman Politics," Seoul finds itself at a crossroads: continue acting as a "pacemaker" for the strategic interests of others, or seize the initiative as a sovereign "peacemaker" on its own terms.

The stakes could not be higher. The era of predictable diplomacy is over. From the political paralysis in Israel to the hardening of the North Korea-China-Russia triad, the traditional safety nets of the post-Cold War era are fraying. For a foreign observer, the current Korean landscape might seem focused on domestic transitions—particularly following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—but the real story is how the Lee Jae-myung government intends to navigate a world where "might makes right" is becoming the new international standard. To understand Korea's next move, we must look at the convergence of military sovereignty, regional alliances, and the grassroots movement for peace.

The Netanyahu Deadlock and the American Distraction

To understand the pressure on the Korean Peninsula, one must first look toward the Middle East. The recent election results in Israel have created a "high-order equation" that remains unsolved. Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud Party—Israel’s major right-wing party—managed to secure 27 seats, remaining the largest single party in the 120-seat Knesset (Israel's parliament). However, his broader coalition only reached 53 seats, falling significantly short of the 61-seat majority required to form a government.

This political vacuum matters to Seoul because it forces the United States into a reactive posture. When Israel is paralyzed, U.S. foreign policy inevitably becomes bogged down in Middle Eastern mediation, often at the expense of its focus on East Asia. For Mr. Netanyahu, the current deadlock is a desperate gamble for survival. By pushing for potential re-elections, he maintains his status as acting prime minister, using the office as a shield against ongoing corruption trials and legal scrutiny.

The "Trump variable" adds another layer of complexity. During his first term, Donald Trump maintained a "honeymoon" relationship with Netanyahu, but Trump is a transactional leader who discards partners as soon as they lose their utility. If Trump returns to the White House, his approach to the Middle East will likely be driven by unilateralism rather than stable alliances. For South Korea, this serves as a cautionary tale: relying on the domestic stability or the personal whims of a foreign leader is a precarious foundation for national security. The chaos in Jerusalem is a reminder that when the world's superpower is distracted by its allies' internal strife, middle powers like South Korea must be prepared to stand on their own.

The Battle for Military Sovereignty: Reclaiming OPCON

The most critical pillar of South Korea's pursuit of "sovereign defense" (jaju gukbang) is the transfer of Wartime Operational Control, commonly known as OPCON. Currently, while South Korea maintains control of its military during peacetime, a U.S. general would take command of South Korean forces if a full-scale war broke out. This arrangement is a relic of the Korean War, and for many in the current administration, it is an obstacle to true national independence.

Recent developments in Washington have signaled that the U.S. is not eager to hand over the keys. The 2027 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)—the annual bill that sets the budget and expenditures for the U.S. Department of Defense—passed the Senate Armed Services Committee with additional hurdles for the OPCON transfer. The U.S. is increasingly viewing South Korea not just as an ally, but as a "logistics base" and a "forward operating base" for its broader strategy to contain China. By maintaining command over the South Korean military, the U.S. ensures that Seoul remains tethered to Washington’s regional strategic goals.

The Lee Jae-myung administration’s stance is clear: military sovereignty is not a gift to be requested from Washington, but a right to be exercised. Within the government, there is a growing sentiment that Korea should not "beg" for the return of OPCON but should instead declare its intent to exercise it. This isn't just about pride; it's about historical lessons. During the Gwangju Uprising of May 18, 1980, when the military was used against civilians, the U.S. later claimed it had no control over those specific units, despite the overarching command structure. This historical "double-talk"—claiming control when it suits strategic interests but denying responsibility during moral crises—has fueled the argument that South Korea must be the sole master of its own armed forces.

Navigating the "Strongman Era" of Trump and Xi

The world is bracing for the return of Donald Trump, whose "America First" policy is often described in Seoul as "gangster diplomacy." Unlike traditional politicians who are constrained by Congress, Trump’s first term proved that he could bypass institutional checks. To override a presidential veto in the U.S., a two-thirds majority is needed in both the House (290 seats) and the Senate (67 seats)—numbers that are nearly impossible to reach in today’s polarized climate. If Trump 2.0 becomes a reality, South Korea expects intense pressure to increase its share of defense costs (the Special Measures Agreement) and perhaps even threats regarding the withdrawal of U.S. troops.

Simultaneously, Xi Jinping’s consolidation of power in China has reached its zenith. Following the recent Central Committee plenums, Xi has filled the corridors of power with his closest loyalists, making his fourth term a certainty. This means China will be more assertive, more centralized, and less willing to compromise on its periphery.

Caught between these two giants, North Korea has already made its choice. Kim Jong Un’s recent diplomatic maneuvers suggest that Pyongyang is no longer interested in the "theatrics" of North Korea-U.S. summits. By strengthening its "triangular alliance" with China and Russia—bolstered by military cooperation with Moscow during the war in Ukraine—North Korea has built a new survival strategy that does not require Washington’s approval. For South Korea, this means the old "pacemaker" model—where Seoul simply facilitated U.S. policy toward the North—is dead. The current government realizes that if it wants peace, it must design the "party" itself, rather than waiting for an invitation to a feast hosted by others.

The Peacemaker Movement: A Citizen-Led Security Strategy

In response to these global pressures, a unique grassroots movement has emerged in South Korea: the "Peacemaker Movement." The philosophy is simple: peace is too important to be left solely to professional diplomats and generals. The movement encourages citizens to join for a one-time "lifetime membership fee" of just 1,000 won (about $0.70). The goal is to reach ten million members, creating a massive, democratic mandate for sovereign diplomacy.

The Lee Jae-myung administration views this movement as the "engine" for its policy goals. These goals include:

  • Restoring the September 19 Military Agreement: Reinstating the 2018 de-escalation deal that sought to reduce border tensions, which had been undermined during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office.
  • Normalizing Relations with "Joseon": Using the official name of the North (the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, or Buk-han/Joseon) as a sign of mutual respect and recognition as a legitimate dialogue partner.
  • Expedited OPCON Transfer: Moving toward a self-reliant defense posture that allows South Korea to lead its own security operations.

The logic is that when ten million citizens contribute to a peace fund, no foreign power or domestic political shift can easily reverse the momentum. It is an attempt to move away from the "vulnerable" diplomacy of the past—where South Korea's fate changed every time a new president was elected in Washington—toward a consistent, national consensus on sovereignty.

APEC and the Complex Diplomacy of the Future

The upcoming APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) summit will be the first major test of this "Peacemaker" strategy. President Lee Jae-myung is expected to engage in a series of high-stakes "shuttle diplomacy" meetings with leaders from China, Japan, and the U.S. Unlike the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, which was criticized for an overly subservient, U.S.-centric approach that damaged relations with Beijing and Moscow, the current government is pursuing "pragmatic diplomacy."

This pragmatism extends as far as Europe. The government is closely watching Poland, which has become the primary logistics hub for the war in Ukraine. About 90% of all war materiel enters Ukraine through Polish territory. The security of Poland is now inextricably linked to the security of East Asia; as Russia focuses its resources on the western front, its reliance on North Korean military support increases, which in turn changes the power balance on the Korean Peninsula. In this interconnected world, a "Peacemaker" must look at the globe as a single, interlocking machine.

The transition from a "pacemaker" for the U.S. to a "peacemaker" for the world is not just a change in terminology. It is a fundamental shift in how South Korea views itself. For decades, the narrative was that Korea was a "shrimp among whales." Today, as a top-ten global economy with a world-class military and a thriving cultural export industry, the "shrimp" has grown up. The challenge now is to ensure that the "party" on the Korean Peninsula—the feast of peace and prosperity—is one where Koreans are the hosts, not just the ones cleaning up after the giants have left.

What to Watch Next

  • The OPCON Timeline: Watch for whether the Lee administration begins to set a firm, unilateral deadline for the transfer of wartime control, potentially bypassing the "conditions-based" delays favored by the U.S. Senate.
  • The APEC Summit Results: Look for signs of a "thaw" in Korea-China relations. A formal summit between President Lee and Xi Jinping could signal a shift toward a more balanced, multi-polar foreign policy.
  • The "Peacemaker" Membership Count: The growth of the 1,000-won membership movement will serve as a barometer for how much domestic support President Lee has for his sovereign defense initiatives.
  • North Korean Responses: As Seoul begins to use more respectful, formal terminology toward Pyongyang, monitor whether North Korea softens its rhetoric or engages in lower-level communication channels.
