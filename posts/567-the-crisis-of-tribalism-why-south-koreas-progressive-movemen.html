The Crisis of Tribalism: Why South Korea’s Progressive Movement is Turning on Itself

October 02, 2026

Walk through the wealthier districts of Seoul, or talk to lifelong supporters of South Korea’s center-left who live in deeply conservative strongholds like Daegu, and you will find a strange kind of silence. These are people who spent decades enduring social friction, arguing with their neighbors, and persuading skeptics that their side—the progressive camp, currently anchored by the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s main opposition and reformist party—offered a better future. Yet today, many of these reliable volunteers have stopped talking. They are not convinced by the opposition; rather, they are simply embarrassed.

This internal paralysis was the focal point of a recent political commentary shared widely on digital media platforms, featuring writer Cho Sung-eun. The discussion cuts past the routine partisan mudslinging that dominates daily headlines and zeroes in on an uncomfortable question for anyone who cares about South Korean democracy: When a political movement builds its identity entirely around fighting an external enemy, what happens when it quietly spawns bad governance within its own ranks?

The Dilemma of the 'Modern Partisan Independents'

To understand the current malaise on the South Korean center-left, it helps to look at the people political insiders half-jokingly call the "modern independence fighters." These are progressives in wealthy, conservative-leaning neighborhoods like Gangnam in Seoul, or steadfast liberals in Daegu—a city historically hostile to progressive politics—who spent years absorbing criticism to defend figures like Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the Democratic Party and a central, polarizing figure in South Korean politics.

For a long time, these supporters kept campaigns funded and precinct offices running because they believed they were building a fairer society. But that urgency has curdled into a heavy sense of disillusionment. Under the banner of internal unity, the progressive camp has increasingly treated any dissenting voice as a betrayal. A machine designed to defeat conservative rivals has been turned inward, silencing honest debate and weaponizing the good intentions of its own grassroots base.

Political tribalism in South Korea is notoriously fierce, but the pathology differs across the aisle. While the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, often fractures openly over policy and leadership disputes, the progressive bloc demands absolute conformity. Dissent is frequently reframed as an attack by external enemies. As a result, the very supporters who prided themselves on rational, reform-minded criticism are told that asking hard questions is an act of treason against their own history.

Monopolizing Power Versus Building Legislative Coalitions

The internal contradictions of the progressive movement are nowhere more visible than in the tense, fragile relationship between the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller progressive splinter party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. For true reform to happen in South Korea's National Assembly, strategic alliances are essential.

Historical precedent exists. Back when the center-left was fractured, the splinter party People’s Party held enough seats in the legislature to act as a crucial swing vote, helping pass major legislation and even steering presidential impeachments. In South Korea's parliamentary system, the key to this leverage is official status as a "negotiating bloc"—a formal parliamentary group with enough seats to sit at the table where committee agendas and bill sequences are actually decided.

If the Democratic Party were genuinely interested in maximizing legislative reform, conventional wisdom suggests it would lend a handful of its own lawmakers to the Rebuilding Korea Party to help it cross the threshold for a negotiating bloc. Instead, the Democratic Party has resisted this entirely. By keeping the Rebuilding Korea Party isolated and maintaining a rigid two-to-one dynamic where it calls all the shots, the Democratic leadership ensures that it holds a monopoly on power within the bloc.

This dynamic reveals a darker truth about modern South Korean progressive politics: the leadership prefers subordination over partnership. Rather than building a broad, collaborative coalition to pass sweeping reforms, the dominant faction under Lee Jae-myung appears more focused on keeping potential allies in a subordinate position. Lawmakers who once aligned with the faction of former President Moon Jae-in have rapidly pivoted to pledge allegiance to the current leadership, demonstrating just how fluid political loyalty has become when survival depends on reading the direction of the wind.

When Politics Becomes a Clash of Egos

When political competition is stripped of policy debate and reduced to raw exercises in dominance, governance suffers. Observers of South Korean politics frequently note that when a leader treats every policy disagreement or moderate critique as a personal slight—a dynamic captured in the colloquial frustration of "How dare they ignore me?"—the entire political sphere degrades into a theater of egos.

Lee Jae-myung is widely recognized as a politician of immense drive and determination, traits that his supporters praise as decisive leadership. Yet critics argue that this same uncompromising style calcifies into authoritarianism. When a leader's survival instinct and need for absolute control override democratic deliberation, internal debate is stamped out. The tragedy of this approach is that it alienates the very public it needs to retain.

Consider the career pivots of prominent politicians who fluidly transition from one faction to another depending on who holds the levers of the party apparatus. While such agility ensures personal political survival—securing second and third terms in parliament—it hollows out the ideological integrity of the party. Voters are left asking a weary question: If politicians only care about staying close to the center of gravity, what lasting values are they actually fighting for?

The progressive camp has proven itself remarkably adept at demanding accountability and orchestrating the impeachment of conservative leaders. Yet it has no mechanism—and often no appetite—to police its own abuses of power.

What to Watch Next

As South Korean politics heads into its next electoral and legislative cycles, several concrete markers will reveal whether the progressive bloc can reform its internal culture:

  • Parliamentary Negotiations: Watch whether the Democratic Party makes any procedural concessions to allow smaller allied parties, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party, a genuine seat at the committee-level negotiating tables.
  • Treatment of Internal Dissent: Pay attention to how the party leadership handles lawmakers or grassroots organizers who voice policy disagreements rather than toeing the strict factional line.
  • Voter Fatigue in Key Regions: Monitor polling and engagement levels among traditional progressive strongholds outside the core party apparatus, particularly whether apathy continues to replace activism among moderate liberals.

Democracy requires constant friction and the courage to look inward. Until South Korean progressives realize that silencing internal critics ultimately serves no one but entrenched power, the movement will continue to struggle with the very ghosts it helped create.
