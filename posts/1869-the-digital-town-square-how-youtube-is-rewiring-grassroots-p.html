The Digital Town Square: How YouTube is Rewiring Grassroots Politics in Lee Jae-myung’s Korea

November 02, 2026

Imagine a dimly lit hall, buzzing with the energy of hundreds of political activists, while tens of thousands more watch through the glowing rectangles of their smartphones. This wasn't a formal government briefing or a polished campaign rally. It was a live-streamed "retreat" hosted by Bak Si-yeong TV, one of South Korea’s most influential liberal YouTube channels, featuring Jung Chung-rae, a senior leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s current ruling party. What was originally planned as a private, pre-recorded event was suddenly thrust into the public eye via YouTube Live, responding to a groundswell of demand from party members across the country.

To an outsider, a change in a broadcast schedule might seem like a minor technicality. But in the context of South Korean politics today, it represents a profound shift in how power is exercised and how political "wounds" are healed. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the DPK is navigating a complex transition. While the party holds the presidency and a significant legislative mandate, it is also grappling with the internal exhaustion and emotional friction that followed a high-stakes national convention. For the DPK base, this livestream was more than just a talk show; it was a virtual town square where "party member sovereignty"—the idea that the rank-and-file, not just the elites, should drive the party—was put into practice.

The Pivot to Transparency and the Rise of Digital Sovereignty

The decision to go live was a direct response to the "fandom" politics that now defines the DPK's core support. Originally, the Bak Si-yeong TV team had envisioned a controlled environment—a private recording where Jung Chung-rae could speak to a select group of attendees. However, as the date approached, the channel was flooded with messages from supporters who felt left out. They didn't just want to watch a edited clip later; they wanted to be "in the room" as it happened.

This demand highlights the concept of "Party Member Sovereignty," a central pillar of the DPK under President Lee Jae-myung. In the past, political parties in Korea were often top-down organizations where decisions were made in "smoke-filled rooms" by elderly power brokers. Today, the DPK’s base—often referred to as "reform-minded" or "pro-Lee" members—expects radical transparency. By switching to a live format, the organizers lowered the barrier between the leadership and the public, creating a sense of shared ownership over the political process.

The result was a digital assembly that bypassed traditional media filters. While the production team risked the technical hiccups and unpredictability of a live broadcast, the payoff was a surge of authentic engagement. The live chat became a rolling commentary of encouragement, policy suggestions, and collective venting. It served as a reminder that in the era of high-speed internet and political polarization, the most effective way to maintain a political movement is to give the members a seat at the table—even if that table is a virtual one.

Healing the Scars of Internal Conflict

To understand why this event felt so urgent, one must look at the recent history of the Democratic Party. While the party is currently in power, the road to the Lee Jae-myung administration was marked by intense internal rivalries. The DPK’s national convention and the primary process that preceded it left many grassroots members feeling alienated or emotionally drained. There was a sense of "convention fatigue," where the struggle to unify different factions of the party had taken a toll on the morale of the volunteers and activists who form the party’s backbone.

The YouTube live event functioned as a form of political therapy. Jung Chung-rae, known for his fiery rhetoric and staunch loyalty to the party's reformist wing, used the platform to acknowledge these "emotional dregs." He didn't just talk about legislation; he talked about solidarity. For many viewers, seeing a high-ranking official engage so directly and informally was a way to "wipe away the tears" of past political battles.

"Politics should not just be about the technique of winning elections or announcing policies. It is about the process of cleaning the wounds of the comrades who fought together and recharging their energy."

This focus on emotional resonance is a hallmark of the new Korean political landscape. The DPK base isn't just looking for a manager; they are looking for a movement. By addressing the psychological state of the party members, the event aimed to transform individual frustration into collective action. It was a calculated effort to ensure that the party’s "grassroots engine" doesn't stall out during the difficult work of governing.

Grassroots Action vs. Media Framing

The event also highlighted a growing tension between the DPK's digital base and the traditional media. Following the retreat, some smaller news outlets, such as Pyeonghwa Namu, ran sensationalist headlines suggesting the event was "chaotic" or that the retreat had "flipped upside down." These reports often use provocative framing to paint the DPK's enthusiastic base as an unruly mob or a source of internal instability.

However, the reality on the ground—and on the screen—told a different story. Far from a chaotic shouting match, the event featured structured group discussions among party members on very specific, practical goals. Two key initiatives emerged: the "Member Doubling Campaign" and the "Activation of Regional Meetings."

  • Member Doubling: A grassroots drive where every existing party member is encouraged to recruit at least one new member, effectively doubling the party's ground-level strength.
  • Regional Activation: Shifting the focus away from central party headquarters in Seoul to local neighborhoods, ensuring the party has a "capillary" system that reaches deep into the provinces.

The presence of various liberal figures—including Choe Kang-wook (a former lawmaker and prominent reformist), Kim Young-hwan (a current DPK member of the National Assembly), and various lawyers and influencers—added weight to these discussions. These figures argued that the "dignity" of the party members is best expressed through organized action, not just online comments. By focusing on these "boring" but essential tasks of party-building, the DPK is attempting to professionalize its base and insulate itself against the "unruly" label often applied by the opposition and conservative media.

The Road Ahead: Governing in the Shadow of the Past

The broader context for this mobilization is the ongoing effort by the Lee Jae-myung government to "normalize" the state. The source material reflects a deep-seated grievance among the DPK base regarding the previous era. They refer to the time under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former president who was impeached and is currently in prison—as a period of democratic regression. For the DPK, the mission is not just to pass new laws but to dismantle the systems they believe were corrupted during that time.

This is where the People Power Party (PPP), now the main conservative opposition, comes in. The DPK views the PPP as an obstacle to these reforms, accusing them of "holding the government's ankles" (a Korean idiom for obstructing progress). Because the political environment remains so polarized, the DPK feels it cannot rely solely on parliamentary maneuvers. They believe they need a "permanent campaign" footing, where the public remains constantly engaged and mobilized to provide the political cover necessary for bold reforms.

Channels like Bak Si-yeong TV are the infrastructure for this permanent campaign. They act as a bridge between the Blue House (the presidential office) and the "street." By providing a platform where party members can feel like "owners" of the party, they create a loyal guard that is ready to defend the administration’s agenda against both the opposition and critical media coverage.

What to Watch Next

As the Lee Jae-myung administration moves into its next phase, the success of this "YouTube-driven democracy" will be tested by several factors:

  • The "Member Doubling" Results: Watch for official party data on membership growth. If the DPK can actually double its active, fee-paying membership, it will have a financial and organizational advantage that the opposition PPP will find difficult to match.
  • Legislative Breakthroughs: The DPK often uses these live events to build consensus for specific, controversial bills. Watch if Jung Chung-rae and other leaders start using these platforms to "pre-sell" major reform packages before they hit the floor of the National Assembly.
  • The 2026 Local Elections: The "Regional Activation" mentioned in the retreat is a clear signal that the party is already looking toward the next major electoral test. The strength of these new local cells will determine whether the DPK can maintain its hold on provincial and municipal governments.
  • Media Conflict: Expect the "war of words" between YouTube-based media and legacy news outlets to intensify. As the DPK base becomes more self-reliant for information, the influence of traditional newspapers and broadcasters may continue to wane, further changing the "market" for political news in Korea.

The livestreaming of a party retreat might seem like a small digital footprint, but it is a footprint on a new path. It is a path where the line between the viewer and the activist is blurred, and where the legitimacy of a government is increasingly tied to the real-time approval of its most dedicated digital followers.
