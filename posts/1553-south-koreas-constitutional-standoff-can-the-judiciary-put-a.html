South Korea’s Constitutional Standoff: Can the Judiciary Put a Sitting President on Trial?

October 27, 2026

Imagine a democratic nation where a sitting president, newly vested with the popular mandate to govern, faces the prospect of being forced into a criminal courtroom every single week. Behind the scenes, conservative factions within the judiciary drop subtle hints that existing criminal trials—instigated before the election—might simply resume. In the corridors of the National Assembly, the ruling party holds a crushing legislative majority, yet hesitates to use its full constitutional power to tame what critics call an unelected judicial overreach. This is the legal and political reality currently unfolding in South Korea, where a fierce battle over the interpretation of a single constitutional clause is threatening to trigger an unprecedented constitutional crisis.

At the epicenter of this war is Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution. While the clause was designed to protect the executive branch from paralysis, it has become a weapon in a high-stakes tug-of-war between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and a deeply entrenched judicial establishment. To understand why this technical legal dispute matters far beyond Seoul, one must look closely at how South Korea’s legal mechanics, political anxieties, and broken public trust in the courts are colliding.

Understanding Article 84: Immunity, Lawfare, and the Presidential Mandate

To foreign observers, South Korean constitutional law can seem surprisingly rigid, yet its interpretation remains intensely political. Article 84 of the Constitution of the Republic of Korea states plainly: "The President shall not be charged with a criminal offense during his tenure of office, except for rebellion or treason." On its face, this provision guarantees presidential immunity to ensure that the head of state and head of government can fulfill executive duties without bad-faith interference.

However, a bitter debate has erupted over the precise legal definition of the word "charged" (sochu in Korean). Textual literalists within the legal community and the conservative opposition—the People Power Party (PPP)—argue that "charging" refers strictly to the initial indictment by prosecutors. Under this narrow interpretation, any indictment handed down before a president takes office remains valid, and ongoing trial proceedings can theoretically continue uninterrupted. Critics of this view dismiss it as sophistry, pointing out that forcing a sitting head of state to sit in a criminal dock as a defendant inevitably paralyzes the executive branch and subverts the democratic choice of the electorate.

The Purpose of Head-of-State Immunity

Constitutional scholars consistently argue that presidential immunity is not a personal privilege granted to an individual, but a structural protection afforded to the office. If a president must divide their time between state dinners, national defense briefings, and defending themselves in court, normal governance becomes impossible. The Constitutional Court of Korea has repeatedly emphasized that executive stability is paramount during a president's single five-year term.

How Other Democracies Handle Presidential Immunity

This principle is hardly unique to South Korea. Leading democracies across the world place strict limits on judicial action against incumbent heads of state:

  • France: Following controversies during former President Jacques Chirac’s tenure, France amended Article 67 of its Constitution to explicitly suspend all civil and criminal proceedings, investigations, and court appearances against a sitting president until one month after leaving office.
  • United States: Since the 1973 Watergate era, the U.S. Department of Justice has maintained a firm policy that a sitting president cannot be indicted or tried, a stance reinforced by recent U.S. Supreme Court rulings on executive immunity for official acts.
  • Parliamentary Systems: While Prime Ministers in countries like Italy or Israel have faced trial while in office, parliamentary leaders serve at the pleasure of the legislature and do not occupy the distinct constitutional role of a presidential head of state.

Despite these global precedents, elements within South Korea's judiciary have signaled an eagerness to explore literal loopholes. During parliamentary audits, senior judicial officials, including Kim Dae-woong, head of the Seoul High Court, openly noted that while trials for the sitting president are currently suspended in practice, the theoretical possibility of resuming them rests entirely with individual court benches. By holding the threat of trial resumption over the presidency, critics argue the courts are treating constitutional immunity not as settled law, but as political leverage.

A Hesitant Majority: DPK’s Decision to Investigate Rather Than Impeach

The political drama deepened when the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), which commands a comfortable majority in the National Assembly, faced a decisive choice regarding Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Allegations of judicial interference and politicized trial scheduling had led allied progressive parties, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party, to demand the immediate drafting of an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice.

Instead of taking decisive legislative action, the DPK leadership opted for a parliamentary investigation (gukjeong josa). Party leaders argued that a formal legislative inquiry was necessary to gather definitive evidence of judicial misconduct and win over public opinion. However, this decision has drawn sharp criticism from the party’s own base, who view the move as an exercise in bureaucratic timidness.

The Flaws of Parliamentary Inquiries

Relying on a parliamentary investigation to check judicial overreach carries severe tactical limitations. Setting up a special legislative committee requires weeks of partisan debate over witness lists and schedules. Past political battles—such as previous inquiries into prosecutorial misconduct—degenerated into endless partisan bickering while losing political momentum. Meanwhile, the Supreme Court and the court administrative body retain the procedural power to stall proceedings or refuse attendance, neutralizing the inquiry's impact.

The Trap of "Backlash Anxiety"

The DPK's hesitation stems largely from a persistent fear of alienating moderate voters or triggering a political backlash. Yet this risk-averse posture risks projecting weakness. When an unelected judicial branch signals its willingness to challenge democratic legitimacy, delaying a response to conduct preliminary inquiries resembles a general asking for a committee report in the middle of a battle.

Historical Parallel: The Lesson of Canossa
In 1077, Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV held vast military power, yet found himself excommunicated by Pope Gregory VII over the investiture controversy. Failing to assert his authority early, Henry was forced to trek through the snow and kneel in humiliation at Canossa to retain his crown. Political analysts note that when an elected legislative majority cowers before the moral or legal posturing of non-elected authorities, it risks a similar surrender of sovereign power.

Public Distrust and the Urgency of Structural Court Reforms

The tug-of-war over Article 84 comes at a time when public faith in South Korea's judicial system has plummeted to historic lows. The crisis is not merely about one trial or one president; it reflects deep structural decay within the court system itself.

According to the OECD’s 2024 Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions report, only **33% of South Korean citizens expressed trust in their national legal system**. This places South Korea near the very bottom among OECD nations, far behind the OECD average of 54% and trailing Nordic democracies like Norway (77%), Denmark (75%), and Finland (74%).

The Shadow of Revolving-Door Corruption

A primary driver of this public cynicism is the deeply entrenched culture of Jeongwan-yeowu—the informal privilege granted to retired judges and prosecutors turned high-earning defense attorneys. While countries like Germany enforce strict five-year litigation bans for former judges within their old jurisdictions, and jurisdictions like Singapore ban retired senior judges from private practice entirely, South Korean courts have long faced criticism for protecting internal cartels at the expense of equal justice.

Abolishing the National Court Administration

True judicial reform requires dismantling the internal bureaucracy that enables chief justices to exert top-down control over individual judges. The National Court Administration (Beobwon Haengjeongcheo), long criticized as the epicenter of administrative power plays, allows the Chief Justice to hold immense influence over promotions and judicial assignments. Reformers insist this body must be abolished and replaced with an independent judicial administration council composed of non-judge citizens and legal experts.

Furthermore, the National Assembly possesses the clear legislative authority to amend the Criminal Procedure Act to explicitly clarify Article 84, statutorily codifying the automatic suspension of all court proceedings for a sitting president during their term. Passing such legislation would eliminate ambiguous gaps exploited by political factions.

Internal Distractions, Opposition Attacks, and What Comes Next

Rather than maintaining a laser focus on constitutional and judicial reform, the political landscape has been cluttered by partisan sideshows and policy shifts that confuse the electorate.

Within the ruling DPK, leadership recently expended significant political energy addressing unverified rumors of mass infiltrations by the religious group Shincheonji into party membership rolls. Despite public claims of an ongoing investigation, internal reports acknowledged a complete lack of concrete evidence, turning the issue into an unhelpful internal distraction.

On the policy front, the DPK faced intense backlash from labor groups after Representative Park Sang-hyuk introduced a special semiconductor bill containing an exemption from the statutory 52-hour workweek for R&D personnel. Critics pointed out that during the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the DPK had fiercely opposed labor-hour relaxations on grounds of worker health. Reversing course to accommodate corporate demands risks eroding the party's core principles.

Meanwhile, the opposition People Power Party (PPP) has continued aggressive political attacks. PPP Representative Joo Jin-woo recently sparked controversy during parliamentary audits by alleging that First Lady Kim Hea-kyung had received expensive private golf lessons from professional golfer Lydia Ko at Taereung Country Club. When photos surfaced disproving the claim, Joo quietly edited his social media post while shifting focus to whether golf had been played at all—a move widely condemned as irresponsible smear politics.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this high-stakes constitutional friction, international observers should monitor several key developments:

  • Legislative Action on the Criminal Procedure Act: Will the DPK use its numerical dominance to pass statutory amendments that explicitly freeze ongoing trials for incumbent presidents, effectively closing the Article 84 loophole?
  • The Scope of the Parliamentary Inquiry: Will the National Assembly's investigation into court administration yield actionable evidence of judicial overreach, or will it stall due to non-cooperation from judicial leadership?
  • Courts' Procedural Moves: Will individual trial benches attempt to schedule hearing dates for the sitting president, forcing a direct showdown between executive legitimacy and judicial power?

Power retains its legitimacy only when exercised with constitutional clarity and purpose. For South Korea's elected representatives, navigating the Article 84 controversy is no longer just a legal dispute—it is a defining test of whether democratic sovereignty rests with the people's elected government or within the cloistered halls of the judiciary.
