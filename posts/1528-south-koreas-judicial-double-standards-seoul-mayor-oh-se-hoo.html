South Korea’s Judicial Double Standards: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon and the Han River Bus Scandal

October 27, 2026

For observers watching South Korean politics from afar, the country’s legal and political systems often appear to operate on two entirely different sets of rules. Depending on who is standing in the dock, the law can either be a razor-sharp instrument of accountability or a flexible guideline interpreted with baffling leniency. This friction has once again taken center stage in Seoul, where Mayor Oh Se-hoon is weathering a storm of escalating controversies—ranging from a questionable judicial acquittal on campaign finance violations to allegations of multi-million-dollar kickbacks and a massive budget sleight-of-hand involving a public housing corporation.

To understand why these developments matter beyond domestic Korean politics, one must look at how power, public funds, and judicial consistency intersect under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. Far from being isolated administrative hiccups, the controversies surrounding Mayor Oh’s flagship "Han River Bus" project and his legal battles expose systemic vulnerabilities in how oversight mechanisms function when handling high-profile conservative politicians.

The Anatomy of an Acquittal: When the Judiciary Presumes Innocence

The skepticism surrounding South Korea's judiciary intensified following a series of legal rulings that critics argue bend over backward to protect political elites. The pattern bears striking similarities to past legal decisions concerning former leader Yoon Seok-youl, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment. When a court previously cleared Yoon of allegations regarding free opinion polling provided by controversial political broker Myung Tae-kyun, the presiding judge was Gu Hoe-geun. In a twist that raised few eyebrows among cynical observers, it was the exact same judge, Gu Hoe-geun, who handed down a similarly lenient, unexpected acquittal for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon.

Legal analysts and civic activists, such as Green Forum Chairman Park Tae-woong, have dissected the court's reasoning, pointing out its departure from standard legal logic. Rather than objectively evaluating whether financial transactions violated the Political Funds Act, the court's rationale began with a subjective premise: an underlying assumption of character. In essence, the ruling argued that "Oh Se-hoon is simply not the type of person to violate election law."

This judicial logic relies on a five-step miracle of reasoning that bypasses hard evidence. First, it asserts the defendant's good character. Second, it dismisses documented monetary transfers between associates as unrelated to polling costs, despite clear chronological and circumstantial ties. Finally, it accepts the extraordinary premise that a wealthy donor would hand over tens of millions of won to a political broker without the candidate ever asking or knowing—an arrangement that defies common sense in democratic politics. By twisting ordinary legal standards to shield a major political player, critics argue the judiciary has once again traded impartial justice for political expedience.

The Han River Bus: Kickbacks, Concealment, and Dragged Feet

While the courtroom battle over campaign finance has grabbed headlines, the administrative reality surrounding Mayor Oh's pet project—the Han River Bus—is drawing scrutiny for entirely different reasons: alleged corruption and systemic cover-ups.

According to disclosures by Democratic Party lawmaker Park Joo-min, the Seoul Metropolitan Government's audit committee uncovered evidence last September pointing to roughly 320 million won (about $230,000) in suspicious financial flows tied to the manufacturing process of the Han River vessels. Crucially, this was not merely a matter of suspicion; investigators had already secured incriminating statements and definitive call recordings outlining who directed the funds and why.

Yet, rather than forwarding the case immediately to law enforcement, city officials allegedly buried the findings in a drawer for over a year. When the city finally did request an audit from the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI), it omitted core evidence and key details—a maneuver seemingly designed to trigger an automatic dismissal or rejection. Only after facing intense bipartisan grilling during a parliamentary national audit did the city hastily rush to request a police investigation the very next day. This belated scramble has fueled fierce accusations of evidence tampering and bureaucratic obstruction.

A Flawed Project and SH Corporation’s Bypass Financing

Beyond potential kickbacks, the entire financial architecture of the Han River Bus project has raised alarm bells among fiscal watchdogs. Independent financial projections show the river-transport initiative heading toward perpetual operational deficits. To paper over these losses, the Seoul metropolitan government has relied on wildly optimistic advertising revenue projections—anticipating figures double those of the previous year without any concrete commercial justification.

With central government ministries expressing deep skepticism—Minister of the Interior and Safety Yoon Hee-keun has labeled the plans "effectively unapproved"—Mayor Oh's administration allegedly sought alternative ways to fund the initiative. Enter the Seoul Housing and Communities Corporation (SH Corporation), a public entity mandated to stabilize housing for low- and middle-income Seoul residents.

Lawmaker Park revealed that SH Corporation has funneled an astonishing 1.072 trillion won (about $770 million) in loans and capital investments into Han River Bus-related ventures. Critics argue that utilizing public housing funds to bail out an unviable leisure transport project constitutes a blatant violation of the Local Public Enterprises Act, effectively treating public housing capital as a personal slush fund for the mayor’s vanity projects.

False Statements and the Ticking Clock of the Statute of Limitations

Compounding the administrative and financial missteps, Mayor Oh now faces legal jeopardy over statements he made regarding the project's delays. In various media appearances and city council sessions, the mayor publicly claimed that the Board of Audit and Inspection had declined to pursue further reviews because a parliamentary audit was already underway.

However, official documents released from the audit board tell a completely different story. The agency had actually rejected Seoul's request because the city was fully equipped to investigate the matter internally, bluntly telling local officials to handle their own oversight. No mention was made of parliamentary interference.

Because Mayor Oh has publicly signaled his intent to run for future public office, legal experts note that his public misrepresentation of official audit documents could run afoul of the Public Official Election Act regarding the dissemination of false information. With the statute of limitations for these remarks ticking down toward the end of the year, the Democratic Party's legal committee is actively reviewing formal complaints.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea’s political landscape adjusts to the Lee Jae-myung administration's aggressive push for administrative transparency and institutional fairness, the controversies surrounding Seoul's municipal government are reaching a critical juncture. Observers should monitor several key developments in the coming weeks:

  • Police and Anti-Corruption Investigations: Watch whether law enforcement acts swiftly on the newly filed police requests regarding the Han River Bus kickback allegations and whether investigators examine potential evidence destruction during the year-long delay.
  • Ministry of the Interior and Safety Audits: Keep an eye on central government actions concerning SH Corporation's financial exposure, particularly whether the ministry enforces strict accounting audits to halt the alleged misuse of public housing funds.
  • Electoral Law Developments: Monitor if the prosecution formally indicts Mayor Oh Se-hoon for disseminating false information regarding the audit board's communications before the statutory deadline expires at year's end.

The unfolding saga of the Han River Bus and its associated legal battles will serve as a definitive test of whether South Korea's accountability mechanisms can effectively police high-ranking local executives, or if political impunity will continue to override basic administrative law.
