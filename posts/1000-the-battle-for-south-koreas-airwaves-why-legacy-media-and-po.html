The Battle for South Korea's Airwaves: Why Legacy Media and Political YouTubers Are at War

September 22, 2026

Picture the scene in a typical South Korean living room or commuter subway car: millions of people are scrolling through their phones, listening not to the evening news broadcast by traditional television networks, but to live-streaming political commentators on YouTube. These digital channels have become massive powerhouses, pulling in revenues and audiences that rival, and often dwarf, mainstream media. Yet, this shift has triggered a bitter culture war over influence, ethics, and who gets to define the truth in South Korean politics.

Lately, online forums and comment sections have exploded over a coordinated campaign targeting two of the country's most popular progressive current-affairs channels: Maebul Show, hosted by broadcaster Choi Wook, and Kim Ou-joon's News Factory (now operating under the banner Humility is Hard News Factory). Critics claim these giants are losing their grip on the public, pointing to screenshots showing plunging viewer counts. But step back from the partisan crossfire, and this controversy reveals a deeper, more troubling anxiety about the collapse of traditional journalism and the rise of a parallel digital media ecosystem in South Korea.

The Anatomy of a Smear: Manipulating Metrics

The recent online offensive began when posts flooded Korean web forums claiming that Maebul Show was a spent force—a "has-been" channel. The evidence presented seemed damning: screenshots showed live viewer counts dropping below 20,000, with some spin-off segments drawing barely 9,000 live viewers compared to peak numbers of over 200,000.

The reality behind these numbers, however, is a masterclass in digital manipulation. The critics had simply captured the broadcast feeds before the shows had even started, while producers were still setting up the equipment. Once the actual broadcast got underway, the audience numbers routinely surged back toward 80,000—a far cry from a dead channel.

Why manufacture such a narrative? The claim that a channel down from 200,000 to 80,000 viewers is "ruined" relies on a statistical sleight-of-hand. Those peak figures occurred during a uniquely volatile period in South Korean politics—an era of high-stakes political turmoil that acted as an extraordinary ratings spike for all news outlets. Comparing those abnormal highs to everyday baseline metrics is not honest analysis; it is an intentional attack designed to delegitimize independent digital media.

At the same time, these channels face intense scrutiny from within the broader progressive camp. Some supporters of the mainstream liberal Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition party) accuse Maebul Show of straying too far from the party line or fostering an uncritical loyalty to former Justice Minister Cho Kuk. This creates a bizarre double standard: political insiders attack these independent broadcasters for not being loyal enough to party leadership, while conservative critics attack them for being overly partisan. The result is a toxic loop of factional infighting where nuance is completely erased.

Legacy Media's Blind Spot and the Courage to Refuse State Money

While internet trolls obsess over pre-show screenshots, these YouTube channels continue to break stories that mainstream media outlets routinely ignore. For instance, investigative reporter Bong Ji-wook has used these platforms to air detailed allegations of misconduct against Han Dong-hoon, a prominent conservative politician and former Justice Minister. The broadcasts dissect Han's actions with razor-sharp criticism, asking hard questions about legal and political accountability.

Yet, the country's major legacy newspapers and television networks have responded with absolute silence. They refuse to touch the allegations against Han, choosing instead to focus their editorial firepower on attacking Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea. This selective enforcement of journalistic standards exposes a glaring bias. Mainstream media outlets often dismiss YouTube commentators as "not real journalists," yet they hold these same independent creators to an impossible standard of public accountability while giving powerful establishment figures a free pass.

Perhaps even more revealing is the financial independence of these channels. While mainstream newspapers and struggling cable channels routinely chase and receive lucrative government advertising contracts, Maebul Show made the deliberate choice to reject state advertising altogether. Mainstream outlets like the conservative newspaper The Chosun Ilbo face no public outcry for accepting government funds, but any state support given to a progressive YouTube channel would instantly trigger coordinated attacks from conservative critics. By turning down the money before anyone could use it as a weapon, the producers built a powerful shield for their editorial independence.

The Paradox of Political YouTube: Superchats and Ideological Shifts

To understand the enduring appeal of these digital platforms, one has to look at how money and communication flow between creators and their audiences. Recent joint research from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) and the University of Hong Kong sheds light on this dynamic. The data shows that "Superchats"—financial donations made by viewers during live streams—function as more than just digital cheers. They act as a direct feedback loop, allowing viewers to push back against hosts, challenge their premises, and steer the conversation.

During periods of intense political crisis, such as the martial law controversies that have shaken the country, even moderate and conservative YouTube channels receiving these donations saw their editorial tone shift significantly. Political YouTube is not a one-way lecture hall; it is a chaotic, highly responsive marketplace of ideas. While partisan polarization is an undeniable problem, these platforms serve a vital safety valve function—allowing public frustration, debate, and dissent to find an outlet when traditional political and media channels feel locked down.

Critics often reduce figures like Choi Wook and Kim Ou-joon to caricatures—opportunistic shock-jocks chasing wealth and fame. Yet the reality of their daily existence is grueling. Spending hours every day under intense public scrutiny, facing relentless legal, political, and social attacks, is not an easy life. The people who tune in every day are not gullible dupes; they are citizens looking for an alternative to sanitized establishment news.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea's political landscape grows increasingly fractured, the friction between traditional broadcasters and digital creators will only intensify. To understand where the country's public discourse is heading, keep an eye on several key indicators over the coming months:

    Regulatory pressures: Watch how government agencies and tax authorities handle independent digital media channels. Calls for special audits or new digital platform regulations are often used as political tools to pressure independent voices.
    Mainstream media adaptation: Observe whether traditional networks begin adopting the direct, unscripted style of political YouTubers to claw back younger audiences, or if they double down on institutional defense mechanisms.
    Audience behavior and funding shifts: Pay attention to how financial support models like Superchats evolve, and whether regulatory bodies attempt to clamp down on digital donations under the guise of campaign finance reform.

The ongoing war over viewer numbers and media influence is ultimately about power. When legacy media loses its monopoly on reality, the gatekeepers panic. But as long as mainstream outlets continue to look away from uncomfortable truths, audiences will keep searching for answers elsewhere—regardless of how many manipulated screenshots appear online.
