Inside South Korea’s Constitutional Showdown Over Impeaching Its Chief Justice

November 23, 2026

In South Korea, a single half-hour inside the Supreme Court rarely reshapes the balance of power among the branches of government. Yet within thirty minutes in the same Seoul courtroom, two rulings laid the groundwork for one of the sharpest confrontations between the legislature and the judiciary in the country’s modern democratic history. Days later, after the Supreme Court overturned and remanded the insurrection conviction of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo in just nine days, ruling-party lawmakers stood before cameras to announce an impeachment drive against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

For international observers, the dispute goes far beyond technical arguments over criminal procedure. It sits at the center of South Korea’s reckoning over the emergency martial law crisis involving Yoon Suk Yeol, who was later impeached, removed from office, and imprisoned. Today, under President Lee Jae-myung, the Democratic Party of Korea governs as the ruling party against the conservative People Power Party in opposition. To Democratic Party lawmakers and their allies, the Supreme Court’s handling of Mr. Han’s case—paired with explosive allegations about how Chief Justice Cho responded on the night martial law was declared—raises an existential question about judicial accountability. To critics of the impeachment push, including conservative opponents, the move tests the boundaries of legislative pressure on an independent court.

Thirty Minutes That Split the Supreme Court’s Legal Standard

At the heart of the outcry among legal critics is how quickly and differently the Supreme Court moved. Completing a reversal and remand in nine days is widely described by legal observers in Seoul as virtually unprecedented for a major Supreme Court case, especially when ordinary citizens often wait years for final rulings. Inside the bench itself, Justice Oh Kyung-mi issued a rare dissenting opinion warning that courts must apply strict evidentiary standards consistently, regardless of whether a defendant is politically powerful or ordinary.

Critics of the majority decision argue that the inconsistency was laid bare in the span of thirty minutes. Earlier that same day in the same courtroom, the Supreme Court issued a ruling in the case of Kim Keon-hee, assessing her conduct comprehensively as a connected whole rather than dividing events into isolated fragments. Half an hour later, in Mr. Han’s insurrection appeal, the court took the opposite approach: instead of evaluating the alleged disruption of the constitutional order as one continuous sequence, the majority broke individual actions apart.

Questions about impartiality deepened over the participation of Justice Kwon Young-jun. According to disclosures cited by critics, between 2018 and 2022, while working as a law professor, Mr. Kwon wrote 63 legal advisory opinions for South Korea’s seven largest law firms and received more than 1.8 billion won (about $1.3 million). Over 1 billion won (about $700,000) of that total came from 30 opinions written for Kim & Chang, the country’s most prominent law firm—and the firm where Mr. Han previously served as an advisor. Under South Korean civil and criminal procedure, recusal rules exist to sever conflicts of interest between judges and litigants. Legal critics contend that Mr. Kwon’s refusal to step aside from a major trial involving a former Kim & Chang advisor breached judicial ethics and eroded public trust in the verdict.

The Procedural Case Behind the Impeachment Task Force

In response, the ruling Democratic Party launched a formal Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae Impeachment Promotion Task Force, chaired by Rep. Yang Bu-nam alongside senior deputy head Rep. Jeon Yong-gi, lawmakers Kim Ki-pyo, Kim Dong-ah, Lee Geon-tae, Lee Yong-woo, Lee Ju-hee, and Noh Jong-myeon, party legal figure Cho Sang-ho, and attorneys Moon Geon-il and Lee Ju-hwa. Democratic Party leaders have taken pains to argue that their target is judicial process, not merely an unpopular verdict.

Speaking at a press briefing, Rep. Noh Jong-myeon framed the party’s legal theory in narrow procedural terms while accusing conservative rivals of politicizing the dispute:

"The content or legal reasoning of this ruling is not the grounds for impeachment. I want to state once again that the various procedural problems leading up to this ruling, and other issues, are the grounds for impeachment. Looking at the reaction from the People Power Party camp, their intention to deliberately stir up a backlash seems all too blatant."

Specifically, the task force alleges that Chief Justice Cho directed the proceedings in violation of deadlines and trial rules under the Special Prosecutor Act and ignored South Korea’s "6-3-3 principle," a statutory benchmark calling for rulings within six months in the district court, three months on appeal, and three months at the Supreme Court. Other task force members echoed that framing in stark terms. Rep. Yang accused the Chief Justice of using judicial authority entrusted by the public to interpret the law favorably for insurrection participants, while Rep. Lee Yong-woo argued that the head of the judiciary was destabilizing the balance among all three branches of government. Reps. Lee Geon-tae and Kim Ki-pyo vowed to pursue removal, and attorney Cho Sang-ho said the team would scrutinize each of Chief Justice Cho’s actions for constitutional and statutory violations.

Meanwhile, public anger spilled into weekend candlelight rallies. Speaking after attending one protest, Rep. Seo Young-kyo, who chairs the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee, accused the Supreme Court of misleading the public through its post-verdict press release. In her view, the court’s summary gave the impression that it had firmly recognized the gravity of insurrection, while the full written ruling carved out legal escape routes for Mr. Han and other key defendants.

Redefining Insurrection Intent Under Article 87

To understand why the ruling triggered such fierce legal pushback, it helps to look at Article 87 of South Korea’s Criminal Act, which defines insurrection. Convicting a defendant under Article 87 requires proving two elements together: a "purpose of subverting the constitutional order" and an act of "riot." Attorney Lee Kwang-chul, a former presidential secretary for civil affairs, noted that in landmark Supreme Court precedents covering the December 12, 1979 military coup and the May 18, 1980 insurrection cases, the court treated a chain of actions assaulting constitutional institutions as a single, integrated whole.

In Mr. Han’s case, critics say the Supreme Court dismantled that framework. While affirming guilt on specific unlawful directives—such as ordering power and water cutoffs or coercing signatures—the court reversed and remanded the core charges regarding conspiracy to suspend constitutional order and creating the outward appearance of a Cabinet meeting. As Mr. Lee characterized it, the court left a few branches intact while severing the trunk of the insurrection charge.

Even more consequential, according to both Justice Oh’s dissent and outside legal analysts, was how the majority redefined intent for purpose-based crimes. Under longstanding precedent, proving the "purpose of subverting the constitutional order" required only willful awareness—recognizing and accepting the risk that one’s actions could bring about that result. In this ruling, however, the court raised the threshold significantly, demanding proof that the defendant actively aimed to realize that constitutional disruption and actively accepted the criminal outcome. Justice Oh warned in her dissent that abruptly tightening that standard threatens judicial fairness, and critics worry the precedent could also undermine future prosecutions requiring proof of intent, including enemy-benefiting charges under the National Security Act. Compounding the criticism, although the ruling acknowledged Mr. Yoon’s insurrectionary acts, it stopped short of a direct holding on Mr. Han’s participation, relegating the point to vague commentary—known as obiter dicta—stating that one view sees him as having stepped into acts of insurrection.

A Martial Law Allegation and What to Watch Next

Adding fuel to the impeachment drive is an allegation that predates the verdict altogether. According to a Yonhap News report cited by critics of the judiciary, a Supreme Court official alleged that immediately after emergency martial law was declared, Chief Justice Cho issued an internal instruction indicating that command and supervision over judicial authority would pass to the martial law commander. Under South Korea’s Constitution, judicial power rests exclusively with the courts and the Constitutional Court, and judicial independence cannot be surrendered even under martial law. If substantiated, critics argue, voluntarily ceding judicial oversight to military authorities would constitute a profound breach of the separation of powers.

Whether the impeachment drive succeeds—or triggers a broader institutional crisis—will hinge on three concrete developments in the days ahead:

  • The National Judges’ Representatives Conference on the 30th: Roughly 120 elected judges representing courts across South Korea are scheduled to convene. Although the body is autonomous and holds no legal authority to vote on impeaching a Chief Justice, it played a decisive role in mobilizing internal reform pressure during past judicial scandals. Observers will be watching closely to see whether rank-and-file judges stay silent or publicly criticize the Chief Justice’s handling of the case and alleged martial law directive.
  • The December 2 National Assembly plenary session: Democratic Party lawmakers aim to bring the impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho to a floor vote alongside the national budget bill. Passing the motion would immediately suspend him from his duties pending a constitutional trial.
  • Parliamentary audits and hearings: The Legislation and Judiciary Committee plans to investigate the alleged martial law instruction, the actions of the National Court Administration, and the procedural path of Mr. Han’s nine-day remand—setting up an intense public test of both judicial independence and legislative oversight.
