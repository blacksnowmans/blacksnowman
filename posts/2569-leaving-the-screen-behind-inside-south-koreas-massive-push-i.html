Leaving the Screen Behind: Inside South Korea’s Massive Push into Physical AI

November 21, 2026

For years, the global artificial intelligence boom has been defined by what happens inside glass screens: chatbot windows, synthesized audio, and generative art engines running in distant cloud data centers. But inside South Korea’s industrial heartland, a fundamental shift is underway. AI is breaking out of the monitor and stepping directly onto factory floors, tactical military zones, and disaster sites. This hardware-bound intelligence—known as Physical AI—combines machine learning with robotics, on-device silicon, and physical sensors to sense, move, and manipulate the real world.

On November 20, a wave of announcements across corporate boardrooms, government ministries, military commands, and tech startups highlighted how rapidly South Korea is pivoting toward this new paradigm. From major executive reorganizations at leading conglomerates to defense trials with autonomous combat vehicles and specialized silicon for disaster drones, physical AI is no longer a futuristic R&D exercise. It is becoming the core industrial strategy of South Korea.

Corporate Realignment: Why LG and Global Tech Are Betting Big on Hardware AI

The clearest signal of this paradigm shift came from LG Group, one of South Korea’s largest industrial conglomerates. In its major executive reshuffle for 2027, LG placed physical AI and AI infrastructure specialists at the absolute forefront of its corporate structure. Rather than keeping AI isolated within software R&D labs, the group is integrating artificial intelligence directly into its manufacturing, consumer electronics, and advanced component divisions.

This restructuring is a tactical move designed to protect South Korea’s manufacturing edge. As global competition moves beyond sheer production volume toward real-time autonomous operation, LG is shifting its identity from a traditional hardware maker to an embodied-intelligence provider. By connecting research labs directly with commercial business units, the conglomerate aims to commercialize robotics and smart manufacturing systems at speed.

LG is hardly alone in this realization. At the global level, physical AI is fast becoming the primary focus of technological innovation. Submissions in the robotics category for the upcoming CES Innovation Awards surged by 74% year-over-year. Unlike previous years, which leaned heavily on novel household vacuums or novelty display devices, the latest wave of submissions focuses on precise object manipulation, autonomous navigation, and industrial-grade deployment.

The venture capital ecosystem is following the exact same trend. India-based robotics startup Humin Labs recently closed a $47 million Series A funding round. Humin Labs operates by deploying physical robots into real-world environments to gather physical action data—the precise movements, tactile responses, and spatial adjustments made by human workers. In the physical AI race, high-quality real-world behavior data is the ultimate currency, and global investors are eagerly funding the infrastructure required to collect it.

From Battlefield to Forest Fires: Defense and Public Safety Implementations

While private enterprise seeks productivity gains, South Korea’s public sector is turning to physical AI out of strategic necessity. South Korea’s Ministry of National Defense recently held its 5th Defense AI Competition, placing urban tactical operations using Unmanned Ground Vehicles (UGVs) at the top of its agenda. Simultaneously, the Defense Agency for Technology and Standards hosted the 2026 Defense Physical AI Forum to lay out plans for integrating civilian robotics into military hardware.

Navigating dense urban warfare scenarios requires high-level environmental perception, obstacle avoidance, and rapid tactical decision-making in real time. For South Korea, developing autonomous ground platforms is not merely about modernization—it is a critical response to severe demographic pressures that are shrinking the available pool of military personnel. By deploying physical AI to handle high-risk reconnaissance and tactical support, military planners hope to reduce casualties and bridge manpower shortages.

In civil safety, local tech firm Bon AI secured a major government contract valued at 10.75 billion Korean won (approximately $7.7 million) to test domestically produced system semiconductors in real-world disaster management. Bon AI will integrate these South Korean chips directly onto wildfire-detection drones and disaster AI transformation (AX) systems.

Why On-Device AI Matters in Disasters: When wild fires break out in remote mountain ranges, cellular networks often fail or lack coverage. By using on-device physical AI powered by domestic chips, drones can analyze high-resolution thermal images onboard and detect ignition points instantly without relying on cloud connections or remote servers.

As these physical systems enter the public domain, the legal and technical support ecosystems are rushing to adapt. Lee & Ko, one of South Korea’s premier law firms, announced an upcoming academic seminar titled "Designing Norms in the Era of Physical AI" to address regulatory frameworks, liability issues for autonomous hardware, and data privacy. On the tech infrastructure side, software provider SugarsAI partnered with Taiwan-based hardware maker Wistron to co-develop ultra-low-latency video transmission solutions essential for real-time remote control of physical AI drones and industrial machinery.

Solving Hard Physical Problems: Startups and Next-Gen Hardware

Bringing artificial intelligence into the real world requires solving complex physical challenges that traditional software algorithms never had to consider. A computer vision model can easily recognize a picture of a bag of bread, but instructing a robotic arm to pick up a soft, flexible, uniquely shaped plastic bag without crushing its contents is notoriously difficult.

South Korean robotics startup XYZ announced that it has successfully deployed a physical AI system engineered specifically to classify and handle non-rigid items like bagged bread. By pairing high-definition optical sensors with real-time tactile feedback algorithms, XYZ’s robots can gauge item deformation and dynamically adjust their grip pressure. This breakthrough targets a major bottleneck in logistics centers and retail distribution hubs, where irregular items have historically resisted automation.

At the same time, industrial software startup Omelet showcased its proprietary AI platform, OASIS, at the XTC AI:360 Summit in Silicon Valley. OASIS is built to optimize massive variables across complex manufacturing lines and logistics operations, moving beyond simple computer simulations to offer direct algorithmic control over factory equipment and machinery.

Underpinning all of these applications is the need for faster, more power-efficient hardware. German specialty chemical company Wacker was selected for a South Korean government R&D initiative focused on developing optical interconnect materials for Co-Packaged Optics (CPO). As AI processing demands skyrocket, traditional electrical wiring between chips creates data bottlenecks and excessive heat. Wacker’s research into light-based data transmission materials aims to dramatically reduce power consumption and latency inside next-generation physical AI computing modules.

Building the Physical AI Ecosystem: Regional Hubs and Mainstream Adoption

The push into physical AI is reshaping regional politics, academia, finance, and popular culture across South Korea:

  • Local Politics: Municipal leaders are competing fiercely to host physical AI testbeds. In Wanju County, North Jeolla Province, local councilor Shim Bu-geon urged the local government to establish the area as a dedicated physical AI industrial hub. Similarly, Gwak Dong-yun, a city councilor in Anyang, Gyeonggi Province, called for city redevelopment planning to actively align with national physical AI infrastructure initiatives.
  • Academia & Healthcare: Educational institutions are revamping their curricula. Sungshin Women’s University launched targeted career guidance programs focused on robotics and autonomous driving. Meanwhile, Sungkyunkwan University’s Advanced Institute for Health Sciences and Technology (SAIHST) announced a specialized symposium on medical physical AI, exploring clinical applications for surgical robots and automated patient care systems.
  • Finance & Culture: Capital markets are moving quickly to capture value. DB Financial Investment hosted specialized investment seminars analyzing physical AI supply chain stocks. Even the entertainment sector is leaning into the trend: the organizers of the global MAMA Awards revealed a planned stage performance featuring actor Park Bo-gum alongside advanced physical AI-driven robots.

What to Watch Next

Physical AI represents a profound transition from digital software consumption to real-world hardware transformation. For international observers tracking South Korea’s tech and industrial trajectory, several concrete indicators will show how quickly this shift matures:

1. LG’s Execution Timelines: Watch how quickly LG transfers its physical AI executive appointments into tangible commercial products—particularly in home robotics, smart factory automation, and automotive components.

2. Field Results for Domestic Silicon: Bon AI’s $7.7 million wildfire drone project serves as a crucial test case for South Korea’s domestic system-on-chip capabilities. Successful edge-computing performance in harsh, off-grid mountain environments will validate the nation's strategy to reduce reliance on foreign AI silicon.

3. Military UGV Integration: Follow the speed at which the Ministry of National Defense converts its urban tactical UGV trials into active procurement contracts for army units.

4. Regulatory Frameworks: Keep an eye on legal debates surrounding autonomous robot liability and industrial safety rules driven by policy seminars like Lee & Ko's. South Korea's ability to establish clear legal frameworks could make it a global blueprint for physical AI adoption.
