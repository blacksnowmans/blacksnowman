From Elite Track to Buddhist Monk and Back: What One Korean Thinker's Spiritual Odyssey Teaches Us About Burnout

September 10, 2026

Most of us spend our lives walking down tracks we never laid ourselves. We choose majors because they promise stability, take jobs because they sound impressive on a résumé, and measure our worth by milestones handed down by a demanding society. But somewhere in our thirties, the momentum begins to wear thin. The daily machinery of earning a living, managing a career, and maintaining social relationships starts to hum with an unsettling question: Where did I go, and who is actually living this life?

This quiet vertigo is the starting point of a growing conversation in South Korea around a new book titled Shim-Sum-Seom (a Korean portmanteau blending concepts of resting, breathing, and islands). Its author, Kim Han-soo—who also writes under the name So-eun—lived a life designed precisely to win society's approval, only to shatter it piece by piece in search of something real. Her journey from elite international politics scholar to Buddhist monastic, and finally back to secular life, offers a rare window into a culture choked by anxiety and overachievement. It is a radical case study in what it takes to step off the treadmill and reconstruct the self from the inside out.

The Anatomy of an Elite Breakdown

To understand why Kim’s eventual rebellion matters, you have to understand the sheer weight of the track she left behind. Born into modest circumstances, she studied furiously, attending morning Japanese classes before dawn. Her hard work paid off with a position at the Consulate-General of South Korea in Osaka, followed by a prestigious path through Japan's Rikkyo University and the University of Tokyo graduate school, where she specialized in international politics. She eventually worked for an international organization—the gold standard of global career success.

Yet internally, the machinery was empty. Raised as a devoted member of South Korea’s powerful Full Gospel Church—a massive Pentecostal movement—her early faith was fierce enough that she engaged in aggressive street evangelism during her student days in Tokyo. But as she sang in the choir at Rikkyo University, a quiet doubt began to gnaw at her: What am I actually believing in?

The turning point came when she encountered the writings of O Kang-nam, a scholar whose work challenged static, dogmatic religious interpretations. Seeking a deeper well, she received baptism at Seoul’s historic Anglican Cathedral, adopting the Christian name Clara, and spent years living as a "double-belonging" believer. But institutional religion—even when broadened—could not quiet the existential hunger in her chest. That hunger finally led her toward seon (Korean Zen meditation).

On Christmas Eve in 2004, after completing three thousand ritual bows, she handed in her resignation and walked away from her career. She entered the Muamsa Zen temple on Mount Gyeryong. In 2006, she shaved her head and took the monastic name Gwang-woo. Later, reflecting on those days, she recalled the years spent rushing between hair salons in Seoul’s wealthy Gangnam and historic Gangbuk districts, her moods rising and falling with the styling of her hair. By shaving her head, she wasn't just shedding her hair; she was shedding the skin of other people's expectations.

Stepping Back into the World

After three years of monastic life, Kim made a decision that baffled outsiders: she returned to secular life. To critics, it looked like a failure of will. But Kim viewed it differently. She saw her time in the monastery not as a permanent escape, but as studying abroad. She had used the radical container of monastic discipline to solve her internal equations; now, she had to bring those lessons back into the messy marketplace of ordinary life.

Today, she lives under a new pen name, Nokmyeong—derived from the ancient Chinese Classic of Poetry, signifying a deer that discovers food and immediately calls out to share it with its herd rather than eating alone. When people suggest she open a formal meditation academy, she refuses. Meditation, in her view, is not a esoteric skill that requires expensive certification; it is simply a basic tool for resetting a life whose buttons have all been buttoned up the wrong way.

This philosophy cuts against the grain of modern wellness culture, particularly in South Korea, where mindfulness is frequently packaged as a luxury consumer product or a temporary sedative to help workers survive punishing corporate hours. Kim argues that true meditation is not about escaping reality. It is an act of defiance against the momentum of modern life. The chronic loneliness plaguing young people today is not caused by physical isolation, she suggests, but by an inability to form a relationship with oneself. When we spend our days scrolling through social media, we swallow other people's desires whole, mistaking them for our own—a recipe guaranteed to trigger burnout and mental exhaustion.

What Neuroscience Tells Us About the Pause

This cultural diagnosis aligns closely with modern neuroscience. Contemporary research highlights the delicate dance between the amygdala (the brain's emotional and stress processing center) and the prefrontal cortex (responsible for rational decision-making). Under chronic stress, the amygdala hijacks the brain, flooding the system with panic and trapping us in reactive loops.

We pride ourselves on multitasking, treating it as a badge of competence. But neuroscientifically, multitasking is a relentless assault on the brain's energy reserves. When we try to outrun our anxiety by constantly doing, we starve the brain of genuine rest—even during sleep, our minds chatter endlessly. True neurological recovery requires what Kim calls "paradoxical concentration": the discipline of doing just one thing at a time, anchoring the mind entirely in the present moment.

Studies on brain-wave vibration meditation, including joint research involving London University and Korean brain science institutes, have demonstrated that even brief daily meditation practices measurably improve emotional regulation, reduce depressive symptoms, and enhance neuroplasticity. The brain physically reorganizes itself around what we pay attention to. Trading five minutes of frantic scrolling for five minutes of focused breathing does more to alter the trajectory of a day than any productivity hack.

Reclaiming 'Desires That Belong to You'

A common critique of meditation is that it asks people to extinguish their desires. Kim flips this notion on its head. Desire, she argues, is the very engine of a vital life; the tragedy is not that we desire things, but that we chase desires that do not belong to us.

Why do millions of young Koreans exhaust themselves trying to enter the same handful of elite universities and conglomerates? Because those ambitions are rarely homegrown. They are carefully engineered proxies handed down by anxious parents and a conformist educational system. Chasing these counterfeit desires guarantees endless comparison, fierce competition, and, ultimately, chronic misery.

Finding one's authentic desires requires stripping away the noise. Kim recommends treating yourself the way you would treat a person you are trying to court: check in on your own state, ask yourself what you actually need, and pay close attention to your body's signals. When you live entirely for the gaze of others, you become a psychological homeless person, wandering a property that isn't yours.

Simple Rituals for Rebuilding the Self

You do not need to shave your head or move to a mountain monastery to begin reclaiming your mind. Kim’s work suggests a few concrete anchors for daily life:

  • Morning Ventilation: Upon waking, express simple gratitude and immediately open a window to clear out stale indoor air, signaling a fresh start to the nervous system.
  • The 3-Minute Breathing Switch: Use unavoidable micro-waits—waiting at a traffic light or for a computer to boot up—as triggers to drop the shoulders and focus entirely on the breath.
  • Intentional Pauses: Treat mental friction not as an annoyance to be crushed with more caffeine, but as a signal that the mind has wandered away from its home address.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea continues to grapple with intense socioeconomic pressures, high youth unemployment, and one of the highest suicide rates in the developed world, conversations around burnout are shifting from economic policy to existential philosophy. Books like Shim-Sum-Seom are striking a nerve because millions of readers recognize the symptoms in themselves.

Watch for how mainstream Korean institutions—from major corporations to universities—increasingly attempt to integrate mental health infrastructure, and whether these programs remain superficial "healing" perks or evolve into genuine structural permissions to slow down. More importantly, watch the growing counter-culture of individuals quietly dropping off high-prestige tracks to build smaller, self-directed lives. The real story in South Korea's markets and culture is no longer just how fast the country can run, but how many people are finally daring to stop.
