Breathing Underwater: Director Ahn Jae-hoon and the Fight for Korean Animation

October 04, 2026

Imagine a small, dimly lit studio filled with the scratch of pencils on paper. Here, thousands of individual drawings are meticulously layered to create a single second of movement. This is the world of Ahn Jae-hoon, a filmmaker who has spent decades working in the often-neglected margins of South Korea’s massive entertainment industry. His latest project, Agami (Gills), is not just another film; it is a testament to the survival of independent animation in a country that is globally famous for its live-action dramas and K-pop, yet strangely indifferent to its own animated history.

Ahn, who leads the studio Yeonpil-ro Myeongsanghagi (Meditation with a Pencil), is a veteran of the craft. Since his 1988 debut, he has been known for works that capture the unique, melancholic emotional landscape of Korea. Now, with Agami—a story about a boy born with gills—he has taken a daring step: he has moved his setting from the streets of Seoul to the historic canals of Venice and the boulevards of Paris. Why would a director known for "Korean sentiment" uproot his story? And why did A-list actors like Jung Hae-in and Kang Ha-neul agree to lend their voices to this niche, independent project?

The Philosophy of "Self-Negation"

Ahn Jae-hoon’s career is defined by a relentless drive to discard his past successes. He believes that creativity begins with "the power of self-negation." To him, an artist must constantly try to surpass their previous work, treating every frame as a new challenge. This philosophy extends beyond his desk; he is known for sketching his fans at public events, not just as a service, but as a way to observe the unique "narrative" written on a person's face. He believes that every human has a story to tell, and it is his job to find the visual language to translate that into animation.

His aesthetic is also deeply rooted in his youth. Growing up, he was moved by the protest songs of the 1980s—the era of iconic artists like Kim Kwang-seok. In those days, a simple acoustic guitar and a raw, honest voice were enough to stir a nation. Ahn views his animation as that same vessel: a way to capture the raw, trembling feeling of being alive. Whether he is adapting Korean literature for schoolchildren or working on a long-form independent project, he sees himself as a "worker" documenting the human experience in an industry that rarely rewards the long, slow, and expensive process of hand-drawn animation.

The Structural Void in Korean Culture

There is a glaring irony in the South Korean content market: while "K-content" is a global juggernaut, animation remains the one medium that has failed to gain a "K-" prefix or significant international recognition. Unlike Japan, which has cultivated a massive, state-supported ecosystem for "Japanimation," Korea’s animation scene suffers from a lack of structural backing.

Ahn points out that even the nation's most prestigious film festivals are often more interested in importing verified international history than in nurturing their own. This is why Agami matters. It is a stand against a system that ignores the creators building the foundation of Korean animation. The production team chose Agami—based on the novel by Gu Byeong-mo—because the story of a boy with a "deformity" (the gills) resonated with the collective pain of a society navigating turbulent political and social changes. The film’s core message is poignant: the "wounds" we carry are not things to be cured or hidden, but the very "gills" that allow us to survive in a suffocating world.

Transcending Borders: Why Paris?

The decision to shift the setting from Korea to Paris and Venice was a calculated artistic risk. Initially, the team considered depicting poverty in a realistic Korean setting, but Ahn realized this would risk "fetishizing" that poverty. If the film were set in a Korean slum, the audience would focus on the socio-political frame of the setting rather than the boy’s internal journey. By placing the story in the unfamiliar, somewhat cold landscapes of Europe, the director amplified the character’s sense of isolation and longing. The audience is no longer watching a story about "Korean poverty"; they are watching a human story about the universal experience of being an outsider.

The Power of Voice: Why A-List Stars Joined In

It is almost unheard of for top-tier actors like Jung Hae-in and Kang Ha-neul to participate in an independent animated film where their faces never appear on screen. Their participation was an act of goodwill, born from a desire to support a dying art form. Jung, having observed Ahn’s obsessive process of matching music to drawings, was drawn to the director’s sincerity. Kang, who has long dreamed of starting an animation studio himself, understood the weight of the work.

Ahn didn't cast them for their fame; he cast them for their "vocal architecture." He spent immense time ensuring the actors' natural vocal ranges matched the jawlines and bone structures of the animated characters, creating an uncanny, immersive reality. These actors didn't just record lines in a booth; they visited the studio, sat with the animators, and breathed in the dust of the workplace. This collaborative spirit resulted in a film where the performances serve the characters, not the other way around.

What to Watch Next

For those looking to catch Agami, it is currently showing in select art-house cinemas across Korea, including Art Nine in Seoul and the Film Forum. For a foreign observer, the film serves as a barometer for the health of the Korean independent arts scene.

As you watch, consider the director’s hope for his audience: that the film doesn't just prompt a critique, but a reflection. Ahn wants viewers to leave the theater feeling compelled to write their own stories. In a world that demands we be loud and visible, Agami is a quiet, powerful reminder that our most "deformed" or hidden traits—the gills we hide from others—are often the only things keeping us afloat. Watch for how this film performs in the independent circuit; its success or failure will say as much about the audience as it does about the state of Korean animation itself.
