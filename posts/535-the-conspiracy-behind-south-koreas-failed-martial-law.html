The Conspiracy Behind South Korea’s Failed Martial Law

October 01, 2026

Every time the news cycle refreshes in South Korea, the headlines feel more surreal. As investigations continue into the December 3 “emergency martial law” attempt, the testimonies provided by key figures—specifically former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun—have veered so far from democratic norms that they feel like scenes from a dystopian thriller. As these officials attempt to justify their actions in court, they aren’t offering apologies or remorse; they are doubling down on a bizarre, conspiratorial worldview that suggests the nation’s defense was in the hands of people profoundly detached from reality.

The Secret Meetings and the “Hybrid War” Delusion

Recent court filings and testimony have revealed that during his tenure, former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun held regular, private meetings with Roh Sang-won, then-commander of the Defense Intelligence Command (DIC). These weren't routine briefings; the two met one to two times a week at the Minister’s official residence. While the defense team claims these were for organizational restructuring, the content of their discussions paints a picture of two men obsessed with fringe theories.

At the core of their discussions was the concept of "hybrid warfare." Kim and Roh reportedly convinced themselves that China and North Korea were manipulating South Korean public opinion and rigging national elections to weaken the state. They spoke of the Chinese government’s “Belt and Road Initiative” not as a geopolitical strategy, but as a mechanism to turn South Korea into an “economic colony.”

For a Minister of National Defense, this is a staggering failure of professional judgment. Rather than relying on intelligence reports grounded in objective analysis, Kim and Roh effectively treated the Ministry of Defense as a sandbox for far-right internet conspiracy theories. They viewed the democratic process itself—specifically election management—as an existential threat, a belief system that directly paved the way for the chaos of December 3.

Weaponizing Intelligence Against Democracy

Perhaps the most damning aspect of these revelations is the attempt to use the military’s intelligence apparatus to subvert the state. Kim has admitted that once he received the directive for martial law from President Yoon Suk-yeol, his first priority was to seize the servers of the National Election Commission (NEC)—the constitutionally independent body responsible for overseeing elections.

The plan was brazen: dispatch military personnel to “secure” the servers under the guise of preventing data tampering, effectively decapitating the country’s electoral infrastructure. The fact that the Defense Intelligence Command and the Defense Counterintelligence Command were coordinated to perform this task suggests a premeditated effort to dismantle the pillars of South Korean democracy. They were not merely acting on presidential orders; they were planning a strategic takeover of the very systems that guarantee the legitimacy of the government.

Even more shocking is the legal defense currently being mounted. Kim’s lawyers argue that the declaration of martial law is a “unique emergency power” of the President and therefore immune to judicial review. They are essentially resurrecting arguments from South Korea’s authoritarian past, ignoring the 1997 Supreme Court precedent that established that martial law declarations aimed at subverting the constitution are subject to criminal review.

A Crisis of Military Integrity

The tragedy of this situation lies in the profound damage done to the honor of the South Korean Armed Forces. For decades, the military has worked to shed its image as a political actor, striving to be a professional body dedicated solely to national security. By involving high-ranking commanders in political conspiracies to protect a regime, Kim Yong-hyun and his associates have betrayed the core oath of the soldier: to serve the nation, not the individual in power.

This incident serves as a grim reminder that democracy is not a self-sustaining system; it is a fragile structure that requires constant vigilance. When the people entrusted with the nation’s defense turn their focus inward, using their power to monitor civilians and seize electoral data, the state is no longer a democracy—it is a target.

What to Watch Next

  • The "Prime Minister" Angle: Kim’s defense team has claimed that they reported the plan to Prime Minister Han Duck-soo before moving forward, citing legal protocols. The Prime Minister's office has denied this, but this finger-pointing suggests the crisis may reach deeper into the cabinet than previously known.
  • Judicial Precedent: The courts must now decide if the "Presidential emergency power" defense holds any water. Expect a landmark ruling that will either reinforce the supremacy of the constitution or leave a dangerous loophole for future administrations.
  • Military Reform: The political fallout will likely force a massive institutional overhaul of the Defense Intelligence Command. The public demand for civilian oversight of these agencies is at an all-time high, and legislators are expected to push for laws that strictly forbid military involvement in domestic political affairs.

As the trial progresses, South Korea stands at a crossroads. The attempt to turn back the clock to the era of military coups has failed, but the scars on the democratic process remain. The ultimate test will be whether the judicial system can hold these individuals accountable for what was effectively an attempt to hijack the state, ensuring that such a scenario never becomes a reality again.
