The "Core Collapse": Why South Korea’s Political and Judicial Systems are at a Breaking Point

September 27, 2026

For those watching South Korea from afar, the daily barrage of political scandal and judicial maneuvering can feel like white noise. However, beneath the surface, there is a pervasive sense of dread among the Korean public—a feeling that the very foundations of society are beginning to buckle. This is what critics are calling a "core collapse": a state where the law is no longer a shield for the weak, but a weapon for the powerful, and where political leaders have stopped viewing each other as rivals and started viewing each other as mortal obstacles to be crushed.

This erosion of democratic norms is not merely a political trend; it is a psychological breakdown. By examining the mechanics of power through the lens of neuroscience and historical precedent, we can better understand why South Korea’s political climate has become so volatile—and why the current decline in public trust is not just a temporary dip, but a warning of systemic failure.

The True Purpose of the Law: Protection or Prosecution?

Attorney Jung Pil-seung, a prominent legal critic, often emphasizes a fundamental truth that many have forgotten: "The law was not designed to protect us from 'bad guys'; it was designed to protect us from the state." While we often view the law as a sword of justice meant to punish criminals, its historical purpose in democratic societies is to act as a defensive line against state overreach. Concepts like the principle of taxation by law and the principle of "nullum crimen sine lege" (no crime without law) exist to prevent the government from seizing property or imprisoning individuals at a whim.

The crisis in South Korea arises because this defensive line has been breached. When a single institution—specifically the Prosecutor General’s Office—monopolizes the power to investigate and indict, the law ceases to be a shield and becomes a weapon. In South Korea, the prosecution has historically enjoyed unchecked power, often acting as the ultimate arbiter of political fate. While the public once held the prosecution in higher regard than the courts, that era ended with the administration of President Yoon Suk-yeol. The mask has slipped, and now, the public is asking a vital question: Is the prosecution’s vast power being used to protect human rights, or is it being used to eliminate political rivals?

The current push to strip prosecutors of their investigative powers—a major reform initiative slated for 2026—is not just an administrative change. It is an essential step toward restoring the separation of powers. Rule of law depends on how the state treats the individual, not how efficiently the state can convict its enemies.

The "Prosecutorial Rebellion" and the Trauma of the Cho Kuk Scandal

The turning point in this public disillusionment was the "Cho Kuk scandal," involving the former Justice Minister. Attorney Jung describes the prosecution's actions during this period as a "successful coup." Unlike military coups of the past, this was a bureaucratic takeover: the prosecution acted as a political force, manipulating the media and using unrelated criminal investigations to force their targets into submission.

This event shattered the public’s illusion of neutral law enforcement. Citizens realized that if the investigative agencies decided someone was a "problem," there was no escape. The realization that the prosecution could bypass legal standards to conduct targeted investigations turned a vague fear into a concrete reality. To prevent a repeat of this "prosecutorial rebellion," the complete separation of investigation and indictment has become an unavoidable requirement. Establishing independent agencies to handle investigations and prosecutions separately is now viewed by many as the only way to restore public faith in the system.

The Monster of "Political Prosecution"

South Korea’s legal system has effectively become a "Prosecutorial State," where the monopoly on criminal justice allows subjective intent to override objective evidence. When the state is not subject to democratic control, the law becomes something citizens fear rather than something they rely on. Unless this cycle is broken, the democracy itself remains under the grip of an unelected, unaccountable power structure.

The Neuroscience of Power: Why Empathy Dies

Why do politicians become so cold-blooded, treating their colleagues and even their own supporters as mere tools? Neuroscience offers a chilling answer: the degradation of "mirror neurons." These are the brain cells that allow us to empathize by simulating the actions and feelings of others. When individuals gain absolute power, the necessity to "read" others for survival disappears. Because they no longer need to worry about the opinions of those below them, their capacity for empathy literally begins to atrophy.

In this environment, politicians categorize people as either "obstacles" or "tools." This is why modern political parties, such as the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition party), often descend into bitter internal strife. When political leaders view their partners as competitors for a finite pool of power rather than as comrades, empathy is the first thing to vanish. To a politician with atrophied mirror neurons, the suffering of the public is an abstract variable in a power calculation, not a moral imperative.

The Danger of Betraying the "Core"

Just as a human needs a strong core for balance, a political leader needs a base of supporters. But when leaders begin to believe they are entitled to that support, they stop treating their base as a partnership and start treating them as a resource to be exploited. This is the essence of the "core collapse."

History has shown that this arrogance is fatal. During the Sewol Ferry disaster protests, families of the victims famously warned politicians: "We may not have the power to elect you, but we have the power to vote you out." When politicians lose touch with the reality of those they represent, the base will inevitably look for alternatives. Much like in the tech world where Apple and Samsung compete for dominance, the electorate is not loyal to a brand name—they are loyal to values. If the Democratic Party ignores the public's needs, they will eventually be replaced by a more responsive alternative, just as history has seen many seemingly invincible parties crumble into sand.

What to Watch Next

As we head into the next phase of South Korean politics, watch for these three indicators of systemic health:

  • The Progress of Judicial Reform: Look for how the new independent investigation agencies are established. If they remain subject to political influence, the "Prosecutorial State" will simply move into a new guise.
  • Internal Party Dynamics: Pay attention to how current political leaders handle internal dissent. If leaders continue to purge potential rivals, it is a sign that the "core collapse" is accelerating.
  • Public Sentiment Shifts: Watch real-time metrics—not just legacy media polls, but the health of independent political commentary and digital engagement. If the public begins to pivot away from traditional party structures, it signals that voters are preparing to abandon the current binary system entirely.

The "core collapse" is a warning that the house is built on a shifting foundation. Whether South Korea can avoid a total collapse of its democratic norms depends on whether the public can reassert its role as the true sovereign, rather than remaining the passive audience to a game of power played by those who have forgotten how to listen.
