South Korea at a Crossroads: Power, Prosecution Reform, and the Battle for the Public Conscience

October 03, 2026

To watch South Korean politics unfold right now is to witness a society wrestling with the raw mechanics of power. Turn on a political YouTube livestream in Seoul, and the chat rooms are racing a mile a minute, white-hot over leaked phone calls, prosecution budgets, and bitter infighting within the progressive camp. At the same time, packed theaters are turning a gritty historical drama into a box-office phenomenon, while the nation's high courts prepare to deliver potentially career-ending verdicts against some of the country's most prominent conservative figures. These currents are not separate; they are part of a singular, turbulent national conversation about who holds power, who abuses it, and how a democracy cleans house when the institutions meant to protect it are the ones that need fixing.

Culture as a Barometer: Box Office Hits and Political Misses

The mood of the South Korean public often shows up first in the arts, and the current box office tells a very clear story. The historical feature film Assassins has stormed South Korean theaters, easily crossing the 2-million-viewer mark just ten days after its release and eyeing the coveted 10-million milestone. Audiences are packing cinemas not merely for escapism, but because the film forces an unvarnished look at the dark, painful corners of the country's modern history. It strikes a chord because it speaks directly to what ordinary citizens are angry about and where they seek a sense of justice.

By contrast, the far-right-leaning documentary The Birth of Korea has quietly withered at the box office. Nearly a month after its premiere, its total audience crawled to a meager 80,000—a figure that effectively amounts to a commercial eviction. No matter the genre, attempting to rewrite historical facts and force a partisan narrative falls flat when it collides with the sharp critical eye of the contemporary public. Propaganda wrapped in cheap packaging is being systematically rejected, while content driven by historical truth commands massive public solidarity.

The Legal Reckoning Facing Seoul’s Mayor

While the cultural sphere votes with its ticket stubs, the legal and political worlds are being shaken by high-stakes trials. The most seismic of these involves Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon. The legal jeopardy facing Oh and his close associates is severe: prosecutors in his appeal trial have not just maintained the lower court's push for fines—such as the 10 million won (about $7,000) fine originally handed down to Oh—but have slapped him with a staggering demand for a 1-year and 6-month prison sentence.

The core of the case ties Oh and his camp to Myung Tae-kyun, a controversial political broker who allegedly provided free political polling data during past local elections, with campaign supporters stepping in to foot a 33 million won (about $24,000) bill. Under South Korean political funding laws, an elected official facing a prison sentence or a fine of 1 million won ($730) or more that is finalized by the Supreme Court is automatically stripped of their office. With the appeals court set to rule soon, legal analysts suggest that Oh is staring down a nearly 99% certainty of losing the Seoul mayoralty. Observers note the irony of a powerful politician caught crying in the courtroom, pleading ignorance while his defense unravels under the weight of recorded phone calls and conflicting witness statements.

Shifting Tides in Public Approval and Progressive Infighting

The shifting political landscape is also reflected in public approval rankings. In the latest regional government brand reputation index published by the Asia Brand Research Institute, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has finally lost his long-held perch at the top. The new number one spot for provincial and metropolitan government heads belongs to Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae.

Paradoxically, this surge to the top is largely the result of relentless, friendly-fire attacks from within the wider progressive camp. Figures like lawmaker Kim Min-seok and former Democratic Party leader Song Young-gil have frequently targeted Choo, attempting to marginalize her role in ongoing reform efforts. Yet every time she was singled out for criticism, public attention locked onto her, and ordinary citizens rallied behind her stance, turning hostile political maneuvers into an accidental branding campaign.

This internal friction highlights a deeper fracture. While reform-minded citizens view Choo Mi-ae as a steadfast champion, certain establishment figures within the progressive bloc—sometimes labeled by critics as cautious or protective of the old order—have pushed back against radical change. Lawmaker Jeong Seong-ho recently drew fierce backlash from party members by arguing that the push for thorough prosecution reform amounts to attacking the administration itself, even defending controversial ideas like handing a "security investigation" authority back to the prosecutors. To reform advocates and party members, these compromises look less like party unity and more like self-sabotage.

Dismantling the Prosecutor’s Office: Lessons from Military Dictatorships

To understand why prosecuting prosecution reform in South Korea is so agonizingly difficult, one must recognize that the prosecution service is not merely a bureaucratic administrative agency. It has historically operated as a formidable political power center. When the government reorganizes economic ministries or merges regional administrative districts, the public barely blinks. But trying to dismantle the Supreme Prosecutors' Office and replace it with a stripped-down public prosecution service triggers ferocious institutional resistance.

History offers a blueprint for handling entrenched, unaccountable power structures. When South Korea finally shook off its military dictatorships and built its democracy, true civilian control only became possible after systemic roots were pulled up—such as stripping the military academy system of its untouchable privileges. The prosecution service demands the exact same structural surgery. True reform cannot happen simply by changing a sign on a building; the systemic mechanisms that allowed prosecutors to act as an unbridled political police force must be entirely excised.

Yet, progress has been slow. As lawmaker Kim Yong-min pointed out during a recent National Assembly press conference, even after the legal framework to abolish the traditional prosecutor's office was put into motion, the institutional transition was intentionally dragged out. Old investigative units, budgets, and personnel remain largely intact, leaving the door open for targeted investigations and institutional self-preservation.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves forward, several concrete milestones will test whether real reform can survive political maneuvering:

  • The Seoul Mayoral Appeal Verdict: Watch for the upcoming appellate court ruling on Mayor Oh Se-hoon. A finalized sentence above 1 million won will trigger an immediate leadership vacuum in the capital and force a high-stakes by-election.
  • The Implementation of Investigative-Prosecutorial Separation: Keep an eye on how the government handles actual budgets, personnel assignments, and organizational charts for the newly restructured public prosecution and investigative agencies. Changing laws on paper is only half the battle; how executive bodies allocate resources will prove whether the reform has teeth.
  • Internal Party Dynamics: Observe how the ruling and progressive blocs manage figures pushing to dilute reforms versus grassroots party members ("Kwonli-dangwon") who demand accountability. The tension between institutional preservation and radical transparency will shape upcoming candidate nominations and legislative agendas.

Ultimately, South Korea's ongoing political and legal dramas serve as a stark reminder that institutions do not reform themselves out of goodwill. Whether dealing with compromised local executives or entrenched investigative bodies, the force driving accountability has always come from the watchful eyes of ordinary citizens refusing to look away.
