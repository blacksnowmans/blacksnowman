The High-Stakes Gamble in Seoul: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Eyeing the Chief Justice

October 18, 2026

Inside the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly in mid-October, the political temperature reached a boiling point. The legislature was entering the second week of its annual parliamentary audit—a marathon constitutional ritual where lawmakers cross-examine government ministries, public institutions, and state agencies. But rather than policy scrutiny, the spotlight swung sharply toward a constitutional showdown: senior figures within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea began openly mooting the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the head of the nation's Supreme Court.

For an administration and ruling party navigating governance under President Lee Jae-myung, floating the impeachment of the country’s top judge is an extraordinary step. Yet on the very same day this high-stakes offensive was launched, the Democratic Party found itself scrambling to extinguish a series of self-inflicted domestic fires, ranging from offensive remarks about public servants to awkward protocol mishaps on the campaign trail. The resulting scene reveals a governing party exercising sweeping legislative power while simultaneously struggling with internal discipline and message control.

A Direct Clash with the Judiciary: Targeting Chief Justice Cho

The suggestion to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae did not emerge in a vacuum. Deep-seated friction between the Democratic Party and the judicial branch has simmered for years, driven by lingering grievances dating back to the previous administration during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office, as well as several recent high-profile court rulings. Party hardliners have increasingly argued that the judiciary's institutional neutrality has fractured and that court decisions are undermining constitutional principles.

By bringing impeachment onto the formal agenda, ruling party leaders signaled that they no longer view traditional judicial reform bills as sufficient. More than thirty media reports erupted in a single day as lawmakers debated the move in committee rooms and leadership meetings. Moving from political rhetoric to procedural preparation marks a dramatic shift in gear, demonstrating the party's determination not to yield ground on pending court battles and structural legal reforms.

Yet pursuing the sitting head of the Supreme Court is inherently hazardous. In South Korea's constitutional framework, targeting the head of another branch of government invites immediate accusations of dismantling the separation of powers. The conservative opposition, the People Power Party, pounced on the development. Opposition floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik framed the push as an unconstitutional assault designed solely to "shield President Lee Jae-myung." Warning of a rapid political collapse and an immediate descent into "dead duck" status for the government should an impeachment bill actually be filed, the People Power Party even floated retaliatory rhetoric regarding presidential accountability.

When both sides weaponize existential constitutional threats, public fatigue sets in rapidly. For the Democratic Party, sustaining this offensive requires proving specific, objective unconstitutional conduct by the judiciary rather than relying on broad ideological grievances that opponents can easily dismiss as political retribution.

A National Tragedy and an Unforced Rhetorical Blunder

While the party attempted to project institutional strength against the courts, a sudden crisis of empathy caught its leadership entirely off guard. The country had been plunged into mourning following the tragic death of Lieutenant Kim Min-seok, a firefighter who lost his life in the line of duty while tackling a commercial blaze in Bucheon, Gyeonggi Province. Tributes had poured in from across the political spectrum, with promises to overhaul safety protocols and improve working conditions for frontline personnel.

The solemn mood was abruptly derailed when a past remark by veteran Democratic Party lawmaker Choo Mi-ae resurfaced. In a previous debate over regional government financing, Choo had referred to firefighting allocations with the derogatory metaphor of a "fiscal straw" siphoning away provincial funds. In the sensitive atmosphere of a state funeral, the resurfaced phrasing struck an immediate, painful nerve with the public.

Recognizing the immense political hazard, party leader Kim Min-seok stepped in directly to limit the damage. In a swift and unusual public intervention, Kim bowed his head before the public, issuing a formal apology to firefighters and grieving citizens. Crucially, he publicly directed Choo herself to offer an unreserved apology, signaling zero tolerance for unforced errors.

"The mouth is a door to disaster, and the tongue is a sword that cuts the body," senior party lawmaker Jeong Seong-ho remarked, invoking an ancient proverb to urge humility and genuine remorse across the party’s ranks.

Democratic Party floor leader Han Byung-do and other key executives made an urgent visit to the fallen firefighter's memorial altar, pledging expanded state support and formal honors for the bereaved family. While the leadership’s rapid crisis management contained the immediate backlash, the episode highlighted how easily a tin-eared comment from an individual lawmaker can undermine the credibility of a governing party.

Courting the South Coast While Tripping Over Festival Etiquette

Against this backdrop of legal battles and apologies, party leader Kim Min-seok spent mid-October touring the southeastern industrial belt known as the PK region (encompassing Busan and South Gyeongsang Province). The region has long served as an electoral battleground: traditionally conservative-leaning, but home to a historic pro-democracy heritage tracing back to the 1979 Buma Democratic Uprising against authoritarian rule.

Kim’s three-day itinerary was designed to reassure local voters on bread-and-butter economic issues. At Busan's bustling Jagalchi fish market, he shook hands with vendors struggling with slow retail turnover. He later visited the massive North Port redevelopment project and the proposed site for an entertainment Dome Arena, pledging full legislative backing for critical regional infrastructure—including the long-planned Gadeokdo New Airport and long-term Arctic commercial shipping routes. The goal was straightforward: demonstrate that the party is focused on balanced national development and reviving regional economies.

However, the grassroots messaging was overshadowed by a minor but widely publicized protocol controversy during a stop in Sunchang, North Jeolla Province. At a local community festival, a live concert by popular vocalist HYNN was reportedly cut short midway through her set so that political figures, including Kim, could take the stage to deliver congratulatory greetings. Online commentary quickly erupted, accusing the politicians of outdated, heavy-handed privilege—often referred to locally as gapjil—at the expense of ordinary festivalgoers. While the disruption may have stemmed from clumsy on-site event coordination, it ran counter to the party’s public pledge to discard authoritarian political habits.

Paralysis at the National Audit and Rumors of Infiltration

Back in Seoul, the parliamentary audit deteriorated into partisan gridlock, particularly in the Science, ICT, Broadcasting, and Communications Committee. The committee descended into shouting matches and procedural walkouts over mutual recusal motions. Conservative lawmakers demanded the resignation of Democratic Party committee director Han Joon-ho, claiming he had misrepresented official answers from public broadcaster KBS. In response, ruling party members aggressively challenged the qualifications of Lee Jin-sook, head of the Korea Communications Commission.

Rather than providing an exhaustive review of broadcast regulation or tech policy, the hearing devolved into a bitter platform for traded insults between senior figures—such as verbal clashes between lawmakers Kim Jang-gyum and Park Jie-won—seemingly staged for short-form video clips on social media. For an electorate expecting substantive governance, the theatrical rancor offered little value.

Meanwhile, an internal vetting headache emerged behind closed doors. Major national dailies, including the progressive Hankyoreh and the conservative Dong-A Ilbo, published investigative reports regarding allegations that members of Shincheonji, a controversial fringe religious sect, had engaged in coordinated, mass party registration. While the sect has previously been scrutinized for attempting to influence internal primary votes across both conservative and liberal factions, reports that members may have infiltrated the ruling party have prompted calls within the Democratic Party for a comprehensive internal audit. Handling these allegations transparently represents an unexpected test of organizational integrity for party headquarters.

What to Watch Next

As the political calendar moves forward, several concrete milestones will indicate whether the Democratic Party can steady its ship or whether it faces escalating friction:

  • A formal impeachment motion: Watch whether hardline calls to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae translate into an officially introduced resolution in the National Assembly, or if leadership opts to use the threat primarily as leverage for pending court reform bills.
  • The conclusion of the parliamentary audit: With standing committees mired in reciprocal ethics filings and walkouts, the final reports will test whether the ruling majority can pass its upcoming legislative agenda or remain trapped in partisan warfare.
  • Party membership verification: Any decision by party leadership to launch an internal audit into religious sect infiltration will signal how aggressively the current leadership is prepared to clean house before the next electoral cycle.

Holding substantial legislative power grants the ruling party the ability to set the national agenda, but it also magnifies every stumble. In South Korean politics, bold constitutional maneuvers rarely succeed unless accompanied by restraint, empathy, and absolute attention to ordinary public sentiment.
