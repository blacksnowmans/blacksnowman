The Double Standard of Justice: Examining the Controversies Surrounding the Han Dong-hoon Investigation

October 12, 2026

For those watching South Korean politics from afar, the machinery of the country's legal system often appears as a grand, impartial arbiter of justice. However, recent developments in the ongoing saga regarding the investigation into the daughter of Han Dong-hoon—the former Justice Minister under the administration of the now-imprisoned former leader, Yoon Suk-yeol—have once again exposed the fragile nature of that impartiality. The accusations of a "selective" judicial process are no longer just whispers; they are front-and-center in the National Assembly’s audit, casting a harsh light on how law enforcement agencies operated under the previous conservative regime.

When the scales of justice tilt based on who is standing before them, the foundation of a democratic society begins to crack. As we examine the records of the investigations from the early days of the Yoon Suk-yeol era, the discrepancy between the treatment of political rivals and those at the heart of power is stark. It is a story of "looking the other way" and institutional cowardice that continues to fuel public distrust.

Why Police Avoided Scrutiny: A Study in Selective Policing

The investigation into allegations surrounding Han Dong-hoon’s daughter—which include potential academic fraud and copyright issues—has been marked by a staggering lack of rigor. Critics point out that while the accusers were summoned for questioning six separate times, those accused—including Han himself and his wife, a lawyer specializing in economic intelligence—were barely touched. The daughter, a minor at the time, faced no meaningful questioning, and the key figures escaped the kind of face-to-face scrutiny that is routine in typical criminal probes.

The behavior of the National Office of Investigation (NOI) was particularly egregious. Despite claims by the then-head of the office that the investigation was thorough and included numerous witness interviews, data later revealed that not a single witness had actually been called. This stands in sharp contrast to the aggressive tactics used during the investigation of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, where the police and prosecution raided dozens of locations to hunt for any shred of incriminating evidence. While the authorities felt comfortable conducting over 100 raids in other cases, they failed to secure a single search warrant in this instance. The message sent to the public was clear: the law is a tool that applies differently depending on one's proximity to power.

The Disparity: 131 Raids versus Zero

To understand the depth of this bias, one need only look at the numbers. During the investigation into allegations concerning First Lady Kim Hye-kyung—involving the misuse of a corporate credit card for charges totaling roughly $55 (about 76,000 won)—authorities conducted 131 raids on restaurants and businesses. Yet, when faced with multifaceted allegations involving academic misconduct and copyright theft by the daughter of a sitting Justice Minister, the police could not produce a single search warrant. This "elastic" application of the law, which stretches to ensnare the opposition while shrinking to protect the powerful, is the antithesis of the rule of law.

Blocking International Cooperation: The Prosecution’s Silent Shield

The investigation hit another wall when it came to international cooperation. The allegations involved potential intellectual property violations and ghostwritten papers—matters that necessitated cooperation with international entities. In one instance, a Kenyan representative even went public, claiming he had received payment for ghostwriting work. Despite the gravity of the claims, the police’s request for international assistance was effectively neutralized by the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office.

When the police attempted to route their requests for information from U.S. institutions through the prosecutors' office, the prosecutors blocked the path, refusing to forward the requests to the Ministry of Justice. Their excuse? That the police requests were poorly drafted and unlikely to be honored by American agencies. However, legal experts noted that it is standard procedure to forward such requests regardless of the perceived likelihood of success. The refusal to even initiate the process suggests an intentional effort to ensure that no incriminating evidence could ever arrive from abroad. One cannot help but conclude that if the requested data had actually materialized, the prosecution would have been forced to act—an outcome they were determined to avoid.

Given the reporting hierarchy, it is difficult to imagine that Han Dong-hoon, as the sitting Justice Minister at the time, was unaware of these maneuvers. The optics of a system shielding its own leader against the very investigations he was supposed to oversee created a profound crisis of legitimacy.

The Road Ahead: Rebuilding Trust in a Changing Political Landscape

Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the political landscape has shifted, yet the shadows of the previous regime's judicial legacy remain. The current judiciary, led by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, faces intense scrutiny as it handles the "judicial risks" associated with various political figures. There is a palpable fear among the public that if the courts continue to operate with the same perceived bias, the government’s ability to drive reform will be severely hampered.

The upcoming weeks are critical. With cases involving former First Lady Kim Keon-hee heading toward the Supreme Court, the judiciary faces a test of its own integrity. If the court process devolves into yet another instance of "selective justice" or a failure to hold those in power accountable, the public's faith in the legal system may reach a point of no return. A government that cannot ensure equal justice is a government that loses its mandate to lead.

The truth may be obscured for a time, but it cannot be erased. As the National Assembly and the public continue to demand transparency, the institutions of South Korea are at a crossroads. Will they choose to uphold the principle that everyone is equal before the law, or will they continue to be instruments of the powerful? The coming months will provide the answer, and for the sake of the nation's democratic future, the stakes could not be higher.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the Supreme Court’s handling of the pending cases regarding the former First Lady and the potential for a special prosecutor's investigation. These events will serve as the final litmus test for whether the judiciary has truly moved past the era of selective, politically motivated investigations.
