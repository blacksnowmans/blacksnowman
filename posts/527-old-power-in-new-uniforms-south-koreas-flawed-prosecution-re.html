Old Power in New Uniforms: South Korea’s Flawed Prosecution Reform and the Shadow of the Past

September 10, 2026

Picture a courtroom where the rules of the game are being rewritten overnight. For decades, South Korea’s prosecution service has held near-absolute power, controlling both the investigation and the indictment of crimes. It is a formidable apparatus that has brought down presidents and chaebol billionaires alike, yet it has long operated with minimal external accountability. Now, as the country prepares to abolish the traditional Prosecutor’s Office and introduce a brand-new investigative body—the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—the promise of reform is colliding with the hard, messy reality of institutional politics. What was supposed to be a historic division of power is beginning to look less like a revolution and more like a rebranding exercise.

Who is the Reform For? The Problem at the Top of the New Agency

The controversy starts with the nine-member committee tasked with selecting the inaugural head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. For a body designed to strip away the entrenched influence of career prosecutors, its composition has raised more than a few eyebrows. Nearly a third of the committee—three out of nine members—are former prosecutors themselves. One is a former aide from the administration of President Yoon Suk-yeol, the conservative leader who himself rose through the prosecutorial ranks. Another is reportedly tied to controversial disciplinary investigations from that era. Notably absent from the selection panel are any representatives with a background in police investigations.

This insular structure exposes a fundamental flaw in how South Korea approaches bureaucratic overhauls. When former prosecutors select candidates, and when the presidential office’s public discipline secretary—also heavily staffed by former prosecutors—handles the vetting, the result is a revolving door. Critics, including lawmaker Park Eun-jung, have argued that this process guarantees the continuation of the old guard’s influence. The agency is ostensibly being built to separate investigating from indicting, but if the people running it come directly from the prosecutor’s traditional stronghold, the public is left wondering whether this is truly a new institution or simply "Prosecution Season 2."

The Spectre of Pro-Yoon Appointments and Past Controversies

The skepticism surrounding the agency deepens when examining the backgrounds of the specific candidates being floated for its leadership. Observers have pointed out ties to politically sensitive, high-profile cases from recent years, such as the contentious investigations surrounding past travel bans and media-prosecution collusion allegations involving prominent political figures. To critics, these are not neutral civil servants; they are figures steeped in the prosecutorial culture of the conservative Yoon administration.

When questioned about whether these backgrounds would compromise the new agency’s impartiality, candidates have typically fallen back on standard assurances, arguing that their dedication to the investigative mission will prevail. But institutional reform cannot rely on good intentions alone. The core debate in South Korea is not about the personal integrity of any single individual; it is about dismantling a dominant legal culture where a single institutional mindset dictates the flow of justice. Observers argue that without leaders who possess a genuine mandate to break up established privileges, structural changes risk becoming entirely superficial.

A Mountain of Cases, A Drop in the Budget

Even if the leadership question were solved, the new agency faces an immediate operational crisis: a staggering backlog of more than 100,000 cases set to be transferred from the traditional prosecution service. Managing this massive shift requires robust infrastructure, yet the financial backing provided by the government has left experts bewildered.

Reports indicate that the proposed increase for investigative operating expenses amounts to a meager 300 million won (about $220,000)—an increment so small that, when adjusted for inflation, it represents a practical budget cut. Investigators cannot operate on paperwork alone; complex financial crimes, digital data analysis, and field operations require substantial resources. Handing over tens of thousands of complex cases without expanding the necessary investigative infrastructure is, critics argue, a recipe for institutional paralysis. It risks creating a system where cases are endlessly bounced between agencies while victims wait years for resolution.

Unresolved Legal Tensions and Whispers of Judicial Interference

Beyond staffing and budgets, the broader legislative framework surrounding the new criminal justice system contains dangerous ambiguities. Debates over supplementary investigation rights between different anti-corruption and investigative bodies threaten to spark turf wars rather than cooperation. Past friction between prosecutors and police over who holds ultimate authority is well-documented, and overlapping jurisdictions inevitably lead to confusion on the ground.

Adding fuel to the fire are explosive allegations emerging from political circles regarding improper judicial lobbying. Recent disclosures in the National Assembly involving high-level discussions about altering judicial panels in sensitive political cases have shocked the public. While specific allegations continue to face scrutiny and verification, the mere fact that such backdoor maneuvers are suspected points to a deeper malaise within South Korea’s legal establishment. When powerful figures are accused of attempting to influence courtroom assignments, it strikes at the heart of public trust in the rule of law.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward the full implementation of its new investigative framework, several critical milestones will determine whether this ambitious reform succeeds or quietly fails:

  • The Final Leadership Appointment: Watch closely to see who ultimately assumes the head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, and whether the appointment reflects genuine compromise or simply rewards politically connected prosecutors.
  • Budget Revisions in the National Assembly: Track upcoming legislative debates on investigative funding to see if lawmakers address the severe resource shortfalls facing incoming cases.
  • Inter-Agency Protocol Agreements: Monitor how the Ministry of the Interior and Safety and other legal bodies iron out jurisdictional overlaps between police, prosecutors, and the new agency ahead of 2027 enforcement deadlines.
  • Public and Opposition Pushback: Pay attention to how opposition lawmakers and civic groups leverage parliamentary hearings to challenge questionable appointments and alleged procedural overreach.

Institutional reform is rarely clean, and in South Korea's polarized political landscape, the stakes could not be higher. Changing the name plates on government buildings is easy; changing a deeply ingrained culture of legal power is where the real battle begins.
