A Disastrous Confirmation Hearing: Why the Scandal at the Major Crimes Investigation Agency Matters

October 16, 2026

Watching the confirmation hearing for Kim Ji-yong, the nominee to lead the newly established Major Crimes Investigation Agency (MCIA)—South Korea's powerful new body tasked with separating investigative and prosecutorial powers—was an exercise in pure dismay. For a public that has long hungered for genuine judicial reform, the spectacle unfolding in the National Assembly felt like a betrayal of the very principles the agency was built to uphold.

As Kim sat before lawmakers, dodging questions and refusing to express even a shred of remorse for his past actions, he embodied the exact "political prosecution" culture that the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung promised to dismantle. The question haunting many observers is how the vetting system, designed to usher in a new era of judicial integrity, could have allowed such a candidate to emerge.

The Case of the "Retaliatory Prosecution"

The hearing quickly spiraled into a confrontation over the prosecution of Prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong. During the infamous "Channel A" investigation, a physical altercation occurred between prosecutors, leading to the indictment of Jeong. Critics have long characterized this move as a retaliatory, politically motivated strike designed to shield Han Dong-hoon—the former Minister of Justice—from scrutiny.

During the hearing, lawmaker Yang Bu-nam of the ruling Democratic Party—South Korea's main center-left party—delivered a pointed interrogation. He revealed that at the time of the indictment, even officials within the Ministry of Justice and the Ministry of the Interior had voiced severe objections, warning that the prosecution was unjust. Despite these internal red flags, Kim Ji-yong pushed the case forward.

When pressed on whether he still believed the indictment was justified—despite the Supreme Court eventually acquitting Jeong—Kim replied without hesitation, "Yes, that is correct." His refusal to acknowledge the error confirms that he prioritized the interests of the "prosecution cartel" over the rule of law. It suggests that he acted not as an objective legal mind, but as a loyalist protecting a powerful figure.

A Pattern of Evasion and Institutional Arrogance

Throughout the hearing, Kim relied on a tactic that has become all too familiar to the Korean public: the "I do not remember" defense. Whenever lawmakers presented evidence of his dubious past conduct, Kim retreated behind a veil of convenient amnesia. For a man who held key positions within the nation's most powerful legal institutions, the sudden loss of memory regarding major, high-stakes decisions was clearly a calculated effort to avoid potential perjury charges.

"A leader of an investigative agency must be built on clear principles and transparent accountability. When a nominee uses selective memory as a shield, they forfeit the right to lead the nation's fight against major crime."

What is perhaps most unsettling is that Kim openly admitted to the committee that he knew the case against Jeong was shaky and that he had consulted with superiors who set the policy to "push it through." He viewed his role not as a seeker of truth, but as a functionary fulfilling a directive from above. To promote such a person to lead an independent investigative body is to ensure that the MCIA remains a mere extension of the old, corrupt power structures.

Why This Threatens the Lee Jae-myung Administration

The establishment of the MCIA was intended to be the crown jewel of judicial reform under President Lee Jae-myung—a move to break the prosecution’s long-standing monopoly on investigative power. By stripping this power away, the government aimed to protect the basic rights of citizens. However, by nominating someone who embodies the worst habits of the "old guard," the administration risks turning the agency into a farce.

Many observers noted a chilling resemblance between Kim’s arrogant, condescending demeanor and that of the imprisoned former president, Yoon Seok-youl, who is currently serving time for insurrection. Just as Yoon once weaponized the prosecution to protect his own political interests, Kim’s performance in the hearing suggests he is cut from the same cloth. If the administration proceeds with his appointment, it risks repeating the same mistakes that led to the collapse of the previous regime.

What to Watch Next

The ball is now firmly in the court of the Blue House. The public anger surrounding this hearing is palpable, and for good reason. If President Lee Jae-myung decides to force this appointment through, he will be sending a signal that "reform" is merely a hollow word. True reform requires the courage to admit when a vetting process has failed and the integrity to withdraw a flawed candidate.

  • Withdrawal vs. Appointment: Watch whether the President bows to public pressure and withdraws Kim's nomination. A failure to do so will likely result in a significant political backlash.
  • Democratic Party Internal Pressure: Observe if lawmakers like Yang Bu-nam and Yoon Gun-young continue to push for the nomination to be scrapped, signaling a potential rift between the party and the administration's personnel office.
  • Public Sentiment: Keep an eye on civil society groups that have championed prosecutorial reform. If they turn against this appointment, it could undermine the legitimacy of the entire MCIA from its inception.

The appointment of Kim Ji-yong would not be a victory for the government; it would be a self-inflicted wound. The MCIA was meant to be a tool for justice, not a reward for a loyalist. To regain public trust, the Lee Jae-myung administration must act decisively: reject the shadow of the old guard and find a nominee who truly understands that the law exists to serve the people, not the power-hungry.
