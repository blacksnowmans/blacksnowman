The Pragmatist and the Radical Left: How Lee Jae-myung Built and Broke His Alliance with the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition

October 20, 2026

For observers outside South Korea, the ascent of President Lee Jae-myung from municipal outsider to the nation’s highest office can seem like a triumph of pure populist charisma. Coming into power after the turbulent presidency of Yoon Suk-yeol—whose tenure unraveled in political disaster and impeachment, leading to his current confinement behind bars—Lee presents himself as an agile, pragmatic leader capable of delivering tangible results. Yet beneath his meteoric rise lies a complex, decades-long relationship with one of South Korea's most controversial political factions: the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition.

Far from a shared ideological crusade, the bond between Lee and this hard-left activist faction was forged in the cold calculus of electoral necessity. It was an alliance built on mutual interest, tested in the municipal corridors of Seongnam, expanded into the national apparatus of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, and ultimately fractured by mutual accusations of betrayal. Understanding this relationship offers critical insight into the mechanics of South Korean political machines and the pragmatic instincts that define President Lee's governance.

A Chameleon's Rise: Pragmatism Over Ideological Purity

To grasp Lee Jae-myung's political instincts, one must look back to 1996, long before he entered mainstream national awareness. At the time, Lee was a human rights lawyer working in Seongnam, a satellite city southeast of Seoul, alongside civil society activists like Shin Sang-jin, a medical doctor who would later become a conservative lawmaker and Seongnam mayor. According to recollections shared by Shin, Lee made an unexpected phone call ahead of the 15th general election.

Lee asked Shin for his thoughts on running for parliament as a candidate for the New Korea Party—the direct predecessor of today’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. Shin was stunned. Both men were street-level progressive activists fighting the conservative government’s labor and social policies. When asked why he was entertaining such a move, Lee explained that Kim Young-sun, a fellow lawyer who soon entered parliament under the conservative party's proportional banner, had encouraged him to run. When Shin adamantly opposed the idea, Lee quickly dropped the subject, quietly cautioning Shin: "Please do not mention this to anyone else."

While conservative opponents have used this episode to question Lee's progressive bona fides, it illustrates a more fundamental trait: an early willingness to explore any institutional vehicle to secure political power. Ideological orthodoxy took a back seat to electoral viability.

A decade later, Lee shifted course entirely. In 2006, preparing for his first run for Seongnam mayor, he published a fiery post on his personal blog. He declared that after years of habitually backing the Democratic Party, he could no longer discern any meaningful difference between it and the conservative Grand National Party. Instead, he pledged his vote to the radical Democratic Labor Party (DLP) and leveled a blistering critique at then-President Roh Moo-hyun:

"The Roh Moo-hyun administration is a cowardly government that evades reality and refuses to fight head-on."

For modern Democratic Party supporters who revere Roh Moo-hyun as an icon of principled reform, Lee's past rhetoric remains startling—particularly given Lee’s later pilgrimages to Roh’s memorial in Bongha Village to claim his mantle. But in 2006, Lee was an outsider shut out by the Democratic Party's established mainstream. His attack on the center-left establishment was a strategic overture to the DLP and the potent activist networks operating within Seongnam, chief among them the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition.

The 2010 Pact: Trading Municipal Clout for Political Power

The pivotal breakthrough in Lee's career arrived during the 2010 local elections. Running for mayor of Seongnam on the Democratic Party ticket, Lee brokered a formal alliance with Kim Mi-hee, the candidate of the Democratic Labor Party. Kim withdrew her candidacy, consolidating the progressive vote behind Lee in a fiercely contested suburban battleground. The maneuver propelled Lee into the mayoral office, but it also opened the gates of city administration to the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition.

The Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition had roots tracing back to the 1991 National Alliance for Democracy and Reunification, representing the National Liberation (NL) faction of the South Korean student movement. Characterized by fierce organizational discipline and anti-imperialist rhetoric, the faction had built a formidable grassroots presence in working-class neighborhoods of Seongnam.

Following the 2010 victory, the spoils of governance were shared systematically:

  • Control of the Transition Team: Kim Mi-hee was appointed head of the mayoral transition committee. She was joined by prominent coalition leaders, including Jung Hyung-ju, former head of the DLP’s Gyeonggi provincial chapter, and Han Yong-jin, former co-chair of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition.
  • Campaign Logistics: CNP Strategy Group, a political consulting and PR firm founded by Lee Seok-ki—a radical figure later convicted in a high-profile insurrection conspiracy case—was hired to design and produce Lee Jae-myung’s 2010 official campaign materials, policy booklets, and business cards.
  • Municipal Contracts: A newly established social enterprise named Nanum Environment secured lucrative multi-billion-won municipal waste-collection contracts from the city. The firm’s representative was Han Yong-jin, the same coalition heavyweight who had served on Lee's transition committee.

The municipal contracts triggered fierce controversy, with critics alleging corrupt political patronage masquerading as support for social enterprises. Lee staunchly defended the procurement process, stating that Nanum Environment was selected through a rigorous and transparent screening process. While state prosecutors launched a full-scale investigation and ultimately closed the case without formal indictments, the episode established a lasting public perception: in exchange for organizational foot soldiers, Lee had granted a fringe radical faction access to public resources and municipal governance.

Marching on Yeouido: Capturing the Democratic Party Machine

The alliance forged in Seongnam did not remain local. As Lee set his sights on national politics, the disciplined mobilization techniques honed by the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition were deployed inside the Democratic Party itself.

During the 2017 Democratic presidential primary, where Lee challenged Moon Jae-in, outside supporter groups around Lee began chanting an aggressive slogan: "Let's take over the Democratic Party with Lee Jae-myung." Rather than working within the party's institutional traditions, the strategy focused on swarming the party apparatus from the outside.

Following Lee's defeat in the 20th presidential election, this effort intensified. Key figures from his Seongnam circle spearheaded mass registration drives for fee-paying party members (gwonli-dangwon). Lee Heon-wook, a close associate who ran for Seongnam mayor, publicly stated that bringing in 100,000 committed Lee supporters could rewrite the Democratic Party's internal balance of power. Another longtime Seongnam ally, Jeong Jong-sam, framed the drive in confrontational terms, urging activists to join the party and clash directly with loyalist factions backing Moon Jae-in.

This entryist playbook closely mirrored how the radical left had previously captured minority progressive parties like the Democratic Labor Party and the Unified Progressive Party. By flooding the ranks with highly organized, ideologically cohesive members, Lee's faction overwhelmed the traditional establishment, paving the way for Lee to seize the party chairmanship by historic margins.

Simultaneously, ideological rebranding efforts gained momentum. STI, an opinion research institute that evolved from Lee Seok-ki’s former Society Trend Institute in 2015, began steering prominent voter panel surveys. STI figures subsequently participated in strategic forums organized by Lee-aligned lawmakers, championing the narrative of a "New Lee Jae-myung." The core argument was stark: the Democratic Party needed to discard the legacy of former presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in, severing ties with traditional factions to rebuild the party entirely around Lee as an unencumbered national leader.

The Bitter Aftermath: Betrayal, Severance, and the Fate of Grassroots Politics

Yet the relationship between Lee Jae-myung and the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition was never an unbreakable fraternity. As Lee transitioned from local administration to the national stage, the coalition's ideological baggage and legal controversies became severe liabilities. To protect his viability as a mainstream presidential contender, Lee increasingly severed ties with the very activists who had helped him secure power.

This deliberate distancing left deep scars. During Lee's tenure as governor of Gyeonggi Province, veterans of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Coalition openly vented their fury in private circles, slamming tables and accusing Lee of discarding them the moment they outlived their usefulness (paeng-dang-haet-da). One bitter remark summed up their disillusionment: "Lee Jae-myung is not human." They viewed him as an ungrateful opportunist; Lee viewed the break as an inevitable shedding of radical baggage required to run a modern democracy.

Today, as President Lee governs South Korea, the legacy of this transactional pact continues to reverberate at the grassroots level. In Seongnam, where this political machinery was first assembled, civil society is actively fighting to reclaim municipal governance from partisan spoils. Recently, a coalition of 14 civic organizations—including the Seongnam Alliance of Civic Organizations—held a joint conference at the Seongnam City Council to propose the "12 Core Policies for Seongnam's Future," urging local leadership to end decades of factional patronage and restore genuine public interest.

What to Watch Next:

  • Down-Ballot Local Elections: Watch whether independent civil society groups in Seongnam and Gyeonggi Province can successfully assert non-partisan policy agendas, or if entrenched party machines and activist remnants will retain control over local procurement and administration.
  • Party Factionalism: Observe how the Democratic Party balances Lee's centralized authority against traditional pro-Roh and pro-Moon loyalists, particularly as legislative battles with the opposition People Power Party demand broad party unity.
  • Personnel and Policy Direction: Monitor key appointments within the presidential office and ministries for any lingering presence of Gyeonggi-era figures, which will reveal whether President Lee has entirely closed the chapter on his earliest political alliance.
