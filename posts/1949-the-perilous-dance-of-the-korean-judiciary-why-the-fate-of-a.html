The Perilous Dance of the Korean Judiciary: Why the Fate of an Imprisoned Ex-Leader Matters for President Lee Jae-myung

November 04, 2026

The political atmosphere in Seoul today is thick with a tension that belies the fact that the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, has already found its footing. While the government attempts to move forward with its legislative agenda, a shadow continues to loom over the capital: the Supreme Court. In recent weeks, the possibility of a final, definitive guilty verdict for Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the former leader who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence—has sent ripples through both the ruling and opposition camps. To a casual observer, this might look like the simple closing of a dark chapter in Korean history. However, for those navigating the corridors of power, the looming verdict is viewed as part of a high-stakes "drag-down" strategy orchestrated by a judiciary that is increasingly seen as a political actor in its own right.

The stakes are not merely about the legal fate of one man. What is actually happening is a complex game of political engineering. Under the leadership of Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, the judiciary is navigating a survival strategy that could fundamentally alter the financial viability of the opposition and, paradoxically, create a legal trap for President Lee. To understand why a verdict against a man already in prison could threaten a sitting president, one must look past the courtroom drama and into the machinery of Korean campaign finance and legal precedent.

The $31 Million Time Bomb Facing the Opposition

The most immediate and tangible impact of a final guilty verdict for Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is the potential bankruptcy of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition party. Under South Korean election law, the state reimburses major political parties for their campaign expenses if their candidate reaches a certain threshold of the vote. During the last presidential election, the PPP received a massive payout from the national treasury to cover these costs. If the candidate is later found guilty of certain election law violations, the party is legally obligated to return that money to the state.

Internal estimates within the opposition suggest the figure is staggering: roughly 43.4 billion won (about $31 million). For a political party, this is not just a fine; it is a liquidation event. The prospect of having to return tens of millions of dollars has sent the PPP into a state of quiet panic. There is a growing realization that "clinging" to the legacy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and his wife, Ms. Kim Keon-hee, may no longer be a matter of loyalty, but a threat to the party’s very existence.

"The moment the guilty verdict is finalized, the People Power Party faces a situation where they must cough up the election costs. This isn't just about money; it's about the survival of the party's foundation."

As the financial pressure mounts, fissures are appearing within the conservative camp. The judiciary, led by Chief Justice Cho, appears to have sensed this shift. Observers note that the Supreme Court seems less interested in shielding the former leader and more interested in its own long-term influence. By "cutting ties" with the imprisoned Mr. Yoon, the judiciary can present an image of impartiality to the public while simultaneously preparing its next move against the current administration.

The 'Water Ghost' Strategy: Precedent as a Weapon

In Korean folklore, a "water ghost" (mulgwisin) is a spirit that tries to pull the living down into the depths with it. In the context of Seoul's current legal warfare, the "water ghost strategy" refers to the judiciary’s attempt to use a guilty verdict against Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol as a legal template to ensnare President Lee Jae-myung. This is the "profitable trade" that critics accuse the courts of making: sacrificing a former, disgraced leader to set a precedent that will eventually be used to hobble the sitting president.

The core of this strategy lies in how the court interprets political speech and campaign promises. If the court finds that certain statements made by Mr. Yoon during his campaign constituted illegal acts, the opposition and the judiciary can immediately turn around and apply that same logic to President Lee’s past statements. It is a cynical but effective form of legal leveling. By ensuring Mr. Yoon is found guilty, the courts gain a "fairness" shield—they can claim they are not being partisan when they eventually turn their sights on the Democratic Party leadership.

  • Standardization of Guilt: By establishing a broad definition of what constitutes a "false statement" in a campaign, the court creates a net that can be cast over any politician.
  • Selective Prosecution: While the courts move with "microscopic" precision against the President’s circle, critics point out that they have been far more lenient toward groups perceived as destabilizing the current government.
  • Judicial Supremacy: This maneuvering allows the judiciary to sit above both the legislative and executive branches, acting as the ultimate arbiter of who is allowed to hold power.

This creates a dangerous situation where the law is used not for justice, but as a tool for balancing power between political factions. If the judiciary is allowed to trade one conviction for another, the very concept of the rule of law in Korea is at risk of being replaced by a system of judicial "deals."

The Stalled Engines of Innovation

While this legal battle rages in the courtrooms and the headlines, the actual business of governing South Korea is suffering. A nation’s executive energy is a finite resource, and the constant need to defend against judicial "raids" has a measurable cost on policy. One of the most glaring examples is the recent stagnation of the government’s Artificial Intelligence (AI) Task Force.

Early in President Lee’s term, there was a significant push to position South Korea as a global leader in AI, competing with the likes of major tech firms and international innovators. However, analysts have noted that these discussions have largely ground to a halt. The timing is particularly telling: the momentum slowed significantly around the time of major party conventions and the intensification of these legal challenges. When the President’s office is forced to focus on political defense and managing the fallout of judicial maneuvering, the "golden time" for national industries is lost.

The cost of this distraction is high. In a world where global companies are racing to dominate the AI landscape, a few months of stalled policy can mean years of falling behind. The tragedy of the current situation is that the "priorities" of the state are being dictated by the court’s calendar rather than the needs of the economy or the people. For the Korean public, the sight of a government mired in legal defense while the future of technology is decided elsewhere is a bitter pill to swallow.

Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the Double Standard

At the center of this storm is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. To many, his leadership of the Supreme Court represents a "calculated neutrality." By appearing to allow the prosecution of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol to proceed, he insulates the court from accusations of being a conservative holdover. Yet, the underlying concern is that this is merely a tactical retreat. By sacrificing the "baggage" of the previous, failed administration, the judicial elite can maintain their grip on the levers of power and continue to act as a check—or a barrier—to the Lee administration’s reforms.

The perceived double standard is particularly sharp when it comes to the investigation of Ms. Kim Keon-hee. While the courts and prosecutors have been relentless in pursuing the President’s associates, the investigations into the former first lady’s various controversies have often seemed to stall or vanish. This lack of balance reinforces the suspicion that the judiciary is not weighing evidence on an even scale, but is instead managing a political portfolio.

"The judiciary should be a hall of justice, not a den of political assassins. When legal precedents are used as bargaining chips for factional revenge, the very foundation of the separation of powers is destroyed."

The danger for South Korea is that the judiciary is no longer seen as a neutral referee, but as a player on the field—one that is currently trying to "win" by ensuring that if one side falls, the other is dragged down with it. This "water ghost" politics ensures that no matter who is in the Blue House, they are always looking over their shoulder at the courthouse.

What to Watch Next

As we move into the next quarter, several key indicators will reveal whether the judiciary’s "drag-down" strategy will succeed or if the government can break the cycle of legal warfare.

  • The PPP Financial Crisis: Watch for the exact date of the Supreme Court’s final ruling on Mr. Yoon. If a guilty verdict is handed down, the immediate question will be how the People Power Party plans to pay back the $31 million. Any move to restructure the party or declare a form of political bankruptcy will signal a total collapse of the old conservative guard.
  • Resumption of the AI Task Force: A clear sign of the government’s health will be whether President Lee can successfully restart stalled initiatives like the AI TF. If the administration can push through significant tech policy despite the legal noise, it will show a high level of resilience.
  • Precedent Application: Legal experts will be watching the lower courts to see if they begin citing the (potential) Yoon verdict in new cases brought against Democratic Party officials. This will be the clearest indicator of whether the "water ghost" strategy is being actively deployed.
  • Public Sentiment on Judicial Reform: If the public begins to perceive the Supreme Court’s actions as a "profitable trade" rather than justice, we may see a renewed and much more aggressive push for judicial reform from the National Assembly.

In the end, the fate of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol in a prison cell is less important than the legal "ghost" he leaves behind. Whether South Korea can move past this era of judicialized politics will depend on whether its institutions can return to their proper roles: the government to governing, and the courts to the impartial application of the law, free from the calculations of a political marketplace.
