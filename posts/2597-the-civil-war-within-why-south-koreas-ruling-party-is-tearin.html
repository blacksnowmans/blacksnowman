The Civil War Within: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Tearing Itself Apart

November 21, 2026

On a typical Friday morning at the National Assembly in Seoul, the high-stakes theater of South Korean politics usually pits the ruling party against the opposition. But recently, the most dramatic confrontation has been happening behind the closed doors of the Democratic Party’s own headquarters. When the clock struck 9:30 AM for the weekly Supreme Council meeting, three of the party’s top leaders were missing. Instead of taking their seats alongside Party Chairman Kim Min-seok, they were standing before a bank of microphones in the press room, launching a public rebellion against their own leadership. This wasn’t just a scheduling conflict; it was a declaration of internal war.

The Democratic Party, South Korea’s main liberal party and currently the party in power under President Lee Jae-myung, is engulfed in a fratricidal conflict that threatens to derail its legislative agenda. At the heart of the storm are two powerful figures: Chairman Kim Min-seok and Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee. What began as a dispute over "slips of the tongue" and private conversations has spiraled into a fundamental battle over the party’s soul, its disciplinary processes, and the legacy of the movement that led to the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

For those outside Korea, the intensity of this infighting might seem disproportionate. However, in the context of South Korean political culture—where party loyalty is paramount and "factional logic" often dictates survival—this rift represents a significant threat to the stability of the Lee Jae-myung administration. It is a struggle over who gets to define the party’s history and, more importantly, who will control the gates for the next round of election nominations.

The Remarks That Shattered the Peace

The immediate fuse for this explosion was a leaked report regarding remarks made by Choi Min-hee, a high-profile member of the party’s Supreme Council, during a private meeting with about twenty supporters in her district. According to reports, Choi suggested that during the period when current President Lee Jae-myung was serving as the party leader, he had initially been hesitant about the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. She reportedly claimed that Lee believed he needed to win over moderate voters to become president and therefore favored a "voluntary resignation" or "step-down" approach rather than the forceful impeachment that eventually led to Mr. Yoon’s removal and current imprisonment.

This claim is radioactive within the Democratic Party. The impeachment and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is viewed by the party’s base as a crowning achievement of democratic justice. To suggest that the sitting President, Lee Jae-myung, was ever lukewarm about that process is seen by many as a betrayal of the party’s core identity. To make matters worse, Choi was also caught in a private chat group referring to press photographers as a "pack of hyenas," further fueling the fire.

Chairman Kim Min-seok, representing the party leadership, moved with startling speed. He immediately ordered an ethical investigation into Choi, referring her to the party’s Ethics Tribunal. This "hardline" response shocked many within the party. While Choi argued that her words were taken out of context—explaining that she was actually defending the President against claims made by public intellectual Rhyu Si-min—the machinery of party discipline had already been set in motion. The "reformist" faction of the party, which includes several other Supreme Council members, saw this not as a pursuit of ethics, but as a "frame-up" intended to silence dissenting voices within the leadership.

The Shadow of Shincheonji and the August Convention

To understand why Choi Min-hee’s supporters are so angry, one has to look back to the party’s national convention on August 17. During the heat of the campaign for the party chairmanship, Kim Min-seok—then a candidate—raised a shocking allegation. He suggested that his rivals, including former party leader Jung Cheong-rae and the "reformist" faction, were part of a "secret three-way alliance" with Shincheonji.

In South Korea, Shincheonji is a highly controversial religious movement that became a household name globally during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. In political terms, being linked to Shincheonji is a "kiss of death," often used to imply secretive, cult-like manipulation of political processes. Kim Min-seok promised that if elected, he would launch a full investigation into these "anti-party, divisive" forces.

However, after winning the chairmanship and forming a task force to investigate, Kim was forced to backtrack. On November 3, during a general meeting of party lawmakers, he admitted there was no evidence for the Shincheonji claims. His "sob apology"—an emotional public display of regret—was not enough for his critics. They argue that Kim’s convention tactics were a far more serious "anti-party act" than any private comment made by Choi Min-hee.

The argument from the pro-Choi faction is simple: if Choi is being investigated for a private conversation, why is the Chairman not being held to the same standard for making false, public accusations about a "secret cult alliance" during a televised convention? This perceived double standard has turned a simple disciplinary matter into a broader debate about "party democracy."

A Systemic Failure of Political Ethics

The skepticism toward the party’s internal investigation is rooted in a long history of toothless disciplinary bodies in Korean politics. Data from the Center for Legislative Watch at People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy (PSPD), a prominent Korean NGO, reveals a bleak picture. During the 21st National Assembly, 53 disciplinary motions were submitted to the Ethics Special Committee. Of those, 27 involved members of the Democratic Party. However, 46 of those 53 cases were never even deliberated; they simply expired at the end of the legislative term. Only one resulted in actual punishment.

This pattern extends to local government as well. Investigations into 243 local councils showed that while 169 disciplinary actions were taken since mid-2022, the vast majority were mere "warnings" or short suspensions, even for serious offenses like drunk driving or abuse of power.

"When the system is only used to target political rivals while ignoring genuine misconduct, the very idea of an 'Ethics Tribunal' becomes a weapon of factional warfare rather than a tool for self-reflection."

This background is why the current investigation into Choi Min-hee feels so different to party members. It isn’t being seen as a routine ethical check, but as a targeted strike. When the legal and ethical systems of a party are perceived to be partisan, any attempt at discipline backfires, creating "martyrs" and deepening the divide. This is why heavyweights like Chu Mi-ae—the former Justice Minister and a powerful provincial governor—have entered the fray. Chu has been vocal on social media, stating that the party cannot simply "forgive and move on" regarding Kim Min-seok’s convention allegations, arguing that his actions constituted a much graver threat to party unity than Choi’s remarks.

The Presidential Office and the Ghost of Factions Past

The conflict has become so severe that it has forced a response from the Blue House (the Presidential Office). Kang Yu-jeong, the Senior Spokesperson for President Lee Jae-myung, issued a formal statement of "strong regret" regarding the reports of Choi’s comments. The Presidential Office flatly denied that Lee had ever opposed the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, calling such claims "groundless."

While the President’s office likely intended to clear the air and protect the President’s legacy, the intervention has had the side effect of emboldening the party leadership’s crackdown on Choi. This has led to a debate about President Lee’s own leadership style. Critics on YouTube channels like Bak Si-young TV, which are influential among the party's hardcore supporters, argue that the President’s calls for "unity" are ringing hollow if his administration is seen as taking sides in a factional purge.

Analysts are drawing parallels to the "Ddong-pari" incident during the previous Moon Jae-in administration. "Ddong-pari" (a derogatory term meaning "dung flies") referred to a fringe group of extremist supporters who claimed to be the only "true" followers of the president while viciously attacking anyone they deemed insufficiently loyal—including Lee Jae-myung when he was a provincial governor. That movement eventually collapsed because mainstream politicians eventually distanced themselves from such divisive rhetoric. The fear now is that the current leadership is failing to learn that lesson, instead allowing internal "split-and-attack" tactics to become the norm.

What to Watch Next: Nominations and Sovereignty

Why does this matter right now? Because the Democratic Party is approaching a critical juncture: the preparation for future elections and the "systemic nomination" process. In South Korean politics, the power to nominate candidates is the ultimate leverage. If the party leadership can use ethical investigations to disqualify rivals, they can effectively reshape the party in their own image before a single vote is cast by the public.

The "reformist" faction, led by figures like Jung Cheong-rae and supported by Chu Mi-ae, is pushing for "party member sovereignty"—a system where the rank-and-file members have more power than the central leadership. They see the current crackdown as an attempt by Chairman Kim Min-seok and his allies to secure their grip on the nomination process.

In the coming weeks, there are three key developments to watch:

  • The Ethics Tribunal Ruling: If the party follows through with a heavy-handed punishment for Choi Min-hee, such as suspension or expulsion, expect the "reformist" boycott of the Supreme Council to turn into a full-scale breakaway or a formal motion of no-confidence against Chairman Kim.
  • The "Shincheonji" Fallout: Watch if the party members successfully force a more formal accountability process for Kim Min-seok's convention remarks. If the "sob apology" is deemed insufficient by the base, his leadership will be effectively lame-ducked.
  • President Lee’s Public Stance: So far, the President has spoken through spokespeople. If the infighting begins to tank the party’s approval ratings or stall key legislation, President Lee may be forced to intervene personally to broker a "grand bargain" between the factions.

The Democratic Party currently holds the presidency and a significant presence in the legislature, but this internal rift proves that its biggest enemy might not be the opposition People Power Party, but its own inability to manage the very "party democracy" it claims to champion. For a public that is still processing the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's impeachment, this spectacle of the ruling party eating its own is a sobering reminder of the volatility of Korean politics.
