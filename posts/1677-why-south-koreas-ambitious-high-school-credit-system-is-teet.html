Why South Korea’s Ambitious High School Credit System Is Teetering on the Brink

October 29, 2026

Inside a South Korean high school counseling office in late autumn, a fifteen-year-old freshman sits across from a teacher, thumbing through a thick booklet of course electives. Under the country’s newly introduced High School Credit System (gogyo hakjeomje), this teenager is expected to tailor their curriculum to a specific career ambition, choosing specialized classes in sciences, humanities, or social studies much like an American university undergraduate. Yet in a hyper-competitive educational landscape where a single misstep can derail admission to a top Seoul university, this newfound freedom feels less like empowerment and more like an impossible gamble. If a student chooses the wrong track at age fifteen, can they ever recover?

What was conceived as South Korea’s most sweeping secondary school reform in decades has collided head-on with the unforgiving realities of the university entrance race. In regional school districts and local assemblies across the country, discontent is boiling over. Once hailed as a progressive shift toward student autonomy, the High School Credit System is now facing calls for radical restructuring—with some prominent local lawmakers and educators arguing that the policy requires reforms bordering on outright abolition.

An American-Style System in an Exam-Obsessed Pressure Cooker

To understand the current revolt against the credit system, one must understand how South Korean high schools traditionally operated. For generations, secondary education followed a rigid, unified curriculum. High schoolers sat in uniform homeroom cohorts, studying identical government-approved textbooks designed to prepare them for the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), widely known as the Suneung. While predictable, the model drew heavy criticism for fostering passive, rote memorization and treating teenagers as standardized exam-taking machines.

The High School Credit System was meant to dismantle that assembly line. Modeled in part on modular secondary systems used in the United States, Canada, and Finland, it allows students to earn individual credits across various elective subjects and graduate once they reach a cumulative benchmark. The stated objective was noble: encourage teenagers to explore their aptitudes, pursue self-directed learning, and escape the one-size-fits-all conveyor belt.

In practice, however, the reform placed an unbearable burden on fifteen-year-old freshmen. Expecting young teenagers to map out specialized vocational and academic roadmaps before they have even adjusted to high school life has created widespread anxiety among students and parents alike. Rather than exploring subjects out of genuine curiosity, students scramble to calculate how admissions officers at elite universities will evaluate each elective on their transcripts.

That disconnect led Kim Han-seul, a member of the Gyeonggi Provincial Council representing the province surrounding Seoul, to publicly call for reforms "approaching total abolition." Kim warned that educational authorities are squandering the critical "golden time" to intervene. Without an immediate and comprehensive course correction, Kim argued, the policy's compounding structural flaws will be offloaded entirely onto the students themselves, reducing what was supposed to be a career exploration tool into an auxiliary weapon for entrance exam maneuvering.

The 77-School Diagnostic: How the Reform Deepened Institutional Inequality

While the debate in the capital region often focuses on parental angst and admission strategies, regional educators are grappling with a much more physical problem: basic institutional capacity. A revealing study conducted by the South Chungcheong Provincial Office of Education underscores the growing divide between well-funded urban campuses and resource-strapped regional schools.

Evaluating operations across 77 general academic high schools over a two-year diagnostic period, the provincial office presented its findings at Kongju National University. The final report painted a sobering picture of how a uniform policy creates asymmetric hardship:

  • Resource and Faculty Disparities: Larger schools in prosperous municipal districts can afford to staff dozens of specialized electives, ranging from advanced microeconomics to niche programming languages. Smaller, rural high schools simply do not have the certified teaching personnel to offer comparable variety, putting their students at a built-in disadvantage.
  • Administrative Burnout: Teachers reported that their working hours are increasingly swallowed by the logistics of managing bespoke student schedules, juggling multi-track grading frameworks, and coordinating external course offerings.
  • Loss of Pastoral Care: As teachers become administrative paper-pushers struggling to fulfill state-mandated elective quotas, the essential face-to-face interaction and emotional guidance that adolescents need has sharply diminished.

The South Chungcheong findings demonstrated that simply decreeing curricular diversity from above does not create the physical teachers or classroom infrastructure required to deliver it. Without targeted regional subsidies and dedicated faculty allocations, the credit system risks cementing an uneven playing field where a student's educational choice is strictly bounded by their school's postal code.

Teachers at the Breaking Point: Demanding a Policy Freeze

Discontent inside faculty lounges has now reached an organized boiling point. South Korea's three major teachers' organizations have joined together to issue an urgent demand: the central government must convene a comprehensive, pan-governmental consultative body to re-examine the credit system's rollout before classroom operations completely unravel.

The teachers' core grievance centers on reckless policy sequencing. The very cohort of students navigating the early rollout of the credit system will soon become high school seniors, facing an upcoming overhaul of the national university entrance framework scheduled for 2028. Yet educational authorities have continued pushing forward with secondary assessment reforms—such as introducing subjective short-answer and essay-based evaluations—before the foundational logistics of the credit system have even stabilized.

"Teachers in the field are not guinea pigs for bureaucratic policy experiments. Trying to force an unready system into schools without resolving the underlying evaluation mechanics has exhausted both students and educators."

Critics argue that the policy reflects the pitfalls of top-down bureaucratic mandates. The accelerated rollout was aggressively pushed through during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose conservative administration pursued major institutional shakeups without securing the necessary frontline consensus or building sustainable infrastructure. Following Mr. Yoon's subsequent impeachment and imprisonment, the responsibility of untangling this bureaucratic quagmire has fallen to the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

Educators insist that no curricular innovation can succeed in South Korea if it treats high schools in isolation from the university admissions regime. When university admissions officers still rank candidates according to cutthroat, relative grading matrices, any idealistic elective system inevitably gets contorted into a strategic gaming exercise. Unless the entrance examination machine itself is decoupled from regular secondary schooling, the credit system's aspirational ideals will remain an expensive illusion.

What to Watch Next

The crisis surrounding the High School Credit System is fast approaching a decisive inflection point. Over the coming months, several key indicators will determine whether the reform survives or undergoes a managed dismantling:

  • The Pan-Governmental Consultative Body: Watch whether the Ministry of Education yields to the three major teacher unions' demands to launch a formal multi-agency commission. If established, this body will likely serve as the mechanism to prune, delay, or radically modify core components of the credit mandate.
  • Revisions to the 2028 College Admissions Overhaul: The true test of the credit system lies in how South Korean universities will evaluate elective coursework. Keep an eye on announcements from the Ministry of Education and major universities regarding whether course variety will genuinely factor into admissions or if traditional standardized test metrics will continue to reign supreme.
  • Legislative Pushback from Regional Councils: Following the alarm sounded by council members in Gyeonggi Province, regional assemblies in other provinces may begin pushing their own resolutions to halt or defund specific operational guidelines, forcing central policymakers to renegotiate implementation timelines.
  • Teacher Workload Interventions: Track whether the central government allocates dedicated funding for auxiliary administrative personnel to relieve high school teachers, or if the administrative overload continues to fuel resignations and early retirements across the public education sector.

South Korea’s struggle with the High School Credit System offers a cautionary tale for any nation attempting progressive pedagogical reform within a brutally competitive society. You cannot simply import student-centered Western electives and drop them onto a century-old exam sorting mechanism. Until educational authorities confront that fundamental contradiction, the dream of liberating South Korean classrooms will remain trapped beneath the weight of its own good intentions.
