The Heavy Toll of Betrayal: Assessing the 23-Year Sentence for South Korea’s Former Prime Minister

September 05, 2026

The air in a courtroom is always heavy, but the atmosphere inside the Seoul Central District Court on the 21st was distinct. Twenty-three years in prison. The sentence handed down to Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister of South Korea, is far more than the standard political fall from grace. It stands as the judiciary’s first stern verdict regarding the "December 3 Insurrection"—a failed attempt by those sworn to protect the Constitution to systematically dismantle it.

A Coup from Within: The Unique Weight of the December 3 Insurrection

In the history of South Korean jurisprudence, the December 3 Insurrection occupies a space entirely different from the military coups of the 20th century. While past insurrections were examples of "violence from below" or from the military periphery attempting to topple the system, this event was a "palace coup"—an attempt by the highest-ranking officials, who had been granted democratic legitimacy, to overthrow the constitutional order themselves.

This was not merely a case of neutralizing state institutions; it was a profound act of betrayal that eroded the very trust citizens have placed in the democratic system they built over decades. The court made it clear that it would not adhere to historical precedents regarding insurrection cases. Given South Korea's current status as a robust global democracy—a massive leap from the military-authoritarian era of 45 years ago—the shock to the nation's foundation was immeasurable. The judiciary concluded that existing sentencing guidelines were woefully inadequate for capturing the anti-constitutional nature of these crimes. The core factor driving this 23-year sentence was the cold reality that the highest officials in the land, rather than soldiers on a battlefield, used emergency decrees and troops to trample on the fundamental rights of their own citizens.

The Unforgivable Gap in Accountability

Han’s defense team appealed for leniency, citing his advanced age, health issues, and the standard "customs" of governance. The court, however, remained unmoved. It emphasized that as Prime Minister, Han had a constitutional duty to uphold the law, a duty he deliberately abandoned. Standing at the pinnacle of power, Han participated in a plot to overthrow the state based on the delusional hope that such an insurrection might actually succeed. The court viewed his behavior—denying the truth and claiming "lack of memory" even in the face of CCTV evidence—not as repentance, but as a cowardly attempt to evade responsibility until the very end.

Why 23 Years? The Judiciary’s Stern Warning

Article 87 of the South Korean Criminal Act deals strictly with insurrection, but the logic behind this 23-year sentence goes beyond a simple reading of the law. The December 3 Insurrection violated the values protected by the Constitution in the most malicious way possible. The fact that elected officials aimed their weapons at the people, with the Prime Minister acting as an architect of that process, represented a violent regression of South Korea's democratization history.

Some have questioned whether the sentence is excessive. However, such a perspective overlooks the "quagmire of dictatorship" that the insurrection almost forced upon the nation. Had the plot succeeded, the public might have been stripped of the freedoms they enjoy today and forcibly dragged back into a dark, authoritarian past. The court’s verdict serves as a powerful social warning, ensuring that "palace coups" never find a foothold in this country again.

Within South Korea's legal system, insurrection is among the most severe crimes, as it strikes at the very existence of the state. Compared to the sentencing of the leaders of the 1979 military coup, this verdict reflects the current era's demand for accountability, prioritizing the nation's international standing and the public's unwavering belief in democracy.

What We Witnessed: Democracy Under Fire

The details of the event are chilling. Following the proclamation of martial law, allegations surfaced that the Minister of National Defense and the head of the Counterintelligence Command were attempting to provoke a conflict with North Korea to justify their actions. The six-hour standoff, where lawmakers rushed to the National Assembly to pass a resolution overturning martial law while troops swarmed the building, was not a scene from a political thriller—it was the reality of modern South Korea.

The special prosecutor’s report argued that the cabinet ministers acted as if they were a singular entity with the former President, Yoon Suk-yeol, illustrating just how close the nation came to losing its democratic path. For Han Duck-soo, 50 years of public service and honors were reduced to ash in a single night of catastrophic decision-making. The moment he was taken into custody, it served as a grim reminder that history does not grant a permanent pardon to those in power. Our society is currently learning a simple but powerful truth: if you destroy the Constitution, you will pay the price.

What to Watch Next

As the legal dust begins to settle, several key developments remain on the horizon:

  • Final Appeals: Expect the defense to pursue every available avenue to reduce the sentence, potentially reaching the Supreme Court. The consistency of the lower court’s reasoning will be tested there.
  • Political Fallout: The conviction has sent shockwaves through the conservative political landscape. Watch for how the People Power Party (the main conservative party) attempts to distance itself from the architects of the December 3 incident to rehabilitate its image.
  • Legislative Reform: There is growing pressure to revise laws governing martial law and the powers of the President to prevent any future exploitation of these mechanisms.
  • Public Vigilance: The true test of South Korean democracy will be whether the public remains engaged in monitoring state powers or if the fatigue of political crisis leads to apathy. The verdict is a victory for the rule of law, but the "constant vigilance" required to maintain it has only just begun.
