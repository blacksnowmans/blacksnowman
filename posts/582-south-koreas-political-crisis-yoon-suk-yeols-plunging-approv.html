South Korea's Political Crisis: Yoon Suk Yeol's Plunging Approval and the Controversial Prosecutor-Turned-Reformer Pick

October 02, 2026

South Korea's political landscape is walking on thin ice. President Yoon Suk Yeol is watching his approval ratings hit historic lows day after day, with public disillusionment reaching a critical boiling point. Recent data from major pollsters like the National Barometer Survey (NBS) and Korea Information Research paint a grim picture for the administration: this is no longer a gentle downward slope, but a collapse of its core voting base—including those in their 30s, where negative sentiment has surged past 70%. Into this volatile mix, the administration dropped the nomination of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural head of the newly established Serious Crime Investigation Agency. Elevating a career prosecutor—someone from the very institution slated for sweeping reform—to lead the agency designed to rein in prosecutorial power has added fuel to an already blazing fire. How did Seoul reach this breaking point, and what does it mean for the future of Korean governance?

The Anatomy of a Crisis: Plunging Approval and the Backlash from Core Supporters

To understand the depth of the current crisis, one must look at the numbers. Polling data released by the research firm Choguk Flower shows a sharp 6.4 percentage point drop in party support, hitting a new low. Meanwhile, Korea Information Research places the president's approval rating at 37.0%, down 39 percentage points from the previous week, flashing bright red warning lights for the ruling bloc. Beyond the cold statistics, the palpable anger among voters in their 30s—nearly 70.7% of whom now believe the president is doing a "very poor job"—suggests a fracture that will be extraordinarily difficult to repair.

Public sentiment is unforgiving. With the administration's political momentum effectively drained, the Blue House's decision to push forward with a "frontal breakthrough" strategy has alarmed observers. Polls show that only 37.9% of respondents support Kim Ji-yong's nomination, while 53.6% oppose it—a number that climbs even higher in regional surveys, such as a dismal 21.4% approval rating recorded by the Honam Maeil Shinmun. Voters are sending a clear message: they will no longer tolerate unilateral, my-way-or-the-highway personnel policies.

This dynamic has trapped the government’s traditional supporters in a painful dilemma. It is akin to a beloved local restaurant earning a Michelin star, deciding to change its recipes in the name of health, and watching its long-time regulars plead in agony for the original taste to return. The dropping approval ratings are not just numbers; they are the cries of citizens worried about the community's direction. Yet, rather than listening, the administration has tended to label these expressions of anxiety as "obstruction of state affairs" or the work of opposition forces. By refusing democratic dialogue and dismissing internal criticism from ruling party members as groundless, the government is only driving a deeper wedge between itself and the public.

The Great Contradiction: Putting the Subject of Reform in Charge of It

The core absurdity of Kim Ji-yong's nomination lies in its inherent contradiction: placing an insider from the old guard at the helm of a new agency meant to dismantle old prosecutorial habits. Lawmaker Kim Yong-min of the main opposition Democratic Party offered a stark analogy, comparing the move to establishing a special committee to investigate pro-Japanese collaborators during colonial times, only to appoint a collaborator as its chairman. This is not mere rhetoric; it targets a substantive reality. During his active years in the prosecution, Kim Ji-yong was known for systematically obstructing prosecutorial reform and participating in collective actions to protect then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol.

Former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae added her voice to the criticism, noting that those who have experienced a system firsthand have a duty to warn others about its dangers. "If you enter a restaurant with a flashy sign, only to find the hygiene and ingredients appalling, you would never recommend it to anyone else," she remarked, capturing the public's bitter sentiment. The essence of reform is people. No matter how many times an organization is split or renamed, if the person sitting in the chair continues to practice old habits, the reform amounts to little more than a cosmetic name change.

Kim’s own resume raises profound questions about his suitability. In April 2022, as a senior prosecutor at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, he publicly opposed legislative efforts to strip prosecutors of their investigative powers—a policy often referred to as *geomsuwanbak*. Two years prior, in November 11, 2020, he joined 17 senior prosecutors in issuing a collective statement to block disciplinary action against then-Chief Yoon Suk Yeol, drawing fierce criticism for violating the State Public Officials Act regarding collective action. Expecting someone who fought tooth and nail to preserve prosecutorial power just a few years ago to suddenly champion reform defies logical deduction. If a person changes their fundamental convictions overnight to suit the political winds, how can they be trusted with the nation's most sensitive structural overhauls?

A Catalog of Allegations: Parliamentary Hearings as Mere Formalities

Beyond his ideological baggage, Kim faces a laundry list of specific controversies brought to light by opposition lawmakers like Yong Hye-in. These range from allegations that he attempted to cover up or distort an investigation involving a high-profile physical altercation linked to the Channel A media-prosecution scandal, to accusations regarding the controversial indictment of prosecutor Jeong Jin-woong. Most damning, perhaps, is his tenure in 2018 as the head of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office's 1st Inspection Division, where he faced accusations of covering up a sexual assault case involving a female junior prosecutor and the brother-in-law of a prominent political figure.

Another flashpoint involves the high-profile perjury and defamation allegations surrounding First Lady Kim Keon Hee’s mother, Choi Eun-soon. Although Kim ordered a reinvestigation in July 2021, the case was abruptly closed as "unfounded without indictment" just four months later—a decision that requires high-level approval from the Supreme Prosecutors' Office. Critics argue this reinvestigation was merely a pre-arranged ticket to exoneration. Entrusting such a figure with the nation's new anti-corruption investigative body feels, to many, like leaving the cat in charge of the cream.

The government defends its choice through the lens of realism, arguing that only a seasoned veteran of the prosecution possesses the expertise required to establish and stabilize a new investigative agency. But critics dismiss this as a cowardly excuse that misses the entire point of reform. The Serious Crime Investigation Agency was created precisely to cut ties with the prosecution's legacy of political bias and overreach. Filling it with officials steeped in the old prosecutorial DNA is not reform; it is political patronage rewarded to loyalists.

What to Watch Next

As this political drama unfolds, several key milestones will determine whether the administration can weather the storm or sink deeper into a terminal decline:

  • The Parliamentary Confirmation Hearings: Watch closely for how opposition lawmakers press Kim on his past involvement in high-profile cover-up allegations and whether the ruling party attempts to shield him from intense scrutiny.
  • Public Opinion Trends Post-Hearing: Monitor subsequent polls from NBS and other major research firms to see if public outrage translates into an even steeper drop in presidential support, which could trigger broader calls for a cabinet reshuffle.
  • Ruling Party Internal Dissent: Pay attention to whether moderate or reform-minded members of the conservative People Power Party begin to publicly break with the presidential office over the nomination, signaling a fracturing of the legislative safety net.
  • The Administration's Next Move: Observe whether the Blue House doubles down on its "my-way" approach or quietly withdraws the nomination in an effort to stabilize a rapidly accelerating lame-duck phase.

Ultimately, South Korea's struggle over the Serious Crime Investigation Agency is a test of whether democratic institutions can transcend the shadows of their past. If reform becomes merely a process of trading one set of political elites for another under a new banner, the public's exhaustion will only deepen. For now, all eyes remain on Seoul, waiting to see if the administration will listen to the rising chorus of public warning before it is too late.
