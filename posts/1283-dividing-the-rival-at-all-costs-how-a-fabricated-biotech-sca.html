Dividing the Rival at All Costs: How a Fabricated Biotech Scandal Exposed the Mechanics of Korean Political Warfare

October 18, 2026

In June 2019, a former lawmaker stood before an audience in Seoul and offered a remarkably candid lesson on how to win power in modern South Korea. Electoral triumph, she explained, is rarely about offering superior policies or comforting the public in troubled times. Instead, it boils down to crude political framing: consolidate your own base while relentlessly driving wedges into the rival camp until it shatters from within. The politician was Lee Un-ju, then an independent voice migrating into the conservative mainstream, and her explicit doctrine was simple: divide the opponent, reap the windfall.

To foreign observers, South Korean political discourse often appears intensely polarized, characterized by sudden online outrage cycles, hyperbolic corruption allegations, and dramatic reversals. Yet this volatility is not accidental. It is frequently the deliberate product of political engineering known locally as galachigi—the calculated tactic of carving up the electorate and opposing coalitions along ideological, generational, or factional fault lines. Just months after outlining her strategy, Lee put it into practice by seizing on one of the most painful financial catastrophes of that year: the collapse of the biotech darling SillaJen. What followed was a textbook case of how unverified political claims are weaponized, debunked by rigorous journalism, and then quietly scrubbed when accountability comes due.

The Anatomy of Engineering: "Unite Our Side, Fracture Theirs"

To understand the dynamics at play, one must look at the climate of mid-2019. The progressive administration of then-President Moon Jae-in was grappling with mounting economic anxieties and high-stakes diplomacy, while South Korea's conservative opposition was seeking an aggressive comeback after the impeachment of former President Park Geun-hye. Inside conservative speaking circuits, the discussion centered not on structural welfare or economic modernization, but on how to construct a hostile narrative architecture capable of tearing apart the progressive voting bloc.

Speaking at a lecture in June 2019, Lee Un-ju laid bare this tactical blueprint. She declared that electoral politics is fundamentally about structural setup rather than persuasion, stating flatly that "you must continuously divide the other side." The underlying theory was that by presenting one's own partisan narrative as absolute truth, one could bind conservative voters together while sowing suspicion within the progressive camp. If internal fractures could be induced among the ruling figures of the Democratic Party of Korea—such as party leaders Kim Min-seok and Lee Hae-chan—the opposition could capture an easy victory off the rebounds.

This approach treated the democratic public sphere not as a shared deliberative space, but as a battlefield where dialogue was an obstacle. When an opponent is cast not as a legitimate interlocutor but as an enemy to be splintered and dismantled, the space for cross-party compromise vanishes. For young South Koreans enduring job shortages, skyrocketing living expenses, and housing insecurity, politics was supposed to act as a vital safety net. Instead, political elites were preoccupied with calculating how to leverage societal grievances for immediate factional gain.

The SillaJen Conspiracy: Turning Financial Despair into a Partisan Weapon

The practical test of this divisive playbook arrived in autumn 2019, amidst the sudden implosion of SillaJen, a prominent bio-pharmaceutical company traded on the Kosdaq (South Korea's tech-heavy stock exchange). Thousands of retail investors—often referred to as "ants" in the Korean market—saw their life savings evaporate overnight after independent safety monitors recommended halting the Phase 3 clinical trial of Pexa-Vec, the company's flagship oncolytic virus therapy. The stock plunged into freefall, creating widespread public distress.

Rather than examining the systemic risks of the biotech bubble or the lax oversight of technology-tier listings, Lee intervened by launching an explosive narrative. She branded SillaJen a "Moon Jae-in beneficiary stock," insinuating that senior officials in the progressive government had illicit ties to the company and had enriched themselves while ordinary citizens suffered. Rumors were spun to entangle prominent progressive cultural and political figures, including progressive writer and former health minister Rhyu Si-min, transforming a complex corporate drug-trial failure into a purported state-level corruption ring.

The allegation spread rapidly through right-wing YouTube channels, message boards, and social media feeds. It offered an emotionally satisfying, conspiratorial explanation: everyday investors had not lost money because of the inherent, high-risk nature of clinical biotech research, but because high-ranking politicians had supposedly orchestrated a scheme to fleece them. In treating market instability as partisan fuel, the narrative bypassed substantive policy debate—such as reviewing bank lending procedures by major institutions like Shinhan Bank or strengthening regulatory standards—in favor of outright vilification.

The Cold Light of Data and the Disappearing Facebook Post

The lifespan of the conspiracy, however, proved short-lived when subjected to rigorous scrutiny. In October 2019, the financial news outlet Edaily published an exhaustive investigative report entitled "Fact Check: Examining Lee Un-ju’s Claim That SillaJen Was a Moon Jae-in Beneficiary Stock." The reporters cross-referenced regulatory filings, corporate registries, and executive timelines against the accusations.

The findings dismantled the political talking points point by point:

  • The Timing of the Listing: SillaJen entered the Kosdaq through a special fast-track listing for technology ventures in December 2016, during the conservative administration of Park Geun-hye—not under Moon Jae-in.
  • Lack of Policy Favoritism: The capital infusions, state research support programs, and major structural decisions that propelled SillaJen occurred during the late stages of conservative rule, framed under conservative-era biotech promotion initiatives.
  • Absence of Financial Links: Financial review agencies found no corroborating administrative or financial evidence linking the Moon administration to executive actions or privileged share trading prior to the disastrous August 2019 clinical halt recommendation.

The investigation showed that the claim of special favors under Moon was entirely unfounded. When presented with concrete public records, the high-stakes narrative collapsed into an unsubstantiated internet rumor.

The reaction from the lawmaker who initiated the controversy was telling. Faced with undeniable factual proof, there was no public clarification, no policy counter-argument, and no apology to either the accused figures or the confused public. Instead, the inflammatory post was quietly and swiftly deleted from her official Facebook page. This silent retreat highlighted a recurring pathology in digital-era political warfare: generate maximum viral noise to polarize the electorate, and when caught fabricating reality, delete the evidence and offer total silence.

From Polarization to Realpolitik: What Lies Ahead for Korean Public Discourse

The lessons of the 2019 SillaJen controversy resonate far beyond that single news cycle. They reveal the direct hazards of treating governance purely as a game of wedge politics. When political movements prioritize factional warfare over verifiable truth, the broader political culture pays a devastating price.

South Korea experienced the extreme conclusion of this divisive politics during the turbulent term of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. His governance, defined by aggressive hostility toward political rivals and institutional norms, culminated in widespread public backlash, his historic impeachment, and his subsequent removal from power. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol now remains imprisoned, standing as a stark historical warning against the perils of running a country through permanent partisan conflict and institutional overreach.

Today, under President Lee Jae-myung and the governing Democratic Party of Korea, the country faces a different landscape. The current administration has pivoted toward social safety net expansion and domestic economic restoration, while local leaders such as Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae concentrate on tangible administrative outcomes across regional governments. Concurrently, the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, continues to seek a renewed identity that can balance robust legislative critique with civic credibility.

For international watchers trying to gauge where South Korean politics is heading next, the crucial indicators will not be found in sensational social media blasts, but in several structural battlegrounds:

  • Accountability in the Information Space: Whether Korean news organizations continue to invest in granular, timeline-based fact-checking that forces high-profile politicians to defend their claims rather than quietly erasing their digital tracks.
  • The Opposition's Policy Pivot: Whether the conservative People Power Party abandons the reflexive instinct to run wedge campaigns, opting instead to challenge President Lee Jae-myung on regulatory, labor, and fiscal alternatives.
  • Voter Literacy and Market Prudence: South Korean retail investors, who increasingly look to data-driven platforms for transparency, are becoming far less receptive to politicians who attempt to exploit corporate stock volatility for partisan point-scoring.

As South Korea navigates thorny challenges surrounding demographic shifts, structural pension reform, and industrial transformation, the margin for error has narrowed. Political victories engineered by tearing society apart produce fragile governments and corroded institutions. If the country is to sustain its vibrant democracy, its public square must reward those who build policy on verified facts rather than those who treat societal division as an electoral shortcut.
