The 821 Trillion Won Battle: Why Korea’s Post-Audit Politics is Shifting to the Purse and the Past

October 31, 2026

The shouting matches have barely subsided in the halls of the National Assembly, but the silence is far from peaceful. As the annual National Audit—a month-long marathon where lawmakers scrutinize every corner of the government—came to a close on October 30, the atmosphere in Seoul shifted from investigative theater to a high-stakes fiscal war. The prize is a record-breaking 821 trillion won (about $600 billion) budget, and the combatants are more polarized than ever.

Inside the committee rooms, the remnants of the audit still smoldered. The People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, was still reeling from the absence of key witnesses. Names like Kim Hyeon-ji and Jeong Jin-sang—close associates of President Lee Jae-myung—dominated the discourse, even in their absence. For the opposition, these figures represent a "shield" protecting the administration from accountability. For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DP), the audit was a successful defense of their "pro-people" agenda against what they call political character assassination.

But as the dust settles on the audit, three major storylines are emerging that will define the Korean political landscape for the remainder of the year: a massive budgetary clash, a strategic realignment within the conservative opposition, and a painful reopening of historical wounds that many thought had finally begun to heal. These are not merely administrative squabbles; they are battles over the very identity of the Korean state under the Lee Jae-myung administration.

The $600 Billion Question: Fiscal Responsibility vs. Populism

The transition from the audit to the budget season was instantaneous. The Lee Jae-myung administration has proposed a "super budget" of 821 trillion won (about $600 billion). To put this in perspective, this represents a significant expansionary stance aimed at fueling the President's signature social welfare and economic revitalization programs. However, the opposition sees it through a much darker lens.

Jeong Jeom-sig, the Floor Leader of the People Power Party, wasted no time in framing the debate. He called for President Lee to "declare a divorce from populism." The PPP has signaled its intent to slash at least 134 trillion won (about $97 billion) from the proposal, targeting what they call "wasteful expansion." The core of the conflict lies in the "Future Response Fund," a 200 trillion won (about $145 billion) initiative that the government claims is necessary for long-term national growth, but the opposition labels as a slush fund for political projects.

This is a classic ideological divide, but with higher stakes. The DP argues that in an era of high interest rates and global instability, the government must act as the spender of last resort to protect the vulnerable. The PPP, meanwhile, warns of "fiscal sclerosis," arguing that the debt being piled up today will be a crushing burden for the next generation. They argue that "budgetary integrity" has been discarded in favor of short-term political gains. For the average resident in Korea, this isn't just theory—it’s about whether subsidies for small businesses or local heating credits will survive the legislative meat-grinder.

Han Dong-hoon’s Gambit: Building a "Reason-Based" Alliance

While the budget battle rages in the foreground, a quieter but equally significant movement is happening within the opposition. Han Dong-hoon, the leader of the People Power Party, is attempting to redraw the map of Korean conservatism. Han, a former elite prosecutor, has recently begun signaling a "big tent" strategy that could include some unlikely allies.

The buzzword in Seoul is "alliance based on reason." Han has been making overtures to Oh Se-hoon, the influential Mayor of Seoul, and has even left the door ajar for figures like Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party. This is a strategic necessity. Since the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the conservative wing has been fractured and demoralized. Han’s goal is to unite the "Non-Yoon" conservatives and centrists under a single banner to oppose the Lee Jae-myung administration’s dominant legislative power.

"I will work with anyone, as long as our reasons for doing so are the same," Han recently stated.

This approach is a departure from the traditional factional politics of the past. Instead of loyalty to a specific person, Han is trying to pivot to "cause-based" politics—specifically, the cause of checking the power of the Democratic Party. However, this path is fraught with landmines. Ahn Cheol-soo, a veteran politician and current PPP lawmaker, has already warned against "bundle deals" for returning members, signaling that the internal struggle for leadership within the opposition is far from over. Whether Han can actually build this coalition or if it remains a "mirage of unity" will determine if the opposition can mount a serious challenge in the next election cycle.

The Rebellion of the Local Councils

While the heavy hitters in Seoul argue over national policy, a grassroots political movement is gaining steam. On October 30, representatives from local councils in Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Busan—all from the People Power Party—gathered to demand the enactment of the "Local Council Act."

To an outsider, this might sound like dry administrative reform, but it is actually a fight for the survival of local democracy. Currently, local councils in Korea are structurally subservient to the heads of their local governments (mayors and governors). They lack independent authority over their own budgets and personnel. In effect, the people who are supposed to monitor the mayor are often dependent on that same mayor for their office's funding.

The call for a Local Council Act is a demand for "real" decentralization. Leaders like Kim Gil-young of the Seoul Metropolitan Council and Bang Seong-hwan of the Gyeonggi Provincial Council are arguing that without independent power, they are "handcuffed" in their ability to serve residents. This is one of the few areas where bipartisan consensus is actually possible; even in the DP-heavy Gyeonggi council, there is strong support for this independence. It represents a rare moment where local interests are attempting to break the stranglehold of Seoul-centric, top-down governance.

Reopening the Wounds of Jeju 4.3

Perhaps the most disturbing development on October 30 was the reignition of a historical controversy that many hoped had been settled. An Chang-ho, the chairperson of the National Human Rights Commission of Korea, sparked outrage with comments regarding the Jeju 4.3 Incident.

The Jeju 4.3 Incident (1947–1954) was a period of sustained violence on Jeju Island where thousands of civilians were killed by state security forces. For decades, the truth was suppressed, but recent administrations have made significant strides in apologizing to victims and providing reparations. However, An suggested that there needs to be further "investigation" into whether North Korea was behind the uprising, a narrative frequently used by the far-right to justify the original massacre.

The backlash was immediate. Victims' groups and local leaders in Jeju, including Governor Oh Young-hun, condemned the remarks as "far-right YouTube-level historical perception." For a person in charge of national human rights to question the established facts of a state-perpetrated tragedy is seen by many as a dangerous regression. It highlights a growing trend where public officials are increasingly comfortable airing views that challenge the democratic consensus built over the last thirty years.

What to Watch Next

As Korea moves into the final two months of the year, the political calendar is packed. Here is what to keep an eye on:

  • The Budget Deadline: The statutory deadline for passing the budget is December 2. Watch for whether the PPP can actually force the 134 trillion won in cuts they’ve promised, or if the DP uses its legislative majority to push the 821 trillion won package through largely intact.
  • The "Han-Oh" Alliance: Watch for more public appearances between Han Dong-hoon and Oh Se-hoon. If they begin to coordinate on specific policy critiques of the Lee Jae-myung government, it signals that a formal "New Conservative" coalition is truly forming.
  • The Human Rights Commission Standoff: There will likely be mounting pressure for An Chang-ho to resign or clarify his remarks. How the Lee Jae-myung administration handles this will be a litmus test for how they intend to defend historical truths against revisionist pressure.
  • Local Council Legislation: Keep an eye on whether the "Local Council Act" gains any traction in the National Assembly. If it does, it could be the most significant shift in Korean local governance in a generation.

South Korean politics is often a theater of the loud, but beneath the noise of the National Audit's end, the structural foundations of the country—its money, its local power, and its history—are being contested. For those watching from outside, the next few weeks will reveal much about the durability of Korea's institutions under the current administration.
