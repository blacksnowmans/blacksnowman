Inside the Crisis of South Korea's Democratic Party: Factionalism, Pride, and the Perils of Unquestioning Loyalty

October 16, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is increasingly suffocated by a familiar and toxic swamp: hyper-partisan camp logic. Within the Democratic Party of Korea—the country’s primary liberal party—a bitter internal fracture is growing. What appears on the surface to be ordinary political squabbling actually reveals a profound moral and institutional crisis. The core principles that generations of progressives fought to establish are being steadily eroded from within.

Since the inauguration of President Lee Jae-myung, a pervasive mantra has taken hold among his most fervent supporters: "We must protect the President." This demand is frequently deployed as a universal shield to deflect any and all criticism. Yet, in the rush to defend the executive, a fundamental question has been lost: Are supporters defending the human being in the Blue House, or are they defending the democratic values that the office is supposed to represent?

The Echo Chamber of Partisan Loyalty

Within the progressive camp, self-identified partisans have turned unconditional support into a litmus test for purity. Genuine passion for protecting a leader is understandable in volatile political climates, but when reasonable doubt and constructive criticism are instantly labeled as "betrayal" or "hostile acts," the political movement loses its compass. Paradoxically, the forces driving President Lee into a corner may not be his external opponents, but rather the radical factions inside his own base who have blinded him to reality with relentless, uncritical adulation.

As partisan camp logic solidifies, power inevitably closes its ears to dissenting voices. When the slogan "protect the President" becomes a tool to rationalize executive missteps rather than correct them, the public bears the cost. Real political support means steering a administration back to safe ground when it wanders off-course, not applauding as it marches toward a cliff.

This dynamic is amplified by prominent progressive media figures—often called "big speakers"—who frame complex national debates through a crude, black-and-white lens, casting disputes as battles between "independence fighters and pro-Japanese collaborators." Politics is inherently gray, requiring constant negotiation, compromise, and friction. Reducing state affairs to an existential battle between absolute good and absolute evil makes dialogue impossible. While such extreme framing may temporarily rally the base, it accelerates the alienation of moderate voters and deepens the toxic hatred dividing the country.

Opaque Administrations and the Arrogance of Power

Recent controversies surrounding government appointments—most notably the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as head of the Investigation Bureau—have inflicted serious wounds on the Lee Jae-myung administration's moral credibility. When it became blatantly obvious that the presidential office had misled the public during the vetting process, there were no apologies or attempts to conceal the fallout; instead, the administration maintained a defiant posture. For many observers, the realization that the administration acts as though it is entirely unaccountable for being caught in a falsehood is far more frightening than a routine vetting failure.

Personnel choices are the ultimate indicator of an administration's governing philosophy. By forcing through controversial appointments and dismissing valid criticisms as partisan attacks, the Blue House has mirrored the exact heavy-handed tactics it once condemned in conservative administrations.

President Lee himself appears dangerously disconnected from public sentiment. Rather than addressing the heavy socio-economic burdens weighing on ordinary citizens, the administration frequently retreats into its own echo chamber, celebrating modest or dubious policy steps as "historic achievements." When an administration loses the capacity for self-reflection, governance devolves into enforced consensus. True communication requires acknowledging shortcomings and empathizing with public anxiety, not demanding applause for numbers that do not match the lived reality of working citizens.

The Erosion of Progressive Heritage

The golden eras of past liberal governments—under former Presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in—were defined not by unchecked power, but by a distinct ethos of political humility. Kim Dae-jung practiced politics of national integration, even forgiving those who had previously sentenced him to death. Roh Moo-hyun actively dismantled authoritarian structures by lowering himself before the public. Moon Jae-in exercised immense self-restraint to preserve procedural justice.

Their humility was never a mere strategic posture; it stemmed from a deep-seated respect for the democratic system and a healthy fear of the sovereign electorate. While past administrations occasionally struggled against hostile media landscapes and sought to publicize their genuine accomplishments, their efforts were anchored in a restrained, sober approach to authority.

Today, that aesthetic of restraint has entirely vanished. In its place stands an assertive, performance-obsessed governance that revels in displaying power, mocking critics, and treating every policy outcome as a monument to the President's personal capability. President Lee's strong narcissistic streak is increasingly evident in his laser-like focus on self-vindication. Where past leaders pursued institutional reforms—such as early efforts to reform the prosecution service—to establish systemic fairness, current initiatives frequently bear the heavy taint of protecting personal and political risks.

Distorted Reforms and the Dilemma of Influential Voices

Prosecution reform has long been a sacred mission for the Democratic Party. However, under the current administration, the push to overhaul the prosecution service has been severely distorted. Observers note that the blatant exercise of executive muscle currently on display exceeds anything attempted even during the tenures of jailed former leader jailed during corruption scandals, and far surpasses the methods used during the era of disgraced former leader jailed following his impeachment, Yoon Suk-yeol. When power is consolidated to crush political rivals or shield allies from scrutiny, reform degrades into regression.

Influential progressive commentators and media operators now face a momentous crossroads. For years, they acted as the vanguard defending progressive values against conservative crackdowns. But now that President Lee’s actions frequently clash with the foundational principles of the liberal movement, these media figures must decide whether they will remain independent monitors or devolve into unconditional cheerleaders.

If the partisan loyalty championed by these figures simply demands that "our side is always right no matter what," it ceases to be democratic engagement and mutates into a form of political fundamentalism. History demonstrates that parties which abandon internal critique and internalize unquestioning obedience eventually rot from the inside out.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks ahead to upcoming local elections and parliamentary shifts, several concrete indicators will show whether the ruling party can correct its course:

    Internal Dissent: Watch whether dissenting lawmakers within the Democratic Party continue to be branded as traitors, or if the leadership begins to tolerate open debate over controversial personnel appointments and policy execution. Handling of State Audits and Investigations: Pay close attention to how the administration responds to independent inquiries into executive appointments and institutional overreach. A willingness to submit to transparency will signal a return to democratic norms. Shifts in Public-Facing Messaging: Monitor whether the Blue House pivots away from self-congratulatory claims of "historic achievements" and begins directly addressing inflation, housing pressures, and livelihood concerns.

The Democratic Party was built on the sweat and sacrifices of citizens who demanded justice, equality, and government accountability. If the party continues to prioritize raw survival and absolute power over procedural integrity, it risks becoming the very monster it once fought to depose. Restoring the party's future requires returning to an uncomfortable truth: power is only legitimate when it is willing to be questioned.
