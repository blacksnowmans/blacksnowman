Shuttle Diplomacy in the Shadow of Giants: The Lee-Takaichi Summit in Manila

November 18, 2026

In the humid, high-security corridors of a Manila convention center, the leaders of East Asia’s two most vital democracies met this week for a conversation that was as much about the future as it was about the immediate present. On November 17, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi sat down for their fourth summit since taking office. While the setting was the ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) summit, the subtext was far broader, stretching from the volatile energy markets of the Middle East to a high-stakes meeting of global superpowers scheduled just 48 hours later.

For those watching from outside the peninsula, the meeting marks a significant milestone in the stabilization of Seoul-Tokyo relations. Only a few years ago, the relationship was defined by trade wars and the dissolution of intelligence-sharing pacts. Today, under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—the former governor and leader of the Democratic Party of Korea who ascended to the presidency following the political upheavals of the previous era—the tone has shifted toward a pragmatic, if still cautious, alignment. This summit in Manila, occurring six months after their last meeting in the historic Korean city of Andong, suggests that "shuttle diplomacy"—the practice of regular, informal visits between leaders—is no longer a rare breakthrough, but a functioning routine.

The Strategic Buffer Against a Tripolar World

The most critical aspect of the Manila summit was not what was on the agenda, but when it took place. The meeting occurred exactly two days before a scheduled summit between the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia. For South Korea and Japan, the prospect of the world’s three greatest powers negotiating the future of global security is a moment of profound anxiety. There is a persistent fear in both Seoul and Tokyo of being "passed over"—of having the security architecture of Northeast Asia decided by Washington, Beijing, and Moscow without their input.

During the summit, President Lee and Prime Minister Takaichi emphasized the need for "strategic communication." In diplomatic parlance, this is a signal to the world that Korea and Japan are moving in lockstep. The leaders discussed the "Trump variable"—the potential for a shift in U.S. foreign policy that could lead to direct, unpredictable dialogues between Washington and Pyongyang. For Japan, a hawkish stance on North Korea is a domestic necessity; for President Lee, maintaining a leading role in any peninsular peace process is a core mandate of his administration. By meeting now, they have attempted to create a united front, ensuring that whatever emerges from the U.S.-China-Russia talks, the interests of the two primary regional anchors are not ignored.

Prime Minister Takaichi also brought the "Quad" into the conversation—the security dialogue between the U.S., Japan, Australia, and India. While South Korea is not a formal member, the mention of the Quad’s "tangible achievements" in a bilateral meeting with President Lee suggests a deepening of Korea’s informal association with the group’s regional stability goals. For a foreign observer, this signals that the Lee administration is successfully balancing its traditional Democratic Party focus on autonomy with a very modern need for multilateral security umbrellas.

Securing the Lifeblood of Industry: Energy and Trade

Beyond the high-altitude talk of geopolitics, the summit addressed the literal fuel that powers the Korean and Japanese economies. Both nations are "energy islands"—highly industrialized, export-driven economies with almost no domestic fossil fuel reserves. With instability mounting in the Middle East, the two leaders agreed to move toward a joint response to energy crises.

One of the more intriguing proposals discussed was the "Asian version of the IEA" (International Energy Agency). Currently, the IEA serves as a coordinator for Western energy security, but a regional body focused on the specific needs of Asian importers could revolutionize how Seoul and Tokyo manage their strategic reserves. Cooperation here isn't just about altruism; it’s about market power. If the world’s two largest LNG (liquefied natural gas) importers coordinate their buying and stockpiling, they can effectively hedge against the price volatility that threatens their manufacturing sectors.

On the trade front, the shadow of the CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership) loomed large. Japan is a leading member of this massive trade bloc, and South Korea has long been weighing the costs and benefits of joining. While no final deal was signed in Manila, the fact that it remains a central pillar of the conversation indicates that the Lee administration sees economic integration with Japan as a necessary tool to diversify away from an over-reliance on the Chinese market. For global investors, this signals a more predictable and integrated East Asian supply chain, reducing the "geopolitical risk premium" that often attaches to Korean and Japanese equities.

The Lingering Ghosts and a Record High in Public Favor

Perhaps the most surprising takeaway from the summit was President Lee’s remark that public favorability between the two nations has reached "all-time highs." To an outsider, this might seem counterintuitive given the decades of historical friction stemming from the colonial era. However, it reflects a generational shift. In both countries, younger citizens are increasingly bonded by shared pop culture and a common concern over regional authoritarianism, even if the older political guard remains divided over history.

Yet, the summit was not without its "difficult problems"—a phrase President Lee used explicitly to signal that historical grievances and territorial disputes have not vanished. The limits of this "new era" were starkly highlighted on the very day of the summit by a domestic controversy. Cho Kuk, the former Justice Minister and a prominent political figure in Korea, was reportedly denied entry into Japan. While the Japanese government maintains its right to control its borders, the timing of the denial—coinciding with a summit intended to celebrate "shuttle diplomacy"—served as a cold reminder that the bureaucratic and political machinery of the two nations still grinds against one another.

"While we celebrate the brightening of our relationship, we must also acknowledge that 'difficult problems' require tangible progress, not just warm words," President Lee noted during the session, according to several Korean news outlets.

This duality is the hallmark of the current Korea-Japan relationship: a high-level strategic embrace coupled with a ground-level reality that remains sensitive to the slightest political friction. The Lee administration is betting that by focusing on shared existential threats—like energy security and the shifting "Great Power" dynamics—the two nations can build enough "trust equity" to eventually tackle the more emotional, historical disputes that have derailed previous attempts at rapprochement.

What to Watch Next

The Manila summit was a prelude to a much busier diplomatic calendar. As we look toward the end of the year and the start of 2025, several key indicators will determine if this momentum is sustainable:

  • The January Summit in Tokyo: President Lee has accepted an invitation to visit Japan in January. This will be the fifth meeting between the two leaders and will likely focus on the concrete implementation of the energy security pacts discussed in Manila. Watch for specific agreements on "LNG swaps" or shared storage facilities.
  • The Great Power Fallout: The results of the upcoming U.S.-China-Russia summit will dictate the next moves for Seoul and Tokyo. If that summit yields a new grand bargain that excludes middle-power interests, expect Korea and Japan to accelerate their bilateral security cooperation even further.
  • The "Difficult Problems" Working Groups: Watch for whether the two governments establish low-level working groups to handle the "difficult problems" mentioned by President Lee. If these issues are left to fester without a process, they could easily be triggered by a single domestic political event in either country.
  • Trade Milestones: Any movement on South Korea’s formal application to the CPTPP will be a major signal of the Lee administration’s long-term economic strategy and its willingness to align with the Japanese-led trade order.

The Manila summit proved that the Lee-Takaichi partnership is currently the most stable pillar of Northeast Asian diplomacy. In a world where the three largest powers are increasingly at odds, the two neighbors have decided that, for now, they are better off facing the storm together.
