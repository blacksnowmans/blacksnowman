Beyond Policy Success: Why South Korea’s Ruling Alliance is Losing Public Trust

October 30, 2026

Step inside Seoul’s political theater today, and you will see a ruling administration driving hard at ambitious reform goals, yet finding itself bogged down by a public that is growing increasingly skeptical. When the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea’s ruling liberal party, took office under President Lee Jae-myung, it did so with substantial momentum. Public approval once reached nearly 70 percent in major polls like the National Barometer Survey (NBS). Today, however, that honeymoon has given way to a razor-thin margin: a recent survey by the Korea Society Opinion Institute (KSOI) showed positive approval slipping to 47.8 percent against a negative rating of 46.8 percent, driven by a sharp drop among voters aged 18 to 29, whose approval fell to just 26.7 percent.

To understand why this downward curve matters, one must look beyond standard economic headwinds like stubborn inflation, stock market volatility, or generational spats over housing policy. The core issue facing the administration is not simply whether its policies succeed or fail on paper. Rather, it is a broader erosion of public trust in the leadership itself. When voters lose faith in the personal integrity, consistency, and fairness of those at the top, even generous state handouts and bold policy promises begin to feel less like genuine benefits and more like political coercion.

The Anatomy of Fading Trust: Comparing Past and Present Leadership

In South Korean presidential politics, stable governance relies on three primary pillars: personal trust in the leader as an individual, consistent execution of campaign pledges, and visible fairness in key political appointments and budget distributions. When all three engines sputter at once, approval ratings inevitably stall.

To grasp why today's rating dip feels so precarious, foreign observers must understand how South Koreans evaluate their leaders emotionally. Historically, progressive presidents like Roh Moo-hyun managed to weather severe political storms and plunging approval ratings because they retained a deep, baseline affection from their base. Citizens believed in Roh’s fundamental sincerity. Even when his policies faced heavy backlash, that core emotional bond allowed his supporters to rally behind him during major political turning points.

A similar dynamic was evident during the presidency of Moon Jae-in. Despite intense criticism over rising real estate prices, legislative bottlenecks, and slow reform progress, Moon left office with an unprecedented 45 percent approval rating. His supporters maintained a sympathetic view: even if the policy results fell short, they believed Moon was an honest actor who tried his best against difficult structural odds.

The current landscape under President Lee Jae-myung presents a sharp contrast. While the administration projects legislative strength and aggressive reform, critics argue that a mix of unmanaged internal friction, abrasive rhetoric, and unpredictable communication is eroding the emotional trust of everyday citizens. When baseline trust breaks down, policy initiatives lose their persuasive power. As former progressive lawmaker Choe Kang-wook recently noted in a broadcast discussion with Social Democratic Party lawmaker Han Chang-min, providing tangible policy benefits to a public that does not trust you backfires; voters interpret those benefits not as goodwill, but as calculated political ploys.

A prime example of this disconnect is the long-stalled relocation of the Gwangju military airport to Muan County in South Jeolla Province. President Lee publicly pledged to resolve the issue quickly, even calling for rapid execution during regional town hall meetings. Yet, on the ground, execution clashed with central promises. In Muan’s Mangun township, business-minded residents favored the move due to expectations of industrial park designations and new high-speed rail connections. However, nearby residents in new urban developments like Namak and Oryong fiercely protested, citing jet noise pollution and lingering resentment over failed bids to secure a regional medical university. When government assurances yield local chaos and shouting matches at traditional five-day markets, voters come to believe that high-level promises are detached from administrative reality.

Judicial Skepticism and the Weight of Personal Risk

Compounding the administration’s trust deficit is a growing public cynicism toward South Korea's judicial and political systems. Recent court rulings have provoked anger across the electorate, raising troubling questions about double standards for the powerful.

Public outrage flared following a first-instance acquittal for military personnel involved in a controversial meeting at a Lotteria fast-food restaurant during the martial law crisis under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the impeached former president who is now imprisoned. For many citizens, watching the courts clear key figures involved in that crisis created deep unease about whether constitutional subversion is being properly addressed by state institutions.

Skepticism deepened further after a court acquitted Kim Gi-hyeon, a prominent figure in the main conservative opposition People Power Party, over allegations involving a luxury Roger Vivier handbag tied to lobbying scandals during the previous administration. While the politician himself was cleared on grounds that he was unaware of the exchange, his spouse was handed a minor fine of 5 million won (about $3,600). To the public, such verdicts look like a legal blueprint for political corruption: as long as a spouse receives the gift and a third party pays, the primary political figure walks away unscathed.

Yet, while public frustration with the judiciary runs high, the ruling Democratic Party’s parliamentary maneuvers have often appeared hesitant and overly calculated. Rather than moving decisively through National Assembly audits and impeachment measures to restore constitutional norms, legislative leaders are frequently seen as weighing immediate election math over systemic justice.

At the same time, the administration’s aggressive battles with judicial authorities are heavily influenced by President Lee’s own unresolved legal troubles. Personal judicial risks carried over from his pre-presidential career continue to shadow his term. As political analysts observe, prolonged legal vulnerability can create a feedback loop where leaders seek ever-greater executive control to protect themselves. When an administration demonizes the judicial system as a whole to counter its own legal vulnerabilities, it risks damaging the very constitutional checks and balances it promised to defend.

Coalition Friction and the Fight Over Electoral Reform

Tensions are also building within the broader progressive movement. Under senior figure Kim Min-seok, the Democratic Party launched a "Grand Coalition Promotion Committee" headed by lawmaker Park Beom-kye, alongside lawmakers Jin Sung-joon, Lee Hae-sik, Hwang Myeong-seon, and Lee So-young. Its stated purpose was to unify the center-left and broaden the party's reach.

In practice, however, the initiative has created friction with smaller political allies. Rather than fostering an open alliance among the five progressive parties—the Democratic Party, the Rebuilding Korea Party led by Cho Kuk, the Progressive Party, the Basic Income Party, and the Social Democratic Party—the ruling party has engaged in behind-the-scenes merger talks aimed at absorbing Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party. Minor allies complain that this strategy breaks the trust of the wider coalition, treating minor parties as targets for absorption rather than equal partners in governance.

This coalition tension underscores a larger structural problem in South Korean politics: the rigid duopoly between the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party. Just as economic monopolies harm consumers, a political duopoly forces voters into binary choices, stifling policy innovation and driving polarization.

To break this duopoly, reform advocates argue for two critical institutional changes:

  • Two-Round Runoff Voting: Implementing a runoff system (결선투표제) for presidential and local executive elections. Currently, voters often cast defensive votes for major party candidates out of fear of splitting the vote. A two-round system would allow citizens to support smaller parties in the first round, forcing major parties to build transparent, formal coalitions to win the runoff.
  • Lowering the Parliamentary Caucus Threshold: Reducing the requirement to form an official parliamentary negotiation group (원내교섭단체) from 20 seats down to 10 seats—a standard that existed prior to the authoritarian Yushin era. Granting 10-seat parties formal status would give smaller factions access to state subsidies, committee leadership roles, and agenda-setting powers, creating space for moderate, reform-minded caucuses on both the left and right to act independently of extreme partisan bases.

Unfiltered Social Media and the Road Ahead

Perhaps the most visible symptom of the administration’s communication trouble is President Lee’s direct use of social media. The president’s habit of publishing unrefined, late-night posts on personal accounts has drawn criticism even from sympathetic scholars like Professor Han In-seop. Han outlined five major drawbacks to this direct messaging style:

  • It bypasses structured government communications, broadcasting raw executive frustration rather than thoughtful statecraft.
  • It escalates minor political disputes into national crises by forcing the highest power in the land to directly spar with critics.
  • Firing off multiple emotional posts daily degrades the institutional dignity and authority of the presidency.
  • It hazards stoking social division when posts inadvertently leverage online gender conflicts or toxic forum culture.
  • It risks alienating the core base while chasing volatile online subcultures—essentially losing loyal supporters in pursuit of short-term internet outrage.

When presidential aides act as passive managers rather than advisors willing to offer tough critiques, unscripted presidential messaging turns into a self-inflicted wound. Trying to engineer a political majority by courting reactionary internet forums while ignoring core progressive values undermines the very moral clarity that brought the administration to power.

What to Watch Next: Over the coming months, keep a close eye on whether the Democratic Party moves forward with lower negotiation group thresholds in the National Assembly, or if it continues to prioritize absorbing Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party. Additionally, monitor whether President Lee adjusts his direct social media strategy in response to falling youth poll numbers, and how the administration navigates upcoming judicial rulings that test both its legislative resolve and its commitment to institutional fairness.
