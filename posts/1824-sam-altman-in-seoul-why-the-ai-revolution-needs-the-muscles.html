Sam Altman in Seoul: Why the AI Revolution Needs the "Muscles" of Korean Hardware

November 02, 2026

The scene at the offices of South Korea’s tech giants this week felt less like a standard corporate visit and more like a high-stakes diplomatic summit. Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI and the man often dubbed the "father of ChatGPT," touched down in Seoul for a whirlwind 48-hour trip. While the global media often focuses on the ethereal world of algorithms and large language models (LLMs), Altman’s itinerary—which included a marathon meeting with 50 top executives from Samsung Electronics and a lunch with SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won—underscored a gritty reality: the AI revolution cannot happen without the physical "muscles" provided by South Korean manufacturing.

For an international observer, it might seem strange that the leader of a Silicon Valley software powerhouse would fly halfway across the world just to sit in a boardroom in Suwon or central Seoul. However, the logic is starkly pragmatic. As OpenAI attempts to scale its infrastructure to unprecedented heights, it has hit a physical wall. The software is ready, but the hardware—specifically the specialized memory and the foundries required to bake the chips—is in dangerously short supply. In this new era of "Artificial Intelligence Transformation" (AX), South Korea is no longer just a supplier; it is the gatekeeper of the physical infrastructure that will determine the winners and losers of the next decade.

The hardware bottleneck and the HBM scramble

To understand why Sam Altman is knocking on doors in Seoul, one must understand the current state of the global AI supply chain. For the past year, the industry has been dominated by Nvidia, the American chip designer whose Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) are the standard for training AI. But a GPU is useless without High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—a specialized, high-performance DRAM that allows data to flow into the processor at lightning speeds. Without HBM, even the fastest AI chip becomes a Ferrari stuck in a traffic jam.

Currently, the world’s supply of HBM is controlled almost entirely by two South Korean companies: SK Hynix, which currently leads the market and serves as a primary supplier to Nvidia, and Samsung Electronics, the world’s largest overall memory manufacturer. For Altman, whose OpenAI is reportedly seeking to raise trillions of dollars to build a global network of AI chip factories and data centers, securing a direct line to these two giants is not a luxury; it is a necessity for survival.

The urgency of the visit reflects a shift in the power balance. In the previous era of computing, software companies dictated terms to hardware manufacturers. Today, the roles are beginning to blur. Altman’s "surprise" visit suggests that even the most well-funded tech stars in the West are feeling the heat of the supply bottleneck. By engaging directly with the leadership of Samsung and SK, OpenAI is signaling its desire to reduce its total dependence on the Nvidia-led status quo and perhaps even co-develop proprietary "AI accelerators"—custom chips designed specifically for OpenAI’s workloads.

Samsung’s "AX" pivot and the 50-CEO summit

When Sam Altman arrived at Samsung Electronics, he wasn't met by a small team of engineers. Instead, he faced a phalanx of roughly 50 CEOs and presidents from across Samsung’s various divisions. This unprecedented gathering highlights how seriously Samsung is taking the AI threat—and the AI opportunity. For Samsung, the meeting wasn't just about selling more memory chips; it was about "AX," or AI Transformation.

Samsung is currently at a crossroads. While it remains a global leader, it faces intense pressure to integrate AI not just into its products—like the Galaxy smartphones—but into its very DNA. The company is looking to use AI to automate its massive semiconductor fabrication plants (fabs), optimize its supply chains, and revolutionize how its tens of thousands of employees work. During the meeting, Altman reportedly presented the executives with a "Codex Micro"—a symbolic gift representing OpenAI’s coding assistant capabilities—carrying a message that software and hardware must now be treated as a single, integrated organism.

"AI alone provides no competitive advantage," Altman reportedly warned the Samsung leadership. "The advantage comes from how quickly you can integrate and utilize AI across your entire operation."

This message is a sobering one for a traditional manufacturing giant. It suggests that being the best at making "things" is no longer enough. If Samsung cannot become the fastest at using AI to design those things, it risks becoming a "dumb" foundry for smarter companies. The presence of 50 top executives indicates that the message was received. Samsung is now moving beyond "Digital Transformation" (DX) and betting its future on becoming an AI-centric organization from the factory floor to the boardroom.

The SK alliance and the dream of custom AI chips

The second leg of Altman’s journey took him to a meeting with Chey Tae-won, the Chairman of SK Group and current head of the Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry (KCCI). While Samsung is a diversified giant, SK Group—specifically through SK Hynix—has become the darling of the AI world due to its early and aggressive lead in HBM technology.

The discussion between Altman and Chey reportedly moved beyond simple supply contracts. They discussed the future of AI data centers, which are becoming increasingly difficult to build due to their massive power requirements. This is where SK Group’s broader ecosystem comes into play. Beyond chips, SK has significant interests in energy, cooling systems, and construction—all the components needed to build the "AI fortresses" of the future.

The underlying subtext of these meetings is the desire of Big Tech companies like OpenAI, Microsoft, and Google to break the "Nvidia tax." By partnering directly with Samsung or SK, these companies could potentially design their own AI accelerators and have them manufactured in Korea, bypassing the astronomical costs and lead times associated with current market leaders. For Korea, this represents a golden opportunity to move from being a component supplier to a strategic partner in the very architecture of the global AI ecosystem.

Technology sovereignty in the post-Yoon era

This visit also carries significant political weight within South Korea. The Lee Jae-myung administration, representing the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party), has placed a heavy emphasis on "technology sovereignty" and state-backed support for the semiconductor industry. This is a marked departure from the previous administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment.

Critics of the previous era point to the significant cuts in R&D (Research and Development) budgets under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol as a moment of strategic weakness that almost cost Korea its lead in the AI race. The current government is working to repair that damage, viewing the semiconductor sector not just as an economic engine, but as a primary pillar of national security. When figures like Sam Altman come to Seoul, they aren't just visiting companies; they are interacting with a national infrastructure that is increasingly being shielded and promoted by the state.

However, a lingering question remains for many Koreans: will the country remain a "high-end subcontractor" for Silicon Valley? While it is lucrative to build the HBM for OpenAI, the real power and the highest profit margins lie in the software platforms and the AI models themselves. There is a growing sense of urgency that Korea must develop its own sovereign AI capabilities to ensure it isn't simply manufacturing the shovels for someone else's gold mine.

What to watch next

As the dust settles on Altman's 1-night, 2-day visit, the focus shifts to concrete outcomes. Here is what to watch for in the coming months:

  • The Samsung-OpenAI Foundry Deal: Watch for announcements regarding whether OpenAI will officially tap Samsung’s 2nm or 3nm foundry processes for its own custom-designed AI chips. This would be a massive blow to competitors and a huge win for Samsung’s contract manufacturing business.
  • HBM4 Roadmap: SK Hynix and Samsung are racing to develop the next generation of memory, HBM4. The technical specifications of these chips will be heavily influenced by the feedback Altman and his team provided during this visit.
  • The Energy Factor: Keep an eye on SK Group's energy divisions. If OpenAI and SK announce a joint venture in "green" AI data centers, it will signal a new phase of the partnership that goes far beyond semiconductors.
  • Government Incentives: The Lee Jae-myung administration is expected to announce further tax credits and infrastructure support for the "AI Semi-belt" south of Seoul, aimed at ensuring that visitors like Altman find it impossible to look anywhere else for their hardware needs.

Sam Altman’s visit has proven that while the AI "brain" may live in San Francisco, its "heart" and "muscles" are firmly beating in the industrial complexes of South Korea. The challenge for Korea now is to ensure that it isn't just providing the strength, but also the intelligence that guides it.
