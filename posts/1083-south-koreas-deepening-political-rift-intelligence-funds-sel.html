South Korea’s Deepening Political Rift: Intelligence Funds, Selective Justice, and the Crisis of Public Trust

October 13, 2026

Walking through the streets of Seoul or any major South Korean city right now, you are bound to hear the same underlying hum of frustration. Storefronts sit empty, business owners sigh over flat registers, and ordinary people wonder why a headline-grabbing economy on paper feels so exhausting on the ground. Yet, if you turn on the news or look at the political landscape, you would never know regular people were struggling. Instead of policy debates or relief measures, the airwaves are dominated by endless political warfare, mutual recriminations, and a brand of partisan hostility that treats governance like a blood sport.

The institutional rot left behind by the disgraced administration of former leader Yoon Seok-yeol—who was impeached and is now imprisoned—continues to weigh heavily on South Korean society. From alleged intelligence budget misappropriation to bending the judiciary and an unprecedented display of institutional hypocrisy, the country’s democratic foundations are facing a severe stress test. To understand why these scandals matter so deeply to outsiders watching South Korea, we have to look past the partisan shouting and examine how power, money, and the law have been mishandled.

The Misuse of State Power: Intelligence Funds and Selective Justice

At the center of the current reckoning is the alleged misuse of state funds under the previous conservative government. According to data revealed by lawmaker Yoon Kun-young, a special intelligence budget designated for the National Intelligence Service—South Korea's main intelligence and security agency—was treated less like a classified national security fund and more like an informal petty cash drawer.

Out of a roughly $130,000 (170 million won) fund nominally earmarked for an international influence operation between July 2022 and June 2023, only a tiny fraction actually went toward contacting foreigners. The rest leaked into domestic academic seminars, advisory fees for conservative figures, and even box lunches for internal security office meetings. Opposition critics argue this was a blatant violation of the National Financial Act and a misuse of public money to manage elite political networks. Yet, key figures like former First Deputy Director Kim Tae-hyo have brushed off accountability, claiming they cannot remember receiving the funds.

This impunity stands in stark contrast to the aggressive, dust-clearing investigations thrown at political opponents in the past. Take, for instance, the allegations surrounding the daughter of Han Dong-hoon, a prominent conservative figure and former Justice Minister. When civic groups filed complaints in May 2022 accusing her of academic ghostwriting, plagiarism, and inflated volunteer records, the police sat on the case for a year and a half without seeking a single search warrant. An international legal assistance request sent to U.S. authorities was reportedly blocked at the Seoul Central District Office before it even reached the Ministry of Justice—headed at the time by Han himself. Observers note the glaring double standard: critics face relentless prosecutorial pressure, while political elites use the legal system as a shield for their own families.

The Judiciary Under Fire: Delayed Justice and Compromised Courts

The erosion of public trust is not limited to investigative agencies; it extends deep into the court system. South Korea's Supreme Court has faced a prolonged vacancy crisis, leaving key positions unfilled for well over 200 days despite warnings from the Constitutional Court. This delay has paralyzed major deliberations and left a massive backlog of appeals unaddressed, directly harming citizens' constitutional right to a timely trial.

Meanwhile, the legal proceedings surrounding Yoon Seok-yeol—who faces a recommended sentence of one year and six months in prison on public election law violations—have triggered frantic political theater from the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party. Facing massive potential forfeiture of election subsidies, conservative politicians have leaned heavily on familiar playbooks of playing the political victim rather than addressing legal accountability.

The institutional crossover between politics and the judiciary has also drawn sharp scrutiny in cases involving high-profile financial scandals. Prosecutors who previously cleared Kim Keon-hee of stock manipulation charges in the Deutsch Motors case are now facing trial themselves. Yet, the judge assigned to oversee their case is none other than Judge Woo In-sung—the very jurist who previously acquitted Kim. Critics argue that splitting government misconduct into separate categories for decision-makers and low-level operatives is an effort to shield the upper echelons of power, further fueling public cynicism toward judicial independence.

Degraded Discourse and the Retreat of State Human Rights

As institutional checks fray, the tone of public discourse has hit a new low. During recent parliamentary audits, ruling and opposition politicians exchanged biting insults, with conservative figures leaning into aggressive rhetoric that critics argue drags South Korea's democratic culture backward. Instead of debating the nation’s future, much of the political class appears locked in a race to claim leadership of the conservative fringe by weaponizing outrage.

This degradation has also infected independent watchdogs. The National Human Rights Commission of Korea recently issued a long-delayed public apology regarding past recommendations concerning Yoon Seok-yeol's legal rights. The fact that conservative-recommended members abstained from the vote underscores how deeply partisan pressure has compromised institutions originally built to protect ordinary citizens.

When state bodies meant to safeguard human rights are repurposed to defend political elites, the public loses faith in the system entirely. It is a reminder that democratic erosion rarely happens overnight; it happens piece by piece as institutional guardrails are dismantled.

Rebuilding the Economy and Reforming the Social Contract

Beneath the political noise, South Koreans are grappling with a very real economic squeeze. While the global stock market surges on massive corporate artificial intelligence investments, domestic sentiment remains weighed down by high interest rates, sluggish regional commerce, and a deeply dysfunctional housing market. Failed real estate policies from past local administrations have left young people and non-homeowners locked out of stable housing, amplifying economic inequality.

To break this cycle, some reformers are pushing for bold structural changes. Proposals like equity-shared housing models—where buyers can acquire homes with smaller initial stakes and pay off the rest gradually—aim to tackle the housing crisis head-on. More radical ideas, such as state-backed developmental asset accounts providing citizens with baseline financial support at birth, adulthood, and retirement, attempt to address the deep-seated despair of a society obsessed with wealth stratification and low birth rates.

These discussions point back to a style of leadership that many South Koreans feel is missing today: a collaborative, people-centered approach modeled after former President Roh Moo-hyun, who famously welcomed criticism from aides and treated governance as a public trust rather than an instrument of personal dominance.

"Politics should not be about protecting political fiefdoms or trading insults; it should be about preserving the everyday dignity of ordinary people."

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this turbulent political and economic transition, several key developments will signal whether the nation can restore institutional health:

  • The Outcome of Intelligence Budget Investigations: Watch how far law enforcement agencies pursue accountability regarding the alleged misuse of National Intelligence Service funds, and whether courts enforce transparency on executive spending.
  • Judicial Reform and Vacancies: Monitor actions taken to resolve the prolonged Supreme Court vacancies and whether mounting pressure forces structural changes in how prosecutorial and judicial misconduct are handled.
  • Housing and Welfare Policy Debates: Pay attention to legislative movement on innovative housing supply programs and asset-building safety nets designed to ease the cost-of-living crisis for younger generations.
  • Political Rhetoric Ahead of Elections: Observe whether the mainstream parties moderate their language or double down on polarization as the next electoral cycles approach.

Ultimately, South Korea's democratic system has proven remarkably resilient through past crises, but resilience requires constant maintenance. Until the political class shifts its focus from partisan survival to the tangible needs of the public, the shadow of institutional distrust will remain.
