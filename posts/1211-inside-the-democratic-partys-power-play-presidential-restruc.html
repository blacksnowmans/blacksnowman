Inside the Democratic Party’s Power Play: Presidential Restructuring, Factional Hazards, and the Battle for Yeouido

October 16, 2026

October in South Korea’s National Assembly is rarely calm, but the arrival of the annual parliamentary audits turns the political district of Yeouido into an open arena of political warfare. Known as gukgam, these constitutionally mandated inspections give lawmakers sweeping powers to scrutinize state agencies, summon corporate executives, and stage high-profile attacks. For South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), this year’s audit comes at a critical pivot point: the administration of President Lee Jae-myung is settling into its second year, facing fierce headwinds from the conservative opposition, unpredictable internal hazards, and the complex challenge of delivering tangible economic results.

Against this backdrop, the DPK leadership launched an intensive, multi-front campaign to tighten party discipline, realign the triangular coordination between the party, the government, and the presidential office, and fend off aggressive salvos from the conservative People Power Party (PPP). From major personnel shifts at the presidential office to explosive controversies over religious infiltration and crypto taxation, the moves revealed how the ruling camp intends to maintain control over the domestic agenda.

Reinforcing the Presidential Bridgehead: The Rise of a Technocrat

The immediate centerpiece of the ruling camp’s restructuring is the appointment of Lim Gwang-hyun as the new Chief of Staff to President Lee Jae-myung. In the South Korean presidential system, the chief of staff is not simply an administrative manager; the figure acts as the gatekeeper to the executive branch and the primary liaison between the president and the National Assembly.

DPK Leader Kim Min-seok offered high praise for the appointment, describing Lim as a carefully considered choice and characterizing him as a "quietly strong card." Kim highlighted Lim’s administrative competence, pragmatic policy orientation, and relationship-building skills as the ideal mix to stabilize the working relationship between the party, the government ministries, and the presidential office. Floor Leader Han Byung-do echoed this sentiment during a parliamentary audit strategy meeting, emphasizing that Lim is the optimal figure to accelerate President Lee's core national policy tasks as the administration enters its second year.

Not everyone in the party viewed the selection without reservations. Lawmakers like Jeon Yong-gi noted that because Lim is primarily a policy technocrat, his initial political authority within the party and his capacity to manage volatile partisan negotiations will face an immediate trial by fire. Yet, amid grueling legislative battles, the ruling party leadership has prioritized low-drama, practical execution over political showmanship.

At the same time, party leaders are already looking toward the next electoral cycle. Kim Min-seok publicly acknowledged the possibility of lawmaker Kang Hoon-sik running for Seoul Mayor in the upcoming local elections. By framing Kang as a seasoned leader who cannot remain on the sidelines, the party leadership is actively nurturing next-generation talent for the crucial capital battleground, seeking figures who combine centrist appeal with strategic political instincts.

Clashing in the Audit Chambers: Target on the Opposition

While the presidential office reorganized its staff, legislative leaders drew sharp battle lines in the National Assembly. Floor Leader Han Byung-do directed fierce criticism at Jang Dong-hyeok, the leader of the main opposition People Power Party, accusing the conservative leadership of exploiting public safety incidents and national tragedies for partisan attacks against President Lee.

Arguing that the opposition had crossed the line by using human casualties as political ammunition, Han publicly demanded Jang’s immediate resignation and an apology to the public. Using the traditional Korean idiom "if the upper waters are murky, the lower waters cannot run clear," Han claimed the conservative party was abdicating its basic duties as a public institution by hijacking critical oversight hearings to manufacture political scandals.

The ruling party’s offensive also extended to key regional policy issues, particularly the real estate crisis in Seoul. Han targeted Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a prominent conservative figure, accusing him of deflecting blame for the capital's housing supply shortages onto previous administrations. Han urged the Seoul city government to stop passing the buck and instead coordinate directly with the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport on practical solutions, including the redevelopment of available land in the central Yongsan district.

Simultaneously, the DPK leadership reaffirmed its commitment to the Lee Jae-myung administration’s goal of completing the relocation of central administrative agencies to Sejong City. Established in the mid-2000s as a planned administrative hub south of Seoul, Sejong remains central to the DPK's long-term vision of dismantling Seoul’s hyper-concentrated dominance and promoting balanced regional growth.

Managing Internal Hazards: The Shincheonji Dispute and the Push for a Special Counsel

Despite presenting a united front against the opposition, the ruling party found itself forced to extinguish a sensitive internal fire: allegations that members of Shincheonji, a controversial fringe religious organization, had engaged in coordinated, mass enrollment into the Democratic Party.

Religious infiltration is an exceptionally toxic issue in Korean politics, often triggering suspicions of vote-rigging in internal primaries. Party leader Kim Min-seok sought to contain the fallout, stating firmly that the leadership is addressing all testimonies and circumstances surrounding the alleged collective enrollment strictly according to internal party rules and procedural regulations. However, friction inside the party remains visible. High-profile lawmaker Jeong Cheong-rae warned that if these allegations turn out to be false or part of a calculated political setup, those responsible must face strict consequences. Lawmaker Park Ji-won also cautioned against allowing unsubstantiated claims to leak prematurely into the press, warning of potential damage to public support while calling for a principled internal inquiry.

The opposition People Power Party immediately moved to capitalize on the controversy, with conservative lawmaker Jeong Jeom-sik demanding search-and-seizure operations on the DPK’s membership registry—a demand the ruling party swiftly rejected as a political smear campaign designed to distract from the audits.

To seize back the momentum, the DPK is accelerating its campaign for a special counsel investigation into alleged "fabricated indictments" carried out by the prosecution during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned. Supreme Council member Jeon Yong-gi announced a formal reporting conference in the National Assembly scheduled for October 28, arguing that exposing past prosecutorial overreach is vital for restoring public trust.

This push comes at a delicate polling juncture. A recent National Barometer Survey (NBS) showed the Democratic Party’s approval rating dipping to 33%, even as President Lee Jae-myung’s personal approval rating saw a modest uptick. Party strategists believe that taking the offensive on judicial reform and rooting out targeted political investigations will consolidate their core progressive base and rebuild parliamentary momentum.

Policy Pragmatism: Crypto Relief and Diplomatic Balancing

Beyond the partisan skirmishes, the DPK rolled out several key legislative measures aimed at pocketbook issues and vulnerable demographics. Most notably, Representative Park Min-gyu announced plans to introduce legislation postponing the implementation of taxes on cryptocurrency gains by another two years, pushing the start date to 2029.

The rationale centers on practical market realities: the Financial Services Commission (South Korea’s top financial regulatory body) has faced persistent bottlenecks in finalizing the comprehensive Digital Asset Basic Act, leaving the market infrastructure and investor protection frameworks incomplete. Rather than imposing taxation prematurely, the party opted to shield domestic retail investors from regulatory whiplash.

Other lawmakers targeted social safety nets and governance:

  • Elderly Protection: Representative Kim Kyo-heung sponsored a legislative package aimed at eradicating elder abuse within long-term care hospitals and closing institutional oversight loopholes.
  • Gender Policy: Representative Lee Su-jin introduced three governance bills to reform the existing National Gender Equality Committee into a broader Gender Equity Council, seeking to revitalize national gender policy frameworks.
  • Local Coordination: Grassroots party-government coordination continued at the municipal level, highlighted by formal joint consultative meetings in Hwaseong Special City.

On the international front, party leader Kim Min-seok engaged in high-level diplomacy, hosting Japanese Ambassador to South Korea Koichi Mizushima at the National Assembly. Kim articulated a pragmatic foreign policy posture: "Do not forget the past, but build the future together." This approach seeks to differentiate the current ruling party from what it criticized as the unilateral concessions of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, advocating instead for an assertive, national-interest-driven diplomacy that addresses historical grievances while maintaining crucial regional security and economic cooperation.

What to Watch Next

As the parliamentary audits near their conclusion, several key indicators will determine whether the Democratic Party can successfully translate its legislative posture into lasting political capital:

  • The October 28 Special Counsel Report: The scheduled National Assembly briefing on past prosecutorial overreach will show how aggressively the DPK plans to pursue legal accountability against officials from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, and whether this effort rallies its voter base.
  • The Fate of the Crypto Tax Deferral: Lawmaker Park Min-gyu's bill delaying cryptocurrency gains taxation until 2029 will serve as an early test of cross-party economic consensus before the year-end budget battles.
  • The Chief of Staff’s Legislative Debut: Observers will be watching how effectively newly appointed Chief of Staff Lim Gwang-hyun bridges communications between President Lee’s executive office and the National Assembly, especially as the opposition steps up its scrutiny of the administration's second-year budget.
  • Pre-Election Posturing for Seoul: Mentions of figures like Kang Hoon-sik for the Seoul mayoral race indicate that subterranean jockeying for the next local elections has begun, signaling an early fight to recapture key metropolitan seats.
