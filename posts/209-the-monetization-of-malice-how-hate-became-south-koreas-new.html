The Monetization of Malice: How Hate Became South Korea's New Business Model

September 07, 2026

Opening YouTube has become an act of trepidation. A few clicks, guided by the platform's recommendation algorithms, and suddenly the world feels like a claustrophobic chamber of vitriol and animosity. My feed is frequently dominated by videos designed to mock, belittle, or incite hatred against specific targets. When these videos are presented to me under the guise of "recommended content," I find myself wondering: what is happening to our society? This is not merely a collection of isolated outbursts by fringe individuals. The hate we consume daily is a meticulously designed, highly profitable business model.

The Industrialization of Hate

Hate content in South Korea has moved far beyond the realm of hobbyist trolling; it has become a sophisticated industry. Every time we watch a inflammatory video attacking a specific group, every time we feel compelled to leave an angry comment or click "share," the platform registers engagement and boosts its advertising revenue. What was once the isolated ranting of someone on a street corner has been transformed into a highly efficient, scalable commodity flowing through the massive infrastructure of digital platforms.

It is a cruel irony that a structure built on exploiting human suffering and social stigma has become so deeply embedded in our daily lives. Algorithms obsessively track what triggers our outrage, then continuously supply content that pours gasoline on that fire. In this ecosystem, hatred has become a sustainable, self-perpetuating revenue machine that thrives on the very conflicts it helps ignite.

We are all trapped in a self-reinforcing feedback loop. Algorithms, tailored to our browsing history and political leanings, show us only the version of the world we are most likely to accept—a phenomenon known as confirmation bias. When the information we are "predisposed" to believe is mixed with hate toward those we perceive as "the other," it becomes incredibly easy to mistake that content for absolute truth. The digital landscape has effectively locked us into "thought prisons," where anyone who thinks differently is labeled an enemy, and attacking them is framed as a defense of one's own values.

Hate as a Political Asset

The most dangerous application of this model is within the political arena. Traditionally, politics was understood as a process of mediation and consensus-building among different viewpoints. Today, however, South Korean politics is increasingly mired in a "purity war" designed to draw sharp lines between friends and enemies. In particular, some segments of the conservative sphere have been blatant in using extreme hate speech as a tool for political mobilization. For these actors, hatred is the most cost-effective way to solidify their base and demonize their opponents to maintain power.

In this environment, democratic values are pushed to the sidelines. Compromise is dismissed as weakness, and language that dehumanizes opponents is rewarded as political victory. It is chilling to see the echoes of historical movements—where populists harnessed public anxiety and anger to seize power—now being replicated on digital platforms. Democracy is intended to be a society where diverse values coexist, yet this industry of hate is systematically dismantling that foundation.

The most infuriating aspect of this is the tendency for these hate-mongers to hide behind the banner of "freedom of expression." They justify their vitriolic, dehumanizing attacks as a protected right. But let us be clear: hate is not free speech; it is violence that threatens the fundamental rights and safety of others. When hatred becomes a tool of the powerful, democracy itself becomes critically ill.

Breaking the Cycle

The debate over how much we should regulate hate speech is intense. While some fear that regulation could infringe upon constitutionally protected expression, a society that allows unchecked hate to trample the dignity of its members cannot truly call itself a free community. Whether we look at the United States or Europe, the approaches vary, but the consensus is growing: we need institutional guardrails and social agreements to curb rhetoric that undermines the public good.

The priority should not be the intensity of the regulation, but its direction. We must hold platform companies accountable and force them to dismantle the structures that profit from hatred. Simply realizing that the content we mindlessly click on is padding the wallets of these companies is a vital first step.

Beyond legislation, the most powerful defense is citizen education. We must stop falling for the shallow tactics of those who wish to use hatred as a political tool. True political education isn't just about memorizing election laws; it is about learning to recognize the mechanics of hate and mastering the art of coexisting with those who are different from us.

If we do not stop this cycle now, the arrows of hate will eventually turn toward us. The vitriol we consume for entertainment today could easily become the blade pointed at us tomorrow. We must stop being "loyal customers" to this cruel business model. I often think that much of this stems from a profound sense of loneliness—as social safety nets fray and hopes for the future dim, the urge to find a target to blame provides a false sense of comfort. But politics rooted in hate only serves to enrich the elite at the expense of the rest of us.

Creating a world where hate doesn't pay, and where it cannot be used as political capital, is up to us—the people holding the smartphones. Stop the "outrage clicks." Take a moment to actually listen to a voice that contradicts your own. That small, deliberate action is the most powerful shield we have to protect our democracy.
