The Rise of "Scarfrio": Why South Korea’s Gen Z is Flocking to Traditional Markets

November 09, 2026

Picture this: It is early morning at Seoul Station. A group of twenty-somethings, fresh off a high-speed KTX train from a provincial city, aren’t heading to the polished pop-up stores of Seongsu-dong or the luxury boutiques of Gangnam. Instead, they are navigating the chaotic, labyrinthine alleys of Namdaemun Market, the oldest and largest traditional market in South Korea. Their target? Stacks of vintage-style scarves.

This trend has birthed a new Korean buzzword: "Scarfrio," a playful portmanteau of "scarf" and the actor Leonardo DiCaprio. It refers to young people who travel long distances to "raid" the stalls of Namdaemun, treating the hunt for the perfect retro scarf not just as a shopping trip, but as a cultural pilgrimage. But why are members of a generation raised on instant, algorithmic digital delivery choosing to spend their time navigating dusty, non-digitized market alleys?

The Paradox of Efficiency: A Digital Generation’s Search for Texture

For South Korea’s 20- and 30-somethings, the "efficiency" of the digital age is no longer a luxury—it is a source of exhaustion. Having grown up in a country where one-click ordering and "dawn delivery" (where goods ordered at night arrive before sunrise) are the standard, this generation is experiencing a profound "sensory deficit." The smartphone screen is smooth, cold, and uniform. In contrast, Namdaemun Market offers the tactile, the gritty, and the unpredictable.

The "Scarfrio" phenomenon is an act of rebellion against the hyper-curated, artificial "hipness" found in temporary pop-up stores that litter Seoul’s trendier neighborhoods. Those pop-up spaces are often designed for nothing more than the perfect Instagram photo, with long, performative lines and a commercial rigidity that feels hollow to many young people. At Namdaemun, there are no social media mandates, no artificial scarcity, and no pre-planned photo zones. There is only the rough texture of silk and cotton, the clamor of veteran merchants, and the sprawling, chaotic vitality of a place that has been the beating heart of Seoul’s commerce for decades.

Beyond Retail: The "Treasure Hunt" Aesthetic

In a department store, everything is price-tagged, barcoded, and predictable. You know exactly what you are getting, and the experience is sterile. Namdaemun Market, however, operates on the thrill of the "treasure hunt."

Young shoppers find genuine joy in digging through piles of goods to discover a hidden gem—a unique pattern or a high-quality scarf—that costs only a few thousand won (a few dollars). This is what sociologists might call the "aesthetics of inconvenience." Much like the recent resurgence of film cameras and fountain pens among Korean youth, shopping at a traditional market requires effort. You must walk, you must ask questions, and you must navigate human interaction. In a world where AI makes all our decisions for us, the act of choosing for oneself—even if it is inefficient—feels like an assertion of agency.

The merchants themselves are part of the draw. There is a famous story of a 30-year veteran scarf seller who refuses to sell items to customers if he believes the color doesn't suit them. In an era of aggressive, soul-less marketing, this "brutal honesty" from an older generation has become a form of authentic, high-value connection that young consumers are starving for.

The Revival of "Old-School" Cool

This trend is part of a larger cultural shift in South Korea, where the "senior style" of grandfathers and grandmothers is being reinterpreted as the height of fashion. Whether it’s the "aek-magi" (evil-warding) keyrings or the retro ok-banji (jade rings) that have become trendy accessories, the youth are embracing elements of Korean tradition that their parents’ generation once deemed outdated.

Namdaemun is not just a place to buy scarves; it is a living, breathing museum. A visitor might start looking for a scarf and end up wandering through alleys of kitchenware, traditional snacks, and hardware. This "everything under one roof" density is the antithesis of the modern shopping mall, where everything is neatly categorized and partitioned. For the younger generation, this confusion is not a nuisance—it is an adventure. It is a "content-rich" space where one can spend an entire afternoon simply observing the raw, unfiltered rhythm of Korean life.

What to Watch Next

The "Scarfrio" trend signals a fundamental shift in how physical retail space will evolve in Korea. If brick-and-mortar stores want to survive in a world dominated by e-commerce, they must stop trying to compete on efficiency—they will never beat the internet at that. Instead, they must offer what the internet cannot: human connection, sensory richness, and the thrill of discovery.

Moving forward, keep an eye on how traditional markets and older shopping districts in other Korean cities begin to pivot. We can expect to see more "experience-based" marketing that leans into the history and "human touch" of these spaces. However, the true test will be whether these markets can maintain their authentic, messy charm as they inevitably attract more commercial interest. The "Scarfrio" craze proves that when the world becomes too perfect and too fast, the most radical thing you can do is go to a quiet, dusty alley, talk to an old shopkeeper, and look for something beautiful with your own two hands.
