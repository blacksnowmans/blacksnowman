The "Rich Cat" Dream: Why South Korean Parents and Students Are Trapped in a High-Stakes Education Race

September 03, 2026

In South Korea, a bittersweet phrase has begun circulating among students: they dream of becoming a "rich house cat." It isn't a desire for extreme wealth or power. Instead, it is a longing to be a creature that is well-fed, loved, and warm, living a life where they don’t have to constantly "read the room" or worry about the judgmental gaze of others. It is a cynical, yet heartbreaking, admission that the weight currently pressed upon the shoulders of Korean youth has become unsustainable. From the private academies—known as hagwons—that crowd the streets to the dinner tables at home, children are locked in a relentless race for survival.

For parents, the pursuit of a "better environment" often leads to moving to elite school districts, hyper-focusing on advanced curriculum, and viewing a child's report card as a direct reflection of their own success. But the question remains: does this path actually lead to the comfort of the "rich house cat"? Or are we merely trapping children in a cycle where their self-esteem is whittled away by the shackles of test scores? To find out, we look to the insights of veteran educators who have spent two decades observing the threshold of the South Korean classroom.

The "School District" Filter: A Cold Hierarchy

In South Korea, where you live determines where you go to school, and in high-demand areas—so-called "elite school districts"—the atmosphere is distinct and often unsettling. Here, academics are not merely about learning; they are a form of social currency. Students are ranked, and your test score often dictates your social standing. In these hyper-competitive environments, an average test score might be set at 70%, while in high school, it might be deliberately pushed down to 50% just to differentiate the top-tier students from the rest. For a student, a 50% average isn't just a grade—it is a brutal reminder of one's own perceived inadequacy.

Conversely, classrooms in less competitive areas often face the opposite problem, where students who choose to study are sometimes ostracized or mocked for being "weird." Regardless of the setting, the psychological toll is universal. In elite districts, however, the pressure is more insidious: the child feels that every exam is a validation—or rejection—of their very existence. If you perform, you survive; if you don't, you are alienated.

Many parents subscribe to the traditional belief in Maengmo-samcheon-jigyo—a classic proverb about a mother moving three times to find the best environment for her child's education. They believe a better district will automatically produce a better student. But when the environment doesn't match the child’s natural temperament or emotional capacity, the pressure becomes a form of violence. Before moving for a "better" school, parents must ask: does my child have the mental fortitude to survive the competitive grinder until the final year of high school? A school district is merely a setting; it cannot replace the inner strength of a child.

The Poisoned Legacy of Parental Success

The previous generation of Korean parents grew up with a clear formula for success: work hard, get results, and your value is proven. Consequently, they often demand this same path from their children. When a parent tells a struggling child, "You didn't do well because you didn't try hard enough," it may feel like a factual observation to the adult, but to the child, it is a permanent brand that says, "You are a failure."

This is the primary cause of "learning helplessness." When the success formula dictated by parents feels entirely disconnected from the child’s reality, the child stops trying—not out of laziness, but because they have already learned what it feels like to lose. Parents often treat a child’s poor grades like a disease that needs to be "cured" through more tutoring or stricter discipline. They try to seal the wound with a better test score, but the real injury is the social and emotional isolation the child feels. If the child doesn't feel loved for who they are, no amount of academic coaching can save them.

Building a Safety Net: Beyond the Straight Line

The world is changing rapidly, yet many parents still evaluate their children using a 20-year-old yardstick. The obsession with getting into a prestigious university in Seoul or landing a job at a major conglomerate—often referred to as the "straight-line" path to success—creates a narrow vision of life. The moment a child steps off this line, they are treated as a dropout.

Parents must realize that it is perfectly okay for a child to take a detour or even stand still for a while. The most important education a parent can provide today is fostering autonomy—allowing children to choose their own paths and take responsibility for them. When a parent shows disappointment because a child fails to meet an arbitrary expectation, that anxiety is transferred directly to the child as deep-seated inferiority. The goal is to provide a "safety net"—a realization that the child’s life does not end with a single report card.

What to Watch Next

  • Observe the "Emotional Baseline": Instead of focusing on grades, watch for signs of genuine interest. If a child expresses curiosity in a subject outside their core curriculum, treat it as a potential "detour" that could lead to a more sustainable, fulfilling future.
  • Redefine "Success" at Home: Start by changing the nature of conversations. Replace questions about rank or grades with questions about their day and their thoughts. Showing interest in the "person" rather than the "student" is the first step toward rebuilding the child's self-esteem.
  • Evaluate the Move: If you are considering moving to an elite school district, look beyond the test scores. Assess whether the environment will offer the emotional support your child needs to fail safely and get back up, rather than just providing a place to compete.

Ultimately, we have to ask ourselves: are we raising human beings, or are we producing machines meant to generate scores? Children are always more capable than we imagine, but that potential only blossoms when the expectations are directed at the "person" rather than the "paper." Tonight, try looking into your child's eyes instead of checking their report card. To the child who sees their parent as their entire world, the most powerful support is not a high rank—it is knowing that they are enough just as they are.
