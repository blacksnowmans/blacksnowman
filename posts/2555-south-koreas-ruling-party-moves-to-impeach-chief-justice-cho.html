South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae Over Martial Law and Scandal Rulings

November 20, 2026

Inside South Korea’s National Assembly, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea have gathered in a caucus to lock in a rare, binding party-line vote—not against an executive branch official, but against the head of the nation’s highest court. Their target is Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Triggering the constitutional showdown is a sequence of recent Supreme Court rulings involving corruption allegations and martial law-related insurrection charges stemming from the tenure of Yoon Suk Yeol, who was previously impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned.

According to commentary on the South Korean political blog Black Snowman and statements from Democratic Party leaders, these decisions have badly shaken public confidence in the judiciary by appearing to use narrow procedural arguments to shield key political figures. The Supreme Court majority, by contrast, has grounded its decisions in formal statutory thresholds and constitutional procedure. The resulting clash has pushed South Korea into a fresh debate over where judicial independence ends and legislative accountability begins.

For readers outside South Korea, the current standoff reflects a sharp shift in the country’s political landscape. Following Mr. Yoon’s impeachment and removal from office, South Korea is now led by President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea, which governs as the ruling party opposite the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. While the Lee Jae-myung administration has focused on stabilizing state institutions after the martial law crisis, critics of the judiciary argue that the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Cho remains out of step with the public’s demand for accountability.

Selective Accountability? The Kim Keon-hee and Myung Tae-kyun Rulings

The current controversy first gained momentum around a pair of Supreme Court decisions addressing political scandals tied to Yoon Suk Yeol’s time in office. In an en banc ruling—a full-bench decision reserved for major cases—the Supreme Court finalized guilty verdicts against Kim Keon-hee, Mr. Yoon’s wife, in two high-profile matters: the Deutsch Motors stock manipulation case and allegations of accepting luxury gifts linked to the Unification Church, a prominent religious organization.

On the surface, those convictions appeared to show the court holding powerful figures accountable. Yet around the same time, the Supreme Court issued an acquittal for Myung Tae-kyun, a political broker and pollster accused of violating South Korea’s Political Funds Act in connection with opinion-polling operations. That acquittal also effectively finalized an acquittal for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, who had been implicated in the polling-related allegations.

In its ruling, the court concluded that Mr. Myung’s activities could not readily be categorized as a violation of the Political Funds Act. However, critics cited by Black Snowman contend that the court’s reasoning revealed a contradiction. Commentators have gone so far as to claim that the bench devoted 90 percent of its effort in the Myung Tae-kyun case to constructing legal logic that insulated central figures, while using the guilty verdicts against Ms. Kim to deflect public criticism.

From the perspective of these critics, the outcome amounts to "selective justice" that exposes a dangerous loophole in South Korea’s campaign finance laws. In modern elections, commissioned opinion polls carry substantial political and financial value. Critics argue that by declining to treat irregular polling arrangements as illicit political funding, the ruling leaves voters and smaller candidates facing an uneven playing field and weakens electoral fairness.

Han Duck-soo’s Remand and the Debate Over Martial Law Procedures

If the campaign finance rulings fueled skepticism, the Supreme Court’s handling of insurrection charges against former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo ignited outright political confrontation. Mr. Han, who served as prime minister during Yoon Suk Yeol’s tenure, faced charges linked to the declaration of martial law. Reviewing the case, the Supreme Court overturned part of the ruling and remanded it—sending it back to a lower court—with instructions pointing toward an acquittal on the specific portion involving the Cabinet meeting held prior to the martial law decree.

The court majority’s stated rationale centered on constitutional form: recommending and convening a Cabinet deliberation before declaring martial law satisfied the procedural requirements laid out in the Constitution. Under that interpretation, following the formal steps of a Cabinet session weighed against criminal liability for that part of the insurrection charge.

Democratic Party leaders and commentators such as Black Snowman have sharply rejected that reasoning. They argue that when a Cabinet meeting is used as an instrument for what they describe as a self-coup, treating formal attendance and paperwork as a legal shield ignores the substantive crime. Critics compare the logic to excusing a robbery simply because the perpetrators followed a neatly drafted procedural plan.

What has drawn particular alarm from critics is the practical effect of the court’s "partial acquittal" framework. Because the Cabinet deliberation was a core component of the insurrection case against Mr. Han, removing it from criminal liability eliminates more than half of the potential sentence. In the view of Chief Justice Cho’s detractors, the ruling sets a troubling precedent for future crises by signaling to senior officials that they can escape legal consequences for aiding unconstitutional acts so long as they check off formal procedural boxes.

Justice Oh Kyung-mi’s Dissent on Constitutional Duty

The tension over form versus substance did not come solely from outside the courthouse; it divided the Supreme Court bench itself. Amid the majority’s proceduralist approach, Supreme Court Justice Oh Kyung-mi issued a pointed dissenting opinion criticizing the effective immunity granted to senior officials involved in the martial law episode.

In Justice Oh Kyung-mi’s view, the Cabinet deliberation had been reduced to a tool providing a facade of legality for insurrection, and senior public officials bear a primary duty to defend the Constitution rather than blindly follow unconstitutional orders.

According to accounts of her dissent, Justice Oh emphasized that when high-ranking officials unquestioningly execute unlawful presidential directives, that obedience is itself a criminal offense. Focusing exclusively on procedural formalities while overlooking substantive contributions to an insurrection, she argued, amounts to the judiciary abandoning its core responsibility.

For critics of the court’s majority, Justice Oh’s opinion has become a rallying point. Observers cited by Black Snowman describe her as preserving the judiciary’s self-respect at a moment when the institution’s credibility is under strain. Even though a dissenting opinion does not alter the immediate legal outcome for Mr. Han or other defendants, legal observers view her written warning—that a rubber-stamp Cabinet should never become a legal screen for subverting the Constitution—as a crucial historical record inside the court’s own archives.

Why the Democratic Party Pursued Impeachment—and What to Watch Next

Faced with rulings it views as distorting both legal accountability and South Korea’s constitutional history, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea concluded that political persuasion was no longer sufficient and moved toward impeaching Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok summed up the party’s stance by stating that lawmakers could not tolerate a precedent forcing a behavioral norm on the nation that effectively tells officials to "act like Han Duck-soo when an insurrection occurs."

To translate that criticism into legislative action, the Democratic Party has taken several concrete organizational steps:

  • Building a broader coalition: The Democratic Party formed a joint "impeachment task force" alongside smaller progressive parties, bringing together figures such as Democratic Party lawmaker Park Joo-min and progressive party leader Shin Jang-sik to align reform momentum across the progressive bloc.
  • Enforcing party discipline: During a party caucus, Democratic Party lawmakers adopted Chief Justice Cho’s impeachment as an official "party line," establishing formal grounds for disciplinary action against any member who breaks ranks in a plenary vote.
  • Delegating execution to leadership: Lawmakers entrusted the party’s leadership with full authority over the timing, specific charges, and procedural rollout of the impeachment effort.

At stake in this confrontation is a delicate constitutional balance. Proponents argue that pursuing impeachment is not an attack on judicial independence, but rather a lawful constitutional check by the National Assembly against a Chief Justice whom they accuse of forfeiting independence on his own. At the same time, impeaching the head of the Supreme Court directly over the substance of judicial rulings carries profound institutional consequences, testing the boundaries between legislative oversight and the autonomy of the courts.

In the weeks ahead, three concrete developments will signal how far this crisis goes:

  • The impeachment motion’s drafting and timing: Watch how the newly formed task force and Democratic Party leadership structure the formal articles of impeachment and decide when to bring the bill to the National Assembly floor.
  • Party-line cohesion in a plenary vote: Watch whether the binding caucus resolution prevents defections among Democratic Party and allied progressive lawmakers when the motion faces a formal vote.
  • The broader institutional response: Watch how the Lee Jae-myung administration’s governing coalition, the conservative People Power Party, and the judiciary itself respond to mounting pressure for structural judicial reform in the aftermath of these rulings.
