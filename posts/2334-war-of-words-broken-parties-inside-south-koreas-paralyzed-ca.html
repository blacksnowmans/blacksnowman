War of Words, Broken Parties: Inside South Korea’s Paralyzed Capitol

November 15, 2026

In the high-stakes arena of Yeouido, South Korea's legislative heart in Seoul, policy discussions have ground to a absolute standstill. While small business owners grapple with inflation, exchange rate volatility, and a sluggish domestic economy, the country’s political class has retreated into an all-out partisan war. On November 14, the central drama in South Korean politics was not a debate over the national budget or economic relief, but a fiery social media feud between high-profile figures—accompanied by severe internal rebellions inside both the governing and main opposition parties.

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea’s government was expected to press forward with its legislative agenda through the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Instead, both the ruling party and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, are rotting from within. Lawmakers are spending their political capital attacking internal rivals and launching toxic personal barbs at their opponents, leaving critical national governance stranded at the committee door.

The Social Media Brawl: Han Dong-hoon and Choi Min-hee Exchange Fire

The public flashpoint began on the afternoon of November 14, when a bitter exchange erupted on social media between Han Dong-hoon, leader of the main opposition People Power Party (PPP), and Choi Min-hee, a senior lawmaker from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) who chairs the National Assembly’s Science, ICT, Future Planning, Broadcasting and Communications Committee.

The argument started when Han targeted President Lee Jae-myung’s ongoing trial under the Public Official Election Act. Han publicly argued that if President Lee were to receive a sentence that invalidated his election, the very legitimacy of the current administration would be fundamentally shattered. The comment was a direct strike at the ruling party’s core vulnerability: the legal troubles surrounding its leader.

Choi Min-hee immediately stepped up as the ruling party's defense shield, launching a fierce counterattack against Han. She accused the opposition leader of spreading distorted legal logic and continuing the kind of political hit jobs he pursued before taking party leadership. Choi framed Han’s maneuvers as a desperate attempt to seize the political initiative by weaponizing judicial proceedings against the sitting president.

What followed quickly escalated beyond legal debate into raw personal insults:

  • Han Dong-hoon pressed his attack by pointing to audio recordings connected to the case, stating that denying clear physical evidence was "an ugly sight to behold" and accusing Choi of destroying the rule of law for factional gain.
  • Choi Min-hee fired back by accusing Han of spreading blatant falsehoods, publicly demanding that "Han Dong-hoon stop lying" and distorting context to mislead public opinion.

Instead of a dignified debate over judicial principles or parliamentary oversight, voters were treated to two senior political figures engaging in personal attacks on social media feed. The episode highlighted how legal disputes that belong in courtroom arguments are routinely dragged into the court of public opinion to inflame partisan bases.

"When political leaders trade toxic insults to trigger applause from their core supporters, governance is replaced by theater, and the public bears the ultimate cost."

Purges and Audits: The Ruling Party’s Internal Backlash

While Choi Min-hee was busy battling the opposition leader online, her own party was turning against her. Inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, pro-Lee Jae-myung loyalists launched a fierce offensive against Choi, claiming her recent rhetoric and public missteps damaged the party's core identity and undermined President Lee’s executive authority.

The factional anger spilled out into the streets. On November 14, an influential pro-Lee outside organization, the "The Minjoo Gyeonggi Innovation Conference," alongside hardline party members, gathered directly in front of Choi’s constituency office. Holding placards and chanting slogans, they formally demanded her expulsion from the Democratic Party, claiming her controversial statements caused party approval ratings to drop by nearly 5 percentage points.

The internal pressure forced party leadership to act:

  • Ethics Inspection Unit Audit: The Democratic Party’s internal Ethics Inspection Group launched an official inquiry into Choi’s recent comments, including controversial past remarks regarding farmland investigations.
  • Prominent Distance: Veteran Democratic Party lawmaker Park Ji-won swiftly distanced himself from the scandal, angrily demanding to know why his name was being drawn into Choi's factional troubles and denying he had ever consulted with her on the matter.
  • Presidential Warning: Tensions reached a boiling point as political insiders interpreted President Lee Jae-myung's cryptic warning about "arrows flying from behind" as a direct reference to internal dissenters like Choi.

For a ruling party that needs unity to push its legislative agenda through Parliament, watching senior members face factional purges and internal audits within the first phase of the presidency signals deep structural instability.

Rigged Discipline and Boycotts: The Opposition’s Implosion

The opposition People Power Party offered no functional alternative. While the ruling party tore itself apart over loyalty to President Lee, the PPP was engulfed in its own corruption scandal involving allegations of "manipulated disciplinary actions" against internal critics on party message boards.

The controversy gave rival politicians an easy opening to attack Han Dong-hoon’s leadership. Lee Jun-seok, a former PPP leader who broke away to form the Reform Party, mocked Han’s handling of the crisis. Lee pointedly noted that if the allegations of rigged party discipline were true, Han should simply file a court injunction—drawing an explicit parallel to Lee's own past legal battles against the PPP leadership.

Inside the PPP, the rift between Han’s allies and his internal critics widened into open non-cooperation:

  • Outside Rallies vs. Legislative Absences: Jang Dong-hyeok, a PPP Supreme Council member, traveled to Busan to rally public opposition against what the PPP calls "Lee Jae-myung jailbreak legislation." However, the street campaign was undermined when numerous PPP lawmakers conspicuously refused to attend.
  • Internal Boycott: The lack of attendance in Busan exposed a growing internal boycott against Han's leadership style. Key lawmakers chose to express their discontent with internal party discipline by walking away from official party rallies.

As a result, the main opposition party's attempt to project a united front against the government collapsed into embarrassing public dissent.

A Capitol Frozen in Neutral: What to Watch Next

The mutual dysfunction of South Korea's two political giants has created a total paralysis in Yeouido. Rather than offering competing policy visions for an anxious public, both parties are operating on a strategy of negative party loyalty—hoping to profit politically from the collapse of their opponent while ignoring the fires in their own backyards.

In official statements released throughout November 14, party spokespersons traded vicious labels like "power struggles," "self-harm," and "collective paradox." Yet neither side addressed how they plan to pass critical budget bills or economic relief measures currently stalled in parliamentary committees.

As South Korea navigates complex domestic and international pressures, the following key developments will reveal whether this political gridlock deepens or resolves:

  • The DPK Ethics Committee Decision: Watch whether the Democratic Party actually moves to discipline or expel Choi Min-hee, which will serve as a bellwether for how strictly pro-Lee loyalists intend to purge internal party dissent.
  • Han Dong-hoon’s Grip on the Opposition: Keep an eye on whether opposition lawmakers continue to boycott PPP leadership events, and whether internal critics take legal action over the alleged disciplinary manipulations.
  • Judicial Timelines: The ongoing legal proceedings against President Lee Jae-myung under the Public Official Election Act will continue to dictate the rhythm of legislative combat in Seoul.

When politics collapses into social media insults and factional score-settling, the ultimate price is paid not by politicians, but by the citizens waiting for their government to lead.
