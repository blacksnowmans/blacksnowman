Why Micron’s "Perfect" Earnings Are Shaking the Korean Market

October 02, 2026

Whenever the KOSPI—South Korea’s benchmark stock index—stumbles or loses its footing, there is one target investors look at first: semiconductors. No matter how well other sectors perform, the heart of the Korean stock market beats according to the rhythm of chipmakers. Recently, a massive "trailer" for the market's future arrived from across the Pacific in the form of an earnings report from Micron Technology, the third-largest memory chipmaker in the world.

When the results were released, many were stunned. Revenue crushed expectations, margins were otherworldly, and earnings per share were textbook perfect. By any metric, it was a 100-point performance. Yet, the market reacted with bizarre hostility, acting as if it were desperate to find a reason to sell. Understanding why this happened reveals the current, fragile reality surrounding the Korean stock market.

The "Peak-ron" Paradox: Why Perfection Was Met with Scorn

Micron’s fiscal 2026 fourth-quarter results were historic. The company posted $54.2 billion in revenue, far exceeding the market consensus of $51 billion. Earnings per share hit $33 (beating the $31 forecast), and operating cash flow reached $43 billion, smashing the $34 billion expectation. Perhaps most impressively, their adjusted gross margin was 87%, defying the market’s expectation of 86%.

Even AI data center revenue skyrocketed 11-fold year-on-year, proving that the semiconductor industry is standing on firm ground. So why did the stock price dip in after-hours trading? The culprits clung to a microscopic detail: Micron’s guidance for the next quarter suggested margins might dip from 87% to 86.25%—a mere 0.75% difference. Skeptics latched onto this, pushing a narrative they dubbed "Peak-ron." The argument is that since the company reached 87%, it has nowhere to go but down.

This logic is fundamentally flawed. Micron explained that this slight dip in margins isn't due to flagging demand, but rather a strategic consolidation of one-time costs, such as executive bonuses and inventory adjustments, into the current quarter. With next-quarter revenue projected to hit $61.5 billion—nearly quadruple the previous year—it is clear that the industry is not hitting a ceiling. Demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and DRAM is so robust that the internal accounting of costs is a poor excuse to call a "peak."

Beyond 2027: The Era of AI Infrastructure

The primary concern for global investors post-earnings was sustainability. Given the volatile history of "boom-and-bust" semiconductor cycles, everyone is asking: "How long can this last?"

Micron’s answer was bold: Demand is so vast that supply cannot keep up, and this structural growth is expected to hold firm through 2031. For years, the market has been obsessed with short-term survival. Now, for the first time, industry leaders are providing a clear, long-term horizon. For South Korean giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, this changes everything. The days of treating chips as mere commodities to be dumped when inventory piles up are over. Semiconductors are now the essential fuel for the AI civilization.

The Real Challenge: Shareholders and "Artificial" Support

With Micron having set the stage, the focus now shifts to Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. Investors are no longer just looking at profit figures; they are looking for shareholder returns. Samsung has a history of disappointing the market during IR (Investor Relations) sessions, and shareholders are now demanding concrete, aggressive return policies. If these companies fail to deliver, the market will punish them regardless of how stellar their bottom-line profits are.

Furthermore, there is a looming "supply shock" on the horizon: the end of corporate buyback programs. Both SK Hynix and Samsung have been using buybacks as an artificial floor for their stock prices. As these programs reach their conclusion in mid-October, we may see temporary volatility. This is not a sign of corporate collapse, but a return to "natural" market discovery. The companies must now prove their worth through fundamentals rather than artificial support.

The Hidden Risks: Oil and Financial Malpractice

While the tech sector looks toward the future, two dark clouds persist in the real economy: energy prices and predatory lending practices.

The global oil market is currently relying on a precarious "tanker transfer" system to circumvent the bottlenecks in the Strait of Hormuz. While this has helped restore about 80% of oil exports, it is a costly, inefficient, and temporary patch. If Iran senses that its leverage is fading, the risk of direct attacks on regional infrastructure remains a ticking time bomb. With China preparing to re-enter the market as a major buyer, the current stability in oil prices is fragile at best.

Finally, there is a rot within Korea’s secondary financial sector. While the Saemaul Undong Cooperatives (a group of community-based financial institutions) are under the supervision of the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, they have been accused of skirting laws meant to curb predatory lending. Specifically, they have allegedly been forcing small, vulnerable subcontractors to provide "joint and several guarantees" for project financing (PF) loans. This practice—where a third party is forced to take on another's debt—is a relic of a darker era of Korean finance. If a construction project fails, these small companies are left to shoulder the burden, threatening their very survival.

What to Watch Next

  • Samsung & SK Hynix Q3 Earnings: Watch the tone of their shareholder return announcements; this will be the true test of investor confidence.
  • Oil Price Volatility: Monitor Chinese import data. If China’s demand spikes, the "tanker transfer" system will be put to the ultimate test.
  • PF Reform: Keep an eye on government investigations into the Saemaul Undong Cooperatives. The resolution of these debt chains will determine the fate of many small subcontractors in the property sector.
