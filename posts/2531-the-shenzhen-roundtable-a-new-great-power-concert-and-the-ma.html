The Shenzhen Roundtable: A New Great Power Concert and the Marginalization of the Middle

November 20, 2026

Imagine a room in Shenzhen, the shimmering glass-and-steel monument to China’s economic ascent. Inside, three men who hold the literal power to end civilization sit around a heavy wooden table. To their left, the South China Sea; to their right, the sprawling factories that clothe and arm the world. This is not a standard diplomatic photo-op. For the first time in history, the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia have gathered in a trilateral format to decide the fate of the global order. As Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin emerged from their 90-minute session, they didn't just announce a ceasefire or a trade deal—they announced a "Peace Declaration" that aims to rewrite the rules of the United Nations and the management of nuclear fire.

For those watching from Seoul, the scene is both historic and deeply unsettling. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea has attempted to pivot toward a more "pragmatic diplomacy," distancing itself from the rigid, ideological alignment seen during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was removed from office and is currently imprisoned. But as the "Big Three" carve up spheres of influence in Shenzhen, the reality for middle powers is becoming clear: the era of multilateralism is being replaced by a Great Power "Direct Deal" system. What happened in Shenzhen on November 19 isn't just news; it is a tectonic shift that could leave countries like South Korea navigating a much colder, more transactional world.

A 90-Minute Blueprint for a New Global Hierarchy

The core of the summit was a 90-minute trilateral meeting that resulted in a four-point "Peace Declaration." While the document uses the lofty language of diplomacy, its implications are brutally practical. The three leaders reaffirmed the principle that a nuclear war can never be won and must never be fought—a necessary statement given the heightened tensions over the past two years. However, the declaration went further, proposing a total overhaul of the United Nations system and a coordinated expansion of global energy supplies, specifically refined oil.

President Xi Jinping, acting as the host, framed the nuclear threat through a classical lens, comparing nuclear weapons to the "Sword of Damocles" hanging over humanity. His rhetoric suggested a vision where the Great Powers act as the world’s ultimate guarantors of safety, provided they can agree among themselves. Vladimir Putin, meanwhile, used the stage to signal his definitive exit from diplomatic isolation. By sitting as an equal with the U.S. and China, Putin demonstrated that the Western-led effort to make Russia a pariah state has effectively ended, at least in the eyes of the other two superpowers.

The most striking element for international observers was the consensus on UN reform. The "Big Three" agreed that the current UN system is paralyzed and requires a "complete restructuring." To a casual reader, this might sound like a positive move toward efficiency. However, in the context of Great Power politics, it often means streamlining the decision-making process to favor those at the top of the food chain. For South Korea—a country that has long relied on the UN and international law to balance the influence of its larger neighbors—a UN redesigned by Trump, Xi, and Putin could mean a world where the voices of smaller nations are systematically sidelined.

The Trump-Xi Bargain: Trade for Stability

Beyond the trilateral talk, the bilateral meeting between Donald Trump and Xi Jinping showcased the "transactional diplomacy" that has become Trump’s trademark. The American president was blunt: he demanded that China significantly increase its purchases of U.S. agricultural products to address the chronic trade imbalance. This "America First" approach treats geopolitics as a series of balance sheets, where security concessions are traded for export quotas.

In return, there was a surprising level of alignment on Middle Eastern stability. Both leaders expressed a shared interest in deterring Iran’s nuclear ambitions and ensuring the safe passage of oil through the Strait of Hormuz. This is a rare moment of convergence. For China, the world’s largest oil importer, and for a Trump administration focused on domestic energy prices, a flare-up in the Middle East is a mutual enemy. This "Point of Consensus" suggests that while the U.S. and China remain rivals in technology and ideology, they are willing to act as a global duopoly to manage the world’s "energy heart" when their wallets are at stake.

On the sensitive issue of Taiwan, the rhetoric was carefully calibrated. Xi Jinping warned against damaging the foundations of the U.S.-China relationship, while Trump reiterated the standard position of not supporting Taiwanese independence. This was not a resolution of the conflict but a "strategic freezing." By lowering the temperature on Taiwan, both sides cleared the deck to focus on more immediate concerns—like the war in Ukraine and the management of their respective domestic economies. For the Lee Jae-myung government in Seoul, which seeks to avoid being caught in the crossfire of a Taiwan Strait conflict, this temporary cooling is a welcome, if fragile, reprieve.

The Putin Factor and the Nuclear Shadow

The arrival of Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, carrying the "nuclear briefcase," was a potent reminder of the military stakes. His 40-minute sidebar with Donald Trump was perhaps the most scrutinized "short meeting" in recent history. While no formal map for a Ukraine ceasefire was signed, the two discussed the "will for peace" and the necessity of ending the conflict. Putin’s comments after the meeting—suggesting that the interests of the U.S., China, and Russia "overlap" regarding regional conflicts—point toward a future where the war in Ukraine might be settled not by the combatants themselves, but by a Great Power consensus.

This has massive implications for the Korean Peninsula. If the "Big Three" can agree on a framework for nuclear arms reduction and the prevention of proliferation, North Korea’s leverage begins to evaporate. The Shenzhen declaration included a commitment to activate a special dialogue channel for nuclear disarmament. Trump noted that the U.S., China, and Russia are in a "unique position" to dictate terms to other aspiring nuclear states.

"The Great Powers are moving toward a 'Crisis Management System' rather than true disarmament. They aren't getting rid of their own teeth; they are making sure no one else grows any."

For the Lee Jae-myung administration, this presents a double-edged sword. On one hand, a US-China-Russia consensus on denuclearization could pressure Pyongyang in ways a divided international community never could. On the other hand, if the "Big Three" decide to recognize the status quo in exchange for North Korean "containment," Seoul’s long-term goal of a denuclearized peninsula might be traded away for the sake of global "strategic stability."

The New Yalta: What it Means for South Korea

Critics are already calling the Shenzhen summit a "Second Yalta," referring to the 1945 conference where the victors of World War II divided the world into spheres of influence. Putin himself leaned into this comparison, dismissively asking what was so bad about Yalta. The "Peace Declaration" at Shenzhen effectively signals that the era of the "liberal international order"—based on universal rules and the equality of sovereign states—is being replaced by a "Great Power Concert."

In this new order, the "Big Three" handle the big problems, and everyone else is expected to fall in line. The agreement to limit the "abuse of jurisdiction" by the International Criminal Court (ICC) is a clear example. It is a move designed to protect Putin from war crimes charges and to ensure that the U.S. and China aren't hampered by international legal bodies they cannot control. It is a return to a "might makes right" framework, polished with the sheen of a diplomatic declaration.

The Lee Jae-myung government, which has championed a "balanced" approach, now faces a daunting task. The Democratic Party's strategy of maintaining a functional relationship with Beijing while preserving the U.S. alliance is predicated on a world where there is some "space" between the superpowers. As Trump, Xi, and Putin close ranks to manage the world, that space is shrinking. If the Big Three are in agreement, they don't need to woo middle powers like South Korea; they only need to issue instructions.

What to watch next

  • The Energy Action Plan: Watch for specific movements in global oil prices and refinery outputs. If the promised "expansion of supply" manifests, it will be a sign that the US-China-Russia energy entente is real and functional.
  • UN Reform Proposals: In the coming months, look for a joint push by these three nations to alter the voting structures or the veto powers within the UN Security Council. Any move to diminish the influence of the General Assembly will confirm the shift toward a Great Power oligarchy.
  • The North Korean Response: Pyongyang usually reacts poorly to being a "topic" of discussion rather than a "participant." Watch for missile tests or diplomatic provocations as Kim Jong-un tries to reassert his relevance in the face of this trilateral consensus.
  • Seoul’s "Pragmatic" Pivot: How will President Lee Jae-myung adjust his foreign policy? If the U.S. and China are cooperating on trade and Iran, the pressure on Korea to "choose a side" might actually decrease, but the pressure to "pay up" (in terms of defense costs or trade concessions) will likely increase.

The Shenzhen summit was a 90-minute masterclass in the new reality of the 21st century. The world is being managed from a round table where there are only three chairs. For the rest of us, the challenge is no longer about picking the right side—it's about making sure we aren't what's for dinner.
