The Art of Less: Packing for the 500-Mile Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Four years ago, I stood at the threshold of a dream, only to be stopped by the sudden, crushing wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. Five days before departure, with my flight tickets cancelled and my plans dismantled, I spent four days in a haze of frustration and tears. That experience transformed the Camino de Santiago—the ancient network of pilgrimage routes across Spain—from a simple hiking trip into something deeply personal. It became a symbol of unfinished business. Now, as I prepare for a 45-day, 800-kilometer (roughly 500-mile) trek starting March 20th, I am once again packing my backpack. The central philosophy remains the same: the aesthetic of "subtracting to fill."

The Philosophy of the Backpack: Carrying 6.5 Kilograms

The pack is the alpha and omega of the pilgrimage. I have chosen the Klättermusen Delling 30-liter bag, which weighs just 800 grams (1.7 lbs). While it offers water resistance and sleek utility, I’ll be honest: I chose it because it is beautiful. If I am going to live out of a bag for over a month, I need to enjoy looking at it. Including my MacBook Air, my total pack weight is 6.5 kilograms (about 14 lbs). Without the laptop, I’m down to 5 kilograms. The golden rule of long-distance hiking is to keep your pack under 10% of your body weight, and this sits comfortably within that margin.

Functionality, however, cannot be sacrificed at the altar of weight. A water-purifying bottle I received—capable of filtering 387 liters—is a game-changer. By avoiding the daily purchase of bottled water, I am saving money and significantly reducing my environmental footprint. I have also packed health supplements my mother insisted on, and a stash of coin-shaped medicated adhesive pads (a staple in Korean households for muscle aches). One thing I refuse to compromise on is my neck pillow. For those in their 40s, a neck pillow isn't a luxury; it is the difference between arriving in Spain as a functioning human or a wreck.

The Pitfall of "Lightweight" Obsession

Many pilgrims obsess over extreme weight reduction, shedding gear to the point of vulnerability. But the Camino is not a weekend stroll; it is a 500-mile endurance test. Knee braces, specialized hygiene bars, and gear for mental well-being—like my laptop and GoPro—are not "excess weight." They are the tools that prevent injury and keep you going. Wisdom lies not in discarding everything, but in choosing a "valuable weight."

Practical Strategy: From Feet to Finance

The most dangerous thing you can do on the Camino is wear new shoes. I am bringing a pair that is already perfectly molded to my feet. New boots are a one-way ticket to blisters and potential trip-ending injuries. I’ve also packed Compeed blister plasters, which are widely considered the gold standard among pilgrims. Beyond gear, self-care is a discipline: keeping toenails trimmed straight across and performing regular calf stretches are not optional; they are the mechanics of survival.

Managing the "digital" and "financial" side of the journey requires similar caution. I am bringing four different travel-friendly credit cards (such as the Travelog and Travel Wallet cards, which are popular in South Korea for their lack of foreign transaction fees). Distributing cards across different pockets is the best insurance policy against the nightmare of losing a wallet in a foreign country. Similarly, I have pre-purchased two 28-day SIM cards to ensure I’m not scrambling for connectivity the moment I land.

Community and the Modern Pilgrim

The Camino is often viewed as a solitary pursuit, but it is deeply defined by its communal culture. For those of us who start our days in the pitch black of 5:00 AM, a headlamp is not a choice—it is a courtesy to ensure you don’t trip over your fellow pilgrims. We practice a silent etiquette: using earplugs in the communal hostels (albergues) and keeping noise to a minimum. On the trail, you learn that you are never truly alone; you are part of a nomadic community bound by the sight of the yellow arrows that mark the path.

Modern society is often defined by a relentless, shallow search for "value for money." I am reminded of how, in Korea, we saw the rise and fall of buffet chains like Jayeon Byeolgok (a popular Korean buffet franchise). We are always looking for the "essential" experience. Perhaps the 45-day trek and the comfort of a meal are not so different—both are about stripping away the non-essential to find the nourishment underneath. I am bringing the "meal kit" of my own personal hardships, and I intend to find my own recipe for getting through it.

What to Watch For

As I embark tomorrow, I am no longer the person who cried over a cancelled flight four years ago. I am older, and perhaps a bit more prepared for the unpredictable.

  • The Weight Test: I will be monitoring whether 6.5kg truly strikes the balance between comfort and necessity.
  • The Physical Toll: Success will be measured not by speed, but by the absence of injury.
  • The Mental Shift: I look forward to seeing how my perspective on "essential" items changes after 400 or 500 kilometers on the road.

If you have been feeling stuck or stationary, I encourage you to look at your own "backpack." What are you carrying that is holding you back? And what are you leaving behind? I will find my answers on the path to the Cathedral of Santiago, one foot in front of the other.
