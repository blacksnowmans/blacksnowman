South Korea’s Antitrust Watchdog Targets Coupang: Inside the Battle for the Country’s Digital Market

October 26, 2026

In South Korea, where online orders placed before midnight routinely arrive at your doorstep before sunrise, e-commerce is not merely a convenience—it is a central pillar of daily life. At the heart of this hyper-convenient ecosystem sits Coupang, the undisputed heavyweight of Korean online retail. Yet, behind the seamless deliveries lies a deepening crisis for small-scale merchants and independent vendors who claim they are trapped in an increasingly predatory platform ecosystem. Now, South Korean regulators are stepping in with unprecedented force, signaling a dramatic shift in how the nation governs its digital giants.

A 1 Percent Revenue Fine: South Korea’s FTC Takes On Market Giant Coupang

Joo Byung-ki, the Chairman of South Korea’s Fair Trade Commission (FTC)—the nation’s primary antitrust regulator—has delivered a stark warning to the country’s dominant online platforms. Characterizing Coupang as a "heavyweight corporation representing South Korea," Chairman Joo announced that the agency is pushing for a new policy that would impose fines of up to 1 percent of a platform’s total annual revenue if it refuses or obstructs on-site antitrust investigations.

For massive tech and retail platforms generating tens of trillions of won in annual turnover, traditional fixed-sum penalties—which previously capped out at a few hundred million won (roughly a few hundred thousand US dollars)—were viewed by critics as mere slaps on the wrist. Large corporations could easily absorb such fines as a routine cost of doing business while stalling regulatory probes or withholding crucial server logs. Under the newly proposed rules, the FTC also plans to levy administrative enforcement fines on companies that deliberately delay or refuse to submit requested data.

This aggressive regulatory stance represents a clear departure from past practices. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, the government favored platform "self-regulation," a policy framework that critics argue left small business owners vulnerable to monopolistic abuse. Now, under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, the FTC is pivoting toward direct enforcement and severe punitive measures. Chairman Joo warned that when a platform abuses its dominant market power, it "can become a lethal weapon against small businesses." Concerns include platforms pressing merchants to lower supply prices or unfairly favoring their own private-label (PB) products over independent sellers. Although some quarters in the U.S. government have raised concerns about potential trade friction regarding strict regulation of tech firms, the FTC has made it clear that establishing fair market order in the domestic e-commerce space is an urgent national priority that can no longer be delayed.

Retail Battles and Live Commerce: Shinsegae’s Omnichannel Blitz

While regulatory pressures build against platform monopolies, South Korea’s traditional retail titans are doubling down on aggressive promotional campaigns. Shinsegae Group, one of the country’s largest retail conglomerates, recently launched its flagship annual shopping event, "2024 SSG Day." To compete against online-only platforms, Shinsegae is leaning heavily into an omnichannel strategy that bridges online platforms with physical storefronts.

Shinsegae is deploying its vast physical infrastructure—including Emart hypermarkets, department stores, and Starfield shopping malls—alongside its primary online storefront, SSG.com. The company has introduced exclusive promotions, such as dedicated deals on Apple's iPhone duo, alongside immersive offline pop-up events and mobile live-streaming sales.

This strategy highlights how traditional retailers are adapting to survive in an e-commerce ecosystem dominated by pure-play tech platforms:

  • Omnichannel Integration: Driving online traffic through exclusive app deals while enticing shoppers into physical stores for experiential events.
  • Live Commerce Synergies: Utilizing real-time interactive video broadcasts to offer limited-time mobile discounts and direct product demonstrations.
  • Traffic Generation: Capitalizing on year-end shopping enthusiasm to create a rising tide for both corporate brands and platform sellers.

However, the scale of these mega-promotions also highlights an ongoing tension within Korean retail. While massive corporate festivals boost overall market traffic, smaller independent sellers often find themselves priced out of prime advertising slots and main banner visibility on major streaming channels, forcing them to find alternative ways to reach consumers.

Grassroots Resiliency: How Local Sellers and Creators Are Fighting Back

Despite the dominance of corporate giants and large platforms, independent merchants and local producers are carving out survival strategies through direct-to-consumer live commerce. Live commerce—a hybrid of home shopping and real-time social media streaming—has become an essential lifeline for South Korea’s regional small businesses.

A recent success story from the agricultural sector highlights this shift. The Jeongseon Blueberry Cooperative, a local farming group based in Gangwon Province, achieved a complete sell-out of its inventory during a live commerce stream broadcast across regional channels. By bypassing traditional multi-tiered wholesale distribution networks, the cooperative demonstrated the power of authentic storytelling, allowing farmers to showcase fresh produce directly from their fields in real time.

Recognizing the importance of digital transformation for small businesses, local governments are stepping in to build supporting infrastructure. For example, the municipal government of Yeongdeungpo-gu, a district in Seoul, recently launched an "Online Channel Growth Program" designed to train local small business owners in live broadcasting and e-commerce operations.

At the same time, the live commerce ecosystem is being reshaped by the rising clout of specialized creators. At the recent Asia Influencer Festival, creator and show host "Dtoje" An Soo-kyung won the grand prize in the live commerce category. Unlike traditional television show hosts who focus strictly on sales pitches, modern e-commerce creators build trust through personal fandoms and unvarnished product reviews. For brands, collaborating with authentic creators often yields significantly higher conversion rates than traditional celebrity endorsements.

The Global Reality: AI Innovation Versus 40 Percent Platform Commissions

The challenges faced by South Korean vendors mirror broader international dynamics across the global e-commerce landscape. Across Asia, e-commerce is rapidly evolving through short-form video marketing and artificial intelligence. In Chinese-speaking markets, beauty brands are shifting away from expensive mega-influencers in favor of Key Opinion Consumers (KOCs) on platforms like TikTok to drive purchases through short-form content. In Beijing, live commerce is undergoing an AI transformation, where generative AI drafts broadcast scripts, analyzes real-time customer data, and powers 24/7 virtual AI show hosts.

Yet, beneath these technological advances lies an increasingly harsh financial reality for sellers. Recent reports from Southeast Asian markets, such as Vietnam, reveal that combined platform commissions, listing fees, and required marketing expenses can consume up to 40 percent of a vendor’s gross sales.

This crippling fee structure is equally familiar to vendors operating in South Korea:

  • Platform Usage Fees: Base commission fees charged by e-commerce operators for marketplace access.
  • Broadcast & Payment Fees: Additional surcharges for live stream hosting and payment processing.
  • Mandatory Advertising Costs: Rising ad spend required simply to maintain search visibility on crowded marketplaces.

When platform fees eat up nearly half of gross revenue, merchants are left with thin or even negative profit margins, despite generating substantial sales volumes. This structural imbalance underscores precisely why the FTC under President Lee Jae-myung's administration is taking a hardline stance against dominant platforms. Without regulatory intervention to curb unfair commercial practices, the digital marketplace risks turning independent vendors into mere revenue vehicles for platform monopolies.

What to Watch Next in Korea's Digital Market

As South Korea attempts to rebalance its digital economy, several critical developments will determine whether small merchants can achieve long-term sustainability alongside tech giants:

  • FTC Penalty Legislation: Watch for formal legislative amendments introducing the 1 percent revenue fine for investigation obstruction, as well as the implementation of daily enforcement fines for withheld documents.
  • International Reaction: Monitor potential pushback from U.S. trade representatives and foreign tech lobby groups regarding South Korea's tougher antitrust stance on platform monopolies.
  • Platform Fee Scrutiny: Pay attention to whether the FTC expands its probe from investigation obstruction to directly auditing platform commission structures, advertising fees, and self-referencing practices.
  • Adoption of Local Programs: Track how local merchant support initiatives, such as Yeongdeungpo-gu's growth program, help small vendors leverage AI tools and live streaming to reduce reliance on costly platform advertising.
