Steering Power in Seoul: How South Korea’s Ruling Liberals Are Navigating Mega-Regional Reforms, Judicial Clashes, and Party Discipline

October 22, 2026

Inside the main conference room of South Korea’s National Assembly on October 21, the leadership of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) faced a delicate political balancing act. Operating as the governing party under President Lee Jae-myung, the DPK finds itself wielding immense legislative power, yet perpetually tested by the realities of executive responsibility. Passing flagship economic development bills, managing restless progressive allies, reining in loose rhetoric among party members, and maintaining a firm diplomatic stance toward key partners like the United States all collided on a single, intense autumn day in Seoul.

For foreign observers tracking South Korean politics, the day’s developments offer a clear window into how President Lee’s ruling party intends to consolidate power for the long haul—and where the fault lines lie as it attempts to balance economic modernization with core progressive principles.

Building a "Structural Majority": Economic Ambitions Meet Labor Realities

Speaking at a supreme council meeting, Kim Min-seok, a senior DPK leader, laid out what he termed the party’s ultimate objective: building a "structural majority." The strategy, strongly aligned with President Lee Jae-myung’s governing vision, posits that long-term power cannot rely merely on temporary electoral backlash against the conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP). Instead, the ruling liberals must demonstrate concrete competence in improving everyday economic conditions to permanently widen their base among middle-class and independent voters.

The centerpiece of this legislative push is the proposed Super-regional Special Zone Act (초광역특구법). South Korea has long suffered from severe economic centralism, with over half the national population and the vast majority of corporate headquarters packed into the Greater Seoul area. The new bill aims to group non-capital provinces into mega-regional economic blocs—such as the southeastern industrial cluster and southwestern agricultural-tech hubs—backed by deregulation, tax incentives, and major infrastructure investments to prevent regional economic collapse.

However, the bill immediately hit a major snag over a controversial clause: allowing local exceptions to South Korea’s strict 52-hour weekly work cap within these new economic zones. Designed to attract corporate investment in emerging technology sectors, the proposed flex-time provision triggered instant backlash from labor organizations and progressive allies.

Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party—a minor progressive party that frequently aligns with the DPK on reform issues—sharply criticized the ruling party. Cho accused the DPK of shifting to the right to court business interests, warning that eroding hard-won labor protections in the name of regional growth was a betrayal of progressive identity. "The solution to regional economic decline," labor advocates argued, "cannot be forcing provincial workers into longer hours for lower pay."

Recognizing the danger of alienating its working-class base, the DPK leadership quickly pivoted to a two-track strategy. While maintaining its commitment to pass the broader special zone legislation during the current regular parliamentary session, Kim Min-seok announced that the party would review alternative bill drafts that strip out the 52-hour workweek exception, seeking a compromise that incentivizes business investment without undercutting labor rights.

The Judicial Impeachment Dilemma: Fast-Track Reformers vs. Tactical Caution

While economic policy created friction on the DPK’s left flank, a dramatic confrontation over the judiciary highlighted tactical divisions within the broader liberal coalition. On the morning of October 21, Kim Jun-hyung, floor leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, personally delivered a drafted motion to DPK Floor Leader Han Byung-do calling for the immediate impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de.

The Rebuilding Korea Party argued that Chief Justice Cho had compromised judicial integrity during the presidency of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached and is currently imprisoned following a tumultuous period in office. Progressive critics accuse the Supreme Court under Cho of showing political bias and taking a lenient stance toward judicial decisions surrounding former First Lady Kim Keon-hee. Seeking to capitalize on public distrust toward the conservative-leaning judicial establishment, Cho Kuk’s party demanded an impeachment vote before the end of November.

Yet DPK Floor Leader Han Byung-do responded with notable caution, stating only that the party would "discuss the matter deeply internally." Despite possessing more than enough parliamentary seats to pass an impeachment motion on its own, the DPK leadership is wary of rushing into a high-stakes constitutional battle.

DPK strategists are acutely aware of the political risks. If an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice is passed by parliament but ultimately rejected by the Constitutional Court, it could trigger a severe public backlash and allow the opposition PPP to frame the ruling party as autocrats attempting to "tame the judiciary."

Instead of immediate impeachment, the DPK leadership proposed conducting a formal parliamentary investigation (국정조사) into alleged judicial overreach first. By holding public hearings and examining judicial records, the DPK hopes to build an unassailable evidentiary record and mobilize public opinion before making a final decision on whether to wield the ultimate constitutional weapon of impeachment.

Disciplining the Ranks: Managing Missteps and Reasserting Sovereignty

The DPK’s careful maneuvering on policy and judicial reform comes against the backdrop of growing internal anxieties over party discipline. Park Ji-won, a veteran lawmaker and political strategist, publicly warned earlier in the day that the ruling party looked as though "its screws had come loose," cautioning that winning power had made some members complacent and prone to unforced errors.

Floor Leader Han Byung-do echoed these concerns, demanding strict discipline from lawmakers as parliamentary audits drew to a close. Han warned against arrogant behavior, verbal slip-ups, or falling into mudslinging matches with opposition lawmakers, stressing that voters evaluate ruling parties on tangible results, not political theater.

This push for discipline was put to the test over a recent public controversy involving Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae and Lawmaker Boo Seung-chan, whose past comments regarding local firefighter finances drew severe public backlash. Rather than issuing heavy party sanctions, the DPK leadership settled on a formal "stern warning" (엄중 경고), while simultaneously taking legislative action to fix the underlying problem.

To address the structural issue—where municipal and provincial governments carry the heavy financial burden of funding local fire services—DPK Lawmaker Lee Un-ju introduced a major legislative amendment requiring the central government to guarantee up to 80% of firefighter personnel costs. The move aimed to pivot the political narrative from internal party gaffes to concrete fiscal support for public safety workers.

Key Takeaway on Domestic Politics: The DPK is attempting to transition from an aggressive opposition mindset to a disciplined governing party. Missteps by senior figures are being met with swift internal warnings paired with corrective policy legislation to protect the party’s public standing.

On foreign policy and national defense, the ruling party sought to project strength and national sovereignty. Han Byung-do expressed strong regret over recent comments made by General Xavier Brunson, the nominee to command U.S. Forces Korea (USFK), regarding authority over the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), emphasizing that South Korea’s territorial sovereignty must be fully respected by its foreign partners.

At the same time, the DPK reaffirmed its long-standing push to achieve the early transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON) from Washington to Seoul, dismissing conservative opposition claims that the move weakens national security as outdated Cold War rhetoric. To reinforce its diplomatic credibility, the party appointed former National Assembly Speaker Moon Hee-sang and former Unification Minister Jeong Se-hyun as senior advisors to its foreign affairs council, anchoring its security strategy in traditional liberal principles of self-reliant defense and inter-Korean engagement.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters the crucial end-of-year legislative season, the ruling party’s handling of these intertwined issues will determine the trajectory of President Lee Jae-myung’s domestic agenda. Foreign observers should watch three concrete markers in the coming weeks:

  • The Final Draft of the Super-regional Special Zone Act: Watch whether the DPK completely removes the 52-hour workweek exception in committee or crafts a narrow, industry-specific compromise to satisfy both corporate backers and labor allies.
  • Progress on the Judicial Probe: Pay attention to whether the National Assembly formally passes a motion to initiate a parliamentary investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-de’s Supreme Court. If launched, the findings will decide whether an impeachment vote occurs in late 2026 or early 2027.
  • Budget Negotiations for Firefighter Funding: Track Rep. Lee Un-ju’s proposed 80% state funding mandate for firefighters during upcoming national budget deliberations, which will test the central government's willingness to absorb local fiscal burdens.
