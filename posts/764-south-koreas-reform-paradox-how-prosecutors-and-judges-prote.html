South Korea’s Reform Paradox: How Prosecutors and Judges Protect Their Privileges

October 06, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape often feels like an elaborate script written by a cynic. Over the past decade, the country has watched massive, polarized street protests and legislative battles fought under the grand banner of reform—stripping absolute investigative powers from the powerful prosecution service, tightening judicial ethics, and promising a transparent democracy. Yet, step inside the engine room of modern Korean governance, and a dizzying irony emerges: the very figures who built their careers defending the old order are now being tapped to lead the new "reform" agencies.

This dissonance raises an uncomfortable question. When politicians and civil servants capture state power, do they genuinely lose their memories, or was the fierce political theatre of the past simply a performance? Recent investigative reporting from prominent political commentary programs has laid bare a web of political prosecutors, compromised investigative bodies, and an entrenched judicial cartel. From recycled prosecutors heading newly minted investigative offices to Supreme Court justices caught padding dinner receipts, South Korea’s ruling elite are mastering the art of wearing reformist clothing while preserving institutional impunity.

The Metamorphosis of Kim Ji-yong: From Prosecutor General to Reformer

When Kim Ji-yong was nominated to head South Korea's newly established Serious Crime Investigation Agency (a body created to dilute traditional prosecutorial dominance), his acceptance speech struck all the right notes. He grandly declared that, if given the opportunity, he would dedicate himself to fulfilling the true spirit of reform.

For anyone paying attention a few years ago, the claim was breathtakingly brazen. Back in April 2022, while serving as the head of the criminal department at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, Kim led a fierce campaign against legislation aimed at separating investigative and indicting powers. At the time, he argued with evangelical zeal that stripping prosecutors of their investigative mandate would create a dangerous vacuum, leaving crime victims unprotected. He fought tooth and nail to preserve the institutional privileges of the prosecution. Yet, with a change in political winds and a new office on the table, Kim has seamlessly transformed into a self-proclaimed champion of reform, offering not a single word of public self-reflection or apology for his past positions.

This lack of accountability creates a crisis of credibility during parliamentary confirmation hearings. While opposition and ruling party lawmakers—including outspoken critics and former police officers on the National Assembly's Public Administration and Security Committee—prepare their interrogations, the public is left wondering if the hearings will have any teeth. Without rigorous questioning backed by documentary evidence rather than polite handshakes, these confirmation hearings risk becoming hollow rituals that grant absolution to unrepentant institutionalists.

Kim’s defense against specific past actions relies heavily on procedural ambiguity. For instance, regarding his controversial endorsement of the indictment of former presidential secretary Lee Kwang-chul in 2021, Kim claims he personally opposed the move during internal meetings. Yet, within South Korea's notoriously hierarchical prosecution service—where the ghost of the "unified prosecutor" principle still demands strict obedience to superiors—dissenting quietly in a room holds little weight. Prosecutors possess a formal mechanism called an "objection document" to formally record disagreement with dubious orders. If Kim truly stood against the political indictments of the past, he must produce the paper trail: the official objection documents, meeting minutes, or transcripts. Without concrete proof, his retrospective claims of past resistance look less like principled dissent and more like self-preservation.

Blocking the Digital Forensics: The Chul-Ah Scandal and Institutional Cover-ups

Kim Ji-yong’s past is further complicated by allegations stemming from his tenure at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office in 2021, a period marked by high-stakes political investigations. Former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae publicly revealed that the central investigative team handling sensitive cases—including allegations surrounding former Justice Minister Han Dong-hoon's phone—repeatedly requested additional budget to secure specialized digital forensics equipment capable of unlocking modern smartphone operating systems. According to the disclosures, those budget requests were systematically blocked at the upper tiers of the Supreme Prosecutors' Office, where Kim served as a key department head.

Because smartphones require sophisticated, version-specific tools to extract encrypted data, denying budget allocations effectively hobbles an active investigation. While Kim’s defenders argue that senior department heads were walled off from direct investigative command, failing to provide the operational tools requested by frontline investigators amounts to institutional obstruction.

The toxic nature of these turf wars was underscored when Han Dong-hoon lashed out publicly on social media, dismissing critics with vitriolic rhetoric. Rather than engaging in a substantive defense of his tenure, the political discourse sank into playground taunts. Meanwhile, figures like Im Eun-jung—a reform-minded prosecutor who has long exposed internal corruption—have pointed out that Kim was deeply intertwined with systemic cover-ups of past sexual misconduct scandals within the prosecution. For victims of institutional abuse, watching a figure linked to historical cover-ups ascend to lead a major investigative agency is a stark reminder that structural impunity rarely yields to superficial rebranding.

The Supreme Court Chief Justice and the Petty Receipt Scandal

If the prosecution service struggles with institutional accountability, the judiciary is facing an equally damaging moral collapse. Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae initially cultivated an image of judicial independence, publicly resisting executive pressure and projecting the aura of a constitutional guardian willing to face parliamentary audits head-on.

That carefully curated image evaporated in a matter of hours. Just as Cho was scheduled to appear before a parliamentary audit, investigative reporting broke the news that the Chief Justice had allegedly orchestrated at least 13 instances of fraudulent expense claims during the first half of the year, effectively using official operational funds as a personal slush fund.

The mechanics of the scandal were remarkably petty. Senior judicial officials reportedly dined on luxury Korean set menus costing about $150 (200,000 won) per person, then manipulated the receipts to falsely log a larger group dining on cheaper $38 (50,000 won) dishes. Faced with undeniable proof of administrative fraud, Cho abruptly canceled his scheduled parliamentary appearance, dodging public accountability.

When National Assembly members interrogated court administration officials during the audit, the institutional reflex to deflect blame was on full display. Officials scrambled to blame rigid government accounting guidelines and unnamed junior administrative staffers for "arbitrarily filling in attendance numbers." This refusal by top officials to own administrative corruption while hiding behind subordinates explains why public trust in South Korea’s courts continues to erode. When the leadership of the judiciary treats public funds like petty cash, ordinary citizens lose faith in the integrity of the gavel.

Compounding this judicial crisis is the looming shadow of high-profile political trials, including upcoming Supreme Court deliberations regarding former First Lady Kim Keon-hee. As the court weighs complex appeals while facing potential statutory expiration dates for detentions, critics openly suspect that the judiciary is orchestrating legal delays to minimize political fallout for powerful figures. At the center of this administrative apparatus is the National Court Administration—an internal body long criticized for wielding immense bureaucratic and political leverage.

Despite persistent promises from progressive lawmakers to abolish or severely curtail the Court Administration, legislative action has stalled. Within the progressive Democratic Party, a timid form of self-censorship persists: lawmakers whisper that alienating the judiciary is dangerous because politicians themselves frequently end up facing judges in court. This institutional self-protection—the quiet understanding that the political class should not cross the legal class—sabotages genuine judicial reform.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates these overlapping scandals, several concrete developments will indicate whether systemic reform has any genuine momentum or if it will be co-opted by the traditional power elite:

  • Parliamentary Confirmation Hearings: Watch whether opposition lawmakers demand verifiable paper trails—such as formal objection documents and budgetary records—from nominees like Kim Ji-yong, or if the sessions devolve into bipartisan compromise.
  • Legislative Fates of Oversight Bodies: Monitor the progress of bills targeting the National Court Administration. Whether the Democratic Party turns rhetorical criticism into actual structural dismantling will test their political will.
  • Judicial Rulings on High-Profile Cases: Pay close attention to how the Supreme Court handles pending politically sensitive trials, particularly regarding detention limits and evidentiary reviews for major political figures.
  • Internal Whistleblower Disclosures: Keep an eye on ongoing statements from reform-minded prosecutors and judicial union members who continue to leak administrative workarounds and expense manipulations, which remain the primary threat to the institutional status quo.

True reform is rarely handed down willingly by those who benefit from the status quo. Until South Korea’s political class is willing to dismantle the administrative shields protecting prosecutors and judges alike, the promise of systemic change will remain an unfulfilled slogan.
