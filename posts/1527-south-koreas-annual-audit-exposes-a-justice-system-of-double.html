South Korea's Annual Audit Exposes a Justice System of Double Standards

October 27, 2026

Imagine standing in a courtroom where the scales of justice are not balanced, but rather tipped by the heavy hand of political survival. For weeks, the South Korean National Assembly has been wrapped up in its annual audit—a high-stakes ritual where lawmakers scrutinize government agencies, unearth administrative failures, and, all too often, wage bitter partisan warfare. As this year's audit draws to a close, it has laid bare a grim reality about South Korean politics under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung: the persistent, agonizing gap between the rhetoric of equality before the law and the messy, selective reality of how investigations are actually handled.

With the December 2 statutory deadline for next year's national budget fast approaching, the legislature is shifting its focus from retrospective investigations to an intense fiscal battlefield. Yet, the final days of the audit committees were less about constructive policymaking and more about exposing the profound double standards of prosecutors and police. From bizarre procedural clashes—such as Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-de sitting without taking the mandatory oath—to manufactured political scandals, the proceedings offered a masterclass in what is currently broken in Korean governance.

The Anatomy of Selective Silence: The Han Dong-hoon Family Resume Scandal

The core controversy capturing public attention centers on the starkly different treatments meted out by law enforcement depending on who is under the microscope. During the Public Administration and Security Committee audit, lawmakers sharply pressed the Chief of the National Office of Investigation over a glaring discrepancy: why allegations surrounding the daughter of Han Dong-hoon—a prominent conservative politician and former Justice Minister often dubbed the crown prince during the presidency of the imprisoned ex-leader Yoon Suk-yeol—were quietly shelved, while political opponents faced relentless, exhaustive probes.

At the heart of the scandal is a web of corporate patronage and academic overachievement that defies common sense. Opposition lawmakers detailed how Han’s daughter managed to secure 50 donated laptops from the multinational corporation 3M, which she then donated in her own name to an orphanage in Chungcheong Province. That engineered charity work was subsequently used to rack up prestigious civic awards, including citations from the Seoul Metropolitan Government and the Incheon City Council. Stranger still, 3M happened to be a sponsor of the very English essay competition where Han’s daughter won a gold prize. The link? A corporate legal executive at 3M and Han’s wife are alumni of Seoul National University’s prestigious law department.

What makes the case truly staggering is the sheer volume of academic output claimed by the high-schooler. In less than a year, while studying at an international high school, she reportedly authored nine English-language research papers spanning an encyclopedia-like array of disciplines—from artificial intelligence and economics to law, industrial engineering, and public health. Experts note that producing even one rigorous academic paper in a lifetime is a grueling feat for a professional scholar, let alone nine across seven distinct fields by a teenager. Yet, despite formal complaints being filed, prosecutors at the Seoul Central District Police and Prosecutors' Office effectively buried requests for international judicial cooperation and closed the case without so much as summoning the high-profile figures involved.

Ghosts in the Classroom and the 131-to-Zero Disparity

The illusion of the teenager's civic-mindedness unraveled further when investigative journalists from MBC's current-affairs program PD Note examined her supposed volunteer work at a local children's center in Gyeonggi Province. Records claimed she taught English every Tuesday evening, but on-the-ground investigations and attendance checks revealed that she was nowhere to be found on those dates. The signatures logging her volunteer hours were, for all practical purposes, ghost signatures—fabricated records designed solely to pad a university application.

This blatant impunity stands in chilling contrast to the treatment faced by figures associated with the progressive camp. Lawmakers highlighted a breathtaking statistical chasm: First Lady Kim Hye-kyung was subjected to a staggering 131 search-and-seizure operations over a minor restaurant bill amounting to just 100,000 won (about $70). Meanwhile, the massive pile of allegations regarding fabricated credentials and corporate favoritism surrounding Han Dong-hoon’s family resulted in exactly zero searches and zero interrogations of the primary suspects.

Even the Chief of the National Office of Investigation conceded under questioning that the workflow of the investigation appeared unusually lenient. When the justice system treats ordinary citizens and political adversaries to scorched-earth tactics while shielding the powerful, public trust in the rule of law evaporates. Under the current progressive administration, reforming police and prosecutors to ensure that the law is applied blindly and fairly remains an urgent, unfinished mandate.

Manufactured Scandals and 'Emperor Golf' Phantoms

While the conservative opposition has tried to keep the pressure on the ruling camp, their tactics during the audit have occasionally devolved into baseless political theater. A prime example came from People Power Party lawmaker Joo Jin-woo, who launched a scathing attack against First Lady Kim Hye-kyung, accusing her of enjoying an "emperor golf" session alongside world-renowned professional golfer Lydia Ko at the exclusive Taereung Country Club.

The accusation, however, collapsed under the weight of basic chronology. Security and course footage showed the golf session began at 11:05 AM, whereas local fire department records proved that a nearby emergency incident wasn't reported until 11:54 AM. To accuse someone of playing golf *during* a fire when they were already finished before the fire even started exposed a careless disregard for facts in favor of a pre-packaged smear. Lydia Ko’s management team swiftly issued a statement denying any such meeting, noting that the athlete was occupied with official sponsor schedules elsewhere, and warned of stern legal action against the spread of defamation and falsehoods.

Veteran lawmaker Park Ji-won didn't mince words, calling out conservative members for turning legislative sessions into paparazzi-style stings. Taereung Country Club has long operated under established historical precedents dating back decades, yet opposition lawmakers weaponized ordinary athletic facilities to manufacture faux-outrage. As Park pointed out, the irony is thick given how the imprisoned former president Yoon Suk-yeol used to bypass closed military golf facilities or slip out through hidden exits for personal recreation during his tenure, without drawing a peep of criticism from the same politicians now clutching their pearls.

What to Watch Next

As the annual audit winds down and the National Assembly pivots to hammer out the details of the national budget ahead of the December 2 legal deadline, several critical developments will signal whether South Korea’s political class can rise above partisan bickering:

  • Budgetary Showdowns: Watch for intense gridlock in the Special Committee on Budget and Accounts as the ruling Democratic Party attempts to fund key Lee Jae-myung administration policies while the conservative opposition pushes for deep cuts.
  • Responses from Investigative Bodies: Monitor whether the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) or other agencies take formal steps regarding the complaints filed against political figures, testing whether the "selective blindness" of prosecutors can be successfully challenged.
  • Legal Fallout from Fake News: Follow up on potential defamation lawsuits threatened by sports figures like Lydia Ko against politicians who use unverified claims for parliamentary point-scoring, which could set legal boundaries on legislative immunity.
  • Reforms to Prosecution Authority: Pay close attention to legislative debates surrounding structural reforms of investigative agencies, which remain the Lee administration's litmus test for restoring institutional fairness.

The autumn audit session has proven to be a sobering reminder that South Korea's democracy still grapples with deep-seated institutional favoritism. Until the scales of justice weigh evidence rather than political power, the promise of true equality before the law will remain an unfulfilled ideal.
