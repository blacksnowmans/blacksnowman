The Dead Cross: Why Lee Jae-myung’s Sliding Polls Threaten South Korea’s Ruling Party

October 28, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, public approval ratings are often referred to as a "blunt-force instrument." In South Korea’s hyper-centralized presidential system, an administration’s standing in weekly polls is not merely an index of fleeting popularity. It represents the sovereign mandate that keeps state machinery in motion. When approval numbers hold strong, ministries execute bold initiatives and the ruling party falls in line. When those numbers crumble, political authority dissipates with remarkable speed.

The administration of President Lee Jae-myung has now encountered what Korean political pollsters ominously call a "dead cross"—the structural tipping point where negative job performance evaluations overtake positive marks. For an administration that began with decisive public backing, the emergence of this inverted polling trajectory signals a severe operational crisis. More than just a routine dip, the data reveals growing fatigue among moderates and an unprecedented fracture within the progressive base that put the ruling Democratic Party of Korea into power.

The Anatomy of the Fall: From Early Honeymoon to the 35 Percent Trough

Every newly inaugurated South Korean president enjoys a post-election honeymoon, and Lee was no exception. From October 2025 through May 2026, survey trackers across major polling organizations—including Realmeter and Gallup Korea—recorded positive evaluations soaring between the 60 percent range and the mid-70s. Both automated response (ARS) and telephone interview (CATI) methodologies confirmed a robust reservoir of public confidence.

However, the reversal arrived swiftly. By August and September 2026, the positive momentum evaporated into an unambiguous dead cross. A Realmeter tracking poll conducted between mid-June and mid-July captured the scale of the collapse: disapproval surged to 62 percent, while positive marks plunged to 35 percent, holding at that depressed level for over a month. A subsequent Gallup Korea survey in August corroborated the shift, clocking negative evaluations at 50 percent against just 40 percent positive.

To contextualize these numbers, at the close of the first quarter of his second year in office, Lee Jae-myung’s positive rating stood at 46 percent in Gallup Korea’s tracker. By comparison, former progressive President Moon Jae-in maintained an overwhelming 75 percent approval rating at the identical milestone of his presidency. While 46 percent might look respectable in Western parliamentary democracies, in South Korea’s five-year, single-term presidency, it is considered the bare minimum threshold required to steer major policy shifts. Dropping below that into the 30s strips an administration of its legislative leverage.

An ancient political maxim often cited in Seoul holds that "the people are the water, and the ruler is the boat" (gunju minsu). The water keeps the vessel afloat, but when a turbulent undertow gathers strength, it can just as easily overturn the ship.

The Progressive Schism and the Rise of a "Lifeboat" Party

The most alarming development for the Lee administration is not the expected hostility from the conservative opposition, the People Power Party, but rather the rapid erosion of its own ideological core. Historically, when progressive presidents have faced low policy ratings—such as the late Roh Moo-hyun during his tumultuous tenure—deep-seated personal loyalty and a shared sense of political identity kept the base anchored. Today, that psychological shield is missing.

The polling shifts within the progressive camp illustrate a striking parallel development: the dramatic rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party, a minor progressive party founded by former justice minister Cho Kuk. Rather than acting merely as an ideological ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party has begun to function as an escape hatch for voters disillusioned with the governing establishment.

This dynamic accelerated following changes within the ruling Democratic Party’s leadership, specifically under Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok. The numbers speak to an accelerating decoupling:

  • Party Support (Automated / ARS): Following Kim's rise to the party leadership in late August, Democratic Party support sat at 47 percent. By late October, it cratered by 7.3 percentage points to 39.7 percent. Over that same window, the Rebuilding Korea Party climbed from 5.7 percent to 8.8 percent.
  • Party Support (Phone Interviews / CATI): The Democratic Party slid from 44.8 percent to 41.9 percent, while the Rebuilding Korea Party jumped from 3.7 percent to 6.2 percent.
  • The Progressive Base Shift: Among self-identified progressive voters, Democratic Party backing tumbled by 13.8 percentage points, falling from 74.4 percent to 60.6 percent. Concurrently, support for the Rebuilding Korea Party among progressives more than doubled, surging from 10.5 percent to 22.4 percent.

Part of this internal migration stems from abrasive intra-party discipline. Under Kim Min-seok—whose own disapproval rating among the public climbed from the 60 percent range to 63 percent—the party apparatus chose to aggressively target internal dissenters. Critics of the leadership were branded as "factional splitters" or conspiratorially tied to the fringe Shincheonji religious movement. Online partisans regularly hurled epithets like subak ("watermelons"—a slur implying someone is green like a progressive on the outside, but red like a conservative on the inside) at moderates. Rather than consolidating unity, this uncompromising posture alienated thoughtful progressives, who redirected their support to the Rebuilding Korea Party as a principled refuge.

The Institutional Trap: Lessons from Yoon Suk-yeol's Downfall

South Korean political history offers a stark warning about what happens when an administration ignores polling degradation and operates in an ideological echo chamber. The most visceral reminder remains the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

Shortly after taking office, Mr. Yoon saw his approval numbers disintegrate to the 35 percent mark before his first one hundred days had even elapsed. For the remainder of his rule, his administration remained locked in a debilitating pattern of roughly 35 percent positive approval against 60 percent disapproval. That persistent public rejection crippled his domestic agenda, led to a crushing defeat in the 2024 general election, and eventually culminated in an unconstitutional attempt to impose emergency martial rule—a desperate bid that ended in his impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment.

When an administration remains trapped in the 35-to-60 structure for an extended duration, the domino effects across government are immediate and predictable:

  • Bureaucratic Inertia: Career civil servants are acute readers of political vulnerability. Sensing an administration on the back foot, ministries adopt a posture of bokji budong ("lying flat and refusing to budge"), stalling controversial regulatory changes and slowing policy execution.
  • Judicial and Prosecutorial Reorientation: South Korea's investigative agencies and courts, which operate with a sharp sensitivity toward shifting power dynamics, inevitably redirect scrutiny toward the ruling faction.
  • Party Fragmentation: As legislative and local elections approach, lawmakers in the ruling party begin looking to their own survival. Factions emerge demanding the president overhaul his cabinet, abandon signature policies, or even surrender his party membership to prevent an electoral rout.

High Voter Mobility: What to Watch Next

The underlying driver of this fluid environment is what political scientists call "voter mobility." The South Korean electorate of today bears little resemblance to the rigid regional blocs of past decades. Modern swing voters, particularly centrists and younger cohorts, hold minimal tribal loyalty to either the Democratic Party or the conservative People Power Party.

This volatility is painfully clear in the centrist data: moderate support for the Democratic Party tumbled from 49 percent to 41.9 percent over the late summer and autumn. When a governing party treats independent voters as an afterthought while engaging in internal ideological purges, these electors disengage or cast protest votes.

In the coming months, several key indicators will determine whether the Lee administration can break out of this downward spiral or slip further into paralysis:

  • Rhetorical Pivot vs. Base Retrenchment: Watch whether President Lee and his leadership team, including figures like Kim Min-seok, tone down hardline factional attacks and offer concrete, pocketbook policies to win back disaffected moderates.
  • The Trajectory of the Rebuilding Korea Party: If the Rebuilding Korea Party continues to poll in the double digits among progressives, it will fundamentally alter the balance of power on the center-left, forcing the Democratic Party into concessions.
  • Cabinet and Staff Reshuffles: A traditional circuit-breaker for a South Korean president caught in a dead cross is a sweeping personnel overhaul in the presidential office and cabinet to signal a change of direction. Without structural adjustments, the current 35 to 40 percent approval range threatens to solidify into an insurmountable wall.

The tide of public sentiment in South Korea moves faster than anywhere else in East Asia. As the current administration navigates a narrowing channel between a rebellious progressive base and an unforgiving moderate center, the lesson remains clear: power cannot rely on past mandates to survive a gathering storm.
