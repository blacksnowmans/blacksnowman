South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach the Chief Justice, Triggering a Constitutional Showdown

November 24, 2026

On the afternoon of November 23, the floor leader of South Korea’s ruling party stepped up to a briefing microphone in Seoul and declared that the nation’s top judge must go. Han Byung-do, floor leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), announced that his party had formally launched a drive to impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae, vowing to finish drafting the impeachment motion within days. Within hours, veteran ruling-party lawmakers were demanding swift removal, conservative opposition politicians were condemning the effort as an abuse of legislative power, and reports began circulating that judges across the country might stage rare collective protests.

At the center of the confrontation is a fundamental dispute over the boundaries between South Korea’s elected legislature and its courts. According to statements from DPK leadership and supportive political commentators, removing Jo is an urgent step toward clearing away lingering remnants of past constitutional crises and restoring public trust in judicial fairness. Critics in the conservative People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main opposition, see something very different: an aggressive attempt by the ruling camp to intimidate the judiciary and shield President Lee Jae-myung’s administration from legal scrutiny.

For outside observers, the clash illustrates how deeply South Korea’s recent political upheaval continues to reverberate through its state institutions. Impeaching a sitting Supreme Court Chief Justice is an extraordinary measure in any constitutional democracy, and the outcome of this confrontation will test the resilience of South Korea’s separation of powers.

The Reckoning Behind the Impeachment Push

To understand why the DPK is taking aim at the head of the judiciary, it helps to trace South Korea’s recent political timeline. The country is currently governed by President Lee Jae-myung of the progressive-leaning DPK. His predecessor, Yoon Suk-yeol, was removed from office via impeachment and is currently in prison. In the wake of that turmoil, the Lee administration and its parliamentary majority have framed the eradication of what they term "insurrection forces" from Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office as a defining national task.

Speaking at his November 23 briefing, Han Byung-do described this reckoning as an "unavoidable historical calling." In the DPK’s view, the Supreme Court under Jo Hee-dae has failed to uphold its constitutional duties during this transitional period. Ruling-party leaders allege that elements of the judiciary either abetted, turned a blind eye to, or showed undue leniency toward unconstitutional acts and insurrection-related cases tied to the prior administration.

Specifically, DPK officials claim that examples of Jo violating the Constitution and statutory laws while performing his duties are "overflowing." Under Article 65 of South Korea’s Constitution, the National Assembly may pass an impeachment motion against senior public officials, including judges, if they violate the Constitution or laws in the course of official duty. The DPK’s impeachment task force maintains that it has identified systematic and repeated legal breaches—pointing to allegedly biased trial management and legal interpretations perceived as protecting entrenched interests—and intends to lay out those particulars in its formal motion.

Beyond settling past scores, the ruling party openly links the impeachment drive to the Lee Jae-myung government’s policy agenda. DPK strategists argue that personnel renewal at the top of the courts is essential so that major reform legislation and administration policies are not blocked by what they characterize as politically motivated rulings. In their framing, judicial independence was never meant to serve as a sanctuary placing the courts above popular accountability.

Sharp Rhetoric Collides With Judicial Alarm

While party leaders emphasized legal preparation, other voices within the ruling coalition turned up the political volume. Veteran DPK lawmaker Park Ji-won took to broadcast interviews and social media on November 23 with a blunt message, declaring that Jo Hee-dae should be "shut up and impeached." Supporters of the drive welcomed Park’s unvarnished language as a reflection of public frustration, arguing that polite persuasion has failed to move a judiciary resistant to reform.

Park’s sharpest fire, however, was aimed at the possibility of pushback from the bench itself. As news spread of the impeachment announcement, reports emerged that some judges were considering collective action to protest legislative interference. Park preemptively dismissed any such move as "blackmail against the public."

Judicial independence is not a shield to protect the vested interests of individual judges, nor a tool to defend their chief; it is a means to protect the fundamental rights of the people, Park argued, warning that judges who step into politics to defend Jo would be abandoning judicial justice themselves.

In South Korea, where judges are expected to speak strictly through their verdicts and maintain strict political neutrality, even informal talk of organized resistance carries heavy weight. Reports indicate growing unease within judges’ conferences, with some junior judges reportedly raising the prospect of collective statements or action against what they view as an encroachment by the legislature on judicial independence. How broadly that sentiment is shared across the courts—and whether it translates into formal action—could become a major flashpoint in the days ahead.

Opposition Fury and Quiet Doubts Inside the Ruling Camp

The conservative People Power Party has mounted a fierce counteroffensive, rejecting the DPK’s constitutional arguments as a pretext for raw political control. PPP lawmaker Kang Min-guk denounced the move as the height of ruling-party arrogance and "madness of power." Other opposition figures have gone further, branding the effort a "self-destructive impeachment" aimed at dismantling democratic checks and balances.

At the core of the PPP’s critique is the charge that this is a "shield impeachment"—an effort designed to protect President Lee Jae-myung and divert public attention from the current administration’s shortcomings by bringing the courts to heel. To bolster their claim that the drive is politically motivated rather than legally grounded, opposition lawmakers point to a glaring procedural vulnerability: the DPK altered its stated grounds for impeaching Jo three times in the span of three days.

To the PPP, those shifting rationales prove that the ruling party launched the campaign first and scrambled to invent legal justifications afterward. DPK officials counter that the revisions do not represent changing excuses, but rather an effort to reinforce the legal brief as their task force uncovers additional constitutional violations and refines its arguments to survive scrutiny by the Constitutional Court.

Yet skepticism is not confined to the opposition benches. Even inside the DPK, signs of internal strain have surfaced. Lawmaker Kwak Sang-eon, a former legal professional, candidly told interviewers that pursuing Jo’s impeachment is "extremely painful." Kwak voiced concern that the move could easily be interpreted as political retaliation against specific court rulings, cautioning that impeaching a Chief Justice must remain a measure of last resort, completely free of legal flaws or emotional impulse.

Kwak’s hesitation highlights the tightrope ruling-party lawmakers are walking. While legal-minded moderates worry about the long-term institutional fallout of a collision between parliament and the Supreme Court, activist groups outside the National Assembly—such as Seoul Candlelight Action—have pressed reluctant lawmakers not to waver, accusing anyone who hesitates of leaving "insurrection sympathizers" untouched.

What to Watch Next

The DPK’s leadership has set an aggressive timetable designed to turn political momentum into binding parliamentary action before the end of the year. Passing an impeachment motion in the National Assembly does not immediately remove the Chief Justice; it suspends him from duty pending a final trial before South Korea’s Constitutional Court. With both branches of the judiciary watching closely, several concrete developments in the coming weeks will determine how far this crisis goes:

  • The Friday drafting deadline: DPK leaders say they aim to complete the final text of the impeachment motion by the end of the week, consulting constitutional scholars to ensure the charges are solid enough to withstand dismissal by the Constitutional Court.
  • A December parliamentary vote: Once the motion is finalized, the DPK plans to report it to the National Assembly and push for a floor vote using its legislative majority during the December regular session, aiming to minimize leadership uncertainty heading into next year.
  • Collective action by judges: Observers will be watching whether formal judges’ conferences convene or junior judges issue coordinated public statements protesting the motion, a step that could intensify the constitutional standoff.
  • Constitutional Court scrutiny: If the National Assembly passes the motion, all eyes will shift to the Constitutional Court to see whether the DPK’s repeatedly revised impeachment grounds hold up as clear violations of the Constitution or fall short as a political dispute.

Whether the drive ends in South Korea’s first removal of a Chief Justice or stalls under legal and institutional pushback, the confrontation has already redrawn the battle lines between the Lee Jae-myung government’s legislative allies and the country’s courts.
