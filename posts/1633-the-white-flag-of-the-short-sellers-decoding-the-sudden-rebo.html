The White Flag of the Short Sellers: Decoding the Sudden Rebound in Korean Tech

October 28, 2026

For weeks, the South Korean stock market felt like a floorless elevator. Investors watched in silence as the KOSPI, the benchmark index of the Korea Exchange (KRX), surrendered gain after gain, driven down by a relentless wave of foreign selling. But then, the atmosphere shifted. In a sudden, explosive move, SK Hynix—the world’s second-largest memory chip maker and a linchpin of the global AI supply chain—surged, dragging the broader market back from the brink. To the casual observer, it looked like a sudden return of confidence. To the seasoned analyst, it looked like something much more clinical: the "white flag" of the short sellers.

The recent rally wasn't necessarily born from a sudden change in corporate fundamentals. Instead, it was triggered by a short squeeze—a phenomenon where investors who bet on a stock's decline (short sellers) are forced to rapidly buy back shares to limit their losses as prices begin to tick upward. This "short covering" creates a feedback loop of buying pressure that can send a stock vertical. For the thousands of individual investors who have been nursing deep losses, this shift marks a definitive end to a grueling period of price correction. The bears have retreated, and the technical bottom is finally in sight.

The Coreweave Catalyst and the Hedge Fund Retreat

The spark for this domestic fire didn't actually start in Seoul; it was lit in the United States. The catalyst was Coreweave, an American AI infrastructure firm that had become a primary target for global short sellers. After a period of heavy decline, Coreweave’s stock suddenly jumped nearly 8% from its lows. This sent a shockwave through the global hedge fund community.

According to data from Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, short interest in Coreweave had surged by 27% in just twenty days. Global short sellers had been feasting on the AI hardware correction, racking up over $1.7 billion in paper profits from Coreweave alone, and another $1.2 billion from firms like IREN and Applied Digital. Total estimated profits for these "downward bettors" had reached nearly $6 billion (about 8.3 trillion won). But when a trade becomes that crowded, the exit door becomes dangerously small.

As soon as Coreweave bottomed and began its 8% ascent, the algorithms flipped. To lock in their massive paper profits or avoid margin calls, global funds began "buying to cover." This global trend slammed into the Korean market. Foreign investors who had been aggressively shorting SK Hynix saw the writing on the wall. They realized the downward momentum had stalled, and they scrambled to buy back the shares they had borrowed and sold. It wasn't a "voluntary" purchase driven by long-term optimism; it was an "involuntary" scramble for the exit. This is the classic "cat with a bell" scenario: all the short sellers wait for someone else to move first, but once the first fund blinks and starts covering, the rest must follow or face catastrophic losses.

During the peak of the recent panic, foreign net selling on the KOSPI seemed bottomless. However, a closer look at the data reveals a different story. While the headlines screamed about a foreign exodus, the actual net selling by foreigners during the mid-October dip was relatively modest, at around $82 million (113.5 billion won). Much of the "selling" was actually mechanical, driven by program trading and algorithms rather than a fundamental rejection of Korean industry. In fact, even as prices fell, foreign funds were quietly accumulating over 7 million shares of Samsung Electronics. When the tide turned for SK Hynix, and $275 million (380 billion won) in foreign buying flooded in in a single day, the market's internal plumbing finally cleared.

A Dream Number: The 79% Operating Margin

If the short squeeze provided the fuel, the fundamental match was provided by SK Hynix’s staggering earnings report. For years, analysts in Seoul had discussed an "ultra-bull" scenario where the company might one day approach an 80% operating margin—a figure many dismissed as a fantasy. Then, the numbers came in. SK Hynix reported an operating margin of 79% on its latest high-end chips, a figure that effectively silenced the skeptics.

To put this in perspective, Nvidia—the poster child of the AI revolution—maintains operating margins in the mid-60% range. TSMC, the world's leading foundry based in Taiwan, sits in the low 50s. The fact that a Korean memory provider is capturing a larger slice of the profit pie than the designers or the manufacturers suggests that the AI value chain is becoming increasingly dependent on "irreplaceable" high-performance memory (HBM). SK Hynix isn't just a commodity supplier anymore; it is a bottleneck for the entire AI industry.

This data confirms that the "fruit" of the AI boom is concentrating in the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) supply chain, where Korean firms hold a dominant position.

Despite these "dream numbers," foreign investors spent the days leading up to the announcement playing a sophisticated game of chicken. They sold roughly $50 million worth of Hynix shares just 24 hours before the report, fueling fears of a "miss" or poor shareholder returns. This turned out to be a feint. Once the 79% figure was verified, those same investors reversed course, pouring hundreds of millions back into the stock. They were forced to accept the "irresistible fact" of the company's profitability. Even for those betting on a broader economic slowdown, the sheer cash-generating power of the AI memory segment became too dangerous to bet against.

The ETF Trap and the Pain of the Retail Investor

While the institutional players are celebrating a successful pivot, the mood on the "street" remains somber. In the high-rise office districts of Yeouido—Seoul's financial hub—there is a sense of exhaustion rather than triumph. Data suggests that the average entry price for many of the 300,000 individual shareholders in SK Hynix is around 181,000 won (about $130). For those who bought at the "all-time high" fever pitch near 200,000 won ($145), the recent 10% bounce is a relief, but it doesn't even bring them back to break-even.

The tragedy of the Korean retail investor is often one of timing and leverage. During the crash, many were paralyzed by fear. Now, as the market begins to recover, many are likely to "sell at the first sign of green," simply to escape the psychological torture of the past month. Unfortunately, this is exactly the liquidity that foreign long-term funds crave. When retail investors throw in the towel at the start of a recovery, they hand over their shares to institutional players who are just beginning to rebuild their positions for the next cycle.

Adding to this volatility is a structural quirk unique to the Korean market: the prevalence of single-stock leveraged ETFs. These financial products, which track twice the daily return of stocks like Samsung or Hynix, are incredibly popular among Korean day traders. When the market falls, these ETFs are forced to sell shares to maintain their leverage ratios. According to the Bank of Korea and Bloomberg Intelligence, during one particularly bad day in June, these ETFs were forced to dump roughly $6.6 billion (9.2 trillion won) worth of shares purely for "rebalancing" purposes. This accounted for nearly 14% of the total trading volume of the two tech giants. This mechanical selling turns a standard correction into a vertical drop. Conversely, when a short squeeze starts, these same ETFs must "mechanically" buy shares to keep up with the rising price, adding more fuel to the upward spike.

What to Watch Next: The Road to December

While the technical bottom has likely been established, the path forward is rarely a straight line. Investors should be prepared for a transition from "price correction" to "time correction." The explosive short-covering rally usually lasts a few days, after which the market enters a period of consolidation as it waits for the next macro catalyst.

Politically, the Lee Jae-myung administration and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea have been closely monitoring market stability. Unlike the previous era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who remains imprisoned following his impeachment—the current government has prioritized "Value-Up" initiatives and corporate governance reforms to reduce the "Korea Discount." While the market remains sensitive to global shifts, the domestic political environment is currently focused on ensuring that this rebound isn't a one-off event. However, the National Assembly (where the Democratic Party holds a majority) and the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition) continue to debate the specifics of capital gains tax changes, which could impact year-end liquidity.

Looking globally, two factors will determine if this rally has legs. First is the stabilization of oil prices. Despite ongoing tensions in the Middle East, crude oil exports from the region have recovered to 90% of pre-conflict levels. As long as energy prices remain stable, the inflationary pressure that has dogged the U.S. Federal Reserve (Fed) will continue to ease, providing a "macro safety net" for emerging markets like Korea. Second is the behavior of "Long Funds"—the massive pension and sovereign wealth funds that buy and hold. Short covering can stop a crash, but only long-term inflows can sustain a bull market.

Strategic Checklist for the Coming Weeks

  • Watch Foreign Net Buying: Look for "spot" buying (holding shares) rather than just "future" buying (closing shorts).
  • The "Gap Fill" Test: Large-cap tech stocks fell so fast they left "gaps" in their charts. These act as magnets for price recovery but also as zones of high volatility.
  • December Macro Events: The next FOMC meeting and U.S. interest rate decisions will dictate the global "risk-on" appetite for 2025.

In conclusion, the brutal price adjustment that haunted the Korean tech sector for the last quarter appears to have reached its conclusion. The short sellers have blinked, the "dream" earnings have been verified, and the mechanical selling of leveraged products has reversed. For the patient investor, the current "pullbacks" after the initial spike are likely opportunities rather than threats. The "vacuum" created by the vertical drop of the past month means there is very little overhead resistance to a further climb. The bears have left the building; the question now is how fast the bulls are willing to run.
