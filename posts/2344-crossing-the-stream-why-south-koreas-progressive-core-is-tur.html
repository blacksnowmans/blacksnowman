Crossing the Stream: Why South Korea’s Progressive Core Is Turning to Cho Kuk

November 15, 2026

For a progressive voter in their 30s living in Seoul, watching the recent seismic shifts in South Korea’s political landscape brings a complex mixture of anticipation and anxiety. Voters who helped launch the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—driven by a desire for procedural democracy, media independence, and judicial reform—are now watching key pillars of the ruling coalition step back from the governing party. Instead, a striking number of core supporters are casting their eyes toward the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk (currently head of the Cho Kuk Institute).

This surge in support for Cho Kuk’s party is not simply the grand entrance of a brand-new political movement destined to replace the establishment. Rather, it represents an urgent warning siren sounded by the core progressive electorate, expressing deep frustration with the cautious governance style of President Lee’s administration and the leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), headed by Party Representative Kim Min-seok. As cracks widen in the traditional party alignment, liberal voters are demonstrating unprecedented mobility—a development that carries heavy implications for the future structure of power in Seoul.

The Poll Numbers Demolishing the DPK’s Dominance

Recent survey data from Korea Information Research reveals an undeniable shift in public sentiment regarding potential future presidential contenders. While Representative Kim Min-seok, leader of the main liberal Democratic Party of Korea, retains a paper-thin margin in overall popularity rankings, a look into the sub-metrics shows that momentum has swung decisively toward Cho Kuk. Within a single week, the gap between the two figures closed dramatically, signaling a qualitative transformation in voter alignment.

The demographic and geographic breakdowns tell an even starker story about where the heart of the progressive base is migrating. Cho Kuk has consolidated a firm lead among voters in their 40s—historically the most dependable backbone of South Korean liberal electoral victories—and has secured the highest support in the densely populated Seoul Capital Area. The fact that the 40s cohort and metropolitan voters are drifting away from the DPK to back Cho Kuk and the Rebuilding Korea Party poses a direct threat to the ruling party's electoral foundation.

A recent poll indicates that 57% of voters express dissatisfaction with the speed of the government’s reforms. That discontent is now being quantified in public opinion ratings. When focusing exclusively on self-identified progressive respondents, Cho Kuk has actually surpassed Kim Min-seok to claim first place. Around 32% of progressives now explicitly state their support for the Rebuilding Korea Party. In South Korean campaign politics, a 30% departure among core loyalists translates into an immediate 15% drop in overall survey response rates, as enthusiastic supporters who usually drive word-of-mouth campaigns begin to pull back.

This trend is further illuminated by tracking poll series conducted by Korea Research for the national broadcaster MBC. In a survey conducted December 29–30, 2024 (sample of 1,003 adults, 16.2% response rate, margin of error ±3.1%p), Lee Jae-myung led next-presidential-favorite metrics with 40%, followed by Daegu Mayor Hong Joon-pyo at 8% and former conservative leader Han Dong-hoon at 7%. Party support at the time stood at 48% for the DPK, 29% for the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), and 5% for the Rebuilding Korea Party. By April 11–12, 2025 (sample of 1,003, 15.5% response rate), President Lee Jae-myung maintained a solid 42% personal approval rating, backed by 59% of voters in their 40s and 55% of those in their 50s.

Yet despite President Lee’s stable personal standing, voters are separating their view of the president from their evaluation of the DPK’s party leadership. When asked who bears responsibility for the current political crisis, 36% of citizens responded that "both the ruling and opposition parties are to blame," while 33% assigned responsibility to Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached, ousted from office following an illegal insurrection attempt, and currently remains in prison. This indicates that the ruling party is no longer immune to public fatigue over political dysfunction.

Hopping Across a Shallow Stream: Rhyu Si-min’s Diagnosis

Prominent political commentator and author Rhyu Si-min offered a clear diagnosis of this polling dynamic, noting that voters choosing Cho Kuk in phone surveys are not yet seeking a permanent replacement for the DPK. Instead, they are using the pollster's call as a mechanism to signal intense dissatisfaction with President Lee Jae-myung and the DPK leadership.

Within the progressive ecosystem lies a faction of "combative liberals." These highly motivated voters expected swift action on key pledges—such as prosecution reform, social safety expansion, and economic justice. Seeing these initiatives bogged down by moderate hesitation within the DPK or establishment pushback, frustrated supporters are wielding their poll responses like a disciplinary rod. It is a gesture born not of betrayal, but of impatient affection.

To illustrate this dynamic, Rhyu introduced a vivid geographical metaphor: the difference between crossing the Han River and stepping across a shallow stream.

"Voters do not view the Democratic Party of Korea and the Rebuilding Korea Party as belonging to different camps, but as different factions within the exact same camp. Therefore, switching support between them is like stepping across a shallow stream on stepping stones."

In contrast, moving between the conservative People Power Party and the liberal DPK requires crossing a psychological barrier as wide and daunting as Seoul’s Han River. But stepping from the DPK to the Rebuilding Korea Party requires almost no friction. If President Lee’s administration and Representative Kim Min-seok’s leadership demonstrate decisive action and deliver clear reform results, these voters stand ready to hop right back across the stepping stones.

Cho Kuk’s camp understands this vulnerability well. During parliamentary elections, roughly 28% of enthusiastic progressive voters split their tickets—voting for the DPK in local constituency races while casting their proportional representation ballots for the Rebuilding Korea Party. If the DPK fails to satisfy these combative liberals, those voters will remain on the opposite bank of the stream, potentially costing the ruling party over 15 seats in future legislative balances.

From Creative Minority to Dominant Elite: Toynbee’s Warning

To explain how ruling factions lose their grip on power, Rhyu Si-min pointed to the historical theories of Arnold Toynbee. Toynbee posited that civilizations and political regimes are built by a "Creative Minority"—a group that earns the public's trust by successfully devising solutions to societal challenges. South Korea’s progressive movement once acted as this Creative Minority, winning widespread authority by fighting authoritarianism and pushing for democratic reforms.

However, Toynbee warned that when a Creative Minority becomes intoxicated with authority, it risks transforming into a "Dominant Minority." A Dominant Minority no longer produces creative solutions to fresh challenges; instead, it uses power to maintain its privileges and coerce compliance. This transformation marks the beginning of institutional decay.

This historical pattern connects directly to Toynbee’s concepts of Mimesis (the public’s voluntary imitation and following of admirable leaders) and Nemesis (the inevitable retribution when leadership fails). When a ruling party relies on past accomplishments rather than present competence, public Mimesis vanishes. In its place comes Nemesis—voters withdrawing their trust and punishing the incumbent elite.

Modern neuroscience reinforces this political lesson. Studies on power dynamics show that holding authority over time reduces empathy, increases resistance to critical feedback, and encourages what commentators call the "idolization of the self." Leaders easily fall into the trap of becoming victims of their own past successes, assuming that strategies that worked in the past will always justify their current hold on power. When a political leadership closes its ears to criticism, its capacity to adapt breaks down.

The Specter of Ouster: Lessons from the Fall of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol

South Korean political history provides a brutal, recent reminder of what happens when a leader misreads their mandate. Former prosecutor Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol rose to the presidency rapidly, but his administration quickly alienating key allies, ousted party leaders like Lee Jun-seok, and attempted to govern through autocratic pressure. Following his disastrous attempt to declare martial law, Mr. Yoon was impeached, removed from office, and subsequently imprisoned.

The tragedy of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol serves as a stern lesson for the incumbent administration. When a president's approval rating hovers around or below 40%, the political power of a "presidential pick"—the ability of a sitting president to hand-pick successors or dictate party leadership—evaporates. Artificial attempts by party elites to push favored figures fail when the broader public senses stagnation.

For President Lee Jae-myung and Representative Kim Min-seok, the rapid shift in poll numbers toward Cho Kuk is a reminder that political capital is fleeting. Governing requires continuous responsiveness, not reliance on presidential authority alone.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea’s political dynamics evolve, foreign observers and market analysts should monitor three specific indicators over the coming months:

  • Legislative Reform Velocity: Watch whether DPK Representative Kim Min-seok accelerates priority reform bills through the National Assembly to appease the "combative liberal" constituency and stem the flow of core voters to Cho Kuk.
  • The Cho Kuk Factor: Track whether Cho Kuk and the Rebuilding Korea Party attempt to build a permanent legislative platform or continue acting as a tactical pressure group to influence the DPK's agenda.
  • Executive Realignment: Monitor how President Lee Jae-myung adjusts his cabinet strategy and policy priorities to address the 57% of polled citizens expressing frustration with the administration's pace of reform.
