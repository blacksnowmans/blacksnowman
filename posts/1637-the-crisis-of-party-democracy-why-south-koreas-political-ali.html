The Crisis of Party Democracy: Why South Korea’s Political Alignment is at a Crossroads

October 28, 2026

For those watching South Korean politics from afar, it is easy to assume that power is simply a matter of grand debates on the floors of the National Assembly or the calculated maneuvers of elite politicians. However, the true pulse of any democracy lies within its party members—the people who form the structural backbone of the organization. Depending on how their sovereignty is respected or stifled, governments rise, thrive, or collapse into ruins.

Today, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces a warning sign: an approval rating hovering in the 30% range. Is this a mere consequence of aggressive opposition from the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party), or is it a result of a deeper, systemic failure within the ruling camp? To understand the risks ahead, we must look at the historical trajectory of party dynamics, the lessons of democratic failure, and the dangerous "echo chamber" currently surrounding the presidential office.

The Cruel History of Conservative Failure: From Park Geun-hye to the Imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol

The history of the People Power Party’s "responsible member" system—a structure that grants voting power to dues-paying members—is a story of ironic self-destruction. Initially designed to empower the grassroots, this system was once touted as a breakthrough. During the era of former President Park Geun-hye, it allowed for genuine, if occasionally volatile, participation. It even facilitated the emergence of Lee Jun-seok, a young leader in his 30s who challenged the old guard.

However, the events that followed demonstrated how fragile this "member sovereignty" truly was. During the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol, the party effectively stripped away its own democratic legitimacy. Because Yoon felt personally uncomfortable with the party representative, he orchestrated a process to remove him. What was most alarming was that the members and lawmakers of the ruling party, acting as mere subordinates to the presidential office in Yongsan, passively accepted this anti-democratic purge.

When the presidential office decided to install Kim Gi-hyeon as leader, they did so against the internal will of the party. When Kim’s popularity dipped, they forced him out as well. This cycle of purging leaders to suit the whims of a single power figure became the hallmark of the conservative decline. Eventually, the unchecked private power surrounding Yoon Suk-yeol and his wife, Kim Keon-hee, led to a total breakdown of state affairs. Following a series of grave constitutional violations, Yoon was impeached, removed from office, and is currently serving a prison sentence. It serves as a stark, historical lesson: a party that abandons its democratic soul to serve a single leader invites its own extinction.

The Danger of Subservience

A party that neutralizes its own leadership based on the moods and private interests of one individual is no longer a democratic party. The imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol and the fall of the conservatives into opposition status were not accidents; they were the inevitable result of turning party members into mere rubber stamps for the executive. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea must treat this as a cautionary tale.

The Limits of Systems and the Strength of Democratic Participation

In democratic theory, Kenneth Arrow’s "Impossibility Theorem" mathematically proves that there is no perfect, universally superior system for collective decision-making. Whether using a primary-based system, a runoff, or internal party votes, every method has inherent trade-offs. The issue, therefore, is not the "perfection" of the rules, but the legitimacy of the process by which they are set.

Within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the dynamic has been remarkably different from their conservative rivals. When the party’s internal rules were adjusted, leading to debates over factionalism, the members showed their teeth. In recent party conventions, a significant 46% of the vote went to non-mainstream candidates, even under unfavorable institutional conditions. This was not a failure of the system; it was a testament to the "collective intelligence" of the members. It proved that the party’s base remains a healthy force for checks and balances.

Looking ahead, the party faces a choice regarding its relationship with allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party. While smaller, specialized parties can act as "icebreakers" or "lifeboats" in turbulent waters, the Democratic Party of Korea remains the only "aircraft carrier"—the sole platform capable of managing national systems and achieving long-term stability. The goal must be to strengthen the Democratic Party’s internal democracy rather than relying on external crutches.

The Trap of the "Yongbi-eocheonga": Why the President Needs Harsh Truths

Political analysts often frame the presidency as a "lonely" job, suggesting that supporters should offer unconditional comfort. This, however, is a dangerous misunderstanding of power. In political psychology, a leader’s primary drive is not loneliness, but the desire to exert their will. When that will is blocked by public opinion or constructive criticism, the leader often reacts with frustration rather than introspection.

Currently, the environment surrounding the Lee Jae-myung administration is growing increasingly insular. A culture of "Yongbi-eocheonga"—a historical term for poems praising the virtues of kings—has taken hold. Critics are often dismissed by hardcore supporters as "internal enemies" who are hurting the president during a difficult time. In such an atmosphere, no aide or minister can afford to speak the truth. If the presidential office remains a vacuum where only praise is permitted, the administration becomes blind to the reality of public sentiment.

True loyalty to a president is not blind praise, but the courage to deliver harsh, data-driven truths. If the administration continues to ignore the warning signs in the polls and isolates itself from critical voices, it risks losing the very foundation that put it in power.

What to Watch Next

  • The "Emergency Committee" Signal: As the next general election approaches, monitor whether local Democratic Party lawmakers begin to publicly distance themselves from the presidential office. This is a classic indicator that the party’s internal cohesion is fraying.
  • The Response to Data: Watch how the administration reacts to subsequent polling. Will they continue to view dissent as "betrayal," or will they use the data to pivot toward a more inclusive, integrative leadership style?
  • The "Roh Moo-hyun" Lesson: The legacy of the late President Roh Moo-hyun is often invoked by supporters as a reason to "protect" the president at all costs. However, the real lesson from Roh was his intense capacity for self-reflection. Watch to see if President Lee Jae-myung adopts this spirit of introspection, or if his administration repeats the mistake of creating an "echo chamber" that alienates the broader base.

The path to long-term governance is not paved with blind defense, but with the ability to listen to the cooling heart of the public. If the ruling camp can restore its internal democratic mechanisms and embrace constructive criticism, it may avoid the tragic cycles of the past. If not, it risks becoming the next victim of its own unchecked power.
