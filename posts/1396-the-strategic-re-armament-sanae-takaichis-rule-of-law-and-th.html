The Strategic Re-Armament: Sanae Takaichi’s "Rule of Law" and the Future of East Asian Security

October 22, 2026

To understand the current trajectory of Japanese politics, one must look past the polished rhetoric of diplomatic summits and into the calculated maneuvering of Sanae Takaichi, Japan’s Prime Minister and a figure who represents the vanguard of the nation's right-wing movement. Her recent alignment with the Trump administration’s opposition to the International Criminal Court (ICC) is not merely a diplomatic spat; it is a signal that Japan’s shift toward remilitarization is entering a terminal, irreversible phase.

For international observers, the question is simple: Why would Tokyo, a nation that frequently champions the "rule of law," choose to undermine the very institution meant to uphold global judicial integrity? The answer is as cold as it is calculated. It is a dress rehearsal for a "war-capable" Japan, laying the legal and political groundwork to secure regional military hegemony under the guise of being a guardian of international order.

The Paradox of Takaichi’s "Rule of Law"

The irony is palpable. While Takaichi publicly praises the rule of law, her administration’s stance on the ICC suggests that international law is only a virtue when it serves Japan’s interests. By siding with Donald Trump’s administration in its pushback against the ICC, Takaichi is effectively building a defensive barrier. She seeks to ensure that international judicial bodies—or any external authority—cannot interfere with Japan’s future military maneuvers or reopen historical grievances that Tokyo considers "settled."

This double standard is a hallmark of the modern Japanese right-wing establishment. They invoke the "rule of law" to pressure neighboring nations, yet they are quick to ignore the authority of international institutions when those same rules threaten their national security roadmap. This isn't just a shift in policy; it is an attempt to rewrite the post-war international order to suit an ambitious, re-armed Japan.

Takaichi’s rhetoric is designed to launder Japan’s image. By framing the country as a "defender of the rule of law," she aims to shed the "aggressor state" label earned in the 20th century. When she speaks of "opposing changes to the status quo by force"—a common refrain regarding the Taiwan Strait—she is not merely making a statement about Chinese aggression. She is creating a justification for the Japan Self-Defense Forces (JSDF) to operate in waters surrounding the Korean Peninsula and Taiwan. In Takaichi’s framework, "the rule of law" is the pretext, and military intervention is the desired result.

The Quest to Delete the "Enemy State Clause"

The most revealing moment in Takaichi’s tenure occurred on the floor of the United Nations. During the 78th UN General Assembly, she formally proposed the removal of the "Enemy State Clause" from the UN Charter. This clause, which identifies nations like Japan and Germany as former belligerents from World War II, allows for international intervention should they show signs of returning to aggressive military policies.

To Takaichi, this is a shackle that must be removed. By demanding the deletion of this clause, she is signaling to the international community that Japan no longer considers itself bound by its post-war limitations. It is an arrogant rejection of the very foundation that allowed Japan to reintegrate into the global order after its defeat. If the clause is removed, the final legal barrier preventing Japan from maintaining a standing military force capable of external projection—a true "normal military"—will be gone.

This is a chilling development for those who remember the history of East Asia. The Lee Jae-myung administration, which has moved to distance itself from the "humiliating diplomacy" of the previous administration—under which the now-imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol conceded on historical disputes and sovereign territory—faces a critical challenge. The current government recognizes that Japan’s ambition to become a "war-capable nation" is fundamentally incompatible with the peace and stability of the Korean Peninsula.

"China Threat" as a Catalyst for Re-Armament

Japanese conservatives have found the perfect foil in the "China Threat Theory." By treating a potential Chinese invasion of Taiwan as an inevitability, Takaichi justifies massive increases in defense spending and the acquisition of advanced strike capabilities. The slogan "a crisis in Taiwan is a crisis in Japan" is used to manufacture public anxiety, paving the way for a pivot that transforms the JSDF into a de facto national army.

However, beneath this concern for Taiwan lies a deeper desire for military autonomy from the United States and a return to Japan’s status as a regional hegemon. Takaichi’s vision is not one of a junior partner in an alliance, but of a leader that dictates the security architecture of the Indo-Pacific. This ambition is driving an arms race that threatens to turn East Asia into a powder keg. While Japan claims to be ensuring regional stability, its actions are creating the very insecurity it claims to be preventing.

The Path Forward for the Lee Jae-myung Administration

The Lee Jae-myung government must adopt a firm, principled stance. The era of the Yoon Suk-yeol administration—which saw Korea concede on vital historical issues—is over. Any attempt by Japan to expand its military influence, particularly at the expense of Korean security, must be met with strict diplomatic scrutiny and international cooperation.

  • Monitor Military Activity: The government must closely watch Japan’s increased naval and aerial activity near the Korean Peninsula, ensuring that "cooperation" does not become a pretext for Japanese military presence in sovereign waters.
  • Refuse Historical Revisionism: Takaichi’s repeated visits and offerings to the Yasukuni Shrine—a site where convicted war criminals are enshrined—are a direct insult to the victims of Japanese imperialism. The government must maintain that no meaningful future partnership can exist without a sincere reckoning with the past.
  • Strategic Autonomy: Korea must avoid being drawn into Japan’s vision of a "proactive" military alliance that benefits Tokyo’s imperial nostalgia. The focus must remain on independent national defense and a multilateral diplomatic approach that prevents Japan’s re-armament from destabilizing the entire region.

What should we watch next? Keep a close eye on Tokyo’s upcoming defense white papers and any further legislative attempts to loosen the interpretation of Article 9 of the Japanese Constitution. If Japan continues to push for the removal of the Enemy State Clause or deepens its unilateral military posturing, the diplomatic tensions in Northeast Asia will likely reach a boiling point. The Lee Jae-myung administration’s ability to navigate this, while keeping the focus on genuine security rather than historical erasure, will define the regional balance of power for years to come.
