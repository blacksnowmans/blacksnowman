How Toss Rebuilt South Korea's Mobile Experience Through Brutal Design Internalization

November 06, 2026

Picture sitting in a sterile corporate lobby in Seoul a decade ago, clutching a stack of physical security contracts. You are a product designer at an obscure Korean fintech startup, and your job today is not sitting at a sleek Mac tweaking vector graphics. Your job is waiting by the elevators, intercepting strangers as they walk in off the street, handing them a smartphone, and sweating as you watch their fingers fumble across a jagged, poorly aligned screen.

Today, Toss is South Korea's reigning fintech titan—a ubiquitous financial super-app where millions of users check accounts, pay bills, and manage investments through fluid, minimalist interfaces and polished 3D icons. Yet the prevailing myth in tech circles is that great products spring fully formed from the minds of visionary geniuses who hit the aesthetic bullseye on day one. The true history of Toss is almost the exact opposite. It is a grueling, unromantic chronicle of technical limitations, missing engineering roles, and agonizing internal transformations. It is the story of how an app widely mocked for its ugly interface quietly engineered a total revolution in South Korean mobile user experience (UX).

💡 Executive Summary: The Brutal Evolution of Toss Design

  • The Toss 3.0 Legacy: By severing ties with external agencies, the team internalized Cinema 4D graphics, boosted UX writing performance by 300%, established rigorous usability testing, and built proprietary fonts.
  • Merging Tech and Design: Toss secured maximum productivity by developing custom tools like the AI-driven 'tosst', the 'Rally' interaction language, and a dedicated B2B tool product designer track.

The Muddy Roots: Crude Beginnings and the Birth of Simplicity

When Buttons Were Sharp and Experience Was Invisible

Mention Toss today, and people think of clean white backdrops and sophisticated 3D assets gliding effortlessly across sleek screens. But a decade ago, design communities viewed early versions of Toss with undisguised skepticism. Industry insiders routinely muttered, "Sure, Toss lets you send money without those suffocating public security certificates, but when are they actually going to learn how to design?" Early Toss app screens were visually unrefined. Main action buttons featured sharp, unrounded corners that looked like they might cut your thumb, and the overall visual details were remarkably amateurish. Yet beneath this rough exterior pulsed a razor-sharp philosophy. The team defined design not as graphic decoration to make screens pretty, but as the art of engineering invisible experiences. Whenever users faced a screen filled with confusing data, the designers ruthlessly pruned away excessive financial jargon and redundant form fields. They focused entirely on dismantling South Korea’s rigid, mandatory public certificate system—a bureaucratic nightmare that tormented every internet user in the country. Every design choice was driven by hard data rather than subjective aesthetic whims. Just as a government focused solely on propaganda while its internal infrastructure rots will inevitably fail, Toss sacrificed superficial polish to pour every ounce of energy into its core engine: frictionless simplicity. By allowing digital novices and the elderly to send money in a few taps without plowing through endless legal disclaimers, Toss championed a deeply democratic approach to user experience.

Zero Front-End Engineers: Writing Markup in GitHub Trenches

The internal working environment of the early Toss design chapter resembled a wild, untamed frontier. Incredibly, the entire early organization did not employ a single dedicated front-end engineer. While back-end developers handled server architecture and logic, the user-facing interface was entirely the responsibility of designers who wrote raw HTML/CSS markup and pushed code directly. Consequently, meetings in Toss conference rooms bore no resemblance to traditional design reviews. Debating brand color shades or typography line-heights was a luxury the team could not afford. Instead, intense engineering arguments dominated: how to optimize Sass compilation, or how to structure common templates to maximize code reuse. The team's public GitHub repository wasn't filled with visual layout files, but with dense text documents and raw code written by designers. Because of this brutal environment, designers had no room to cling to aesthetic vanity. The primary objective was keeping the product functional and error-free in the user's hands. As the scope of financial products exploded, the early obsession with a heavy blue brand identity began causing severe inefficiencies. Designers made a bold pivot, deciding to completely redraw the app's visual identity for the future. They systematically stripped away the heavy blue backgrounds and replaced them with crisp, clean white spaces and stark typography. This painful process of subtraction laid the foundational UI language that would eventually become the industry standard across South Korean mobile finance.

Toss 3.0: The Internalization War and Reclaiming Creative Assets

Mastering Cinema 4D and Internalizing 3D Graphics

If there is one seismic shift in Toss history, it is the "Toss 3.0" major overhaul. Pushing through this massive project required liquidating years of accumulated visual legacy, clunky UX flows, and inefficient code all at once, imposing crushing pressure on the entire organization. "Humanity always experiences massive advancements in science, technology, and art after living through wars," team veterans recall. "Toss 3.0 was that kind of war for the design chapter." The most severe bottleneck was a complete lack of internal graphic design capabilities. Prior to Toss 3.0, high-quality graphics and 3D assets inside the app were entirely outsourced to external specialized studios. This created painful cultural friction. External agencies—specialized in movie visual effects and dramatic broadcast intro titles—could not grasp granular UI requests like "shift this app icon two pixels to the right" or "dim the lighting brightness ever so slightly to match mobile screens." Whenever brilliant agency assets arrived, they looked isolated and jarring when placed inside compact mobile layouts. Exhausted by endless loops of revisions, the design chapter pulled off an audacious move: they cut off external dependencies entirely and decided internal staff would craft 3D graphics themselves. Designers brought in external instructors for intensive tutoring, studying the 3D software Cinema 4D through the night. Initial trial and error was nothing short of traumatic. Unfamiliar with 3D controls, designers repeatedly broke renders and resorted to manually cutting out backgrounds in Photoshop. Their early outputs looked muddy and bizarre. Team members admitted to shedding tears "thick as Chinese glass noodles" out of pure frustration. Yet within three to six months, this grueling self-study created a miraculous reversal. Mastering Cinema 4D allowed designers to produce custom graphics matching the exact Toss tone and manner in a day or two—tasks that previously took a full month of fighting with external agencies.

UX Writing and a 300% Conversion Surge

Another monumental legacy of Toss 3.0 was the establishment of UX writing as a critical pillar where a single sentence could make or break a product. While tech giants today routinely employ dedicated UX writers, mobile app text back then consisted merely of dry, functional descriptions. Through external consulting, Toss ran an A/B test by tweaking the text on bank account creation buttons. By shedding the intimidating formality of traditional financial terminology and using intuitive phrasing that prompted natural user actions, conversion rates for account opening skyrocketed by 300%.

💭 Democratizing Financial Language Through Text

Traditional banking apps, plastered with dense Sino-Korean legal terms, implicitly signaled to users: 'You lack the qualifications to understand this finance.' Toss rewriting financial jargon into accessible street language was not mere marketing; it was a high-level UX democratization effort that dismantled authoritarian barriers.

Before this numerical proof emerged, internal Slack channels buzzed with skepticism. Colleagues warned that the test risks were too high and that sustainable performance could not be guaranteed. If the design team hadn't proven the value of this minor copy edit with hard data, UX writing would have been dismissed as mere semantic window dressing. Recognizing its massive business impact, Toss immediately created a dedicated UX writer role and built infrastructure that now automatically flags and filters confusing financial jargon through specialized internal tools.

From Lobby Paperwork to the Birth of Toss Product Sans

Toss's usability testing (UT) culture evolved through a similarly grueling journey. In the early growth phase of Toss 3.0, specialized user research infrastructure did not exist. Designers used external recruiters to schedule participants, printed physical security confidentiality contracts, and waited in the building lobby to greet participants personally. Bringing users into conference rooms, handing them smartphones over iced coffee, and watching them hesitate on specific screens taught designers invaluable qualitative lessons. Observing where users stumbled caused funnel retention rates to spike, directly translating to revenue growth. Recognizing the immense power of qualitative data, Toss revolutionized its research system. Today, designers can input user segments into a system one day and conduct remote user interviews by the next afternoon. Simultaneously, the team tackled visual consistency by building the Toss Product Sans font and the Toss Design System (TDS). Because financial apps pack numbers, currency symbols, and Korean text tightly together, existing generic Gothic fonts failed to maintain visual balance on small screens. Toss carved out a proprietary font prioritizing numerical width, symbol placement, and Korean legibility, rolling it out across mobile and web platforms. Designers no longer needed to leaf through heavy style guides; applying pre-built components and proprietary fonts inside TDS guaranteed flawless implementation without a single line of friction with developers.

Scaling Beyond Finance and Weaponizing Design Systems

The Eight-Month Dark Mode QA and MyData Home Screen Overhaul

The expansion era following Toss 3.0 coincided with the rise of 'MyData' platforms, transforming Toss from a simple peer-to-peer money transfer app into a comprehensive dashboard managing citizens' entire financial lives. Consolidating scattered bank accounts, credit card histories, and loans into a single app caused information density on the home screen to explode. Designers tested radical home screen structures without constraints. They experimented with an OS-style layout displaying large icon grids resembling a smartphone operating system, a checklist format organizing recent functions sequentially, and radical UX frameworks separating deposit and lending functions. Ultimately, rigorous usability testing proved that returning to core instincts worked best. The team reorganized the home screen around the core pillars users instinctively needed: 'Inquire, Send, and Benefits.' Despite sprawling funnels across dozens of services—transfers, accounts, loans, cards, insurance, cars, and credit scores—designers mapped out common foundational patterns: identity verification, account input, and authentication. This institutionalized collective intelligence meant users could adapt to brand-new complex financial services within a single second. Meanwhile, the polished Dark Mode feature masking the app hides months of grueling labor. Although Apple and Google released OS-level dark modes years prior, Toss took roughly eight months to roll it out natively. On the surface, possessing a robust design system suggests dark mode is just a toggle switch away. In reality, verifying contrast ratios, text readability, and graph color visibility across thousands of detailed financial screens demanded relentless manual inspection. Running quality assurance checks on thousands of screens every single day for eight months exemplifies the obsessive precision required to achieve modern visual stability.

The Future: Confronting Limits and the Next Decade

As Toss looks ahead, the ultimate lesson of its decade-long design odyssey is not that it conquered mobile aesthetics, but that it survived its own operational chaos. The journey from writing raw markup in GitHub repositories and studying Cinema 4D with tear-stained cheeks to commanding a powerhouse technical organization reveals that true UI innovation is built on structural empathy and rigorous data discipline. For international observers watching South Korea's hyper-fast digital market, the Toss story offers a clear blueprint: true design leadership is forged not when everything is polished and beautiful, but when an organization is willing to pull apart its own machinery to remove every single obstacle standing between a human being and their money.
