South Korea’s Diplomatic Dilemma: Navigating a Reshuffled Foreign Policy Team in a Multipolar World

November 24, 2026

In the halls of power in Seoul, a delicate foreign policy rebalancing act is underway. As South Korean President Lee Jae-myung restructures his national security and diplomatic staff, the global geopolitical landscape is undergoing its most profound realignment since the end of the Cold War. A high-stakes summit bringing together the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia marked a visible shift away from post-Cold War American unipolarity toward a complex multipolar order. For South Korea—a country historically caught at the crossroads of rival superpower interests—the strategic stakes could hardly be higher.

Inside the Lee administration, however, recent personnel choices have touched off an intense debate among foreign policy scholars, veteran diplomats, and national security analysts. At issue is whether Seoul is equipping itself with the flexibility required to navigate a multipolar world, or whether it is doubling down on a traditional, Washington-centric alignment just as the global foundation beneath that alliance begins to shift.

A US-Centric Reshuffle Sparks Concern in Seoul

The centerpiece of President Lee’s recent cabinet choices is the nomination of Hong Ihk-pyo to lead the Ministry of Unification, the executive department responsible for managing inter-Korean relations and dialogue with North Korea. Hong’s credentials on paper are substantial: he holds a doctorate in North Korean studies, served as a presidential secretary for political affairs under the late liberal President Roh Moo-hyun, and acted as a policy advisor during the tenure of former Unification Minister Lee Jae-joung.

Jeong Se-hyun, a respected former Unification Minister, noted that Hong possesses the foundational qualifications for the post, combining solid theoretical knowledge with practical administrative experience. Yet, significant concerns linger regarding internal dynamics within Seoul's key decision-making body, the National Security Council (NSC). With the recent departure of key personnel who previously provided a counterweight to National Security Office (NSO) Director Wi Sung-lac, critics question whether Hong will have the political leverage to ensure the Unification Ministry maintains an independent voice amid a powerful security establishment.

The broader cabinet reshuffle has drawn sharp criticism from observers who view the changes as a pronounced pivot toward Washington. Lee Kyeong-ryeol, South Korea’s former ambassador to Angola, argued that the appointments reflect background coordination with Washington, resulting in a diplomatic lineup recommended through close U.S. channels. This shift accompanied the departure of prominent foreign policy figures like former ministers Chung Dong-young and Lee Jong-seok—both associated with the "autonomous" faction that has historically advocated for greater diplomatic independence from the United States.

Kim Hee-gyo, a professor of Northeast Asian Cultural Industries at Kwangwoon University in Seoul, echoed these concerns, warning that a heavily U.S.-aligned diplomatic staff may choose concession over assertion whenever Seoul’s national interests conflict with Washington’s priorities. In the wake of stalled U.S.-North Korea negotiations, where Washington’s active role as a mediator has visibly waned, critics caution that relying primarily on U.S.-centric diplomatic channels risks leaving South Korea sidelined on affairs regarding the Korean Peninsula.

Great Power Bargaining and the Changing Global Order

While Seoul rearranges its domestic foreign policy apparatus, international relations are being reshaped by summit diplomacy involving U.S. President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin, and Chinese President Xi Jinping. While Western media outlets have at times sought to play down the meeting, foreign policy specialists view the gathering as a landmark moment in the emergence of a multipolar international system.

According to Che Sung-hoon, a professor of Russian studies at Hankuk University of Foreign Studies, the summit yielded substantive results, particularly concerning the war in Ukraine. Crucially, the talks laid the groundwork for addressing the dangerous security void left by the expiration of the New START nuclear arms control treaty.

For Moscow, the summit provided a crucial avenue to break out of Western-led isolation and project influence. By offering to increase its supply of petroleum products, Russia leveraged energy security to undercut Ukraine's justification for targeting Russian energy infrastructure. Furthermore, discussions reinforcing Putin’s diplomatic immunity against International Criminal Court (ICC) mandates underscored how practical realpolitik is competing with international legal mechanisms.

This shifting dynamic leaves Europe and Ukraine facing a difficult diplomatic landscape. While Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has expressed a strong desire to settle the conflict before December 25, Moscow’s negotiating demands remain stringent. Russia is pressing for full participation in a revised European security structure, the rollback of NATO military infrastructure to pre-1997 levels, a formal non-aggression pact, and a streamlined negotiation format featuring just a single representative for Europe. As European nations debate the future of their military aid to Kyiv, Moscow is utilizing its positioning to dictate terms.

East Asia’s Shifting Chessboard and Pyongyang’s Calculus

The broader East Asian diplomatic scene reflects similar volatility. At the recent Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, Japan found itself with diminished maneuverability. Domestic political instability surrounding Tokyo's foreign policy route—where public approval ratings plunged to 20 percent before staging a partial recovery—has left Japan struggling to adjust to a multipolar landscape. By relying heavily on traditional alliance frameworks without developing dynamic regional diplomatic initiatives, Japan risks becoming a passive actor in regional dynamics.

For North Korean leader Kim Jong-un, the shifting dynamic between Washington, Beijing, and Moscow offers a clear strategic opening. Observing the relative strain on U.S. unipolar hegemony alongside the consolidation of a counterbalancing Chinese-Russian alignment, Pyongyang sees little incentive to return to the negotiating table with Washington or Seoul.

Instead, Pyongyang appears intent on leveraging this shift to advance its nuclear weapons capabilities. By deepening ties with Moscow and Beijing, North Korea is securing diplomatic and economic outlets to circumvent international sanction regimes. As Pyongyang uses the multipolar transition to maximize its leverage, the security environment surrounding the Korean Peninsula is entering a complex phase.

Beyond Washington: South Korea’s Path in a Multipolar World

In light of these global shifts, veteran Korean analysts argue that Seoul must adjust its diplomatic outlook. Former Unification Minister Jeong Se-hyun emphasizes that the summit meeting of U.S., Chinese, and Russian leaders demonstrates that the era of uncontested U.S. unipolarity has given way to a multipolar reality.

While the U.S.-South Korea alliance remains a cornerstone of national defense, foreign policy experts warn that an exclusive reliance on Washington limits Seoul’s flexibility. In a multipolar system, treating major powers like China and Russia purely through an ideological lens could isolate South Korea from vital economic and regional security arrangements. Instead, experts advocate for pragmatic, interest-driven diplomacy that maintains open lines of communication across all major powers.

Addressing the North Korean nuclear issue will similarly require moving beyond rigid bilateral frameworks. Analysts suggest that Seoul must explore flexible, trilateral diplomatic formats and take proactive steps to restart inter-Korean dialogue. Without taking the initiative, South Korea risks seeing its regional security priorities dictated by external power dynamics.

What to Watch Next

As President Lee Jae-myung’s newly appointed team takes charge, several developments will indicate whether South Korea is adapting to this changing global order:

  • NSC Internal Dynamics: Whether Unification Minister nominee Hong Ihk-pyo can assert an independent policy posture within the National Security Council alongside NSO Director Wi Sung-lac.
  • Inter-Korean Dialogue Initiatives: Whether the Lee administration will attempt to open independent communication channels with Pyongyang or adhere strictly to aligned strategic postures with Washington.
  • Diplomatic Channels with Beijing and Moscow: How Seoul manages its practical economic and security communications with China and Russia following the recent major power summit.
