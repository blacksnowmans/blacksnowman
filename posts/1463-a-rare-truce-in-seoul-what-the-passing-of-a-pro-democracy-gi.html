A Rare Truce in Seoul: What the Passing of a Pro-Democracy Giant Tells Us About Modern Korean Politics

October 26, 2026

Inside the funeral hall of Seoul’s major hospital on an October evening, a scene unfolded that almost felt out of place in modern South Korean politics. Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the main conservative opposition People Power Party stood shoulder to shoulder, dressed in mourning black. Men and women who just hours earlier had been trading ferocious accusations across parliamentary committee benches were shaking hands, speaking in hushed voices, and bowing deeply before an altar lined with white chrysanthemums.

The man who brought Yeouido’s warring factions together, if only for a brief moment, was Choi Hyung-woo. A central figure in South Korea’s turbulent transition from military dictatorship to modern democracy, the former Home Affairs Minister passed away at the age of 91. Known throughout the country as the irreplaceable "right-hand man" of late President Kim Young-sam (widely referred to by his initials, YS), Choi embodied an era of high-stakes, bare-knuckle struggle that nevertheless operated by an unwritten code of mutual respect. His death has prompted an unusual truce in an otherwise scorched-earth political landscape—and a wave of painful soul-searching from Korea’s elder statesmen.

The Passing of Kim Young-sam’s Fiercest Champion

To foreign observers, South Korea often appears as a consolidated, hyper-efficient democracy. But that system was bought with immense hardship only a few decades ago, and Choi Hyung-woo stood at its bloody center. During the authoritarian military regimes of Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan in the 1970s and 1980s, Choi suffered political bans, police surveillance, severe torture, and imprisonment. Through it all, he refused to break or defect to the regime's side.

Choi belonged to the legendary "Sangdo-dong faction," the tight-knit political camp centered around Kim Young-sam. Alongside rivals in the "Donggyo-dong faction" loyal to fellow dissident Kim Dae-jung, Choi helped engineer the Council for the Promotion of Democracy (known locally as Minchuhyup). That coalition was crucial in bridging bitter rivalries to organize the mass demonstrations of the June Democratic Struggle in 1987, which ultimately forced the ruling junta to concede direct presidential elections.

When Kim Young-sam was finally elected president in 1992, inaugurating South Korea's first civilian administration in over three decades, Choi became one of its chief architects, later serving as floor leader and Minister of Home Affairs (now the Ministry of the Interior and Safety). Colleagues did not revere him simply because he held power; they respected his sheer grit. In an age when opposing the state carried real existential risks, Choi carried the standard of the opposition without flinching. His casket is set for burial at the April 19th National Cemetery in Seoul, an official resting place reserved for the country’s revered pro-democracy figures.

An Unprecedented Joint Funeral in a Hyper-Polarized Era

The most striking element of Choi’s passing was not merely the bipartisan attendance, but the organizational unity behind it. In a rare break from partisan warfare, Representative Kim Min-seok, a supreme council member of the ruling Democratic Party, and Representative Jang Dong-hyeok, his counterpart from the conservative People Power Party, agreed to serve as co-chairs of the funeral organizing committee.

For two senior figures currently engaged in an all-out institutional war to jointly host a state figure's wake is practically unheard of in recent years. Today's National Assembly operates in a state of perpetual crisis. Following the unprecedented turmoil that saw Yoon Suk-yeol impeached, removed from office, and subsequently imprisoned, power transferred to the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung. Yet the partisan temperature has refused to drop. The ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party spend their days locked in zero-sum battles over special prosecutor appointments, ongoing judicial investigations, and state audits.

Yet at Choi’s altar, this hostility gave way to collective reverence. Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a heavyweight conservative figure, arrived to pay his respects and lauded Choi’s unbending courage in dismantling military rule alongside Kim Young-sam. Progressive figures, too, paid homage to Choi's role in the historic alliance between Kim Young-sam and Kim Dae-jung. For a few dozen hours, the shared recognition of Korea’s democratic roots outweighed partisan score-settling.

"Enemies of Enemies": The Elders Deliver a Harsh Rebuke

The quiet civility of the wake, however, could not mask the bitter ironies recognized by those who lived through Choi’s heyday. Senior political veterans and retired legislators who gathered at the hall spoke with noticeable grief—not just for their deceased colleague, but for the broken state of South Korea’s contemporary republic.

Several elders bluntly remarked that modern Korean political parties have devolved into "enemies of enemies," treating opponents not as democratic rivals to be persuaded, but as criminal syndicates to be annihilated through legal probes and viral smears. One retired lawmaker sighed to reporters that he deeply envied "the politics of the grown-ups back then."

"In our day, we would physically wrestle each other on the floor of parliament during the afternoon, but by evening, we would sit side by side in humble back-alley taverns over bowls of makgeolli and glasses of soju, hammering out compromises that kept the country running."

That ability to preserve human rapport despite fierce ideological combat has completely evaporated from Yeouido. Today, ordinary citizens—particularly younger generations struggling with soaring housing costs, inflation, and an unforgiving job market—watch a political class that seems entirely consumed by self-preservation and reciprocal vengeance. The elders’ critique was biting: if opposition politicians under military dictatorships could manage to treat their rivals as legitimate dialogue partners, what excuse do today's elected leaders have in a free, wealthy democracy?

What to Watch Next: Will the Spirit of Compromise Survive the Funeral?

Funerals in South Korea often serve as emotional pressure-relief valves for the political establishment. The test, however, is what happens the moment lawmakers leave the hall and take their seats back inside the National Assembly.

As the official ceremonies conclude on October 27 with Choi's procession to the National Cemetery, the temporary political ceasefire will be tested immediately. Observers should watch two key indicators to see if the calls for a return to "grown-up politics" have any real-world traction:

  • The tone of ongoing legislative hearings: Will floor leaders from the Democratic Party and People Power Party moderate their scorched-earth rhetoric and end the cycle of weaponized judicial battles, or will they instantly return to performative vitriol for online partisan bases?
  • Bipartisan dialogue on the budget and public livelihood: With the Lee Jae-myung administration seeking to implement its core economic agenda against a resistant conservative opposition, will the symbolic handshake between co-chairs Kim Min-seok and Jang Dong-hyeok translate into substantive floor negotiations on bread-and-butter economic bills?

Choi Hyung-woo spent his life fighting for an open system where power could change hands peacefully through ballots rather than bayonets. But democracy requires more than just elections; it requires the baseline assumption that one’s political opponent has a legitimate right to exist. Whether South Korea's current leaders honor Choi’s legacy will not be proven by the length of their eulogies at his grave, but by their willingness to sit down and govern when they return to work.
