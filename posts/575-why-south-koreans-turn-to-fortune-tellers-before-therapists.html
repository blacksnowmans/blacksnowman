Why South Koreans Turn to Fortune Tellers Before Therapists

September 10, 2026

When a fever spikes or a persistent cough refuses to clear, few people hesitate to visit a doctor. A sharp abdominal pain sends most straight to a gastroenterologist without a second thought. Yet when psychological distress sets in and daily life begins to fray, that instinctive path to professional help is routinely abandoned. Instead of seeking clinical treatment, a deep-seated cultural reflex often takes over, treating mental suffering not as a medical issue, but as a spiritual failing, a lack of willpower, or even spirit possession.

This striking contradiction was recently dissected on a popular Korean online broadcast panel featuring family medicine specialist Lee Sun-min, pharmacist Jung Jae-hoon, and Dr. Choi Myung-ki, head of the Seoul-based Cheongdam Harvard Psychology Center. Hosted by prominent media figure Kim Eo-jun, the discussion tackled a blunt truth about modern South Korean society: despite having world-class medical infrastructure, the country continues to grapple with a profound hesitation to treat mental health with the same urgency as physical ailments.

The Brain is Part of the Body: Neurotransmitters, Not Moral Weakness

A common misconception is that mental health exists in a vacuum, entirely separate from physical well-being. Emotional wounds are frequently viewed as invisible burdens that individuals must simply endure through sheer grit and time. However, Dr. Choi argues that this separation is fundamentally false. Mental health is brain health, and the brain is inextricably linked to the rest of the body as a single, continuous biological system.

The brain and the body constantly exchange chemical signals. When physical health collapses, neurotransmitter balances are disrupted; conversely, when the brain becomes exhausted, it manifests as physical distress. True wellness requires treating the entire organism rather than compartmentalizing mind and body.

Yet, deep-rooted skepticism surrounding psychiatric medication persists. Many harbor vague fears that psychotropic drugs will cause permanent dependency or alter their core personality. In modern medicine, however, treatment-resistant psychological issues are relatively rare. According to the panel, standard medications are effective for roughly 70 percent of patients, with noticeable improvements typically appearing within a month. If a prescribed medication proves ineffective, psychiatrists routinely adjust dosages or switch compounds until the right balance is found.

During the depths of depression, patients frequently experience moments of profound hopelessness, believing that no treatment will ever work. Statistical realities, however, show that clinical depression often follows natural cycles that eventually subside. The critical challenge is simply surviving the worst of the acute phase. Tragically, many individuals make irreversible choices just weeks before a depressive cycle would have naturally begun to lift, simply because the chemical imbalance in the brain distorts their perception of time and hope.

Shedding the Stigma: A Psychiatrist's Honest Confession

In a rare display of professional transparency during the broadcast, Dr. Choi shared the exact daily regimen of medication he takes to manage his own mental health: 200mg of bupropion, 100mg of desvenlafaxine, and 10mg of escitalopram. By openly disclosing that he relies on serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine regulators every morning, he aimed to normalize the reality that medical professionals are not immune to psychological strain, and that taking medication is nothing to be ashamed of.

The panel also highlighted how language itself can distort public understanding of mental illness. The Korean word for depression carries historical roots that overemphasize visible sadness and crying. In clinical reality, true depression is frequently characterized not by tears, but by profound stagnation, lethargy, and anhedonia—an inability to experience pleasure in anything. Waking up feels grueling, everyday tasks lose all joy, and the physical exhaustion of a depleted nervous system can manifest as unexplained body aches and persistent fatigue.

Even high-functioning individuals who maintain successful professional lives often suffer from severe internal depletion. Impulsive or high-energy personalities may hide their distress during social hours, only to face suffocating panic when left alone in the dark. Misattributing these chemical lows entirely to external stressors like work pressure or family friction misses the core issue: a depleted brain magnifying ordinary stress far beyond its actual weight.

The Real Competitors: Fortune Tellers and Drinking Establishments

South Korea boasts near-universal healthcare access and a dense network of local clinics, making physical medical care remarkably easy to obtain. Yet the psychological barrier to entering a psychiatric clinic remains remarkably high. When South Koreans experience emotional distress, healthcare utilization statistics and expert observations reveal a striking cultural hierarchy of coping mechanisms: the number one place people visit before seeing a psychiatrist is a fortune teller, followed closely by drinking establishments, and finally, religious leaders.

When life feels profoundly unstable and the future looks opaque, many bypass medical clinics entirely in favor of traditional fortunetelling. Women frequently seek guidance from shamans or astrologers, while men tend to numb their anxieties at local drinking spots. While these coping rituals offer temporary emotional crutches, relying on them as primary interventions often delays necessary medical care until individuals are completely exhausted, financially drained, and emotionally bankrupt.

This reliance on the supernatural to manage invisible anxieties runs deep in Korean culture, occasionally surfacing even at the highest levels of political and corporate decision-making. Treating chemical imbalances and neurochemical dips as spiritual punishments or bad luck prevents individuals from accessing fast, scientifically proven treatments that could resolve their symptoms in a matter of months. Framing mental health care as an extraordinary, taboo event rather than routine medical maintenance remains one of the society's most persistent roadblocks.

Anxiety, Compulsions, and the Spectrum of Modern Stress

Beyond depression, modern life is increasingly shaped by anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive tendencies, and social discomfort. While double-checking whether a front door is locked is an ordinary human habit, severe obsessive-compulsive patterns can trap individuals in catastrophic loops of imagination, forcing them to turn back home repeatedly out of sheer terror.

Similarly, social anxiety leads many to actively avoid gatherings, networking events, and casual personal relationships. Dr. Choi candidly noted his own struggles with social discomfort, explaining how the dread of ambiguous social interactions often drives individuals to isolate themselves. When people with high social anxiety do marry, it is frequently either because an exceptionally outgoing partner pursued them aggressively, or because the fear of lifelong solitude outweighed their discomfort with social exposure.

These conditions—ranging from generalized anxiety and PTSD to clinical depression and social phobias—are not bizarre curses reserved for a broken few. They are common neural signals indicating that the nervous system is overwhelmed. Without stabilizing the emotional core, physical health inevitably deteriorates alongside mental wellness.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea continues to confront high rates of chronic stress and psychological distress, several practical shifts deserve close attention in the near future:

  • Institutional Shifts in Workplace Mental Health: Watch for emerging corporate wellness policies and government initiatives aimed at decoupling psychiatric treatment records from employment background checks, which currently deter many workers from seeking early care.
  • Public Health Campaigns: Monitor whether public health agencies begin framing mental health clinics as primary care destinations rather than specialized facilities of last resort.
  • Integration of Primary Care: Observe if family medicine clinics increasingly adopt baseline mental health screenings during routine checkups, effectively bridging the gap between physical and psychological medicine.

Ultimately, treating the mind with the same casual, preventative care as the body requires dismantling generations of social stigma. Acknowledging brain fatigue and stepping into a clinic is not a sign of moral failure—it is the most practical step toward preserving one's well-being in a high-pressure society.
