The Cracks in the Coalition: President Lee Jae-myung’s Falling Support and the Civil War Within the Democratic Party

November 17, 2026

Imagine standing in the central lobby of the National Assembly in Seoul, where the air is thick with the scent of floor wax and the low hum of hurried conversations. Usually, the lawmakers of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's ruling liberal party, walk these halls with the confidence of a supermajority. They are the party of President Lee Jae-myung, the man who rose to power after the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. But lately, that confidence has been replaced by a palpable sense of anxiety. The latest polling numbers have just hit the group chats of every parliamentary aide, and the news is grim: the "unshakeable" base is shaking.

For months, the Lee Jae-myung administration enjoyed the momentum of a "structural majority"—a belief that the political landscape had shifted permanently in favor of the liberals. However, a series of recent surveys suggests the honeymoon period has ended abruptly. According to a Gallup Korea poll released on November 13, President Lee’s approval rating has dipped to 39%, while his negative rating has climbed to 50%. More concerning for the party is a Realmeter survey from November 16, which shows the Democratic Party’s support falling to 34.6%, trailing the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), by 8.4 percentage points.

This is not just a minor dip in the polls; it is a signal of a deeper, more volatile fragmentation. While the opposition celebrates, the ruling party is turning inward, consumed by a civil war between traditional power players and a new, fiercely loyalist faction. At stake is not just the President's legislative agenda, but the very identity of the Korean liberal movement.

The Wallet and the Ballot: Why the 30s Are Walking Away

To understand why President Lee’s numbers are sliding, one must look past the political rhetoric and into the bank accounts of South Korea’s 30-somethings. This demographic was instrumental in the party's recent victories, yet they are now the most volatile segment of the electorate. While core supporters in their 40s and 50s remain relatively steadfast, the 30s have seen a sharp decline in their approval of the administration.

The primary driver is economic anxiety, specifically in the housing and stock markets. Despite the government’s efforts, the rental market in the Seoul metropolitan area remains unstable, with rising "jeonse" (large lump-sum deposit) costs putting immense pressure on young families. Furthermore, a specific financial crisis has hit this demographic hard: the massive loss in leveraged Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs). Analysts estimate that individual investors have lost approximately 54 trillion won (about $40 billion) in single-stock leveraged ETFs, with the 30-somethings bearing the brunt of these losses. For a generation that viewed asset formation through the stock market as their only path to middle-class stability, these losses feel like a betrayal by the current economic environment.

Political controversies have also taken a toll. In late September, a controversy erupted over the "withdrawal of indictments" related to cases involving the President himself. For many swing voters, this revived concerns about judicial fairness. When Gallup asked respondents for their reasons for disapproval, real estate policy and the handling of the prosecution topped the list. Even the administration's plan to utilize 63 trillion won (about $46 billion) in excess tax revenue for welfare programs—a move designed to boost popularity—has struggled to gain traction in the face of these larger systemic frustrations. When the "wallet" feels empty, grand policy gestures often fall on deaf ears.

The 'New Lee Jae-myung' and the War on the Old Guard

Inside the Democratic Party, the falling polls have triggered a search for scapegoats, leading to the rise of what commentators call the "New Lee Jae-myung" phenomenon. This represents a shift in political culture where loyalty to the leader is prioritized over traditional party institutions. This shift has created a rift between the President’s "core" loyalists and the established figures of the liberal wing—a group often derided by hardliners with the derogatory acronym "Mun-Jo-Teol-Le-Yo."

This nickname refers to a collection of influential figures: former President Moon Jae-in, former Justice Minister Cho Kuk (who now leads the minor liberal Rebuilding Korea Party), pro-government broadcaster Kim Ou-joon, and senior lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae and Rhyu Si-min. These individuals were once the architects of the liberal coalition, but they are now being targeted by "New Lee" supporters who believe the old guard is either too soft or secretly undermining the President to protect their own interests.

The conflict has moved from the shadows to the forefront of party business. A major flashpoint occurred during the run-up to the party’s national convention, when Kim Min-seok, a member of the party’s Supreme Council (the top decision-making body), alleged a "secret alliance" between anti-Lee factions and the Shincheonji Church, a controversial religious group often used as a political bogeyman in Korea. When the allegations failed to produce evidence, Kim offered a lukewarm apology in a private meeting, sparking a furious response from Jung Chung-rae. Jung, a veteran firebrand, publicly blasted Kim, demanding a public apology and accusing him of creating a "guilty" narrative for the party's own members. The spectacle of two senior leaders trading insults over religious conspiracies has left many moderate voters bewildered.

Filter Bubbles and the Death of Internal Criticism

Perhaps the most dangerous aspect of this internal conflict is the digital environment in which it thrives. Political analysts have pointed to the "Filter Bubble" and "Echo Chamber" effects as key drivers of the party's current dysfunction. On platforms like YouTube, supporters of the President are increasingly cocooned in content that reinforces their views while demonizing any form of internal dissent.

This creates a psychological mechanism known as "homophily," where individuals only interact with those who share their opinions. Within the Democratic Party’s online community, any criticism of the President—even if intended to help the party—is often met with immediate labels of "traitor" or "spy for the conservatives." This pressure forces moderate voices to stay silent, creating a false sense of total unity that masks growing cracks in the foundation.

This echo chamber also leads to a double standard regarding objective data. When approval ratings are high, the base praises the "collective intelligence of the people." When they drop, as they have now, the same supporters often dismiss the polls as "fabricated" or "manipulated by the opposition." By rejecting the sensors that detect political danger, the party risks flying blind into the next election cycle. As one media critic noted, "If you only listen to the echoes of your own voice, you lose the ability to hear the actual concerns of the public."

The Road to 2027: What to Watch Next

The Democratic Party now finds itself at a crossroads. To regain the trust of the 30s demographic and the broader public, the administration must move beyond internal power struggles and deliver tangible economic relief. However, the path forward is cluttered with looming obstacles.

First, watch the merger negotiations with the Rebuilding Korea Party. Led by Cho Kuk, this smaller party represents a more radical liberal fringe. While some within the Democratic Party, like Jung Chung-rae, are pushing for an early merger by 2027 to solidify the liberal base, others fear that bringing Cho Kuk’s controversial baggage into the fold will permanently alienate moderate voters. The timing and terms of this potential union will be a major indicator of whether the party is choosing "purity" over "expansion."

Second, keep an eye on the legislative battle over the "Supplementary Investigative Power" of the prosecution. This technical legal dispute has become a proxy war for the party’s soul. Hardliners want to strip the prosecution of all powers immediately, while more pragmatic elements within the government are signaling a more cautious, step-by-step approach. How this is resolved will tell us who really holds the steering wheel in the Blue House.

Finally, the most critical variable will be the economic data in the first quarter of next year. If the housing market remains frozen and the losses in the retail investment sector continue to mount, no amount of political maneuvering will stop the bleeding of support. President Lee Jae-myung rose to power on a promise of competence and results; his survival depends on proving he can still deliver them to a public that is running out of patience.
