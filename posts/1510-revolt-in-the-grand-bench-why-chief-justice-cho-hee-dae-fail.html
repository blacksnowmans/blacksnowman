Revolt in the Grand Bench: Why Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae Failed to Clear Kim Keon-hee

October 26, 2026

Behind the heavy mahogany doors of South Korea’s Supreme Court building in southern Seoul, the Grand Bench gathered in what was supposed to be a decisive closed-door deliberation. The question on the table was momentous: whether to clear and release Kim Keon-hee, the wife of ousted former president Yoon Suk-yeol (who was removed by impeachment and is currently serving prison time). For weeks, judicial observers had speculated that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae—appointed under Yoon's administration—would marshal conservative justices on the high court to deliver a sweeping acquittal, effectively drawing a line under the sprawling scandals that brought down the previous regime. Instead, Cho's offensive hit a concrete wall of judicial dissent.

When the internal vote counts were tallied, Cho found himself stranded. With one seat on the 14-member court left vacant due to an ongoing standoff with the executive branch, 13 justices took part in the hearing. Securing a binding majority ruling required at least seven votes. Cho managed to muster only four, including his own. The dramatic breakdown inside the en banc panel has sent shockwaves through Seoul’s political landscape, exposing an open fracture within the judiciary and igniting what may become the most aggressive push for court overhaul since South Korea’s democratization.

The Arithmetic of Dissent: How the 13-Justice Panel Fractured

In South Korea's highest appellate jurisdiction, major constitutional and politically charged cases are referred from smaller three- or four-judge divisions to the Grand Bench (Jeon-won Hap-ui-che), presided over by the Chief Justice. Here, setting precedent requires an absolute majority of participating justices. The stakes surrounding Kim Keon-hee’s charges—which center on alleged stock manipulation involving Deutsch Motors and illicit favors regarding free political polling worth around 270 million won (roughly $200,000) courtesy of political broker Myung Tae-kyun—are immense.

According to accounts emerging from the court’s internal deliberations, Chief Justice Cho championed an outright acquittal and immediate release scenario, aiming to end Kim's protracted legal battles. Joining Cho were three conservative-leaning judges: Justice Oh Seok-joon, Justice Eom Sang-phil, and Justice Park Young-jae. Together, this bloc argued in favor of tossing out the charges, mirroring the conservative legal stance that sought to dismantle the case against Yoon Suk-yeol's inner circle.

However, Cho's push suffered a fatal breakdown in the middle:

  • The Acquittal Bloc (4 Votes): Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, alongside Justices Oh Seok-joon, Eom Sang-phil, and Park Young-jae.
  • The Firm Guilty Dissent (3 Votes): Justices Oh Kyung-mi, Kim Sung-soo, and Lee Soo-yeon openly pushed back, arguing on rigorous evidentiary and doctrinal grounds that Kim is guilty.
  • The Cautious Center (6 Votes): The remaining justices either directly opposed Cho's motion or took an evasive posture, refusing to join an expedited acquittal package.

Falling three votes shy of the needed threshold of seven, Cho’s plan collapsed. Rather than projecting an image of institutional authority, the failed maneuver exposed the limits of conservative influence within the top court. Observers within the judicial sphere described the outcome as a stunning miscalculation, proving that despite heavy administrative hierarchy, Cho could not simply command high court judges to rubber-stamp an acquittal.

Elite Enclaves: The High Court Reversal Dilemma

To understand why this en banc deadlock has caused such public fury, one must look at a recurring pattern in recent Korean jurisprudence: the jarring dissonance between district trial courts and mid-level appellate tribunals. In trial after trial involving powerful conservative figures, initial convictions by lower courts have repeatedly been softened or outright dismantled upon reaching the Seoul High Court.

In cases surrounding Yoon Suk-yeol and his former top lieutenants—such as former Justice Minister Park Sung-jae, former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, and former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min—district courts handed down severe penalties based on voluminous evidence. Yet, at the appellate level, the Seoul High Court repeatedly chipped away at sentences or handed down surprise acquittals, such as overturning an initial two-year prison sentence in related proceedings.

Critics point to the entrenched elitism of the Seoul High Court, long regarded as the pinnacle of the judicial bureaucracy. Judges assigned to this bench are among the senior-most career jurists in the nation. Critics allege that an insular, conservative institutional culture leads these judges to insulate state power elites, treating political heavyweights with a leniency that ordinary citizens never experience. The attempt to orchestrate a similar wholesale reversal at the Supreme Court was seen as the ultimate attempt to deploy this appellate shield for Kim Keon-hee.

Thirty Years of High Court Precedent vs. Current Politicization

The Grand Bench was not always viewed through the prism of partisan warfare. Between 1993 and 2023, the Supreme Court’s Grand Bench handed down 498 rulings across successive leadership eras: Yoon Kwan (104 cases), Choi Jong-young (65 cases), Lee Yong-hoon (97 cases), Yang Sung-tae (116 cases), and Kim Myeong-su (116 cases).

During the tenures of Chief Justices Lee Yong-hoon and Kim Myeong-su, the rate of unanimous decisions fell below 50 percent. That decline was widely hailed by legal scholars as a sign of institutional health; it reflected ideological diversity, vibrant debate between majority and minority opinions, and a willingness to challenge entrenched administrative doctrines to protect civil liberties. Those panels wrestled with societal transformations and the protection of minority rights.

Under Cho Hee-dae, however, that legacy of robust legal debate is increasingly perceived as being replaced by factional maneuvering. Cho’s institutional standing has also been undermined by an unprecedented, bitter feud with the executive branch. A Supreme Court vacancy has lingered for more than 200 days because Cho abruptly rejected President Lee Jae-myung’s request to re-nominate candidates after the court’s recommendation council proposed Judge Son Bong-ki of the Daegu District Court to replace former Justice Roh Tae-ak.

Cho insisted that the executive cannot interfere with the Chief Justice’s constitutional recommendation authority. Yet legal scholars and parliamentary critics point out that this intransigence effectively paralyzes the appointment process, disregarding the President's constitutional appointment power and the National Assembly’s confirmation authority. Coupled with Cho's refusal to testify before parliament during audits, the standoff has left the top court short-handed at a critical juncture in the country's legal history.

The Coming Storm: Dismantling the National Court Administration

With Cho’s en banc gambit in disarray, lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), led by Parliamentary Legislation and Judiciary Committee Chair Seo Young-kyo alongside progressive lawmakers like Son Sol, are moving aggressively toward institutional retaliation. For the DPK, this episode confirms that the court cannot be reformed from within.

The legislative battleground is now coalescing around several far-reaching reforms:

  • Abolishing the National Court Administration (NCA): The NCA (Beobwon Haengjeongcheo) has long been criticized as the central nervous system of judicial politics—a bureaucratic pipeline where elite judges manage court administrative, budgetary, and personnel decisions to enforce loyalty. Reformers plan to dismantle it and erect a new "Judicial Administration Agency" comprising non-judicial legal professionals and civic representatives to disperse managerial power.
  • Curbing "Former-Judge Privilege" (Jeon-gwan Ye-u): To combat the lucrative revolving door where former justices charge exorbitant retainers to lobby their ex-colleagues, proposed legislation would slap a mandatory five-year ban on retired Supreme Court justices from representing clients in Supreme Court cases.
  • Mandatory Judicial Accountability: Raising disciplinary ceilings for judges who violate ethical neutrality—shifting maximum penalties from light reprimands to substantive suspensions of one to two years—while empowering local general assemblies of rank-and-file judges to check the appointments of court presidents.

What to Watch Next

The immediate consequence of the Grand Bench’s deadlock is that Kim Keon-hee’s legal jeopardy remains fully unresolved. Because the court failed to reach a seven-vote majority for an acquittal, the case cannot be neatly dismissed. The justices must now either re-deliberate, allow the underlying guilty findings from lower proceedings to hold, or remand the cases back to the lower courts—a humiliating retreat for the Chief Justice's circle.

Concurrently, the political spotlight will turn to the National Assembly. Armed with the revelation of the 4-to-3-to-6 split, the Democratic Party-led majority is poised to accelerate impeachment discussions against Chief Justice Cho himself, framing his behind-the-scenes maneuvering as an attempted "judicial coup" against the rule of law. If the ruling coalition tables the NCA dissolution bill and impeachment articles simultaneously, South Korea's judicial branch will face its most profound institutional confrontation in modern democratic history.
