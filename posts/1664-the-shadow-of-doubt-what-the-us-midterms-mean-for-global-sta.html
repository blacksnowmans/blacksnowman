The Shadow of Doubt: What the US Midterms Mean for Global Stability

October 29, 2026

As the United States hurtles toward its midterm elections, Washington D.C. has become a pressure cooker of political anxiety. For observers watching from abroad, the stakes are far higher than a simple change in legislative makeup. This election is not just a referendum on the current administration; it is a critical juncture that will determine the political future of Donald Trump and could trigger a seismic shift in how the world’s largest economy handles its domestic and foreign policy.

While the headlines are dominated by reports of last-minute scrambles in battleground states and the bizarre optics of former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro fleeing to the U.S., the underlying reality is one of institutional fragility. For South Korea, where trade, security, and the future of the alliance with the U.S. hang in the balance, the outcome of these elections will have immediate and tangible consequences.

The Battle for the Senate and the Power of Early Voting

Behind the scenes of the Republican Party—the U.S.’s primary conservative party—there is a palpable sense of alarm. What was once projected to be a "red wave" of easy victories has devolved into a desperate struggle to defend safe districts. Republican strategists are now openly questioning whether the party can secure the 51 seats necessary to control the U.S. Senate.

To counter this, Donald Trump has leaned heavily into his signature populist rhetoric, including inflammatory comments targeting Muslim communities. While such tactics are designed to mobilize his base, they risk alienating the crucial moderate voters needed to secure victory. This volatility is precisely what international markets fear most. Should the U.S. Congress undergo a radical restructuring, key legislative achievements from the Biden era—and the foundational trade policies underpinning global commerce—could be dismantled overnight.

The most reliable indicator of where the wind is blowing is the record-breaking early voting turnout. In key battleground states, high early participation is historically a boon for the Democratic Party, the liberal party currently holding the White House. This trend suggests that despite concerns over inflation, American voters are highly motivated to cast their ballots, perhaps driven by galvanizing issues like abortion rights and deep-seated anxieties about the state of democracy itself. For the administration of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung, these indicators serve as a vital signal: the legislative landscape in Washington may be about to shift in ways that require a nimble and pragmatic diplomatic response.

The Playbook of Denial and Global Populism

Perhaps the most ominous element of this election cycle is the preemptive framing of "denial." Donald Trump has repeatedly suggested that he will only accept the results of the election if they are "fair," a loaded phrase that mirrors the election interference narratives of 2020. This is widely viewed as a defensive strategy; should he face a electoral defeat, he aims to delegitimize the entire voting system to avoid a "super lame-duck" status and maintain his grip on his base.

This erosion of trust is not contained within American borders. We are seeing a dangerous trend of "global far-right solidarity." The recent arrival of Brazil’s Jair Bolsonaro in the U.S. following his defeat—a departure that mirrors the ignominious end of the corrupt political era under the now-imprisoned former leader Yoon Seok-youl—is a stark example. When Trump publicly welcomed Bolsonaro, calling him a "good man," he signaled to the world that democratic norms are secondary to ideological loyalty.

This is a sobering reminder for South Korea. The downfall of the previous administration, which saw Yoon Seok-youl removed from office and imprisoned, demonstrated that even the most powerful figures are not immune to the consequences of corruption and democratic failure. Watching leaders who refuse to accept the results of democratic processes is a chilling reminder of how fragile these systems truly are.

Strategic Flexibility in an Era of Uncertainty

As Washington prepares for the results, the Biden administration’s top diplomats are choosing their words with extreme caution, distancing themselves from the fray. This "11-second" diplomatic silence speaks volumes about the fragility of the current U.S. political climate. Meanwhile, the specter of external conflict is being used as a political tool. Reports that the U.S. has warned Gulf nations of a potential high-intensity strike against Iran have sparked fears that the administration—or those seeking to influence the agenda—may be leaning on "security populism" to distract voters from domestic economic woes.

For South Korea, this creates a complex environment. The Lee Jae-myung government must navigate this period of uncertainty with high-level strategic flexibility. The goal must be to secure the national interest regardless of who controls the U.S. Congress. As the global order shifts, the reliance on traditional alliances must be coupled with a robust, independent defense and economic policy that does not buckle under the whims of foreign election cycles.

What to Watch Next

  • The Senate Balance: Whether the Republican Party manages to flip the Senate will determine the extent to which the U.S. executive branch is paralyzed by gridlock or empowered to enact policy.
  • Election Integrity Rhetoric: Monitor the language used by major political figures in the 48 hours following the vote. A move toward broad challenges to the results would signal a long period of domestic instability in the U.S.
  • Foreign Policy Shifts: Watch for changes in U.S. posturing regarding Middle East tensions and trade tariffs, as these will have an immediate impact on the global supply chains that the South Korean economy relies upon.

Ultimately, the health of a democracy is determined by its citizens' willingness to hold power accountable through the ballot box. As the world watches the American electoral process unfold, the lesson for South Korea remains clear: the most effective way to withstand the tremors of global politics is to ensure that the domestic foundations of democracy, transparency, and economic self-reliance remain unshakeable.
