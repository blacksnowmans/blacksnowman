Israel’s Electoral Deadlock: How Far-Right Kingmakers and Fractured Coalitions Threaten Democratic Norms

October 29, 2026

As the final ballots are counted in Israel, a familiar political crisis has once again taken center stage in Jerusalem. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s right-wing bloc has failed to secure a clear parliamentary majority, plunging the nation’s political landscape into deep uncertainty. Exit polls and official vote tallies have painted a picture of severe gridlock, leaving neither the incumbent nor the opposition with an easy path to form a stable governing coalition. Yet, beyond the immediate arithmetic of parliamentary seats lies a far more unsettling reality: the meteoric rise of extreme nationalist parties whose sudden leverage threatens to rewrite the rules of Israeli governance.

For political observers in South Korea and around the world, the ongoing stalemate in Israel offers a stark case study in the perils of deep political polarization. When mainstream leaders trade policy concessions for personal political survival, fringe movements can quickly transform from peripheral agitators into national kingmakers. The election results demonstrate how anti-minority rhetoric, xenophobia, and institutional distrust can be weaponized into voting power, raising fundamental questions about the resilience of democratic institutions in times of prolonged instability.

Netanyahu’s Slipping Grip and the Paralysis in Jerusalem

Despite Benjamin Netanyahu’s public declarations of intent to form a "right-wing nationalist government," the election results present a harsh numerical reality. His political alliance fell short of the 61-seat threshold required for a majority in Israel’s 120-member parliament, the Knesset. While Netanyahu continues to assert his mandate to lead, his inability to command an outright majority underscores the eroding authority of a leader who has dominated Israeli politics for decades.

This electoral shortfall is more than a routine political setback; it reflects a growing systemic impasse. In response to Netanyahu’s maneuvers, an array of opposition figures—spanning traditional conservatives, centrists, and progressives—have demonstrated heightened cohesion around a single shared objective: ending the Netanyahu era. Meanwhile, Israeli President Isaac Herzog faces the formidable challenge of designating a leader to form a government amidst an intensely divided legislature.

The election also highlighted significant shifts in voter behavior. High turnout among Arab-Israeli voters emerged as a decisive factor, driven largely by resistance against discriminatory platforms championed by far-right factions. By mobilizing at the ballot box, minority communities asserted their influence against political forces seeking their marginalization. Consequently, the opposition’s battle has evolved beyond traditional left-versus-right policy debates into a broader struggle over democratic norms and coalition strategy.

The 13-Seat Power Broker: The Rise of Ben-Gvir and Smotrich

The most alarming outcome of the election is the performance of the far-right coalition led by Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich. Projections indicate that this ultra-nationalist faction is poised to secure approximately 13 seats in the Knesset. Throughout the campaign, both leaders leveraged overtly anti-Palestinian rhetoric and openly advocated for aggressive policies, including the expansion of Jewish settlements in the West Bank.

With 13 seats in hand, Ben-Gvir and Smotrich hold disproportionate leverage in any potential right-wing coalition led by Netanyahu. Their parliamentary strength allows them to demand key cabinet portfolios and ideological concessions as the price of their support. Smotrich’s explicit commitments to settlement expansion challenge international legal norms and risk further isolating Israel on the global stage. Meanwhile, Ben-Gvir’s public statements following initial exit polls signaled a clear message: the far-right expects its hardline agenda to be integrated directly into national policy.

This dynamic illustrates a dangerous trend where political extremism is normalized through strategic necessity. When mainstream politicians rely on fringe elements to secure power, extreme positions move from the margins directly into the cabinet room, altering the state's policy trajectory and undermining long-standing democratic safeguards.

Four Scenarios for Breaking the Coalition Impasse

With no party holding a clear majority, analysts point to four primary scenarios that could unfold as coalition negotiations proceed:

  • A Right-Wing/Far-Right Coalition: Netanyahu successfully bargains with Ben-Gvir, Smotrich, and orthodox nationalist allies to patch together a fragile, narrow majority. However, such a government would face constant internal friction and severe international scrutiny over its hardline policies.
  • A Broad Anti-Netanyahu Coalition: Opposition parties set aside deep ideological differences to form an umbrella government focused primarily on ousting Netanyahu and restoring institutional stability.
  • A Centrist or Third-Party Lead: Emerging contenders, such as political figure Gadi Eisenkot, navigate complex negotiations involving various factions—potentially including Arab-Israeli parties—to assemble an alternative unity government under new leadership.
  • Continued Gridlock and Snap Elections: Negotiations collapse completely, leaving President Herzog with no viable candidate and forcing the country into yet another costly, polarizing parliamentary election.

Each of these pathways carries substantial risk. Should Netanyahu force through a narrow nationalist government, it will signal to voters that democratic procedures can be manipulated for political self-preservation. Conversely, an anti-Netanyahu coalition would require unprecedented compromise across radically different political ideologies, leaving it vulnerable to collapse at any moment.

Polarization as a Strategy: Lessons for Global Democracies

The structural gridlock in Israel is far from an isolated phenomenon. Across North America, Western Europe, and East Asia, democratic systems are increasingly grappling with political models that convert social grievance and economic anxiety into votes through divisive populism. When political parties abandon broad-based consensus-building in favor of base-mobilization driven by exclusion, public trust in democratic institutions inevitably erodes.

As Korean political commentators observing these international trends often point out, hyper-partisanship creates severe governance costs. When holding onto power becomes the paramount objective of political leaders, legislative bodies devolve into arenas of zero-sum conflict. The willingness of seasoned political actors to partner with radical factions in order to preserve power serves as a warning sign for all constitutional democracies: procedural democracy alone cannot survive if the underlying commitment to shared democratic values is abandoned.

What to Watch Next

As Israel enters a critical period of post-election maneuvering, international observers and market analysts should monitor several key developments:

  • Presidential Consultations: Watch how President Isaac Herzog conducts formal consultations with party leaders and whether he grants the initial mandate to Netanyahu or an opposition candidate.
  • Policy Concessions to the Far-Right: Pay close attention to whether Netanyahu formally agrees to the policy demands of Ben-Gvir and Smotrich regarding West Bank settlement expansion and judicial reforms in exchange for their 13 seats.
  • Opposition Cohesion: Track whether challenge figures like Gadi Eisenkot and other opposition leaders can maintain a unified front, and whether Arab-Israeli parliamentary factions actively participate in framing an alternative government.
  • Timeline for Snap Election Triggers: Observe statutory deadlines for coalition building; if no leader can command 61 votes within the designated window, the probability of another snap election will dramatically rise.
