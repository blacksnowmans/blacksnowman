A Supermajority in Chains: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Squandering Its Historic Mandate

November 12, 2026

For months, supporters of South Korea's progressive movement believed they had crossed a historical threshold. Following the impeachment and removal of former President Yoon Suk-yeol, voters elected President Lee Jae-myung and handed his ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) an overwhelming supermajority of nearly 180 seats in the 300-member National Assembly. The stage appeared set for a thorough reckoning with the previous conservative administration's abuses and a sweeping reform of the country's conservative-leaning institutions.

Instead, a quiet desperation is taking hold among the ruling party's base. Week after week, South Korea’s courts are handing down acquittals and reduced sentences to key figures of the former regime, while the ruling party stands by in surprising passivity. For international observers trying to make sense of Seoul's current political climate, the central paradox is striking: the Lee Jae-myung administration holds virtually every lever of democratic power, yet it appears unable—or unwilling—to counter a judiciary that is systematically dismantling the legal cases against the former ruling establishment.

Judicial Backsliding and the String of High-Profile Acquittals

To understand the current anger among ruling party supporters, one must look closely at the courtrooms of Seoul, where a series of controversial rulings has effectively erased legal accountability for the previous administration. The recent decision to reduce the prison sentence of former Justice Minister Park Seong-jae to 16 years was merely the opening act of what many progressives view as a judicial counter-revolution.

The pattern became far clearer with the court's handling of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol himself. Despite the mountain of evidence surrounding his illicit attempts to retain power, the court delivered a clean acquittal for Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. That ruling was quickly followed by a total exoneration of Oh Se-hoon, the conservative Mayor of Seoul, along with key associates involved in the infamous "hamburger meeting" bribery scandal. In case after case, judges have invoked strict technicalities and high standards of proof for powerful figures, while dismissing key testimony as inadmissible.

The collapse of accountability is equally evident in the so-called "Myung Tae-kyun Gate," a sprawling political corruption scandal involving a influential political broker who allegedly manipulated candidate nominations for the conservative People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition. Despite widespread public outrage and extensive circumstantial evidence suggesting illegal collusion at the highest levels of the former government, prosecutors and judges have allowed key figures to walk free under the defense that they "were unaware" of the broker's dealings.

Perhaps most damning for the public conscience was the recent verdict regarding the military personnel deployed during the brief declaration of martial law under the previous administration. A court fully acquitted eight soldiers and their commanding officers who led troops into the streets and toward the National Assembly, ruling that they were merely executing orders from above. The public reaction was swift and furious. "We cannot accept this," became a rallying cry among citizens who view the ruling not as a legal technicality, but as an alarming green light for future state violence.

The Looming Releases of Han Duck-soo and Lee Sang-min

The crisis facing the ruling party is about to reach a fever pitch with the impending release of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. Detained for his role in facilitating the previous administration's constitutional violations, Han’s freedom is now imminent as his detention period nears its end without a decisive conviction.

For supporters of the Lee Jae-myung government, the sight of Han Duck-soo walking out of prison will be a catastrophic symbolic defeat. It represents the complete neutralization of the judicial penalties intended to punish state misconduct. If Han walks free, the entire legal and moral rationale for holding the previous government accountable collapses. The court's lenient posture toward Han has also fueled widespread expectations that Lee Sang-min, the former Minister of Interior and Safety who faced fierce criticism over state mismanagement and disaster response failures, will similarly be cleared of all charges.

This sequential domino effect—from Park Seong-jae's reduced sentence to the acquittals of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, Oh Se-hoon, martial law commanders, and now the prospective freedom of Han Duck-soo and Lee Sang-min—presents an existential threat to the Lee Jae-myung administration. It signals to the public that despite a change in presidential power, the entrenched judicial establishment remains completely untouched and capable of shielding conservative figures from lasting consequences.

A Supermajority in Silence: The Democratic Party’s Paralyzing Passivity

If the judiciary's decisions have infuriated the public, the Democratic Party’s response has turned that fury into profound disappointment. Holding nearly 180 seats in the National Assembly gives the ruling party unprecedented legislative authority. In South Korea's legislative system, such a supermajority allows a party to fast-track reform bills, launch aggressive parliamentary investigations, pass special prosecutor appointments, and initiate impeachment proceedings against non-compliant officials.

Yet, the DPK appears paralyzed by institutional hesitation and administrative timidity. The clearest symptom of this malaise occurred on December 5. The party had promised its supporters that a comprehensive report on its special parliamentary investigation into previous state abuses would be submitted to the National Assembly floor on that date. December 5 came and went in complete silence. The deadline passed without a report, without a press conference, and without a clear explanation from party leadership.

This failure to act is part of a broader trend:

  • Empty Rhetoric Over Action: DPK leaders frequently issue fiery public statements threatening impeachments and judicial reform, but back down when it comes to formal legislative execution.
  • Bureaucratic Self-Preservation: Having transitioned from an aggressive opposition force into the governing party, DPK lawmakers have adopted a defensive, overly cautious mindset, fearing that decisive moves might alienate moderate voters.
  • Alienation of the Base: Voters who braved cold nights during street protests to impeach Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol now feel betrayed by a parliamentary leadership that appears more concerned with courtroom decorum than securing justice.

The party’s critics within its own base argue that the DPK is acting like an opposition party trapped in defensive debates, rather than a ruling power with a mandate to govern. By playing by traditional rules while the judiciary operates under what progressives see as selective political enforcement, the DPK is effectively handing the political initiative back to the conservative opposition.

What Lies Ahead: The Fading Golden Window

Political opportunities of this magnitude are exceptionally rare in South Korean political history. The Lee Jae-myung administration currently enjoys what analysts call a "political trifecta": a president with strong personal ratings, a massive parliamentary supermajority, and an organized, highly motivated voter base. Yet, history shows that such alignment does not last indefinitely.

If the Democratic Party fails to snap out of its current lethargy, the consequences will extend far beyond courtroom verdicts. Public frustration is already beginning to curdle into apathy. When passionate voters conclude that their electoral victories yield no tangible structural changes, they stay home. The ultimate test of this discontent will come in the upcoming legislative general elections, where voters may punish the DPK not for being too bold, but for being inexplicably weak.

In the coming weeks, political observers should monitor three critical indicators:

  • The Official Reaction to Han Duck-soo's Release: Will the DPK passively accept the court's decision, or will it leverage its legislative powers to launch immediate special prosecutor investigations into the judicial decisions themselves?
  • Resumption of the Parliamentary Investigation: Whether the DPK leadership can salvage its credibility by finalizing and publishing its delayed inquiry report, forcing public testimony from key conservative figures.
  • Structural Judicial Reform Bills: Whether the ruling party will finally use its 180 seats to pass long-delayed legislation aimed at increasing oversight of prosecutors and judges, or allow the window of opportunity to close permanently.

The Lee Jae-myung presidency was built on the promise of decisive, pragmatic action to repair shattered public institutions. If the ruling party continues to let the judiciary undo the achievements of the recent political transition while sitting silently on its massive majority, it risks turning a historic mandate into one of the most frustrating failures in modern South Korean politics.
