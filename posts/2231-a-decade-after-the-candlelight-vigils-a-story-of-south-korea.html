A Decade After the Candlelight Vigils, a Story of South Korea’s Most Beloved Political ‘Fool’ Returns to Screens

November 11, 2026

In the chilly autumn of 2016, millions of South Koreans filled the avenues of central Seoul, holding candles aloft to demand the impeachment of President Park Geun-hye. But inside darkened movie theaters across the country, a quieter emotional reckoning was taking place. A low-budget documentary titled Moo-hyun: Tale of Two Cities was quietly pulling audiences into multiplexes, offering a reflective mirror to a nation undergoing a massive political crisis. The film chronicled the tragic, idealistic life of former President Roh Moo-hyun, a liberal icon who had taken his own life seven years earlier after leaving office.

Made on a shoestring budget and facing near-total apathy from major film distributors, the documentary became a phenomenon. Today, ten years after its initial production phase began, director Jeon In-hwan and producer Jo Eun-sung are bringing the film back to Korean theaters in a new director’s cut. Backed once again by a passionate grassroots crowdfunding campaign, the re-release is more than a retrospective. It arrives at a moment when South Korea’s political discourse feels more viciously polarized than ever, serving as a direct challenge to a culture of online mockery, regional division, and historical cynicism.

The Miracle of 2016: How a $2,200 Indie Film Shocked Korean Cinema

When the production team behind Moo-hyun: Tale of Two Cities began work a decade ago, they had virtually no financial backing. The project launched with a meager initial budget of just 3 million won—roughly $2,200. To turn their raw footage into a feature-length film, the filmmakers turned to Tumblbug, South Korea’s premier crowdfunding platform popular among independent artists and creators. Setting an initial goal of 20 million won (around $15,000), the project faltered in its first few days, raising barely a fraction of its target and leaving the crew wondering if the film would ever see the light of day.

Then, word of mouth spread. As political turmoil gripped South Korea in late 2016, ordinary citizens began pouring money into the campaign. The film cleared its funding goal and secured a limited theatrical release. What followed was unprecedented in the realm of Korean independent cinema:

  • Targeted Crowdfunding Success: The campaign mobilized thousands of small-dollar donors who felt the film filled a void during a period of deep national disappointment.
  • Box-Office Breakdown: The documentary eventually drew between 190,000 and 200,000 ticket buyers nationwide.
  • Outsized Cultural Impact: In South Korea’s screen-monopolized movie industry—where mega-conglomerates control distribution and blockbuster titles take up the vast majority of screens—an independent documentary selling 200,000 tickets is widely considered equivalent to a commercial film breaking the 5 million mark.

Moviegoers organized collective ticket-buying drives and traveled long distances to theaters willing to screen it. In the high-pressure environment of Korean multiplexes, the documentary maintained remarkably high seat-occupancy rates, forcing theater managers to extend its run week after week. The film’s success proved that Korean audiences were hungry for narratives grounded in civic principle rather than studio spectacle.

Unseen Tears and the Two Cities: What the Director’s Cut Uncovers

The centerpiece of the upcoming 10th-anniversary director’s cut is a treasure trove of newly restored, unreleased archive footage. Director Jeon In-hwan and producer Jo Eun-sung re-examined the original raw tapes, refining the audio score and editing new sequences into the narrative. The most anticipated addition involves intimate footage from Roh Moo-hyun’s election campaign in Busan’s Buk-Gangseo district.

To understand the weight of these scenes, one must understand the defining curse of modern Korean politics: regionalism. For decades, South Korean electoral maps have been starkly divided along geographic lines:

  • Yeongnam (Southeastern Korea): Home to major cities like Busan and Daegu, historically the firm stronghold of conservative parties like the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party.
  • Honam (Southwestern Korea): Home to the Jeolla provinces, historically the bedrock of liberal support for parties like the Democratic Party of Korea.

Roh Moo-hyun built his legendary reputation by directly defying this system. Though he was a rising liberal star who could have easily won a safe seat in Seoul or Honam, he repeatedly ran for office in Busan—his hometown, but a hostile conservative bastion—demanding an end to regional prejudice. He lost repeatedly, earning him the enduring nickname "Ba-bo" (The Fool), a moniker embraced by his supporters as a badge of unyielding integrity.

The new footage captures Roh inside his campaign headquarters in Buk-Gangseo, weeping quietly as he reflects on his uphill struggle. Intercut with his story is the parallel narrative of another candidate fighting regional entrenched interests in Yeosu, a coastal city in Honam. By juxtaposing these two geographic battlegrounds, the documentary exposes how structural factionalism cripples rational political debate. Ten years after the documentary's first release, those regional and partisan fault lines remain largely unhealed.

Grassroots Backing vs. Digital Backlash: Crowdfunding a Return to Theaters

Bringing an independent film back to Korean theaters years after its premiere is a daunting financial challenge. Even for a simple re-release, costs quickly add up: digital cinema package (DCP) mastering, text re-editing, marketing, and renting venues for guest-speaker (GV) events. Facing these expenses, the filmmakers returned to Tumblbug once more with an initial target of 20 million won (~$15,000).

The response was immediate. Within days—and partly boosted by appearances on independent media broadcasts—donations surged past 30 million won (~$22,000). The production team hopes to reach 50 million won (~$37,000), which would allow them to fund a broader nationwide promotional tour and host interactive Q&A sessions with audiences across the country.

Yet the effort faces hostility from digital space. Korean internet culture has long been plagued by coordinated "star-rating attacks" (review-bombing) and targeted trolling by far-right message boards and conservative internet groups. Both in 2016 and today, user-review sections on major Korean web portals have seen floods of derogatory comments aimed at sinking the documentary's score before it even hits screens.

"When the darkness grows deeper, you must light a candle. The stronger the political mockery becomes, the more vital it is to prove the lasting power of authentic historical records."

Despite these organized campaigns of online harassment, the filmmakers view the crowd-funding drive as a testament to civil society’s resilience. In a media landscape dominated by deep pockets and corporate algorithms, the willingness of thousands of small donors to fund a political documentary represents a rare form of cultural pushback.

Resisting Political Cynicism: Why Roh’s Legacy Triggers Modern Battles

The decision to revive Moo-hyun: Tale of Two Cities is deeply tied to the current political weather in Seoul. In recent years, political discourse in South Korea has taken a sharp turn toward outrage culture and personal caricature. Online spaces and cable commentary frequently feature harsh mockery of political figures, and former President Roh has frequently been targeted by bad-faith memes and historical distortion from conservative circles.

This dynamic has spilled over into mainstream party politics. High-profile figures within the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, have drawn criticism for making provocative remarks aimed at devaluing Roh’s legacy, including controversial references to Bongha Village, the rural community where Roh retired and was eventually laid to rest. For the filmmakers, this rising tide of mockery threatens to erase the actual political lessons of Roh's life: his refusal to compromise on fundamental values, his willingness to risk his career for national reconciliation, and his vision of a society built on mutual respect.

The re-release of the documentary is not meant to build a partisan altar or demand uncritical worship of a past leader. Rather, it acts as a provocative question aimed at the present. Under the current political landscape—where the Democratic Party of Korea holds the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung while facing a fierce parliamentary opposition—voters on both sides are increasingly exhausted by party infighting and mutual delegitimization. The film asks whether modern South Korea has abandoned the idea of political self-sacrifice altogether.

What to Watch Next

As the 10th-anniversary edition of Moo-hyun: Tale of Two Cities prepares for its theatrical run, several key dynamics will reveal how deeply its message resonates with contemporary audiences:

  • Crowdfunding Milestones: Watch whether the Tumblbug campaign hits its stretch goal of 50 million won (~$37,000), which will determine the scale of its nationwide theater placement and public engagement tour.
  • Box-Office Performance & Screen Allocation: Pay attention to how many screens major Korean multiplex chains allocate to the director's cut, and whether seat-occupancy rates match the grassroots surge seen in 2016.
  • Political Fallout & Youth Engagement: Observe how younger generations of moviegoers—many of whom were children during Roh's presidency—react to the film, and whether the re-release sparks renewed debates over political polarization and regionalism ahead of upcoming electoral cycles.

Ten years after it first stirred the hearts of moviegoers during a national crisis, this small documentary is stepping back into the spotlight. In an era where political discourse often feels reduced to soundbites and malice, the return of South Korea's most famous political "fool" offers audiences a chance to reflect on what principled leadership used to look like—and what it might take to rebuild it.
