South Korea's Golf Course Controversy Overshadows a Real Disaster

October 11, 2026

Imagine standing inside a bustling 1994-built commercial building on a quiet weekend morning, attending a routine senior product sales event, only to watch the concrete ceiling above you give way in a terrifying ten-second collapse. Sixty-six people scramble for the exit, dozens are injured, and hospitals rush to treat the victims. Now, imagine that while the dust is still settling and rescue workers are combing through the debris, the entire political apparatus of South Korea ignores the structural failures of the building to argue over a single question: Was the president playing golf?

This is the surreal reality of South Korean politics today, where a tragic structural collapse in Daegu has been almost completely swallowed by a fierce partisan brawl. At the center of the storm is a leaked-evidence-fueled accusation from the conservative opposition, a rapid and defensive pivot from the liberal presidential office, and a weary public caught in the crossfire of perpetual outrage. To understand why this minor weekend outing has turned into a national political earthquake, we have to look past the shouting and examine the deep-seated mistrust that defines modern South Korean governance.

The Opposition's Explosive Revelation at the National Assembly

The controversy broke wide open when Joo Jin-woo, a lawmaker from the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party), held an emergency press conference at the National Assembly communication hall. Armed with detailed photographic and video evidence, Joo dropped a political bombshell: on the exact day a commercial building's ceiling collapsed in Daegu, President Lee Jae-myung had been visiting the Ta릉 Golf Course—a historic military-run course in northeastern Seoul—to play a round of golf.

The visual evidence presented was remarkably specific. Joo displayed footage tracking presidential security motorbikes and vehicles moving in formation, along with shots of the presidential clubhouses wide open and security details lining up as the entourage departed. A clearly legible road sign reading "Ta릉" left little room for dispute over the location. Brandishing this evidence, Joo seized the moral high ground, condemning the president for swinging golf clubs while elderly citizens lay injured and traumatized in a disaster zone.

This kind of high-stakes, theatrical exposure is a classic playbook move for South Korean opposition parties. By presenting definitive visual proof and cornering the administration, the opposition aims to tap directly into the public's emotional outrage. Yet it also raises a tough question about political proportionality: Is a leader's weekend leisure time fair game during a national disaster, or is this simply a cynical weapon designed to inflict maximum political damage?

The conservative attack did not stop at the golf course gates. Joo broadened his scope to lob personal and financial jabs at the first family, questioning whether family members—including the president's eldest son—participated in the round. In an effort to muddy the waters, he even brought up unrelated household grievances, asking whether the high utility bills at the presidential residence were tied to family golf outings, and demanding to know if executive special activities funds had paid for the green fees. This tactic deliberately invoked the lingering public bitterness from the opaque budgeting and private privileges associated with impeached former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently imprisoned. The opposition cleverly repurposed these past grievances as a cudgel against the current administration.

The Presidential Office Breaks Protocol to Answer

Faced with the impending leak, the presidential office did something entirely out of character: it broke its own rule of silence regarding private weekend schedules and issued an immediate, official acknowledgment. Through a spokesperson's briefing, the Blue House confirmed that President Lee had indeed visited Ta릉 Golf Course with his family the previous day.

The administration explained that while private weekend schedules are normally kept confidential, they made an exception to prevent unnecessary political fabrication. This represents a stark departure from past administrations, such as those of Park Geun-hye or the imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol, which typically met controversies with stonewalling, denial, or obfuscation. Even so, critics argue the confession was only dragged out because the opposition held undeniable visual proof.

At the same time, the Blue House's defense hinged on a crucial detail: the president was not ignoring the crisis. According to the administration, President Lee received real-time briefings on the Daegu ceiling collapse while at the golf course, immediately picking up the phone to direct the acting National Police Commissioner and order all-out rescue and safety measures. For the administration, holding a golf club does not automatically mean abandoning one's constitutional duties in the digital age, where secure communication travels instantly.

The Reality of the Daegu Building Collapse

While politicians in Seoul bickered over golf carts and green fees, the actual human cost of administrative neglect was unfolding in Buk-gu, Daegu. At approximately 10:16 a.m., the second floor of a four-story commercial building—constructed in 1994 and worn down by decades of weathering—became the site of a terrifying chain-reaction ceiling collapse.

As seniors gathered inside for a kitchenware promotional event, interior finishing materials suddenly gave way, raining down heavy concrete structures and debris over a frantic ten-second span. Sixty-six people fled for their lives. Thirty-eight suffered minor injuries, eighteen were rushed to hospitals, and five required extended inpatient treatment. It was a hair-breadth away from becoming a mass casualty event.

This disaster is part of a painful historical lineage in South Korea. From the Sampoong Department Store collapse to the Daegu subway fire, the nation’s past is littered with massive tragedies driven by systemic communication failures, overlapping bureaucratic jurisdictions, and a lack of inter-agency cooperation. While disaster management manuals have grown exponentially more detailed over the decades, the public remains hyper-sensitive to the actions of top leaders precisely because they fear the underlying safety net is still brittle.

Politics as a Spectacle and the Path Forward

The Ta릉 golf controversy ultimately reveals less about the physical movements of a president and more about the deeply transactional nature of South Korean partisan politics. For the conservative opposition, a disaster involving injured citizens is less an urgent policy problem to solve and more a convenient weapon to throw at a liberal government.

The core of the issue is not whether a president can take a weekend swing, but whether our political class cares more about scoring points than fixing the crumbling infrastructure that threatens ordinary citizens every single day.

Moving forward, both sides of the aisle face hard truths. The ruling Democratic Party must acknowledge that even if a private weekend schedule is legally permissible, the optics of golfing while citizens lie injured in a hospital clash with public expectations of executive empathy. A leader's role demands a symbolic weight that legal technicalities alone cannot satisfy.

Meanwhile, the opposition must look beyond sensationalist exposures and invest its energy into substantive legislative work—such as reinforcing building safety standards for aging commercial properties across the country. Until both sides abandon the endless cycle of retaliatory outrage, South Korea will remain trapped in a frustrating loop: real people will suffer real accidents in neglected buildings, while the political elite spend their days arguing over who was where, and who held what.
