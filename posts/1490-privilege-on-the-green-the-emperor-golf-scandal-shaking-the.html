Privilege on the Green: The "Emperor Golf" Scandal Shaking the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 26, 2026

The air in South Korea was thick with grief on October 20, 2026. Only a few days prior, a devastating fire in Seoul’s affluent Yeoksam-dong district had claimed 19 lives. Just twenty-four hours earlier, the nation had watched the somber funeral of a firefighter from Bucheon who had died in the line of duty. In a country where the public expects its leaders to share in the national "jeong" (communal sentiment), especially during times of tragedy, the optics of the ruling family's behavior carry immense weight. Yet, while the public mourned, a different scene was allegedly unfolding on the pristine fairways of the Taereung Country Club.

The Taereung Country Club is no ordinary golf course. Located in northeast Seoul, it is a military-run facility primarily reserved for active and retired officers. Because of its exclusivity and its status as a military installation, it is often viewed as a symbol of state-controlled privilege. According to recent allegations, First Lady Kim Hye-kyung—the wife of President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—was not just visiting the course for a casual round. She was reportedly accompanied by a world-class professional golfer for a private lesson, utilizing facilities usually reserved for the highest echelons of the state. This "Emperor Golf" scandal has ignited a firestorm of criticism, centering on the perceived arrogance of a presidency that seems increasingly detached from the public it serves.

The Anatomy of an Allegation: Taereung CC and the Pro-Golfer

The controversy broke wide open when Representative Joo Jin-woo of the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, took to social media to reveal what he called the "truth of October 20." Joo, a former prosecutor known for his aggressive investigative style, alleged that the First Lady engaged in a three-person round of golf that included a famous professional golfer. The term "Emperor Golf" is a specific Korean pejorative used to describe the practice of high-ranking officials or the wealthy clearing out courses or using exclusive facilities to avoid the public eye while enjoying luxury services.

The details provided by Representative Joo are granular. He claims the First Lady utilized the "Presidential Clubhouse," a secure, high-end facility within the military grounds specifically designated for the head of state. Furthermore, the presence of a professional golfer suggests more than just a hobby. In the world of high-stakes Korean politics, private lessons on the field from world-class pros are not just expensive; they are a form of social currency. Joo pointedly asked: "A field lesson from a world-renowned pro costs tens of thousands of dollars (tens of millions of won). Who footed that bill?"

This goes beyond simple recreational choices. Under the "Guidelines for the Management of Military Bases and Military Facility Protection Zones," these facilities are intended for national security and military welfare. For the First Lady to use such a site for personal recreation—particularly a lesson from a private professional—raises serious questions about the private appropriation of state assets. If the Blue House (the presidential office) cannot provide a clear accounting of who paid for these services, it risks a full-blown ethics investigation that could mirror the legal troubles faced by previous administrations.

A Collapse of Security and "Golf Diplomacy" Defenses

The fallout from the scandal has exposed two major vulnerabilities in the Lee Jae-myung administration: a breakdown in presidential security protocols and a startling lack of political intuition among his staff. The fact that Representative Joo was able to secure photos and videos of the First Lady’s private movements at a secure military facility is, in itself, a significant security breach. In South Korea, the movement of the President and the First Lady is classified as "Level A" security information.

Critics have pointed out that even during the administration of Yoon Suk-yeol—who is currently serving a prison sentence following his impeachment and removal from office—private schedules were rarely leaked with such photographic clarity. The breach suggests that the Presidential Security Service and the Blue House staff have lost control of the narrative, or worse, that there is internal leaking. For a foreign observer, this indicates a "lame duck" atmosphere where the traditional walls of the presidency are starting to crumble.

The "Golf Task Force" Explanation

Perhaps more damaging than the initial allegation was the Blue House's attempt to justify the outing. In an effort to shield the First Lady, the administration claimed that the golf session was part of a newly formed "Golf Task Force (TF)" designed to prepare the President for an upcoming summit with the President of the United States. The logic presented was that since American presidents often utilize golf for diplomacy, the South Korean presidential couple needed to "hone their skills."

The administration reportedly appointed Kwon Hyuk-ki, the Presidential Secretary for Protocol, to lead this TF, involving members from the National Security Office and the Security Service. The public reaction was one of disbelief. To the average voter, the idea that a "Golf TF" is a legitimate diplomatic necessity while the country is reeling from a fatal fire is an insult to their intelligence. As one critic noted, it is unlikely any citizen would look at the First Lady taking lessons from a pro and think, "She is working hard for the national interest."

The Political Cost for the Democratic Party

This scandal arrives at a time of extreme vulnerability for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Kim Min-seok, the Chair of the DPK, is currently facing a crisis of confidence. Recent polling by JoWon C&I shows that his positive job performance rating has plummeted to 25.6%, while his negative rating has soared to 67.6%. This dissatisfaction is not limited to the opposition; it is increasingly prevalent among the DPK's own base.

In the hierarchy of South Korean political collapses, there is a predictable pattern:

  • Anonymous Criticism: Lawmakers begin leaking their dissatisfaction to the press without names.
  • Fractional Whispering: Small groups within the party (factions) begin meeting to discuss "alternatives."
  • Open Defiance: Once a leader’s approval rating hits a certain "point of no return," lawmakers begin criticizing them publicly to save their own seats in the next election.

The "Emperor Golf" scandal is accelerating this process. Figures like National Assembly Speaker Woo Won-shik and other senior DPK members have already begun to voice concerns about the party’s direction. If the First Lady is seen as a liability, the loyalty of the party rank-and-file will evaporate. In South Korea, political alliances are often based on the ability to win the next election; once a leader becomes "toxic," the exodus is swift and brutal.

What to Watch Next

The "Emperor Golf" controversy is not a fleeting news cycle; it is a symptom of a deeper disconnect between the Lee Jae-myung administration and the Korean public. For those watching from outside Korea, several key developments will determine if this leads to a permanent shift in the political landscape:

  • The National Audit: Representative Joo Jin-woo has hinted at releasing more definitive video evidence during upcoming parliamentary audits. If the footage shows the use of state resources or security personnel acting as caddies, the legal pressure will mount.
  • The "Pro-Golfer" Identity: If the professional golfer involved is found to have business ties to the government or if the lesson was provided as a gift, it could trigger the Kim Young-ran Act (the Anti-Graft Act), which strictly limits gifts to public officials and their spouses.
  • Polling Trends: Watch for the "negative rating" in the Daegu-Gyeongbuk (TK) region and among moderate voters in Seoul. If these numbers remain above 70%, the DPK may face internal calls for a leadership shake-up or a formal apology from the President himself.
  • Legal Precedents: With Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi currently in prison, the public is acutely aware that presidential immunity is not absolute. The rhetoric from the opposition will likely continue to draw parallels between the "arrogance" of the current administration and the failures of the previous one.

The tragedy of the Yeoksam fire and the sacrifice of the Bucheon firefighter provided a moment for the administration to show empathy and leadership. By choosing the golf course instead, the First Lady and the Blue House have handed their opponents a potent symbol of elitism. In the high-pressure cooker of South Korean politics, such symbols are often the beginning of the end.
