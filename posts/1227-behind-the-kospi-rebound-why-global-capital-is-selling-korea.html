Behind the KOSPI Rebound: Why Global Capital Is Selling Korea's Chip Giants at a Record Discount

October 16, 2026

On trading floors across Seoul, the screens show an uneasy green. The benchmark Korea Composite Stock Price Index (KOSPI) has clawed its way back above the 5,720 mark, recovering from a bruising wave of volatility. Yet among institutional desks and retail trading rooms alike, the mood is anything but celebratory. Underneath the headline index rebound lies a grinding, unresolved tension: foreign institutional investors continue to unload Korean equities en masse, leaving the domestic market dependent on an increasingly strained retail base.

For international observers watching Asia’s fourth-largest economy, the current landscape presents a puzzling paradox. Corporate earnings at the summit of Korea’s technology supply chain remain robust, and corporate governance reform remains high on the political agenda. Yet South Korea’s equity market is decoupling from global peers, weighed down by macro crosscurrents from the United States, shifting dynamics in the global artificial intelligence boom, and long-standing structural anxieties at home.

A 169-Trillion-Won Exodus: The Anatomy of Foreign Capital Flight

To understand the anxiety gripping the Seoul exchange, one must first look at the relentless pace of offshore outflows. Since the start of the year, cumulative foreign net selling has climbed to a staggering 169 trillion won (roughly $125 billion). Whenever global headlines turn sour, foreign desks have pulled liquidity out of Seoul with mechanical regularity.

However, treating this purely as an indictment of the broader Korean macro economy misses what is actually taking place under the hood. The overwhelming majority of that 169-trillion-won selloff has not been spread evenly across the market. Instead, it has been heavily concentrated in just two names: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the world’s two dominant manufacturers of dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) and high-bandwidth memory (HBM).

Foreign funds are essentially taking aggressive profits following massive rallies earlier in the year, rebalancing exposure rather than abandoning Korean corporate solvency altogether. But the domestic response to this withdrawal has created its own vulnerabilities. Neither the state-run National Pension Service (NPS) nor institutional asset managers stepped in with enough firepower to absorb the outflow. Instead, individual retail investors—often referred to domestically as "ants"—stepped into the breach, absorbing tens of billions of dollars in memory stock supply.

While this retail surge prevented a disorderly crash, it has exposed the market to a different vulnerability: fragile sentiment. Individual investors operate with higher sensitivity to daily price swings than multi-year sovereign funds. When market direction becomes ambiguous, capital preservation must take precedence over emotional dip-buying. Until foreign institutions signal a genuine inflection point in their sales cycle, the heavy overhang on Korea's twin tech champions will continue to cap the broader index.

The Macro Paradox: US Inflation Surges While Bond Yields Drop

The domestic standoff in Seoul is complicated by confusing macroeconomic signals from the United States. In typical cycles, Korean equities trade as a high-beta proxy on global trade and American monetary policy. When US data surprises to the upside, Seoul braced for higher rates and currency depreciation.

That textbook relationship recently broke down in dramatic fashion. The US Producer Price Index (PPI) for September delivered an unexpected shock, surging 1.3% month-over-month. In any normal trading environment, a jump of that magnitude would have triggered an immediate equity rout, as bond traders rushed to price in aggressive rate hikes from the Federal Reserve.

Instead, global markets executed a baffling counter-move: benchmark US Treasury yields actually fell, and the US Dollar Index eased below the 100 mark after hovering near 102. The explanation lies in emerging credit anxiety within the private sector. As capital expenditure across the AI sector has escalated, technology firms have flooded the market with corporate debt. Wary of rising corporate credit spreads, global allocators bypassed private credit and parked vast sums of capital directly into safe-haven US government paper.

This rush into sovereign debt lowered yields despite hot factory-gate inflation, neutralizing what could have been a catastrophic external blow for Korea’s currency and equity markets. Furthermore, while monthly inflation prints can produce alarming headline spikes, central banks evaluate trailing year-over-year trajectories. Historically, periods of steep annual producer inflation are followed by cyclical normalization due to high baseline comparisons. For Korean investors, recognizing this macro subtlety is the difference between panic selling and maintaining a coherent portfolio posture.

AI's Stress Test: CoreWeave, Valuations, and the Burden of High Rates

Beneath the macro data lies a deeper, industry-specific stress test: the financial plumbing of the global artificial intelligence infrastructure buildout.

Korea’s stock market is essentially tethered to the capital expenditures of American hyperscalers and specialized cloud providers. When specialized AI cloud firm CoreWeave saw its shares stumble amid surging borrowing costs and credit squeeze concerns, shockwaves traveled instantly to Seoul. The panic only subsided after Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang made a characteristically blunt public intervention, making clear that Nvidia had no need to deploy its own capital to bail out CoreWeave. Had Nvidia intervened with emergency funding, Wall Street would have interpreted it as proof of structural failure; Huang’s public hands-off stance signaled that CoreWeave’s balance sheet could endure without corporate life support.

At the same time, valuation expectations across the sector are experiencing a healthy, if painful, recalibration. Leading AI research laboratory Anthropic has seen private market valuation expectations for its anticipated public debut soften, adjusting down from an eye-watering $2 trillion toward $1.5 trillion as top-line revenue growth rates stabilize. Far from being a catastrophic bust, this marks a necessary deflation of market exuberance, weeding out speculative momentum in favor of sustainable unit economics.

The true adversary facing the AI ecosystem is neither software limits nor hardware demand, but the persistence of restrictive interest rates. For companies financing multi-billion-dollar clusters of graphic processing units (GPUs) and memory modules at borrowing costs exceeding 10%, balance-sheet stamina is becoming the determining factor. Until funding costs decline, procurement schedules will face friction—and that friction translates directly into order volatility for Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.

The Valuation Anomaly: TSMC Soars While Korean Giants Trade at 3x Earnings

The divergence between technological demand and market pricing becomes starkest when comparing South Korea to its regional peers, particularly Taiwan.

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) recently demonstrated the unrelenting strength of the hardware cycle, posting a quarterly operating profit equivalent to roughly 37 trillion won (about $27 billion)—driven by high-performance computing (HPC), which accounted for 68% of its revenue, and pushing net profit up nearly 70% year-over-year. Demand for leading-edge nodes remains so tight that equipment bottlenecks remain the primary constraint.

Yet while TSMC trades at a commanding global premium, Korea’s semiconductor flagships find themselves stranded in deep value territory. Consider the metrics: while the KOSPI has historically traded around an average price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of roughly 10 times, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix have been driven down to trailing multiples near 3 times earnings.

A P/E multiple of 3 implies that a company's profits must either permanently collapse by half or vanish altogether within a few years—a premise entirely detached from the multi-year trajectory of server memory and HBM demand.

Why does this discount persist? Foreign institutions have routinely used Korean listings as high-liquidity ATMs to lock in gains, preferring the relative insulation of TSMC or US peers. But this gap also reflects enduring frustration with Korea’s market architecture, including lingering execution gaps in structural corporate governance reforms.

Political Stakes and What to Watch Next

The ongoing market slump carries direct political consequences. Following his election, President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea placed capital market revitalization and commercial code revisions front and center, promising to clean up legacy governance issues and reward domestic equity participation. Early administrative efforts generated enthusiasm among retail shareholders who had long decried the structural discount applied to domestic listings.

However, during this recent period of global volatility, domestic sentiment has soured. While neighboring markets like Taiwan saw aggressive institutional defense and state-linked capital buffering steep pullbacks, Korean retail investors felt largely abandoned to fight foreign selling alone. If retail portfolios continue to absorb heavy losses, the political goodwill generated by the Lee administration’s equity-friendly agenda risks evaporating.

Market history provides a crucial perspective. In the 2011 European debt crisis, the KOSPI plunged 24% and took years to fully recover; during the 2020 pandemic crash, it collapsed 36% before staging a full recovery within 138 days. Every steep cyclical contraction driven by liquidity mismatches rather than economic insolvency has eventually normalized.

For investors monitoring the market from abroad, three concrete indicators will signal whether the KOSPI is ready to stage a durable recovery:

  • Foreign institutional selling exhaustion: Look for a consecutive weekly slowdown in net foreign sales of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, signaling that global tech rebalancing has run its course.
  • Corporate credit spreads in the US tech sector: A stabilization in borrowing costs for second-tier cloud and data center operators will confirm that capex orders for Korean memory products remain intact.
  • Domestic policy support from the Lee administration: Watch for tangible follow-through on market liquidity support and commercial code implementation to restore institutional and retail confidence.

Korea’s equity market is currently navigating a valley of sentiment where balance sheets are divorced from share prices. The corporate fundamentals remain tied to one of the most lucrative technological transitions in modern history; the question is how long domestic investors must wait for liquidity and trust to catch up with reality.
