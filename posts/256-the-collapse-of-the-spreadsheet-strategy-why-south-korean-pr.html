The Collapse of the Spreadsheet Strategy: Why South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Ratings Are Plunging

September 07, 2026

For any political administration, watching poll numbers slide is painful. But for an administration that has built its entire identity on calculated political engineering—treating voters like cells in an Excel spreadsheet to be dragged and dropped at will—the latest numbers from Gallup Korea feel less like a dip and more like a system crash. President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has fallen to 40%, with negative assessments climbing to 51%. Among young adults aged 18 to 29, support has plummeted to an abysmal 25%, while thirty-somethings hover at just 31%.

To understand why this matters, you have to look past the raw percentages and examine the core myth that has guided South Korea's progressive ruling camp: the obsession with building a "structural majority." For months, the mainstream leadership of the Democratic Party of Korea—the liberal party currently holding executive and legislative power—convinced themselves that winning over the political center and younger generations was simply a matter of pruning away the rough edges. To them, figures like hardline lawmaker Jung Cheong-rae and the lingering shadow of the previous Moon Jae-in administration were liabilities dragging down their youth appeal. In boardrooms and strategy sessions, figures like Jung were labeled as overly aggressive, conflict-driven, and toxic to moderate voters.

So, the party leadership set out to sideline them, operating under the assumption that clearing out the firebrands would automatically welcome the center in. Instead, they were met with a harsh reality: a 25% approval rating among voters in their twenties. The strategy has not just stalled; it has imploded.

When Political Engineering Replaces Public Policy

The core fallacy of the "structural majority" strategy is its fundamental misunderstanding of how majorities are actually formed in a democracy. Politics is not a spreadsheet. Citizens are not passive data points waiting to be categorized into a strategist's "moderate expansion" column. Majorities do not materialize because a party hires clever consultants to rebrand its image; they form organically when people feel tangible improvements in their daily lives—when housing stabilizes, jobs are created, and promised reforms are actually delivered.

By treating "moderate expansion" as an abstract marketing exercise rather than a byproduct of good governance, the administration effectively turned off the engine of public trust while shaking the empty chassis. Critics within the progressive camp have pointed out the obvious irony: if the party leadership spent years arguing that figures like Jung Cheong-rae stood in the way of broadening the party's appeal, the exact same mathematical logic must now be applied to President Lee himself. Yet, instead of confronting policy failures, political parties inevitably default to looking for scapegoats.

This blame game triggers a vicious cycle. When a party's approval ratings drop, it begins turning inward, purging internal critics and demanding absolute loyalty. Dissent is treated as betrayal. But when an organization stops debating policy and transforms into a loyalty test, it alienates its most dedicated volunteers and creates a climate of fear. For ordinary citizens watching from the outside, this looks less like visionary leadership and more like a desperate, insular ruling elite trying to protect its turf.

The Trap of Camp-Encirclement Politics

The irony of trying to widen a political coalition by cutting away its foundation is that it usually achieves the exact opposite: it narrows the battlefield while alienating everyone. In trying to appease the center by marginalizing its own core—including dedicated party members, pro-Moon factions, and outspoken commentators—the ruling camp managed to expand the front lines of conflict rather than its base.

On the left, alienated traditional supporters turned their backs. On the right, conservative voters—who never supported the administration in the first place—consolidated even further. This left the party stranded with a volatile, uncommitted middle that rapidly evaporated the moment tangible economic or social achievements failed to materialize.

Commentators and political analysts in Seoul have pointed out that this mirrors the exact mistakes made by the previous conservative administration under former President Yoon Suk-yeol. Yoon’s government also began with a broad coalition of conservative, anti-liberal, and moderate youth forces, only to systematically alienate factions whenever friction arose, ultimately backing itself into a lonely, isolated corner. When internal party management relies on exclusion rather than persuasion, the governing coalition begins to crumble like a sandcastle.

Voters are remarkably perceptive. They understand when a political party views them as captive customers—voters who are taken for granted simply because "there is no alternative." But voters express their frustration not always through direct confrontation, but through quiet withdrawal: they stop volunteering, they skip the ballot box, and they retreat into the ranks of the undecided. For a major political party, this silent desertion is far more dangerous than loud protests.

What to Watch Next

As the administration navigates this deep trough in public opinion, all eyes are on how the leadership chooses to respond. There are three paths before them: push even harder into a technocratic center that has already rejected them, return to basics by listening to their core base and delivering actual legislative results, or double down on internal purges and scapegoating.

If the past few weeks are any indication, the temptation to find internal culprits remains high. But as poll numbers from Realmeter and Gallup continue to show a widening "decoupling"—where the president's rating falls even lower than the baseline support for his party—the window for cosmetic fixes is rapidly closing.

For outside observers trying to understand Korean politics, this moment serves as a vital case study in the limits of technocratic governance. Keep an eye on several concrete developments in the coming weeks:

  • Internal Party Dissension: Watch whether the party leadership escalates disciplinary actions against outspoken lawmakers or chooses to reconcile with alienated internal factions. Further purges will signal an inward-looking survival mode.
  • Youth and Moderate Poll Trends: Pay attention to whether upcoming weekly polls show any stabilization in the critical 18-29 and 30-39 age brackets, or if the "silent exit" continues to deepen.
  • Shifts in Legislative Focus: Observe whether the ruling party pivots away from internal restructuring toward concrete livelihood and economic legislation—such as housing and job market measures—that directly affect everyday household budgets.

Ultimately, a government cannot engineer its way out of public dissatisfaction using spreadsheet logic. True political stability is built on trust, and trust is earned by making people's lives tangibly better. Until the leadership puts down its political calculators and returns to the hard work of governing for the public, a 25% approval rating among the nation's youth may not be the bottom—it may only be the beginning.
