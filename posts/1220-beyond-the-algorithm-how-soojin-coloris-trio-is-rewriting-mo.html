Beyond the Algorithm: How Soojin Coloris Trio Is Rewriting Modern Korean Jazz

October 16, 2026

Step away from the neon-lit broadcast stages of Seoul and the precision-engineered hooks of the mainstream pop industry, and you will find an entirely different sonic world quietly commanding attention. On an ordinary Friday evening, in a small live space where the clink of glasses and the hush of the audience become part of the acoustics, drummer and composer Suh Soo-jin sits behind her kit alongside pianist Kang Jae-hoon and contrabassist Kim Young-hoo. There are no prerecorded backing tracks and no synthesized shortcuts. When Suh draws a cello bow across the rim of her cymbals and Kim digs his fingers into the upright bass, the music does not simply accompany the room—it alters the air inside it.

This is Soojin Coloris Trio, an ensemble that has quietly emerged as one of the most compelling voices in South Korea's contemporary instrumental landscape. To international audiences accustomed to equating Korean music strictly with high-octane idol productions or lush television drama ballads, the trio represents an overlooked reality: a deeply sophisticated, fearless underground jazz movement that prioritizes raw tactile expression over commercial formulas.

An Ensemble Built on Instinct and Restraint

Formed around 2019, Soojin Coloris Trio released their debut recording in 2020, quickly sending shockwaves through domestic and international critical circles. All three members honed their craft through rigorous formal study in the United States, yet their music avoids academic stiffness. In an interesting trajectory familiar to many avant-garde Korean artists, their refined, uncompromising approach caught the attention of listeners and critics abroad before breaking through widely in South Korea itself.

Their reputation was cemented at the Korean Music Awards—widely regarded as the country's premier peer-voted music ceremony, known for prioritizing artistic integrity over sales charts and streaming counts. The trio captured prestigious honors there, winning Best Jazz & Crossover Album as well as Best Performance, validating years of uncompromising dedication to their craft.

The foundation of this recognition lies in leader Suh Soo-jin’s specific artistic vision. Suh did not assemble a group to simply play standards or show off technical speed; rather, she had a distinct, visual soundscape in mind and spent time seeking out players capable of translating those cerebral sketches into physical reality. As contrabassist Kim Young-hoo has noted about their early rehearsals, the essential spark was finding an intuitive, shared sense of time—a rare lock between bass and drums where tempo is felt organically rather than measured like a clock. Pianist Kang Jae-hoon, who has now collaborated with Suh for seven years, recalls being drawn to her distinct leadership and musical clarity, forming an airtight creative bond that feels closer to an interdependent organism than three separate soloists taking turns.

Translating the Pandemic Era into Fluid Sound

The trio's creative philosophy is on full display in their conceptual compositions, particularly their piece "UNCERTAINTY III - BEGINNINGS AND ENDINGS". Conceived during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, the piece tackles the pervasive instability that defined the period. Rather than delivering a heavy, brooding lecture on hardship, however, the trio chose to translate the condition of ambiguity into a fluid acoustic dialogue.

In structure, the piece purposefully avoids conventional song architecture. Modern pop listeners are trained to expect predictable verses, rising choruses, and neatly resolved conclusions. By contrast, "UNCERTAINTY III" operates on ambiguity:

  • Rhythmic displacement: Kang’s piano fractures the tempo into rapid, driving fragments that push the tempo forward without an obvious anchor.
  • Textural contrasts: Kim alternates between sharp, percussive finger plucking (pizzicato) and the deep, bowed drone of the arco technique on the double bass.
  • Unconventional percussion: Suh moves fluidly between traditional drumsticks and subtle brushwork, occasionally taking a bow to the percussion itself to produce eerie, metallic overtones.
"At first, everything feels unstable, but as the music builds, you find yourself riding the rhythm. It is not music meant to be dissected with the analytical mind; it is music you simply climb aboard and ride."

The piece deliberately refuses clean closures. As it approaches what feels like an ending, it lingers, hints at restarting, and abruptly cuts out. Even in live formats where time constraints might force a performance to end mid-stride, the lingering incompleteness feels entirely deliberate—a mirror of human experiences where closures are rarely neat or announced in advance.

The Art of the Sincere Homage: Honoring Jon Brion

While the trio thrives on structural tension, they are equally capable of breathtaking warmth. This emotional duality was on display in their piece "TEAR DOWN A WALL (FOR BRION)", a dedicated homage to the renowned American film composer and producer Jon Brion.

Brion’s name may not be a household word to casual moviegoers, but his melancholic, vintage-tinged scores for films like Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind and Lady Bird have shaped the emotional vocabulary of contemporary cinema. Rather than concealing her creative influences, Suh has spoken candidly on stage about the joy of transparent admiration: when another artist moves you, you should not bury the debt, but celebrate it through your own vocabulary.

On stage, the composition transforms the atmosphere entirely. The sharp, restless edge of their avant-garde compositions melts away into lush lyricism:

  • Suh swaps heavy sticks for wire brushes, sweeping across the snare drum with a soothing, textured rhythm that acts like a blanket over the room.
  • Kang’s piano introduces an unhurried, luminous melody that gradually swells from tender introspection into an expansive, resonant peak.
  • Kim’s bass anchors the melody with steady, rich undercurrents that never overpower the piano's deliberate phrasing.

The performance carries the atmosphere of an intimate late-night cocktail set atop a metropolitan rooftop lounge, demonstrating that intellectual jazz and deep, unpretentious romance can inhabit the exact same trio without contradiction.

What to Watch Next in Korea's Acoustic Landscape

Soojin Coloris Trio offers a stark, refreshing alternative in an era when much of the international spotlight on South Korea focuses on algorithmic streaming metrics and industrial production methods. Their trajectory demonstrates that real, sustainable artistic momentum is quietly thriving in Seoul's small clubs, supported by artists who view their instruments as direct extensions of their physical breath and internal landscapes.

Moving forward, several concrete dynamics around the trio and their peers are worth tracking:

  • Cross-border touring and festivals: Given their early critical reception abroad and dual recognition at the Korean Music Awards, watch how the trio expands its footprint across European and North American jazz festival circuits, where their boundary-pushing crossover sound finds a natural home.
  • Collaborative side projects: With Suh, Kang, and Kim maintaining deep individual reputations as top-tier session and recording artists in Seoul, look for their distinctive playing styles to ripple into other independent genres, including folk, ambient, and modern classical collaborations.
  • The evolving "Uncertainty" cycle: As the ensemble continues to perform and record works developed from their pandemic-era thematic suites, future releases will show whether their structural experiments with beginnings and endings expand into longer-form, large-ensemble formats.

For listeners outside Korea seeking to understand the country's cultural depth beyond the billboard charts, Soojin Coloris Trio provides an ideal entry point: honest, demanding, and profoundly human.
