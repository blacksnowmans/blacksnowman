Internal Strife and the Shincheonji Shadow: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Turning on its Own

November 17, 2026

Imagine sitting down for a meal with a senior colleague, only to look at your phone and see a breaking news alert: your boss has just ordered a high-level ethics investigation into your conduct. There was no phone call, no prior warning, and no chance to explain. For Choi Min-hee, the Senior Supreme Member of the Democratic Party, South Korea’s ruling party, this was the reality on a recent afternoon. The person who ordered the probe was none other than Kim Min-seok, the leader of the party and a man she sees every day at leadership meetings.

To an outside observer, this might look like a standard disciplinary spat over a politician’s "hot mic" moment or rude language. Choi is accused of calling press photographers a "pack of hyenas" in a private chat group and making controversial remarks about the past during a closed-door meeting with party members. However, beneath the surface of this ethical probe lies a complex web of internal power struggles, a mysterious list of 14,388 religious cult members, and a ruling party grappling with a sharp decline in public approval. At its core, the "Choi Min-hee controversy" is a window into the fragile unity of the Democratic Party under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

The Ethics Probe and the Breakdown of Procedural Democracy

The sudden move by Party Leader Kim Min-seok to refer Choi Min-hee to the party’s Ethics Tribunal has sent shockwaves through the Democratic Party. In the South Korean political context, the Senior Supreme Member is an elected position of significant weight, second only to the party leader. To trigger an investigation without so much as a preliminary conversation is, according to many insiders, a breach of political etiquette and procedural fairness.

Park Gyu-hwan, a former Supreme Member of the party, and legal expert Jung Pil-seung have publicly criticized the move as a "lack of communication." During a broadcast on the YouTube channel Hong Sa-hun Show on November 17, 2026, Park noted that while a party leader has the authority to order "ex officio" investigations, it is almost unheard of to target a high-ranking official who sits at the same table every morning without prior notice. This has led to accusations that the probe is less about ethics and more about "fear politics"—a way to keep party members in line by demonstrating that even the highest-ranking figures are expendable.

The legal stakes involve the party’s internal Rule No. 7, specifically Article 14, which penalizes the spreading of false information that "insults party members" or "harms party unity." The "smoking gun" in Choi’s case is a statement she allegedly made during a private meeting regarding the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Choi reportedly claimed that President Lee Jae-myung (who was party leader at the time of the impeachment) actually opposed the removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and instead wanted to find a way to cooperate with Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister.

If true, this would be a damaging revelation, suggesting the President was hesitant during a pivotal moment in national history. However, supporters argue this was a simple slip of the tongue. They suggest Choi likely confused the names of past figures or was discussing a hypothetical political maneuver in a casual setting. They point to the fact that President Lee Jae-myung himself was once cleared in a court of law for "verbal slips" made during high-pressure broadcasts, arguing that a politician should not be purged for a single confused sentence in a private room.

The Rise of the 'Deomincho' and the Battle for the Ethics Tribunal

The controversy took a more organized turn on November 17, when 55 rookie lawmakers from the Democratic Party—a group known as "Deomincho"—issued a joint statement. These first-term legislators, who represent a significant portion of the party’s 71 freshman members, demanded that the leadership be held to the highest possible ethical standards. They argued that regardless of whether Choi’s words violated specific party rules, she must take "political responsibility" to protect the party’s image.

This collective action has split the party’s newcomers. While 55 signed the letter, 14 refused. Those who declined to sign, including Supreme Members Han Min-soo and Lee Sung-yoon, argue that the collective statement is an attempt to pressure the Ethics Tribunal, which is supposed to be an independent body. By demanding a "stern judgment" before the tribunal has even finished its investigation, the 55 lawmakers are effectively calling for Choi’s resignation, an act some critics describe as a "staged internal coup."

The friction here mirrors past episodes in South Korean politics where freshman lawmakers are mobilized to provide "moral cover" for leadership shifts. Skeptics within the party suggest that the Deomincho statement was not a spontaneous outburst of idealism but a coordinated effort to protect Party Leader Kim Min-seok from his own controversies. By focusing the spotlight on Choi Min-hee’s rude language, the party can pivot away from more damaging allegations involving a religious organization that has become a lightning rod in Korean politics: Shincheonji.

The Shincheonji Shadow and the 14,388 Members

To understand why this internal fight matters, one must understand the name "Shincheonji." Often described as a secretive religious cult, Shincheonji gained international notoriety during the COVID-19 pandemic. In South Korean politics, accusations of ties to Shincheonji are "nuclear-grade" smears. During the last party convention, Kim Min-seok alleged that a "secret alliance" existed between anti-Lee Jae-myung factions and Shincheonji to influence the party’s internal elections.

The party established a "Party Member Cleanup Task Force" (TF) to investigate these claims. The TF eventually reported a startling figure: 14,388 individuals were identified as having dual membership in both the Democratic Party and Shincheonji. However, the leadership has refused to release the full report or the names, citing "privacy concerns" and the risk of legal retaliation.

This secrecy has fueled a firestorm of suspicion. Lawyer Jung Pil-seung points out a logical contradiction: if the TF only has a numerical count and not a list of names, there is no privacy risk in releasing the methodology and the data. If they *do* have the names, then the leadership is sitting on a list of 14,000 people they can potentially use as leverage. Critics argue that Choi Min-hee’s ethics probe is a "frame shift"—a deliberate attempt to drown out the noise regarding the Shincheonji report, which reportedly investigated whether high-ranking DP figures like Jung Chung-rae had ties to the group.

The stakes are high for the 2027 political cycle. If the party does not resolve the Shincheonji issue transparently, every future primary and election will be haunted by claims of cult infiltration. Opponents from the People Power Party, the main conservative opposition, could easily label any Democratic candidate as "the candidate chosen by a cult." By focusing on Choi’s verbal gaffes, the party leadership may be trying to bury a much more dangerous explosive.

Governance Under Pressure: Polls, Budgets, and Diplomacy

This internal feud is happening at a time when the Democratic Party can ill afford distractions. Recent polling shows the party’s approval rating has dipped to 34.6%, a troubling low for a ruling party. This decline is linked to a sense of gridlock and a perception that the party is more focused on internal loyalty tests than on the "livelihood" issues (minsaeng) that affect ordinary citizens.

Furthermore, the government is currently navigating the passage of a massive 821 trillion won (approximately $610 billion) "super budget." In a cooling economy, the management of these funds is critical. However, the news cycle has been dominated not by budget line items, but by the ethics probe and the "pack of hyenas" comment.

The distractions extend to foreign policy and education as well. Cho Kuk, the head of the Rebuilding Korea Party (a minor liberal party), was recently denied entry to Japan, sparking a diplomatic debate. Simultaneously, the Ministry of Education is under fire for its handling of cheating countermeasures for the upcoming 2027 College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), the high-stakes exam that determines the future of South Korean students. When the ruling party is consumed by an internal "civil war," its ability to project competence on these administrative and diplomatic fronts is severely diminished.

What to Watch Next

The resolution of the Choi Min-hee case will serve as a bellwether for the Democratic Party’s internal health. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Ethics Tribunal’s Verdict: If the tribunal issues a heavy penalty, such as a suspension of party rights, it will be seen as a victory for Kim Min-seok and a signal that the party is tightening its ideological ranks. A light warning would suggest a desire to de-escalate.
  • The Release of the Shincheonji Report: Pressure is mounting from rank-and-file party members to see the data behind the "14,388 members" claim. Whether the leadership blinks and releases the TF report will determine if the "cult infiltration" narrative continues to fester.
  • The 14 Non-Signatories: Watch the 14 freshman lawmakers who refused to sign the petition against Choi. If they form a coherent internal opposition, it could signal the end of the "monolithic" support for the current leadership’s tactics.
  • Approval Rating Recovery: If the party’s numbers continue to stagnate below 35%, expect more aggressive internal "purges" as different factions blame each other for the government’s unpopularity.

In the high-stakes world of South Korean politics, a "pack of hyenas" is rarely just a metaphor. As the Democratic Party decides how to handle Choi Min-hee, it is also deciding whether it can survive its own internal instincts for survival.
