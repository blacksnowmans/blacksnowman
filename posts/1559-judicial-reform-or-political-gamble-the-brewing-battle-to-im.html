Judicial Reform or Political Gamble? The Brewing Battle to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

October 27, 2026

In the heart of Seoul’s Seocho-dong district, where the imposing granite facades of the Supreme Court and the Prosecutor’s Office loom over the city, a sense of unfinished business hangs heavy in the air. For many supporters of the current administration, the election of President Lee Jae-myung of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) was supposed to signal a total rupture with the past. The previous leader, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, has been impeached, removed from office, and is currently serving a prison sentence. Yet, to a vocal segment of the Korean public, the "clocks of justice" remain frozen. They argue that while the presidency has changed hands, the inner sanctum of the judiciary remains a fortress for the old guard, protected by Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

The growing demand for the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho is no longer a fringe sentiment whispered in activist circles. It has become a central tension within the ruling Democratic Party. On one side, influential commentators and "street" politicians argue that without removing the head of the judiciary, the "legal cartel"—a term often used to describe the insular network of elite conservative judges and prosecutors—will continue to sabotage the new government’s agenda. On the other side, the party leadership remains paralyzed by "Constitutional Court phobia," fearing that a failed impeachment attempt could trigger a massive political backlash and provide the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition, with a narrative of "judicial persecution."

The Kim Keon-hee Trigger and the Power of the Pulpit

To understand why this matters now, one must look at the influential role of media figures like Kim Eo-jun. Kim, a prolific podcaster and host of "News Factory," occupies a unique space in Korean politics. While often dismissed by the mainstream press as a conspiracy theorist, he possesses an uncanny ability to set the legislative agenda for the DPK’s base. Since July, Kim has been sounding the alarm, suggesting that the judiciary is preparing a "counter-attack" by potentially releasing Kim Keon-hee, the wife of the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

Ms. Kim Keon-hee is currently a central figure in multiple investigations involving stock manipulation and graft. For the progressive base, her continued detention is a litmus test for whether the judiciary has truly accepted the new constitutional order. Kim Eo-jun’s argument is straightforward: if the Supreme Court, under Cho Hee-dae’s leadership, allows her release, it is an act of open defiance against the people’s will. He has called for the immediate suspension of the Chief Justice’s powers through impeachment the moment she walks free.

However, the mainstream and even some liberal media outlets have reacted with skepticism. They frame this as "emotional politics," accusing the DPK and its supporters of trying to use impeachment as a tool of revenge rather than a legal remedy. Reports in outlets like Jugan Kyunghyang (a weekly news magazine) have warned that the DPK is walking into a trap. They argue that by tying the Chief Justice’s fate to the detention of a single individual, the party risks trivializing the grave constitutional mechanism of impeachment, potentially alienating moderate voters who value judicial independence.

The Fallacy of Conditional Impeachment

Inside the Democratic Party, a debate is raging over whether impeachment should be "conditional." Some lawmakers have suggested that the party should wait until October 31st; if Ms. Kim is released by then, they move forward; if she stays behind bars, they hold fire. This "transactional" view of judicial reform is what critics call a fundamental logical error. It suggests that the Chief Justice’s alleged violations of the constitution—such as biased case assignments and the intentional delay of trials related to the previous administration’s "state interference" scandals—are only problematic if they produce a specific outcome in one high-profile case.

"If the Chief Justice has committed acts that warrant impeachment, those acts exist independently of whether a specific defendant is in jail or not. To make impeachment conditional is to turn the highest legal sanction into a bargaining chip."

The core of the argument for impeachment rests on the structural power the Chief Justice holds. In South Korea, the head of the Supreme Court has immense administrative authority, including the power to assign judges to specific courts and oversee the National Court Administration, the body that manages the entire judiciary. Critics argue that Cho Hee-dae has used this "administrative weapon" to ensure that sensitive cases involving the "old power" are heard by sympathetic judges, or simply stalled indefinitely. If these allegations of "judicial monopoly" or "judicial collusion" (terms popularized during previous reform drives) are true, then the case for his removal stands on its own merits, regardless of Ms. Kim Keon-hee's status.

The Shadow of the Constitutional Court

The primary reason for the DPK leadership’s hesitation is the Constitutional Court of Korea. In the Korean system, the National Assembly can impeach a public official with a simple majority, but the final decision to remove them from office rests with the nine justices of the Constitutional Court. Six of those nine must vote to uphold the impeachment.

The DPK currently holds an overwhelming majority in the National Assembly, meaning they can pass an impeachment motion with ease. However, the memory of previous failures looms large. If the Constitutional Court rejects the impeachment of Cho Hee-dae, it would serve as a "total exoneration" for the conservative judicial establishment. It would effectively grant the Chief Justice a shield against any further reform efforts for the remainder of his term.

  • Political Backlash: A rejection would allow the PPP to frame the DPK as an "anti-constitutional" force that ignores the rule of law.
  • Judicial Moral Authority: The Chief Justice would return to his bench with his moral and legal authority bolstered, making it even harder to challenge his administrative decisions.
  • Internal Fractures: A failed impeachment would likely lead to a civil war within the DPK between the "hardliners" who pushed for it and the "moderates" who warned against it.

Yet, proponents of impeachment argue that the Constitutional Court does not live on an isolated island. They believe the Court's decisions are sensitive to the "candlelight sentiment"—the overwhelming public demand for reform. They argue that the DPK’s role should not be to wait for the Court’s permission, but to organize such a powerful wave of public opinion that the justices cannot ignore the systemic failures of the current Supreme Court leadership.

What to Watch Next

The "Golden Time" for judicial reform is narrow. As the Lee Jae-myung administration moves into its next phase, the tension between the executive and the judiciary is likely to reach a breaking point. The upcoming weeks will be decisive for several reasons.

First, keep an eye on the specific trial dates for Ms. Kim Keon-hee. If her detention is not extended, expect a massive mobilization of the DPK’s base and an immediate surge in the impeachment narrative. Second, watch the internal dynamics of the DPK leadership. Figures like Kim Min-seok, Jung Chung-rae, and Lee Un-ju—the party’s high-profile firebrands—are increasingly vocal about "ending the era of hesitation." If they can convince the party’s central committee that the risk of inaction is greater than the risk of rejection, the impeachment motion will move forward.

Finally, watch the reactions of the legal elite. If Chief Justice Cho attempts to make conciliatory gestures—such as reshuffling the National Court Administration or speeding up stalled trials—it may be a sign that the pressure is working. However, if he doubles down on his current path, South Korea may be headed for its most significant constitutional crisis since the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. In the end, the question remains: can a democracy truly thrive when its elected leaders and its judicial guardians are in a state of open, undeclared war?

To delay justice is, in the end, no different from siding with injustice.
