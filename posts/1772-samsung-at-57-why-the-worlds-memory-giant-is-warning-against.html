Samsung at 57: Why the World’s Memory Giant is Warning Against Its Own Success

October 31, 2026

Inside the headquarters of Samsung Electronics, the atmosphere on its 57th anniversary this October was far from celebratory. Usually, an anniversary for South Korea’s "National Team" company—a behemoth that accounts for nearly 20% of the country’s exports—is a time for highlighting record-breaking achievements and visionary milestones. Instead, the messages coming from the executive suite were chilling. While the company is technically reporting massive profits, the mood on the ground is described as "cold and severe."

For global investors, the paradox of Samsung has never been more visible. On paper, it is a money-printing machine. In the markets, however, it is facing a "selling blitz" that has seen its stock price languish while competitors like Nvidia and SK Hynix (Samsung’s primary domestic rival in the chip space) soar. The company recently announced a $900 million (1.27 trillion won) acquisition of a stake in China’s TCL CSOT and a massive $635 million (880 billion won) order for its component-making arm, but these headlines have failed to quiet the skeptics. The central question looming over the Korean peninsula is no longer whether Samsung is big, but whether it can still be the best in an era defined by Artificial Intelligence (AI).

Internal Alarms: The End of the "Top Tier" Ego

The most striking aspect of Samsung’s 57th anniversary was the rhetoric used by its top leadership. Jun Young-hyun, the Vice Chairman of the Device Solutions (DS) division—the arm responsible for Samsung’s world-leading semiconductor business—issued a warning that sounded more like a confession. He stated flatly that "today’s boom does not guarantee tomorrow’s competitiveness."

To an outsider, this might sound like standard corporate humility. To those who follow the Korean market, it is a seismic shift in tone. For decades, Samsung operated under the "super-gap" (cho-gyeok-cha) strategy, maintaining such a massive technological lead that competitors couldn't even see their tailpipes. However, in the high-stakes world of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—the specialized chips required to power AI processors like those from Nvidia—Samsung has uncharacteristically fallen behind SK Hynix.

Jun’s message points to a "crisis of trust" regarding quality and technical prowess. He essentially admitted that Samsung’s internal culture had become complacent, perhaps a lingering side effect of the volatile years during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. During that period, the tech sector often felt neglected as the government focused on prosecutorial overreach rather than coherent industrial policy. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party, there is a renewed push to treat technology as a pillar of national security, but Jun’s comments suggest that the "internal rot"—a loss of the relentless engineering spirit that built Samsung—is what needs fixing first. He is calling for a return to the basics: yield rates, quality control, and the "hungry" mindset that defined the company’s rise in the 1990s.

The $900 Million Insurance Policy in China

While the semiconductor division struggles with its soul-searching, Samsung’s display and consumer electronics divisions are making pragmatic, defensive moves. Samsung Electronics recently decided to acquire a 10.16% stake in TCL CSOT, a major Chinese display manufacturer, for approximately $920 million (1.27 trillion won).

To understand why this matters, one must understand the transition in the television market. Samsung Display officially exited the Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) business years ago to focus on high-end Organic Light Emitting Diodes (OLED). However, Samsung’s TV division still sells millions of LCD-based sets, including its premium QLED line. By securing a significant stake in TCL CSOT, Samsung is effectively buying an insurance policy. In a world where Chinese manufacturers now dominate the LCD supply chain, Samsung cannot afford to be at the mercy of price hikes or supply shortages from external vendors.

This move reflects the pragmatic "Realpolitik" of the current Lee Jae-myung government’s economic stance. While geopolitical tensions remain, the administration has encouraged Korean firms to secure their supply chains through strategic equity rather than ideological posturing. For Samsung, this isn't about re-entering the LCD market; it’s about maintaining "bargaining power" so it can milk the profits of its TV business to fund the incredibly expensive transition to next-generation AI technologies. It is a defensive maneuver designed to protect the "cash cow" while the "future engines" are being repaired.

AI Beyond Chips: The Rise of Samsung Electro-Mechanics

While the "big brother" Samsung Electronics is under fire, its sibling, Samsung Electro-Mechanics (the group’s primary electronic component manufacturer), is quietly winning the AI race in a different lane. The company recently secured a massive $635 million (879 billion won) contract to supply Multi-Layer Ceramic Capacitors (MLCCs) for AI servers.

If semiconductors are the "brain" of an AI server, MLCCs are the "circulatory system." They regulate the flow of electricity to prevent interference and damage. An AI server requires significantly more, and higher-quality, MLCCs than a standard server. This contract has pushed Samsung Electro-Mechanics' cumulative orders for the year past the $3.6 billion (5 trillion won) mark.

This is a critical indicator for foreign investors: the "AI Boom" is not just about the processors. It is an infrastructure overhaul. Samsung is also pivoting its Foundry business (the business of making chips designed by other companies, like Qualcomm or Nvidia). Historically, Samsung’s Foundry was heavily reliant on mobile chips for smartphones. The company has now set an ambitious goal to have High-Performance Computing (HPC) and AI chips account for 50% of its Foundry revenue by next year. This shift is essential for survival. The mobile market is maturing, but the appetite for AI compute is seemingly bottomless. If Samsung can prove its 2-nanometer and 3-nanometer nodes are stable enough for HPC clients, it could finally begin to chip away at the dominance of Taiwan’s TSMC.

The "Samsung Discount" and the HBM Wound

The most painful statistic for Samsung right now is its market capitalization. Despite pulling in revenues that rival or exceed big tech giants like Google or Microsoft, its market value has shrunk to roughly one-fifth of Nvidia’s. This is the "Samsung Discount"—a specialized version of the "Korea Discount" that affects many domestic stocks.

Investors are punishing Samsung for its late start in HBM3E chips. In the semiconductor world, being second can be a death sentence for profit margins. The market currently views Samsung as a "fast follower" that has stopped being "fast." There is also a political dimension to this frustration. Many retail investors in their 30s and 40s—a key demographic in the Korean electorate—openly lament the "lost years" of the previous era. They argue that while Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration was embroiled in domestic scandals and regressive diplomatic moves, Samsung’s leadership was too distracted by legal pressures to make the bold R&D bets needed for the AI era.

Under the current Lee Jae-myung administration, there is a distinct shift toward "pragmatic support." The government is pushing for tax reforms on inheritance and dividends to encourage long-term investment, hoping to cure the "Samsung Discount" by making it more attractive for the company to return value to its shareholders. However, as leadership noted during the anniversary, dividend checks are a temporary fix. The only real cure is a "technological breakout."

The Roadmap to 2027: Can Samsung Reclaim the Crown?

Samsung is not sitting still. The company has laid out a roadmap to begin mass production of HBM4E—the next, even faster generation of AI memory—by early 2027. This is the line in the sand. If Samsung can hit this target with high yields, it could leapfrog the current competition and reclaim its title as the undisputed king of memory.

Beyond the hardware, the company is also leaning into the "Green Premium." It has set a staggering goal of a 99.9% resource recycling rate at its semiconductor campuses, including the massive Pyeongtaek site (the world’s largest semiconductor cluster). In an era where global clients like Apple and Google demand strict ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) compliance, Samsung’s ability to produce "clean" chips is becoming a competitive advantage as significant as chip speed itself.

What to watch next:

  • The January Shareholder Plan: Samsung is expected to announce a new shareholder return policy in January 2025. Look for aggressive stock buybacks or dividend increases as a signal that the company is confident in its cash flow.
  • HBM3E Nvidia Certification: Any news regarding Samsung’s 12-layer HBM3E chips passing final quality tests for Nvidia’s Blackwell architecture will be the primary catalyst for a stock price recovery.
  • Government R&D Subsidies: Watch for the Democratic Party-led National Assembly to pass new semiconductor special laws that could provide direct subsidies for power and water infrastructure at the Pyeongtaek and Yongin clusters, potentially lowering Samsung's operational costs significantly.

Samsung at 57 is a company in transition. It is no longer the invincible titan of the 2010s, but it remains the only company in the world that can design chips, manufacture them, and build the devices they go into. For the global reader, the story of Samsung is the story of whether a legacy giant can shed its bureaucratic weight fast enough to keep pace with the lightning speed of the AI revolution.
