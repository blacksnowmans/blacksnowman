What Do We Live For? Lessons from Tolstoy in Modern Korea

September 27, 2026

During the Chuseok holiday—South Korea’s major harvest festival, often compared to Thanksgiving—it is traditional for families to gather, share food, and reflect on the year. While many might turn to blockbuster films or modern bestsellers, there is something profoundly grounding about revisiting Leo Tolstoy’s classic, What Men Live By. The story of Semyon, a humble shoemaker, is more than a 19th-century Russian folktale; it is a mirror reflecting the anxieties of modern capitalist life.

In a society like Korea’s, where economic competition is fierce and the pressure to accumulate material wealth often overshadows personal well-being, Tolstoy’s fundamental question—"What do humans live by?"—hits closer to home than ever. By stripping away the distractions of modern status, the story forces us to consider whether we are living for ourselves, or for each other.

The Weight of the Fur Coat: Survival in a Harsh Economy

Semyon is a man living on the margins. He possesses no land and no home of his own, renting a small space from a farmer while barely scraping by as a cobbler. The text notes that "bread was expensive and labor was cheap," a sentiment that will resonate with many readers today as they navigate fluctuating inflation and the rising cost of living in South Korea. Semyon’s obsession with a new fur coat represents more than simple vanity; it is his "survival line," a desperate attempt to endure the brutal Russian winter.

Historically, this reflects the aftermath of the 1861 Emancipation Reform in Russia. While the decree technically freed the serfs, it left them economically tethered to the land-owning elite. Even after reform, the distribution of wealth remained staggering: while the land holdings of the aristocracy dropped from 80% to 50%, the peasantry only saw their share rise to a meager 20%. The vast majority were trapped in a cycle of debt and poverty, much like the modern urbanite struggling with credit card debt or the relentless pressure of constant consumption.

When Semyon encounters a shivering, naked man behind a chapel, his initial reaction is not charity, but fear. This is the "bystander effect" of the 19th century—a concern that helping someone else might lead to one's own ruin. However, Tolstoy posits that ignoring the suffering of others is a form of spiritual suicide. When Semyon chooses to share his meager resources, he is doing something counter-intuitive to the capitalist mindset: he is prioritizing human dignity over personal safety.

The Domestic Tug-of-War: Hospitality Under Pressure

Semyon’s wife, Matryona, is the voice of harsh reality. With only enough flour left for one loaf of bread, she is terrified by her husband’s decision to bring a homeless stranger into their home. Her anger is not born of malice, but of the acute, paralyzing anxiety of scarcity. In any society, altruism is viewed as a luxury of the wealthy; for those at the bottom, sharing feels like throwing away one’s own lifebuoy.

Tolstoy does not demonize Matryona for her hesitation. Instead, he highlights the tension between self-preservation and empathy. When Semyon tells her, "Matryona, there is no God in you," he is not making a religious threat; he is pointing out that by turning away from a suffering human being, we lose the very thing that makes us human. Her eventual decision to share the bread is the emotional climax of the story, proving that even in the most dire circumstances, human connection can thaw the coldest hearts.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Semyon’s poverty reflects the structural inequalities of post-emancipation Russia, mirroring modern economic anxieties.
  • The act of overcoming fear to help a stranger is the foundational step toward true human dignity.
  • True charity is not an act of superiority by the wealthy, but a bond of solidarity between those struggling at the bottom.

The Three Truths: Why We Actually Live

As the story progresses, the mysterious stranger, revealed to be an angel named Mikhail, provides three fundamental truths that Tolstoy wants the reader to internalize:

  • What dwells in humans: Love. Despite our obsession with selfishness as the engine of the economy, Tolstoy argues that our true survival instinct is actually our capacity for love—the desire to protect, feed, and care for others.
  • What is not given to humans: Foresight. We are not granted the knowledge of what we truly need for the future. The landlord in the story is certain he needs sturdy boots for a year of life, only to die that same day. We spend our lives preparing for a "tomorrow" that is never guaranteed.
  • What humans live by: Love, again. We live only because we are part of a web of connections. We exist only through the grace of others.

These truths challenge the modern obsession with efficiency and control. We spend our time optimizing our careers and portfolios, yet we are rarely prepared for the human connections that actually define our lives. The landlord, with all his power and wealth, was ultimately as helpless as the poorest serf when faced with the inevitable end.

Looking Ahead: Finding Humanity in the Grind

If there is one thing to take away from this classic during a holiday break, it is the importance of "inner awareness." As we return to our daily routines—working to afford that metaphorical "fur coat," be it a house, a car, or a stable career—we must ask ourselves: are we sacrificing our humanity in the process?

Tolstoy was a critic of the establishment, even being excommunicated by the Russian Orthodox Church for prioritizing human experience over rigid dogma. He encourages us to seek a life of practice, not just belief. This Chuseok, perhaps the most revolutionary thing we can do is not to worry about our status or our bank accounts, but to extend a moment of genuine grace to those around us. In a world that keeps us running, the simplest act of kindness remains the most profound way to live.

What to watch for: As Korea continues to face economic headwinds and demographic shifts, look for how collective empathy—rather than policy alone—shapes community resilience. Pay attention to the small, unrecorded acts of charity in your own neighborhood; they are the true indicator of a society’s health.
