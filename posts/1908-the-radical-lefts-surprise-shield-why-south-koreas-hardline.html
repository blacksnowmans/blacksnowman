The Radical Left’s Surprise Shield: Why South Korea’s Hardline NL Faction Is Rescuing President Lee Jae-myung

November 03, 2026

When a South Korean president’s public approval rating plummets into the 30s, standard political playbook logic dictates two things: the conservative opposition will go on the offensive, and disaffected ideological allies will demand an immediate course correction. Yet inside Seoul’s political landscape, an extraordinary role reversal is unfolding around President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

Instead of turning their backs on a struggling administration, South Korea’s most hardline radical left-wing faction—the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance, which dominates the fringe Progressive Party—has rushed to build a defensive perimeter around President Lee. For seasoned watchers of Korean politics, this tactical alliance is deeply perplexing. This is the very same ideological camp that spent decades relentlessly attacking previous Democratic presidents like Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in as "neoliberal traitors" and "American lackeys." Why has this faction chosen to become the ultimate shield for President Lee Jae-myung precisely as his public standing falters?

The answer lies in a complex mix of tailored public opinion framing, deep-seated factional rivalries on the Korean left, and a shared political history rooted in the industrial city of Seongnam.

The 3.5 Percent Defense: How a Left-Wing Think Tank Reframed the Poll Slump

The defensive operation broke into the open when the Progressive Policy Institute, a think tank affiliated with the radical-left Progressive Party, released a provocative report. With President Lee’s national approval rating sliding to 37.0% against a 59.8% disapproval rating, the report presented a daring argument: the drop in support had virtually nothing to do with disaffection among the progressive core.

The institute acknowledged that roughly 23.9% of voters who initially supported President Lee early in his term had drifted away. However, relying on a survey conducted by the pollster STI, the institute claimed that true "core progressives" accounted for a mere 3.5% of total voters among those drop-offs. The report emphasized that voter defection was relatively modest among loyalists of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (13.3%), whereas a staggering 75.3% of early switchers from the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, had returned to their baseline opposition stance.

The strategic intent behind these numbers is unmistakable. By isolating the disaffected progressive core to a minor 3.5% fraction, Shin Seok-jin, head of the Progressive Policy Institute, argued that public commentary exaggerating a split among core supporters was empirically unfounded. The report effectively provided the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction within the ruling party with a statistical shield: it framed internal criticism from moderate liberals not as a warning sign of structural failure, but as insignificant noise driven by fair-weather moderate-conservatives who were never true allies in the first place.

Yet this statistical maneuver contains a dangerous blind spot. In South Korea’s fiercely competitive electoral system, where national elections and legislative majorities are regularly decided by 1% to 2% margins, trivializing a 3.5% erosion among key supporters as a negligible loss is political recklessness. By signaling that the administration can ignore internal critics and double down on its base, the Progressive Party’s analysis encourages the ruling camp to abandon the moderate center altogether.

Tailored Questions and Ideological Codes: The STI Survey Agenda

A closer look at the survey conducted by STI reveals how deeply the agenda of the hardline National Liberation (NL) faction—the dominant ideological current within the Progressive Party—has been woven into the defense of President Lee.

Rather than focusing on the pressingly urgent anxieties of average South Korean voters, such as runaway housing prices, stock market volatility, or judicial reform, the survey embedded signature NL ideological litmus tests. It polled respondents on their support for the immediate retrieval of wartime operational control (OPCON) from the United States and the urgent restoration of inter-Korean dialogue. When respondents answered positively, the institute used the data to claim that the core base firmly backed the administration's foreign policy and security posture.

This tactic represents a classic "survey-by-design" approach: framing questions to produce predetermined answers that lend scientific credibility to a fringe faction's ideological agenda. For foreign observers, understanding the internal divides of the Korean left requires grasping the historic split between two major factions:

  • The National Liberation (NL) Faction: Focused on anti-imperialism, strong Korean ethnic nationalism, opposition to the U.S. military alliance, and reconciliation or unification with North Korea.
  • The People’s Democracy (PD) Faction: Focused on European-style social democracy, Marxist class analysis, labor rights, and domestic inequality, often holding a deeply critical view of North Korea's authoritarian regime.

Historically, NL activists viewed moderate Democratic presidents as compromised liberals. During former President Roh Moo-hyun's administration, NL groups spearheaded violent street protests against the U.S.-Korea Free Trade Agreement. Under former President Moon Jae-in, they lambasted the government for failing to break with Washington on inter-Korean sanctions. Yet today, prominent young media figures from the Progressive Party regularly appear on pro-government streaming shows to defend President Lee’s administration against all criticism.

From Seongnam’s Radical Roots to Central Power: The Gyeonggi Dongbu Legacy

To understand why the hardline Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance—the most disciplined NL faction originating from the Seongnam and Yongin regions of Gyeonggi Province—is standing so firmly behind President Lee Jae-myung, one must trace their history back to 1991.

The Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance grew out of the National Alliance for Democracy and Reunification (Jeonguk-yeonhap), a massive coalition of post-pro-democracy student movements. In 2001, through the famous "Gunjasan Promise," NL cadres made a strategic decision to enter institutional politics by joining the Democratic Labor Party en masse, eventually capturing its party apparatus. Over the decades, key figures from this network, including former lawmaker Lee Seok-ki, were embroiled in severe national security investigations involving underground organizations (such as the Revolutionary Organization, or RO) and underground pro-Pyongyang networks like the Minhyokdang.

Unlike former presidents Roh Moo-hyun or Moon Jae-in—who belonged to the mainstream 1980s student activist establishment (the 386 Generation) and maintained strong ties to PD-leaning intellectuals—President Lee Jae-myung was an outsider. He did not come through the elite university activist pipeline; he built his political career from the ground up as a human rights lawyer and civic campaigner in Seongnam.

It was precisely in Seongnam that Lee Jae-myung's political trajectory intersected with the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance. As Lee rose from Seongnam mayor to Gyeonggi provincial governor and ultimately to the presidency, the organized, highly disciplined local networks of the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance provided crucial ground-level political backing. In return, this non-mainstream radical faction found in Lee something they had never possessed under previous Democratic administrations: direct political access and a vehicle to displace their longstanding rivals on the left.

The Factional Paradox: The Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance has historically harbor deeper resentment toward PD-leaning liberal intellectuals—such as former Justice Minister Cho Kuk or commentator Rhyu Si-min—than toward political adversaries, viewing them as bourgeois revisionists. Supporting Lee Jae-myung allows them to sideline their traditional rivals within the broader progressive movement.

Internal Infiltration and Factional Warfare: The Strategy of Division

The current behavior of the Progressive Party highlights a familiar tactical pattern long used by hardline NL cadres: infiltrating broader political coalitions, engineering internal divisions, and aggressively silencing moderate critics by branding them as "traitors" or "watermelons" (a colloquial slur used by hardline pro-Lee loyalists to denote politicians who are "green" Democratic on the outside but "red" conservative on the inside).

By publishing custom polling data asserting that core support remains rock-solid at 37%, the Progressive Party provides ideological cover for the ruling party's militant online base to execute purges of internal dissenters. Moderate lawmakers within the Democratic Party of Korea who express alarm over slipping public support find themselves targeted as factional splitters destroying party unity.

This dynamic mirrors the destructive factional battles that dismantled previous left-wing parties in South Korea, such as the Democratic Labor Party and the Unified Progressive Party. By substituting genuine policy reflection with hardline ideological discipline, the alliance between the "New Lee Jae-myung" loyalists and the Gyeonggi Dongbu faction risks insulating the presidency from the political reality felt by everyday citizens.

What to Watch Next: The Real-World Limits of Factional Shields

As President Lee Jae-myung navigates the remainder of his term, relying on a fringe hardline faction to manage public approval carries severe structural risks. International observers and market analysts should watch three key indicators to gauge how this factional dynamic will shape South Korea's political stability:

  • Moderate Voter Alienation in the Capital Area: Watch whether polling in crucial swing districts across Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province continues to deteriorate. If moderate voters reject the administration's hardline turn, pressure will build within the mainstream Democratic Party to break ties with the Progressive Party network.
  • Policy Alignment on Foreign and Security Affairs: Monitor whether the Lee administration adopts the Progressive Party's core demands—such as pushing for rapid OPCON transfer or making unilateral concessions to reopen inter-Korean channels. Action on these fronts will indicate how much policy leverage the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance has extracted in exchange for its media defense.
  • Internal Backlash from Liberal Intellectuals: Pay close attention to reactions from established PD-leaning figures and traditional moderate liberals within the ruling coalition. If figures like Cho Kuk or former mainstream party leaders begin openly pushing back against the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance's influence, a major ideological civil war on the Korean left could erupt ahead of upcoming local and legislative elections.

In the high-stakes game of South Korean politics, statistical shields and factional discipline can temporarily obscure a presidency's vulnerabilities. But when public dissatisfaction stems from real economic pressures, hiding behind ideological loyalists rarely halts a decline—it merely narrows the exit routes.
