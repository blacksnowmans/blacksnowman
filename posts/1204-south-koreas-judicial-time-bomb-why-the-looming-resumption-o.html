South Korea’s Judicial Time Bomb: Why the Looming Resumption of President Lee Jae-myung’s Trials Could Reshape the Republic

October 16, 2026

In the legal district of Seocho-dong in southern Seoul and the legislative halls of Yeouido, an explosive rumor has ceased to be mere corridor gossip: sitting judges are quietly preparing to restart the frozen criminal trials of President Lee Jae-myung. For months, the consensus was that the presidency itself offered an insurmountable political and procedural shield, suspending the legal machinery that had pursued him for years. Yet inside South Korea’s courtrooms, whispers of bench magistrates asserting their intent to "reopen the docket" have grown loud enough to ignite open panic within the ruling progressive camp.

The timing could hardly be more dangerous for the administration. According to recent polling by Gallup Korea, President Lee’s approval rating has tumbled for three consecutive weeks to 35.2%, while the conservative opposition, the People Power Party, has overtaken the ruling Democratic Party of Korea with 41.3% support against the ruling party's 35.5%. When an executive’s political capital dips near the perilous 30% baseline, institutional deference evaporates. As public exhaustion deepens and judicial caution turns into institutional defiance, South Korea stands on the precipice of an unprecedented constitutional clash between the presidency and the courts.

The Fivefold Legal Trap Closing In on the Presidency

President Lee faces an extraordinary web of legal liabilities—five separate criminal proceedings that were paused or slowed following his election. To understand why the prospect of their resumption sends shockwaves through Seoul, one must examine the specific mechanisms of these cases and the legal traps they present.

The most immediate and lethal threat lies in his ongoing trial for alleged violations of the Public Official Election Act. In the court of first instance, Lee received a suspended prison sentence of one year with two years of probation—a penalty that legally nullifies an election victory and strips the convicted official of their office. While the appellate court overturned that conviction, the Supreme Court subsequently quashed the acquittal and remanded the case back to the Seoul High Court with instructions finding guilt. Under South Korean jurisprudence, a remand by the top court carries strict binding force (gisokryeok) over lower courts. In practical terms, the appellate judges are essentially tasked with determining the severity of the sentence rather than debating innocence. Should a fine of 1 million won (approximately $750) or higher be finalized, Lee would face disqualification from the presidency.

Trailing closely behind is a charge of subornation of perjury. Although acquitted at his initial trial, the prosecution appealed, and the case sits paused at the Seoul High Court. Subornation of perjury is treated severely within the Korean legal system as an assault on the administration of justice; a reversal on appeal would deliver a crushing political blow.

Beyond these advanced appellate matters lie three sprawling cases stalled at the district court level:

  • The Municipal Development Scandals: Allegations of illicit preferential treatment surrounding lucrative real estate projects in Daejang-dong, Baekhyeon-dong, and Wirye, alongside corporate donations to the Seongnam FC football club during Lee's tenure as mayor of Seongnam.
  • The Ssangbangwool North Korea Remittance Case: Allegations involving an underwear manufacturer illicitly transferring millions of dollars to North Korea, allegedly on behalf of Gyeonggi Province when Lee served as its governor.
  • The Corporate Card Misuse Allegations: Inquiries into the suspected private misuse of official Gyeonggi provincial credit cards by his household.

While the administration has reportedly explored having prosecutors drop charges (gongso-chwisoh), criminal procedure allows such withdrawals only before a first-instance verdict is rendered. If district courts decide to proceed to judgment, that exit door closes permanently. Moreover, if simultaneous trials resume across these dockets, South Korea would witness the extraordinary spectacle of its sitting head of state spending workdays in criminal courtrooms facing cross-examinations—a logistical and institutional nightmare that would paralyze daily governance.

A Party on the Brink of Financial Insolvency

The crisis extends far beyond the personal fate of the president. Beneath the constitutional debate sits an existential financial threat to the ruling Democratic Party of Korea itself.

Under South Korea’s Public Official Election Act, if a presidential candidate's election is retroactively invalidated due to a campaign finance or election law violation carrying a fine of 1 million won or more, their sponsoring political party must return all state subsidies reimbursed for campaign expenses. In President Lee’s case, that figure stands at approximately 43.4 billion won (roughly $33 million).

For any political party, coughing up over $30 million on short notice is a bankruptcy event. Even liquidating the Democratic Party’s national headquarters in Yeouido and emptying all operational reserves would struggle to cover the sum. It would render the party virtually incapable of funding future national campaigns, dismantling its electoral apparatus and crippling the broader progressive movement for years. This staggering liability explains the quiet desperation within party ranks. Behind closed doors, lawmakers are forced to weigh blind loyalty against the survival of their institutional home, fueling covert discussions about a potential "Plan B" should the judiciary drop the gavel.

Constitutional Hardball Between the Blue House and the Bench

Should judicial panels move to reactivate hearings, the presidential office is expected to mount an immediate constitutional counteroffensive. The likely maneuver would involve filing an adjudication on competence disputes (gwonhan-jaeui) and requesting temporary injunctions at the Constitutional Court, arguing that subjecting a sitting president to active criminal trial violates the separation of powers and disrupts executive function.

Yet this shield is far from foolproof. The judicial power is an inherent, independent constitutional prerogative. Under Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the judicial mainstream has shown little appetite for administrative interference. If trial courts determine that procedural hearings must advance pending constitutional rulings, the presidency possesses few direct, lawful mechanisms to halt them. An overt executive attempt to shut down a remand ordered by the Supreme Court would immediately invite accusations of obstructing justice, compounding the administration's political vulnerabilities.

Tensions have been inflamed further by recent friction over investigative independence. Observers and opposition lawmakers have sharply criticized President Lee’s practice of summoning heads of law enforcement and investigative bodies—including the acting National Police Agency commissioner, the National Investigation Office, and directors of newly reshaped investigative agencies—to regular Cabinet meetings. Critics argue these gatherings border on executive direction over active investigations, recalling earlier controversies when Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, during his tenure as prosecutor general, publicly resisted political interference from the executive branch.

This dynamic has put the spotlight squarely on Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. In South Korean political history, the judiciary rarely challenges the executive when public support for the Blue House is robust. But as approval ratings soften and legislative confirmation battles grow toxic, conservative and institutionalist elements within the judiciary tend to coalesce around procedural rectitude. The perception that the administration is testing constitutional boundaries may give judges the exact institutional justification they need to resume proceedings.

What Lies Ahead: A Mutual Reckoning for Korean Politics?

The current turbulence unfolds against the backdrop of another unprecedented legal saga: the prosecution of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was removed from office via impeachment and remains detained behind bars facing severe insurrection charges. While the progressive base demands the harshest penalties—including life imprisonment or capital punishment—for Mr. Yoon, that very pursuit sets a ruthless precedent. An uncompromising, zero-tolerance standard applied to a disgraced former leader creates immense pressure on the courts to apply an equally unyielding standard to the incumbent president.

This parallel dynamic has generated palpable public fatigue across South Korea. Among swing voters and moderates, there is a visible exhaustion with politicians perpetually enveloped in legal defenses, search-and-seizure warrants, and court dates. The persistent drag of President Lee’s five legal entanglements has overshadowed promised reforms and polarized the National Assembly to the point of gridlock.

In the coming months, several critical benchmarks will signal whether this crisis boils over:

  • High Court Scheduling: Watch whether the Seoul High Court sets calendar dates for the remanded Public Official Election Act case or unfreezes the subornation of perjury docket. Any formal procedural notification will trigger immediate legal warfare.
  • Constitutional Filings: Monitor whether the Blue House formally petitions the Constitutional Court for an injunction against trial proceedings, a move that would mark an open institutional rupture.
  • Legislative Fallout: Observe how ruling party cohesion holds during upcoming parliamentary committee hearings, particularly if approval ratings remain suppressed below 40% while the conservative opposition stays ahead.

South Korea’s democracy has demonstrated remarkable resilience through past presidential crises, but it has never navigated a situation where a sitting president faces active, binding trial dockets that carry immediate disqualification. The coming decisions from Seoul's judges will determine not only the political survival of President Lee Jae-myung, but the balance of constitutional authority between the executive and the bench for a generation to come.
