Riding the Rollercoaster: Why Foreign and Retail Investors are Betting Against Each Other in South Korea's Tech Sector

September 08, 2026

Picture the trading floor in Seoul on a turbulent morning. For weeks, local individual investors have been gripped by panic, dumping more than 7 trillion won (about $5.2 billion) worth of equities in a desperate bid to stem their losses. Yet, as the market dips, foreign capital has moved aggressively in the opposite direction, quietly sweeping up nearly 6 trillion won (about $4.4 billion) in local shares. This stark divergence raises an urgent question: Why are Main Street and foreign institutions looking at the exact same market and seeing two entirely different realities?

The divide essentially boils down to perspective. Local retail investors often view short-term corrections as existential threats, while foreign buyers and institutional players see a structural transition. This friction point has been supercharged by recent industry developments, most notably OpenAI's introduction of its "Astra" system, which has officially kicked off the era of AI agents and injected massive new momentum back into the semiconductor supply chain.

We have moved past the point of simply reacting to day-to-day market volatility. What we are witnessing today is a civilizational turning point on par with the discovery of fire, the invention of the wheel, or the commercialization of the internet. Driven by this structural wave, the earnings power of leading semiconductor companies is projected to trend upward steadily through 2028, and potentially as far as 2030. Instead of panicking every time a correction hits, the real task is to decode why smart money is steadily accumulating shares during the downturn.

AI Agents Are Completely Rewriting Computing Economics

If you follow Silicon Valley tech news lately, you will find no shortage of warnings about a looming "AI bubble," often fueled by dropping token prices. As AI firms engage in a bruising price war, driving the cost per token down from $2 dollars to around $0.98, critics worry that semiconductor makers will ultimately take a financial hit. But this interpretation misses the forest for the trees. From the perspective of developers and consumers, cheaper computing costs mean that complex tasks once deemed prohibitively expensive can now be executed at scale.

Tasks that previously required 100 tokens can now be expanded to 200 or 300 units without hesitation. Artificial intelligence is no longer a tool that merely spits out a few lines of text. It has evolved into an agent capable of instantaneous visual and linguistic processing—the kind of system where you can turn on a smartphone camera, point it at a landmark like Seoul's landmark 63 Building, and ask for immediate visual analysis and spoken explanation. As utilization rates skyrocket, a minor drop in the cost per token does not hurt suppliers; it acts as a massive accelerator, stimulating even broader infrastructure demand.

History suggests that during a gold rush, predicting who will strike it rich is notoriously difficult, but the companies selling the pickaxes and blue jeans always make a profit. Semiconductor manufacturers are the pickaxe-sellers of the AI revolution. Their favorable earnings forecasts are not built on wishful thinking; they are anchored in the inescapable physical reality that an avalanche of new data must be processed somewhere.

At the same time, real-world robotics are moving from the screen to the factory floor. Tesla is rolling out thousands of its Optimus robots across its Gigafactories, bridging the gap between science fiction and everyday reality. These machines are evolving past rigid, pre-written code to make autonomous judgments in human-like environments. This expansion of infrastructure means the benefits of AI are scaling globally.

When these robots graduate from simply transporting parts to offering complex situational advice—akin to Iron Man's artificial intelligence assistant, Jarvis—global computing demand will surge to unprecedented heights. Elon Musk's Grok 5.0 and similar models are nipping at the heels of frontrunners like OpenAI and Anthropic, and this intense rivalry ensures that developers will continually demand better algorithms and heavier workloads.

The Growing Pains of Innovation: It is worth acknowledging that OpenAI posted tens of billions of won in operating losses, driven by skyrocketing compute costs that easily outpaced revenue growth. However, this should be read as a necessary growing pain. Pouring hundreds of billions of dollars into infrastructure is the quintessential "pre-investment phase" tech companies must endure before commercial software yields ironclad profits. While selling infrastructure does not automatically guarantee profit for chipmakers, so long as raw global demand holds firm, market corrections remain buying opportunities.

Decoding September's Volatility: How Foreign Inflows Signal Market Resilience

When foreign investors sweep up large blocks of local equities—such as recent massive net purchases in the Korean spot and futures markets—they are sending a deliberate message. After dumping billions of dollars worth of local shares over a short five-session window in late August, these institutional players quickly returned to buying heavyweight champions like Samsung Electronics, South Korea's dominant memory chip and smartphone manufacturer, and SK Hynix, a premier global memory chip supplier. They are not buying on a whim. Despite macro headwinds, Producer Price Index (PPI) and Consumer Price Index (CPI) releases, and ongoing interest rate debates, foreign desks are placing a higher value on the underlying fundamentals of South Korea's technology sector.

Federal Reserve officials have frequently characterized current economic pressure as a byproduct of underlying growth strength. The heavy capital expenditures flowing into artificial intelligence have triggered temporary reallocations of capital out of sovereign bonds and into higher-yield assets, creating short-term turbulence. So long as interest rates remain stable, these market adjustments should be viewed with a measure of calm.

Currency exchange rates have also begun to stabilize. While external shocks can rattle local exchanges, the resilience of the South Korean market—anchored by its dominant position in advanced semiconductors—remains remarkably robust. Following the institutional order flow has always been a fundamental investing axiom, and right now, foreign capital allocation is the clearest compass available for tracking market direction.

For individual retail investors, psychological safety valves are just as important as technical analysis. Committing 100% of one's liquid capital to equities and checking portfolio values every hour is a recipe for emotional burnout during a correction. Maintaining a cash buffer of 30% to 40% is not merely a defensive tactic; it is an essential psychological safeguard designed to help investors survive high-volatility environments without panic-selling at the bottom.

Investors must abandon the fantasy of scoring an overnight jackpot during choppy periods. Instead, corrections should be treated as vehicles for long-term accumulation. Those obsessed with immediate returns often wash out before reaping the rewards of a multi-year semiconductor cycle. Watch how industry leaders break out of trading ranges and absorb selling pressure—these are classic technical signals of underlying strength preparing for a leg higher.

Positioning for the Future: Energy Bottlenecks and Income Strategies

While the semiconductor sector remains the crown jewel of the current tech rally, investors must also look to adjacent bottlenecks. As artificial intelligence data centers multiply, electricity consumption is hitting historic highs. Naturally, this shortage places a premium on grid infrastructure and nuclear energy development. The steady performance of nuclear-related equities is no accident.

At the same time, alternative energy solutions deserve a closer look. Technologies like geothermal energy and solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are poised to play critical roles in the global energy transition. Building a robust portfolio means keeping semiconductors at the core while surrounding them with strategically chosen energy and power infrastructure stocks.

For investors who prefer a diversified, hands-off approach to individual stock picking, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) offer a pragmatic alternative. Products focused on U.S. dividend aristocrats or covered-call strategies allow investors to capture broader market upside while generating steady monthly dividend income—an ideal structure for those prioritizing cash flow management.

Investing ultimately tests temperament rather than raw intellect. In eras of heightened uncertainty, disciplined asset allocation and proven corporate value chains consistently outperform speculative bets. Just as the bulky tablet computers of the 1970s science fiction era evolved into today's smartphones, the artificial intelligence systems and autonomous robots of today will soon simply be part of the everyday background.

When that future arrives, current market prices will look trivial in hindsight. The corrections we experience today are merely resting phases designed to build the foundation for the next historical wave of technological growth. The underlying machinery in our factories is running louder than ever, and for those willing to look past the short-term noise, the structural rewards of the AI age are just beginning to take shape.
