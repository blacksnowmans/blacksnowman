Testing Times for the Lee Jae-myung Administration: Why Reform Fatigue is Setting In

October 26, 2026

Imagine a voter in Seoul who, just a year ago, stood in the streets celebrating a new era. After the dramatic impeachment and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the inauguration of President Lee Jae-myung felt like a definitive turning point for South Korean democracy. The promise was clear: a "return to normalcy," aggressive economic reform, and a thorough cleansing of the previous administration’s perceived corruption. But today, that same voter is likely checking their smartphone with a sense of growing unease. The stock market is wavering, housing costs remain stubbornly high, and the legal battles that were supposed to be settled are instead metastasizing into a broader crisis of the state.

Recent polling data suggests that the "honeymoon phase" for the Lee Jae-myung administration has officially ended. For the first time since the transition of power, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main progressive ruling party, has seen its approval rating dip below the psychologically significant 40% mark. Meanwhile, the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, is seeing a slight rebound. This isn't just a minor fluctuation in the polls; it is a warning light on the dashboard of Korean politics, signaling that the public’s patience with the pace of reform is wearing thin.

To understand why this matters, one must look past the raw percentages. For international observers, the current gridlock in Seoul reflects a deeper struggle over the nature of judicial power and national sovereignty. The Lee administration is currently caught between a base that demands "Sada-style" (refreshing or bold) action and a complex reality where the remnants of the previous era—specifically within the judiciary and the prosecution—continue to exert significant friction.

The Stagnation of Progress and the Economic Warning

The latest figures from Realmeter, a prominent South Korean polling agency, show President Lee’s approval rating drifting downward. While his core support remains relatively stable, the "negative" assessment has climbed to 62.3%. Even more concerning for the Blue House (the presidential office) is the data from more sensitive Automated Response System (ARS) polls, which show a dip in support among the progressive base itself. When a government’s own ideological foundation begins to express "disappointment," it usually indicates that the gap between rhetoric and reality has become too wide to ignore.

The primary driver of this dissatisfaction is the "bread and butter" issue of the economy. South Korea’s stock market has faced recent volatility, and the "Jeonse" system—a uniquely Korean housing lease arrangement where tenants provide a large lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent—is under severe strain. When the middle class feels their wealth evaporating in the markets and their housing security threatened, they rarely blame the opposition. They blame the person in charge.

However, the dissatisfaction isn't purely economic. There is a growing perception that the administration is being "too soft" or "too slow" in handling the legacies of the previous regime. Mention of the "Shincheonji" religious cult—a group that became a lightning rod for controversy during the COVID-19 pandemic and was frequently linked to the impeached Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—has resurfaced. Allegations that the current government has been lukewarm in blocking the group's influence have acted as a "trigger" for voters who remember the scandals of the previous era. For the Lee administration, being associated with the same "nightmare" scenarios that led to the downfall of his predecessor is a dangerous political development.

The Shadow of the Past: The Case for Arresting Kim Keon-hee

Perhaps no issue better illustrates the current public mood than the status of Kim Keon-hee, the wife of the impeached and currently imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Despite Mr. Yoon’s removal from office, Kim Keon-hee has remained a central figure in the Korean political consciousness due to a litany of unresolved allegations, ranging from stock manipulation to the acceptance of luxury gifts. For many supporters of the Lee administration, the fact that she has not yet faced a formal arrest warrant is a failure of "judicial justice."

The polls reflect this hunger for accountability. Roughly 60% of the population—a 6-to-3 margin—supports the issuance of an arrest warrant for Kim Keon-hee. This is not merely a partisan demand; it is a demand for the realization of the "fairness and common sense" that the Lee government promised. To the average voter, the sight of a former leader’s spouse seemingly evading the same legal scrutiny applied to ordinary citizens undermines the legitimacy of the current judicial system.

This "unresolved justice" is a major reason why the Democratic Party’s support is flagging. The public gave the DPK a massive mandate in the legislature, and they expect that power to be used to settle the accounts of the past. When the government appears to be "calculating" or "hesitating" rather than acting, the base begins to look elsewhere. The current sentiment is clear: "No one should stand above the law." If the Lee administration cannot deliver on this, it risks losing the moral high ground that brought it to power.

The Judicial Frontline and the Impeachment of the Chief Justice

The struggle for reform has now moved into the highest echelons of the legal system. At the center of this storm is Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. In recent months, Cho has become a figure of intense scrutiny, with critics within the ruling party accusing him of "privatizing judicial power." The allegation is that Cho is not acting as an impartial arbiter but as a political actor, potentially positioning himself as a future presidential contender.

This conflict reached a boiling point over recent court decisions that were seen as favoring conservative figures, such as Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon (a prominent member of the opposition PPP), while putting legal pressure on President Lee. Progressive academics and lawmakers argue that the judiciary is being used as a "tool" to weaken the elected government. The phrase "judicial dictatorship" has begun to appear in political discourse, reflecting a fear that the courts are overriding the will of the people.

This has led to a serious discussion about the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho. While impeachment is an extreme measure, proponents argue it is the only way to protect the independence of the judiciary from a leader who has "forgotten his role." They see a Chief Justice who is more interested in "political engineering" than in the law. For the international reader, this highlights a critical structural problem in Korean democracy: the extreme difficulty of reforming a judicial branch that was staffed during the previous administration. The Lee government finds itself in a position where its most ambitious reforms are being blocked not by the legislature, but by the gavels of the high court.

Reclaiming Sovereignty: The Trump Factor and OPCON

Beyond domestic scandals, a significant shift is occurring in how Koreans view their relationship with the world, specifically the United States. A recent poll showed a 5-to-3 margin in favor of South Korea unilaterally declaring its right to exercise Operational Control (OPCON) over its own military if the transition process continues to stall. Currently, in the event of a war on the Korean Peninsula, a U.S. general would command South Korean troops—a relic of the Korean War that remains a point of national pride and contention.

The driver behind this renewed push for military "sovereignty" is the "Trump variable." The possibility of a return to a Trump administration in the United States has created deep anxiety in Seoul. Memories of demands for massive increases in "defense cost-sharing" (the money Korea pays to host U.S. troops) and threats of troop withdrawals have led many Koreans to conclude that they can no longer rely solely on a foreign power for their ultimate security. "Our army must be commanded by us," has become a rallying cry that transcends simple anti-Americanism; it is a pragmatic response to an unpredictable ally.

This sentiment gives President Lee a powerful tool. By taking a firmer stance on military sovereignty, he can tap into a deep-seated desire for national dignity. However, it also places him in a delicate diplomatic position. Navigating the alliance with Washington while satisfying a domestic audience that wants more independence is perhaps the greatest foreign policy challenge of his term. The poll results suggest that the public is ready for a more "assertive" Korea, and they are watching to see if Lee has the "boldness" to deliver it.

What to Watch Next

The current stagnation in the polls is not a death knell for the Lee Jae-myung administration, but it is a clear signal that the status quo is no longer sufficient. To regain momentum, the government will likely need to move from the "defensive" to the "offensive" on several fronts.

First, watch the prosecution’s move regarding Kim Keon-hee. If the administration allows the investigation to stall further, the 60% of the public demanding justice may turn their anger toward the Blue House. A formal arrest warrant or a significant breakthrough in the case would likely re-energize the Democratic Party’s base.

Second, the economic response will be critical. Keep an eye on new measures to stabilize the "Jeonse" market and support the domestic stock exchange. If the administration can show tangible "pocketbook" results, the approval ratings will likely follow.

Third, the confrontation with the judiciary. The talk of impeaching Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is a high-stakes gamble. If the Democratic Party moves forward with it, it will trigger a constitutional crisis. If they back down, they risk appearing weak in the face of a "politicized" court. The outcome of this struggle will determine the fate of Lee’s reform agenda for the remainder of his term.

Ultimately, the numbers—41.2% and 39.7%—are more than just statistics. they represent a public that is waiting for the government to prove it can be as effective in power as it was passionate in opposition. In the high-velocity world of Korean politics, the window for proving that effectiveness is closing fast.
