Inside the Democratic Party's Crisis: Why Kim Min-seok’s Approval Has Plunged to 25 Percent

November 06, 2026

South Korean politics has a way of turning inward with startling speed, chewing up its own narratives and spitting out the bones before the public has even fully grasped what happened. Right now, the ruling Democratic Party—the progressive party currently holding the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung—is tearing itself apart over factional loyalties, aggressive online rhetoric, and a self-inflicted conspiracy theory that has begun to cost them dearly at the polls.

At the center of this storm is Representative Kim Min-seok, whose approval ratings have plummeted to a dismal 25.6 percent according to recent Realmeter data. For a party that prides itself on riding the wave of public reform and livelihood issues, a core leadership figure sitting in the mid-20s is an emergency siren. It points to a deep weariness among voters and party members alike who are watching the progressive camp bog itself down in internal factional warfare, erratic policy rollouts, and defensive posturing reminiscent of the worst kinds of partisan tribalism.

To understand how the Democratic Party reached this juncture, we have to look past the surface headlines and examine the tangled web of fake framing, legal miscalculations, and public reckonings that are currently defining South Korea's progressive landscape.

The Anatomy of the 'Red Guard' Controversy and the Shincheonji Smear

The trouble began in earnest during the party leadership primaries. In an effort to sideline a rival candidate, certain loyalist factions within the Democratic Party weaponized an unverified and explosive conspiracy theory, slapping their opponent with the label of a "Shincheonji triple secret alliance." Shincheonji is the secretive religious sect that has frequently found itself at the center of South Korean political scandals. Using it as a political smear is an effective way to poison a candidate among mainstream voters, but it is also a dangerous game.

When internet sleuths and opposing lawmakers began tracking down a group of 13 pro-Kim Min-seok lawmakers—dubbed by critics as the party's "Red Guards" for their unyielding, aggressive defense of their leader—the strategy boomeranged. Representative Lee Yong-woo openly challenged the hypocrisy on a broadcast program, noting that while pro-Kim lawmakers had used every dirty trick in the book to attack their rivals, they suddenly claimed ignorance when the Shincheonji allegations blew back on them. Instead of clarifying the record, this evasive maneuvering has left the public asking an uncomfortable question: Just who was Shincheonji backing?

The fallout has threatened to brand the entire Democratic Party with the derogatory moniker "Yucheonji Party"—a portmanteau of the party's name and the religious sect. Lawyer-turned-committee chair Lee Ji-eun pointed out that under Article 52 of South Korea's Political Parties Act, spreading falsehoods during a leadership race to elect oneself or defeat another is not protected free speech; it is a clear legal violation. Citing legal precedents, including the trials of imprisoned former leader Yoon Seok-youl, she emphasized that the overall context and how voters perceive a statement matter far more than a speaker's hidden intentions. When politicians throw around unverified accusations of collusion with fringe religious groups without a shred of cross-checked evidence, they cross the line from robust debate into manufactured defamation.

Grassroots Backlash and the Chasm Within the Party

The defensive reactions of party insiders have only deepened the rift with the rank-and-file membership. Pundit and lawyer Noh Young-hee recently drew furious blowback from party members after she appeared on a broadcast program and selectively edited remarks by Representative Jung Chung-rae, dismissing them as "childish." Jung had previously demanded a public apology from the leadership over the Shincheonji mess. Noh, however, framed the dispute as a battle between innocent party members and Jung on one side, and a guilty Kim Min-seok on the other.

Grassroots members, already exhausted by superficial, behind-closed-doors apologies that try to sweep scandals under the rug, were incensed. The logic of party insiders has consistently relied on calling for "unity," but members see through the buzzword. When Representative Kim Young-jin appealed to Jung Chung-rae to stop the infighting and focus on people's livelihoods, members recognized it for what it was: a political trap designed to force the party to swallow past mistakes and grant amnesty to those who manufactured the false Shincheonji frame in the first place.

The 25.6 percent approval rating is the direct result of this disconnect. Party members do not want a party that sacrifices truth and fairness at the altar of factional protection. They want accountability, and they are punishing the leadership for treating grassroots intelligence with contempt.

You Si-min’s Blunt Reality Check and the Media Landscape

Amid the wreckage, veteran writer and political commentator You Si-min stepped forward with a blunt assessment that resonated overwhelmingly with the public. In a joint survey by Munhwa Ilbo and Korea Information Research, 55 percent of respondents—and even higher majorities among core demographic groups in their 20s, 40s, and 50s—judged You's sharp public criticism of President Lee Jae-myung's political environment to be "appropriate," compared to just 36.4 percent who disagreed.

You was not attacking President Lee; rather, he was targeting the hyper-partisan online factions and pro-Kim loyalists who spend their time shouting down internal dissent while leaving the administration to fight its battles alone. President Lee himself has expressed frustration with the limits of modern digital communication, noting how short-form platforms like X (formerly Twitter) easily distort meaning and amplify toxic hostility. In contrast, longer-form media and structured video platforms allow for more nuanced, dialogue-driven governance.

President Lee's push for more direct, accountable communication—alongside directives for officials to aggressively execute reforms and deliver tangible results in local communities—has offered a breath of fresh air to voters exhausted by policy flip-flops. It stands in stark contrast to the stagnant, self-serving drama playing out within the party legislature.

Policy Chaos and the Winds of Change in the National Assembly

That political dysfunction has bled directly into policy execution, most notably in real estate and financial regulations. The government has repeatedly stumbled through inconsistent loan regulations—tightening rules aggressively only to loosen them shortly after, leaving the market in a daze and frontline loan officers scrambling. Long-promised structural reforms, such as the establishment of a real estate supervisory body, have spun their wheels for two years with little to show for it.

When the ruling party's energy is consumed by factional warfare and protecting specific figures, governance suffers. Yet, the recent plenary session of the National Assembly suggested that the pressure from the streets and party members is finally breaking through the legislative stone wall.

Following Kim Min-seok's public apologies, a motion calling for the disciplinary expulsion of conservative Representative Kim Tae-ho passed with a near-unanimous 166 votes out of 167 cast. While pro-Kim loyalists sat through the vote with grim expressions, reform-minded lawmakers made little effort to hide their satisfaction. It was a stark visual indicator that the tide of public opinion is finally shifting the balance of power inside the legislature.

What to Watch Next

As the Democratic Party navigates this multi-layered crisis, several key indicators will determine whether it can recover its footing or slide further into irrelevance:

  • The Trajectory of Kim Min-seok’s Leadership: Watch whether his approval rating remains trapped in the mid-20s or if mounting pressure forces a deeper restructuring of party leadership roles ahead of upcoming local political milestones.
  • Disciplinary and Legal Follow-through: Keep an eye on how the party handles the legal and ethical fallout from the primary season's smear campaigns, particularly whether independent investigative bodies take formal notice of the Political Parties Act violations outlined by legal experts.
  • Policy Consistency from the Executive: Look for tangible stability in real estate finance and loan guidelines. President Lee’s administration must prove it can cut through administrative hesitation and deliver concrete livelihood policies without being dragged down by legislative infighting.
  • The Shift in Assembly Dynamics: Monitor whether the near-unanimous vote on the disciplinary motion signals a permanent loss of leverage for the party’s hardline loyalist factions, opening the door for a more pragmatic, policy-focused legislative agenda.

Politics is ultimately about whose life is being represented in the halls of power. For the Democratic Party, the 25 percent approval rating is either a terminal diagnosis of a party lost in tribal warfare or a harsh, necessary wake-up call to sweep away the old politics of smears and return to the work of governing.
