The 2027 Deadline: Why Seoul’s Property Market Is Bracing for a Wave of Forced Selling

October 13, 2026

If you walked into a high-end cafe in Gangnam or scrolled through South Korean real estate forums recently, you would notice a distinct shift in tone. The frantic buying frenzies of recent years have given way to a nervous, spreadsheet-heavy calculus. Property owners, speculative "gap investors," and institutional landlords across Seoul are realizing that an invisible clock is ticking on their investments. That clock is set to run out on December 31, 2027.

For years, South Korea's housing market—particularly high-end apartments in Seoul—was buoyed by a maze of tax breaks, temporary exemptions, and policy loopholes designed to prevent market crashes during economic downturns. But following a series of regulatory enforcement decree revisions approved back in late September 2024, the tax shelter era is coming to a hard end. By late 2027, multi-homeowners and landlords will face an ultimatum: sell their property before tax grace periods vanish, or suffer punitive tax rates. Here is a deep dive into why Seoul's housing market is facing a wave of forced liquidations, and what it means for the broader market.

Closing the Grace Period: Reverting the Two-Year Disposal Rule

To understand the coming selling pressure, one must first look at how South Korea regulates temporary two-homeowners. In designated "Adjustment Target Zones"—highly regulated, speculative real estate hotspots covering much of Seoul—homeowners who purchase a second property to move homes are usually required to sell their original property within a strict timeframe to retain single-homeowner tax exemptions.

During Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure in office, this disposal window was extended from two years to three years. From an investor’s perspective, that extra year was a massive gift. It allowed affluent buyers to acquire a second luxury home—a strategy locally dubbed "one smart home" (ttol-ttol-han han chae), referring to concentrating capital into a single ultra-prime asset—while holding onto their previous property long enough to ride out price surges and maximize capital gains.

The revised regulatory decrees have now cut that disposal grace period back down to its original two years. While real estate communities are complaining that selling a property within two years during a high-interest environment is difficult, returning to a two-year standard restores regulatory normalization. Special three-year windows were emergency concessions created for abnormal market shocks like the 2008 global financial crisis and extreme post-pandemic inflation. In a stabilized regulatory landscape, maintaining those concessions indefinitely only fueled speculative flipping. With the window shrinking back to 24 months, multi-homeowners are suddenly under intense pressure to list their extra properties before tax penalties kick in.

The Fall of Registered Rental Businesses and the 2027 Tax Cliff

The biggest driver of the forecasted 2027 liquidity avalanche lies in the history of South Korea's registered private rental business operator scheme (known locally as Ju-im-sa). First introduced under the conservative Park Geun-hye administration, the program expanded dramatically under liberal President Moon Jae-in. To incentivize long-term rental supply, the government offered massive tax incentives: complete exemptions from the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (a holding tax levied on high-value property owners), an 80% reduction in capital gains taxes, and reduced income taxes for landlords who registered their apartments and pledged to lease them for a mandatory eight-year period.

Instead of merely stabilizing leases, the program unintentionally unleashed a wave of "gap investing"—a uniquely Korean practice where an investor buys a residential property by taking over the tenant's Jeonse (a massive, interest-free lump-sum lease deposit that typically covers 50% to 80% of the property's purchase value), enabling the buyer to acquire real estate with minimal cash out of pocket. Tens of thousands of investors rushed to leverage debt, buy apartments, and lock them into the eight-year registration program.

Realizing the speculative frenzy it had caused, the government dismantled the registered rental program for apartments in July 2020. However, the existing eight-year commitments remained legally binding. Apartments registered during the peak rush in 2017 and 2018 are reaching their mandatory expiration dates in 2025 and 2026, releasing hundreds of thousands of units nationwide—and tens of thousands in Seoul alone—from their lock-in periods.

Here is where the hard deadline comes in: the government has established that special tax reductions and long-term holding deductions for these former rental units will permanently expire on December 31, 2027. If landlords do not sell their properties before this date, their assets will immediately become subject to heavy multi-homeowner capital gains tax surcharges and full holding tax assessments. Consequently, high-net-worth investors are already planning their exits, with many reallocating capital out of residential real estate and into small commercial buildings (known locally as kkoma buildings) that offer more flexible loan conditions and favorable holding tax structures.

Sunsetting the 'Coexistence Landlord' Loophole in Ultra-Luxury Real Estate

Another major driver of the 2027 deadline is the sunsetting of the "Coexistence Landlord" scheme (Sangsaeng Imdaein). Introduced during a period of market downturn when rental market prices were plunging—creating severe lease-default distress ("reverse Jeonse")—the policy aimed to keep rents stable. It stipulated that if a landlord renewed a lease while capping rent increases to under 5%, the government would treat the owner as having fulfilled the mandatory two-year owner-occupancy requirement necessary to qualify for a single-household capital gains tax exemption.

While sound in theory, the scheme became a massive tax shelter for high-end gap investors. Because the policy lacked price caps on the underlying property, wealthy investors were able to purchase multi-million-dollar ultra-luxury apartments in prime Seoul districts without ever intending to live in them. By simply keeping tenant rent increases under 5% upon renewal, investors magically obtained full capital gains tax exemptions on multi-billion-won assets.

Between 2022 and 2023, speculative buyers rushed into Seoul to secure these "coexistence" leases. Because the policy requires maintaining those compliant leases for two years, properties that entered these agreements between late 2022 and 2024 are hitting their compliance completions. For landlords whose qualifying leases expire by late 2026, the deadline to execute a tax-exempt sale is rapidly approaching. Failure to sell within the designated post-lease grace period means forfeiting their tax-free status, creating a massive incentive to sell before 2027.

Market Polarization: What Lies Ahead for Seoul

While the sunsetting of these tax incentives will dump significant inventory onto the Seoul market—exerting downward pressure on luxury housing bubbles—it highlights a growing divide in South Korea's residential landscape. The real estate market is bifurcating along income lines:

  • The Ultra-Luxury Tier (Top Quintile): Speculative bubbles in high-end Seoul apartments are cooling as tax shelters disappear, forced sales increase, and elevated interest rates make carrying excess debt unviable.
  • Low-to-Mid Tier Housing (1st to 3rd Quintiles): While high-end sales prices face selling pressure, lower-tier rental markets are experiencing sharp price spikes. In some mid-to-low-tier Seoul neighborhoods, standard Jeonse deposit prices have doubled from around 200 million won (about $145,000) to 400 million won (about $290,000), placing immense stress on working-class renters.

For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the coming years present a complex policy challenge. Forcing multi-homeowners to sell off speculative assets helps deflate luxury real estate bubbles, but simply forcing listings onto the market does not automatically solve the shortage of affordable rental housing in downtown Seoul. To prevent working-class housing costs from escalating further, market analysts emphasize that tax normalization must be paired with genuine, large-scale urban housing supply initiatives.

What to Watch Next:

  • Q3 2025 to Q4 2026 Listing Volumes: Watch for a steady rise in inventory in top-tier Seoul districts (such as Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa) as former registered rental operators and "Coexistence Landlord" beneficiaries begin listing their units ahead of the tax sunset.
  • Capital Migration to Commercial Real Estate: Track whether high-net-worth investors exiting the residential market drive up transaction volume and valuations in Seoul's small-to-medium commercial building market.
  • Government Supply Announcements: Monitor urban redevelopment approvals and public housing supply plans under the Lee Jae-myung administration aimed at stabilizing the lower-and-middle tier Jeonse rental market before the 2027 selling deadline culminates.
