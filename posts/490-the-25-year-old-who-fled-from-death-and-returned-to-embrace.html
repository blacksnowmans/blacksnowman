The 25-Year-Old Who Fled from Death and Returned to Embrace It

September 30, 2026

Most of us walk through life with our eyes fixed firmly away from the one destination we all share. Death is heavy, unfamiliar, and terrifying. When people think of morticians—professionals who wash, dress, and prepare bodies for burial—they generally feel a mixture of vague dread and quiet awe. Standing at the absolute boundary between life and death requires a constitution most people never have to test.

Recently, a young South Korean mortician shared his story on YouTube, recounting the winter day at age 25 when he witnessed his first death and arterial embalming preparation. What began as a terrifying brush with human mortality became a personal turning point, offering a profound look at why we run from the end of life, and what it takes to finally turn back around and face it.

Winter in the Morgue: Facing the Raw Reality of Mortality

Having graduated university at a young age, the man was invited by an acquaintance working in the funeral industry to observe an actual preparation room procedure. It was winter. As he later confessed, even experienced morticians feel a shiver of fear when handling bodies with severe trauma, but for a complete novice, the reality was starkly raw.

The metal door of the morgue opened with a cold, echoing screech. As he stepped inside, the winter air felt sharp enough to pierce his skin, amplified by the heavy atmosphere of the room. Then, as his acquaintance moved to adjust the deceased's jaw, blood unexpectedly emerged.

For anyone sensitive to visual stimuli, it was enough to trigger instant trauma. Without hesitation, he turned and fled the room. He spent over an hour pacing outside the funeral hall, shivering in the cold, wrestling with whether to simply go home. While a standard enshrouding and washing ritual takes about an hour of quiet, respectful labor, the young man spent that entire hour outside, trapped in a spiral of physical nausea and psychological shock.

Yet life has a peculiar way of pulling us back right when we are ready to slam the door on a terrifying experience. What finally drew him back inside was not the clinical procedure, but the serene face of the deceased after the washing was complete—and the raw grief of the family waiting outside.

Breaking Through the Wall of Grief

The Cowardly Distance We Keep from Death

Modern society treats death as something quarantined, tucked away in hospital rooms and sanitized funeral home systems. We consume it in news reports and movies, but we are terrified to accept it as something that happens to our own families or to us. We assume that if we close our eyes and pretend it isn't there, the terror will vanish.

When the young man finally stepped back into the building, the atmosphere had completely shifted. The enshrouding was finished, and the grieving family had rushed in. They were weeping over the body, shaking their loved one, crying out, "Wake up, how can you just lie there?" and pressing kisses to the cold skin.

Watching from the background, a sharp, deeply uncomfortable realization hit him. He looked at his own behavior from an hour before—running away in terror—and felt a sudden, crushing wave of shame. A piercing thought flashed through his mind: If that were my mother lying there, would I have run away?

It was not a casual or detached question. It was a fundamental confrontation with his own cowardice. In the presence of a family's raw grief, death suddenly stopped being an abstract, clinical event happening to a stranger. It became a personal reality tied to the people he loved most. That intense wave of shame became the first emotion powerful enough to conquer his fear.

A Choice Beyond a Career: Tending to One's Own

Even after experiencing that shock and self-reflection, he did not immediately decide to become a mortician. The weight of the profession and the lingering fear still held him back. But as the day wrapped up, his acquaintance made an offhand comment that changed everything.

"In the past," the acquaintance noted, "these traditional washing and enshrouding rites were handled directly by family members and close relatives within the household. It is only in modern times that we outsource this to professional morticians."

That piece of history planted a deeply personal and urgent question in the young man's mind. He turned to his acquaintance and asked: "If I learn how to do this work, will I be able to tend to my own parents when their time comes?"

When the answer came back yes, his motivation shifted entirely. This was no longer about choosing a career path or seeking social achievement; it was about ensuring that when the most devastating loss of his life occurred, he would not have to surrender his parents to the hands of strangers. He wanted to give them their final farewell with his own hands.

Most people prefer never to think about their parents' mortality. When the day eventually arrives, the universal modern response is to hand over all responsibilities to a commercial funeral service provider, stepping back to simply weep from a distance. There is no shame in that standard grief, but this young man realized something vital: the deepest regret in the face of death is not fear, but the lingering pain of having failed to give a proper, personal farewell.

Why We Must Stop Escaping the Inevitable

The story of a 25-year-old who fled a morgue in panic only to return out of a fierce, protective love for his parents carries a quiet, resonant lesson. We often look at difficult, stigmatized, or avoided lines of work with confusion, wondering why anyone would choose such burdens.

Yet when a profession exists to safeguard the ultimate dignity of a human being's final exit, it transforms completely. Morticians are not merely handling physical remains; they are acting as sacred bridges between the departed and the living left behind.

The young man's journey forces us to ask ourselves an uncomfortable question: Do we have the courage to face the end of our own lives, and the endings of those we love?

Fear is natural. Wanting to close our eyes and run away is a basic human instinct. But it is only by pushing past that terror—by stepping into the room and witnessing the tears of the bereaved—that we grow into people capable of genuine empathy. The young man who bolted from a cold metal door in the dead of winter is now able to steady his hands and soothe the final departure of strangers, proving that love can pierce even the deepest walls of human dread.
