A House Divided: Internal Strife and the Crisis of Leadership within South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 14, 2026

On the morning of November 13, the wood-paneled halls of the National Assembly in Seoul played host to a scene that felt more like a cold war than a routine political meeting. The Supreme Council of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the nation’s ruling party—was scheduled to meet at 10:00 AM. But as the cameras flashed, the visual was jarring: three of the most prominent seats were vacant. Gone were the nameplates for Choi Min-hee, Lee Sung-yoon, and Han Min-su. In their place was a heavy, suffocating silence.

This was not a mere scheduling conflict or a case of the seasonal flu. It was a calculated boycott, a public "no-confidence" vote against the party’s own leadership. For an administration led by President Lee Jae-myung, which has spent the better part of the last year consolidating power following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, this internal fracture represents the most significant threat to its stability to date. What began as a series of verbal gaffes has spiraled into an all-out factional war that threatens to paralyze the government’s legislative agenda and alienate a public already weary of political theater.

The Anatomy of a Boycott and the Erasure of Nameplates

In South Korean politics, the Supreme Council is the inner sanctum of party power. It is where strategy is set and where the party’s "face" is presented to the public. To have three members of this council—all belonging to what is known as the "Pro-Cheong" faction—refuse to attend is a signal of a total breakdown in communication. The Pro-Cheong faction, traditionally seen as a distinct power center within the party, has grown increasingly disillusioned with the "Pro-Myung" leadership, those most closely aligned with the current party chair, Kim Min-seok.

The response from the leadership was telling. Rather than leaving the seats empty as a placeholder for reconciliation, the staff, under orders from the leadership, removed the nameplates of the boycotting members before the meeting even began. This "erasure" was a potent symbol: the leadership was not interested in waiting for the dissenters to return; they were signaling that those who do not fall in line are no longer welcome at the table. To the outside observer, it looked like a purge in real-time.

Kim Min-seok, the DPK Representative and chair who presided over the half-empty room, was visibly cold. During the meeting, other council members loyal to Kim, such as Park Sun-won and Jeon Yong-gi, characterized the boycott as an act of "divisiveness" that harmed party unity. Jeon went as far as to say that "failing to attend a meeting is a dereliction of duty." However, for the three boycotting members, the "duty" they were upholding was a protest against what they describe as Kim’s autocratic management style and his failure to address brewing scandals within his own circle.

The ‘Ethical Investigation’ as a Political Weapon

The immediate trigger for this implosion was a decision by Kim Min-seok to launch an "ethical investigation" into Choi Min-hee, one of the council members who ultimately led the boycott. In the DPK’s hierarchy, an ethical investigation is the first step toward official censure or even expulsion. For a party chair to turn this weapon on a fellow member of the Supreme Council is an extraordinary escalation of hostilities.

Kim’s justification centers on Choi’s recent rhetoric. In various private gatherings and party meetings, Choi reportedly made claims that touched the third rail of DPK politics. Most explosively, she suggested that President Lee Jae-myung had actually been "against the impeachment" of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during the height of the national protests that eventually led to his removal. Given that the DPK’s current mandate is built almost entirely on the successful removal of the Yoon administration, such a claim is seen as political heresy. Kim argued that Choi’s words were "confusing the party base" and undermining the government's official stance.

The Pro-Cheong faction did not take this lying down. They immediately held their own press conference, pivoting the target back toward Kim Min-seok. They argued that if anyone should be under investigation, it is Kim himself. They pointed to lingering allegations regarding Kim’s supposed ties to Shincheonji—a controversial religious group often treated with deep suspicion in Korean society due to its perceived "cult-like" influence and its role in early COVID-19 outbreaks. They also blamed Kim for policy failures, such as the confusion surrounding the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (종부세), which has frustrated middle-class voters. "The biggest perpetrator of actions harmful to the party is Kim Min-seok himself," they declared, turning the "ethical investigation" into a game of political chicken.

The ‘Hyena’ Comment and the Erosion of Public Grace

The internal war is further complicated by Choi Min-hee’s own tendency for inflammatory language, which has alienated the very people the party needs as allies: the press. Choi recently drew national condemnation after referring to a group of journalists as a "pack of hyenas" during a heated exchange at the National Assembly. The Korea Video Journalists Association issued a scathing rebuke, stating they felt "beyond anger, a sense of humiliation" at being compared to scavengers for simply doing their jobs.

This "Hyena Scandal" has now moved beyond the realm of political gossip. Lee Jong-bae, a figure associated with the opposition People Power Party, filed a formal petition with the National Human Rights Commission of Korea, alleging that Choi’s comments violated the dignity and personality rights of the reporters. For the DPK, this is a nightmare scenario. While the party tries to position itself as the defender of democracy and human rights, one of its senior leaders is being investigated for dehumanizing the press.

Furthermore, the controversy surrounding her claims about President Lee Jae-myung’s stance on Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment has created a "truth contest" that the party cannot win. If Choi is telling the truth, it suggests a level of political duplicity within the Blue House that would shock the party’s progressive base. If she is lying, it suggests that a member of the party’s top leadership is a loose cannon willing to invent history to score internal points. Either way, the DPK’s "united front" against the remnants of the Yoon era is looking increasingly fractured.

"We are witnessing a political suicide pact," remarked one veteran political analyst in Seoul. "When a ruling party spends more time investigating its own leaders than it does drafting legislation for a struggling economy, it is inviting the opposition to walk through the front door."

A Warning from the Polls: The Cost of Civil War

The most concrete evidence that this internal strife is hurting the DPK can be found in the latest polling data. According to a Korea Gallup survey released on November 13, the DPK’s favorability rating has taken a significant hit. Just a few months ago, the party enjoyed a comfortable 50% approval rating. That number has now plummeted to 38%. Meanwhile, the People Power Party, the main conservative opposition, has seen its numbers climb from the teens into the mid-20s.

The math is simple: for every day the DPK spends arguing over nameplates and ethical investigations, they lose the trust of the "swing" voters—the moderate middle class who care more about the price of groceries and housing than they do about factional loyalty. The drop from 50% to 38% represents millions of voters who are signaling that they are tired of the drama. They supported the DPK to bring stability after the chaos of the Yoon administration, not to witness a new version of the same dysfunction.

President Lee Jae-myung finds himself in a difficult position. While he remains the undisputed gravitational center of the party, his silence on the Kim-Choi feud is starting to be interpreted as a lack of control. If he intervenes to save Choi, he risks alienating his loyal lieutenant Kim Min-seok. If he allows Kim to purge Choi, he risks a formal split with the Pro-Cheong faction, which could lead to a "party within a party" that blocks his legislative agenda from the inside.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether the DPK can recover or if it is headed for a formal schism. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • The Results of the Ethical Investigation: If the party’s ethics committee moves forward with a heavy-handed punishment for Choi Min-hee, expect the Pro-Cheong faction to escalate their boycotts and perhaps even move toward a formal split.
  • The 'Shincheonji' Defense: Watch how Kim Min-seok handles the allegations regarding the religious group. If the opposition or internal rivals produce any documented evidence of these ties, his position as party chair may become untenable.
  • Legislative Deadlock: With the Supreme Council in disarray, the DPK’s ability to pass critical budget bills or reform measures will be tested. Any failure to pass the upcoming budget will be blamed squarely on this internal infighting.
  • President Lee’s Intervention: Look for a "grand compromise" orchestrated by the Blue House. If the President does not step in to mediate personally, it is a sign that the rift is deeper than previously thought.

The DPK is currently a house divided against itself. While Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol remains behind bars, the ghost of his chaotic tenure seems to have migrated into the very party that removed him. For the people of South Korea, the hope for a "new era" of politics is quickly being replaced by the familiar sight of factional warfare, leaving many to wonder if the nameplates on the table matter at all if no one is sitting in the chairs to do the people’s work.
