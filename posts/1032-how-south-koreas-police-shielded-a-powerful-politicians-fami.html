How South Korea's Police Shielded a Powerful Politician's Family: The Anatomy of a Flawed Investigation

October 12, 2026

When you sit across a courtroom or watch an investigation unfold from the outside, the true measure of a society's fairness isn't found in its constitution. It is found in the quiet, unglamorous choices made by investigators: who gets brought in for questioning under bright lights, and who is allowed to answer questions from the comfort of their living room.

Recently, during a parliamentary audit by the National Assembly's Public Administration and Security Committee, lawmakers turned their attention to a case that lays bare these uneven scales of justice in South Korea. It is the investigation into allegations that the daughter of Han Dong-hoon—the high-powered former Minister of Justice and a towering figure in the conservative establishment—fabricated academic credentials to pad her resume.

What followed was not an aggressive pursuit of the truth, but an exercise in institutional foot-dragging. For one year and eight months, the police went through the motions of an inquiry while ensuring that the core suspects were treated with kid gloves. As foreign observers look at South Korea's rapidly evolving political landscape, this case serves as a crucial window into how entrenched power structures continue to shape law enforcement, even as the nation attempts to reform its judicial system.

An Investigation Designed to Go Nowhere

In any standard criminal inquiry, the foundational step is a rigorous interrogation of the accused. Yet from its inception in May 2002 to its dismissal in December 2023, the investigation into Han's family moved at a glacial, unearned pace over the course of 20 months.

The treatment of the primary suspect, Jin Eun-jung (Han's spouse), tells the entire story. She was subjected to a single, passive written questionnaire. Law enforcement did not seek a single search warrant against her. Meanwhile, the civic activists who filed the original complaint—Ahn Jin-guk, head of the Institute for People's Livelihood Economy, and Kim Han-me, head of Citizens' Coalition for Eradicating Judicial Injustice—were hauled in repeatedly for intense, face-to-face grilling.

Lawmakers during the parliamentary audit pointed out the obvious absurdity. In what normal jurisdiction does an investigative agency repeatedly interrogate the whistleblowers while letting the targets of the investigation reply via paperwork?

The police defended their record by pointing to 14 formal cooperation requests sent to various bodies. But a closer look reveals these actions to be bureaucratic theater. Of those 14 requests, 11 were merely written inquiries sent to domestic institutions and experts. Even though these domestic bodies were fully subject to compulsory investigation, police bypassed search warrants entirely, opting to trade paper memos instead of seizing evidence. When three overseas academic journals failed to respond to written inquiries, the police simply dropped the matter, letting international judicial cooperation requests stall out after prosecutors sent them back.

Double Standards and Administrative Fabrication

To understand just how lopsided this handling was, one only needs to place it side-by-side with other high-profile investigations of the same era. Consider the inquiry into Kim Hye-kyung, wife of opposition leader Lee Jae-myung, regarding alleged misuse of corporate credit cards. That investigation saw 131 search warrants executed over roughly six months. In the Han family case, the warrant count over 20 months was absolute zero.

This stark disparity reflects an institutional reflex: protecting those favored by the conservative establishment that held power during the tenure of Yoon Seok-yeol, the disgraced former president now impeached and imprisoned. Under Yoon, investigators knew precisely who was untouchable.

The rot went deeper than mere foot-dragging. Woo Jong-soo, the head of the National Office of Investigation, grandly assured reporters in June 2024 that his team had conducted numerous witness interviews. A check of the actual records revealed the number of witness interviews was zero. The nation’s top investigative official had lied directly to the press and the legislature. When confronted, the acting commissioner general of the Korean National Police Agency could only offer evasive answers.

The Road Ahead for South Korea's Justice System

This scandal breaks at a delicate moment for South Korea's law enforcement apparatus. As the country prepares for the launch of the Major Crime Investigation Office, critics are pointing out that basic infrastructure—such as proper video-recording interrogation rooms, holding cells, and transport vehicles—remains woefully unprepared.

More importantly, the legacy of political favoritism under past administrations has left public trust in tatters. Acting police leadership has floated the possibility of reopening the case and launching internal disciplinary reviews, but these promises have been met with public skepticism. True reform requires more than reviewing past mistakes; it requires holding accountable the officials who weaponized administrative convenience to shield political elites.

For foreign observers of Korean politics, the lesson is clear. South Korea possesses a vibrant democracy and a mobilized public that refuses to let old abuses slip quietly into the dark. But transitioning from an era where the powerful operated above the law to a genuinely equitable judicial system will require constant vigilance. If the police cannot account for why a powerful minister's family received preferential treatment, the public will rightly question whether the rule of law applies to everyone equally—or only to those without the right connections.
