The AI Hardware Monopoly Fractures: How Nvidia’s Turmoil and Anthropic’s $90 Billion IPO Are Reshaping South Korea’s Tech Nexus

November 16, 2026

Imagine sitting on a trading floor in Yeouido, Seoul’s bustling financial district, during the second week of November. For two years, the global artificial intelligence boom has run on a simple formula: Big Tech buys Nvidia graphics processing units (GPUs) at almost any price, South Korea’s memory semiconductor titans supply the indispensable high-speed chips, and Wall Street bids every entity in the chain to record highs. But that playbook is suddenly unraveling.

In a single week, global markets are confronting a dual shockwave. On one side stands Nvidia, reeling from the sudden resignation of its long-serving chief executive Jensen Huang amidst workplace abuse allegations and tightening U.S. export controls. On the other side stands Anthropic, the generative AI power player preparing for a massive debut on the Nasdaq exchange. As capital rushes toward software while hardware monopolies splinter, the financial ripple effects are hitting South Korea’s tech-heavy benchmark KOSPI stock index and its flagship semiconductor exporters with extraordinary force.

Leadership Vacuum and Export Blockades: Nvidia’s Dual Crises

The exit of Jensen Huang—who led Nvidia for over three decades as its undisputed visionary—has evolved into a bitter corporate crisis. Reports emerging from inside the chipmaker paint a picture of severe internal strife, with former executives alleging rampant verbal abuse and toxic workplace dynamics, including accusations that Huang told managers he "wished they were dead." The fallout has even reached local culture: a famous neighborhood restaurant known for hosting Huang quietly removed a commemorative plaque dedicated to him to avoid public backlash.

While over 140,000 investors and employees signed an online petition urging Huang’s return—backed by public calls from figures like Elon Musk—the company must face Wall Street’s ultimate judge without its founder. Nvidia’s quarterly earnings report on November 18 serves as a high-stakes litmus test. Beyond historical revenues, analysts are desperate to know whether a leaderless executive team can preserve key customer delivery schedules or prevent major cloud providers from canceling orders.

Compounding this leadership crisis is an aggressive regulatory squeeze from Washington. U.S. officials have zeroed in on Nvidia’s ambitious $34 billion data center expansion plan across Southeast Asia, suspecting that local facilities were being designed to let Chinese firms circumvent U.S. export bans on advanced AI hardware. In mainland China, a separate 4 trillion won (around $3 billion) data center initiative premised on Nvidia hardware has ground to a halt.

U.S. lawmakers are moving to pass bipartisan legislation that would explicitly prohibit Chinese companies from renting advanced computing power via third-country cloud servers. This regulatory encirclement directly threatens Nvidia’s circular financing ecosystem—a 213 trillion won (approximately $160 billion) web of strategic venture investments in AI startups that turned around and bought Nvidia GPUs. With leadership gone and supply corridors closing, the liquidity engine driving Nvidia’s monopoly is showing its first major fractures.

The $90 Billion Debut Sucking Liquidity Out of Global Markets

As hardware networks strain, primary capital markets are pivoting aggressively toward generative AI software. Anthropic’s scheduled Nasdaq listing on November 19 has triggered a frenzy among institutional investors. Retail subscription orders have surpassed available allocations by more than six times, pushing the artificial intelligence firm’s expected valuation to approximately 120 trillion won (roughly $88 billion to $90 billion). Derivatives markets indicate a 21% chance that Anthropic’s market capitalization could surge to between $1.5 trillion and $1.75 trillion on its first day of trading.

Yet behind this record demand lies a stark financial reality: Anthropic reported a staggering net loss of $104 billion over the past nine months, driven by the exorbitant infrastructure costs of training frontier large language models (LLMs). Investors are effectively betting that software monetization will eventually outpace raw training expenses, treating Anthropic as an alternative anchor asset while hardware builders face structural governance risks.

Key Market Indicator: Anthropic’s SEC filings explicitly bar institutional funds from China and Hong Kong from participating in the IPO, reflecting strict U.S. national security directives aimed at keeping Chinese capital out of core American AI architecture.

The sheer scale of Anthropic’s public offering is creating a severe liquidity vacuum across global asset classes. An estimated $64.4 billion in institutional capital was pulled from other risk assets to finance orders for the listing. This massive capital reallocation contributed to a sharp sell-off in digital assets, pulling Bitcoin down 15.6% over an 11-day stretch to the $62,000 range, while the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield topped 5%. Capital flows are concentrating into singular tech offerings at the expense of broader market liquidity.

Cracks in CUDA and the Realignment of South Korea’s Semiconductor Giants

For South Korea, the central question is how this platform shift affects its export-driven economy. For years, Nvidia relied on its proprietary CUDA software architecture to lock developers into its hardware. However, recent technical disclosures revealed that Anthropic successfully ported its AI models to run on chips produced by AMD—Nvidia's chief rival—in just three days. Furthermore, industry estimates suggest that deployment costs using AMD infrastructure drop from $40 billion per gigawatt of power capacity under Nvidia to around $20 billion per gigawatt under AMD.

This breakdown in CUDA’s exclusivity is forcing South Korea’s corporate heavyweights to recalculate their strategies:

  • SK Hynix: The world’s primary supplier of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—the customized, ultra-fast memory chips essential for AI training—has maintained a tight alliance with Nvidia under SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won. A shift away from Nvidia’s absolute dominance introduces unprecedented volatility into SK Hynix’s order visibility.
  • SK Telecom: South Korea’s top wireless carrier is reaping immediate rewards from a visionary early investment in Anthropic, anticipating substantial paper gains and expanded cloud integration partnerships.
  • Samsung Electronics & Samsung SDS: South Korea’s flagship tech giant, Samsung Electronics, has struggled to gain exclusive HBM allocations with Nvidia. However, a broader transition toward multi-cloud architectures and AMD-backed infrastructure opens new commercial avenues for Samsung to supply its memory chips and server solutions.

While South Korean equities on the KOSPI index struggle to regain momentum amidst global tech shifts, corporate leaders must navigate tighter U.S. manufacturing rules and warnings of a potential macro digestion phase in global AI capital expenditures around 2028.

What to Watch Next

As this historic week unfolds, international investors monitoring the South Korean market and broader global tech supply chains should track four critical indicators:

  • Nvidia’s Earnings Forward Guidance (Nov 18): Watch whether interim management adjusts shipment timelines for next-generation AI accelerators or reports order cancellations from major U.S. hyper-scalers.
  • Anthropic’s First-Day Trading Performance (Nov 19): Observe whether secondary market demand can sustain a $90 billion valuation in the face of a $104 billion nine-month net loss.
  • U.S. Congressional Actions on Cloud Leasing: Monitor the progress of U.S. legislation targeting remote compute access, which could further restrict revenue streams for global data center operators.
  • Korean HBM Order Diversification: Pay close attention to contract announcements from SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics indicating supply reallocations toward AMD and alternative AI chip manufacturers.
