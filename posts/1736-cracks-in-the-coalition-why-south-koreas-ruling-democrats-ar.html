Cracks in the Coalition: Why South Korea’s Ruling Democrats Are Losing Their Grip

October 30, 2026

When President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea took power following the historic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the liberal establishment appeared to command an unassailable mandate. Having weathered an extraordinary constitutional crisis that included a botched martial law attempt by the previous administration, South Korea's governing progressives held a commanding legislative majority and the presidency. Yet, barely a year and a half into President Lee’s term, the ground beneath the ruling party is shaking.

Across every major public opinion index in Seoul, the numbers are flashing amber. What initially looked like normal mid-term friction has hardened into a structural crisis: core progressive supporters and independent moderates are abandoning the ruling Democratic Party simultaneously. The gap with the conservative opposition has narrowed to within the margin of error, and the President has crossed into dangerous political territory where his disapproval firmly eclipses his approval. Inside the broader liberal camp, frustration is boiling over into open rebellion, threatening to tear apart the coalition that brought Lee to the Blue House.

The Arithmetic of a "Dead Cross" and the Ghost of 2021

The statistical warning signs are clear across both automated surveys and direct telephone interviews. According to a Gallup Korea poll conducted nationwide between October 27 and 29, 2026, approval for the ruling Democratic Party plunged from 44 percent in late July to 35 percent by late October. Over the same span, the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, rebounded from 23 percent to 30 percent. A comfortable double-digit lead has vanished into a five-point gap, well within the poll's plus-or-minus 3.1 percentage point margin of error.

More alarming for the administration is the job performance rating of President Lee himself. Gallup’s late-October survey found his positive approval sinking to 37 percent, while negative evaluations surged to 49 percent. In South Korean political parlance, this 12-point negative gap represents a decisive "dead cross"—a threshold where the public’s default stance shifts from giving a leader the benefit of the doubt to viewing policy decisions with active skepticism. It is a staggering fall for a president whose support hovered near 70 percent at the start of his term. A Realmeter survey published on August 31 similarly captured the president's approval dropping into the 30s for the first time, coming in at 38.9 percent. The hemorrhage is concentrated precisely where it hurts most: self-described progressives, down 21 percentage points, alongside voters in their 40s and 50s who have long served as the backbone of the party.

Compounding the problem is an expanding bloc of non-aligned voters. Gallup’s party survey revealed that 28 percent of respondents now identify as independent or uncommitted—nearly equaling the share of either major party. In a separate Gallup study conducted from October 13 to 15 regarding the upcoming 23rd general election, 47 percent of voters stated that an opposition majority should be elected to keep the administration in check, while only 34 percent believed ruling party candidates should win to support the government.

To seasoned strategists in Seoul, this atmosphere feels eerily reminiscent of the run-up to the April 2021 municipal by-elections. At that time, a series of missteps and insular politics led to a catastrophic collapse in public trust for the Democrats. In the 2021 Seoul mayoral race, conservative candidate Oh Se-hoon routed the Democratic nominee by more than 18 percentage points, sweeping 57.5 percent of the vote. Behind the scenes, the party's actual standing had deteriorated long before election day, obscured only by the party's refusal to heed warnings from swing voters.

What is at stake today is the ruling party’s legislative super-majority. In the South Korean National Assembly, holding 180 seats is the golden threshold of parliamentary power. While 200 votes—a two-thirds majority—are required to amend the constitution or impeach a president, 180 seats allow a party to override the National Assembly Advancement Act. It enables the majority to unilaterally designate bills as "fast-track" priority items and forcefully terminate an opposition filibuster within 24 hours. A simple majority of 150 seats leaves an administration vulnerable to endless procedural gridlock. If the Democratic Party drops below 180 seats in the next election, the engine room of Lee’s policy agenda will grind to a halt.

An Insular Inner Circle and the Warning of "Overturning the Boat"

The root of this rapid decline is not an external economic shock, but rather a pattern of insular governance that has alienated natural allies. Following the appointment of Kim Min-seok as party chair, the Democratic Party’s internal communication grew increasingly closed. Key party broadcast and public relations responsibilities were handed to Representative Kim Nam-jun, a prominent figure from the so-called "Seongnam faction"—the close circle of loyalists who worked alongside Lee Jae-myung during his early days as mayor of Seongnam.

Rather than engaging in constructive policy debate, party-affiliated media channels began elevating vocal loyalist commentators who openly disparaged other progressive figures and internal dissenters. For more than a year, internal critics were sidelined, and alternative voices within the liberal movement were treated as hostile elements. The result has been a self-inflicted narrowing of the governing coalition.

This dynamic has invited biting critiques from within the intellectual left. On October 28, during an appearance on the progressive political commentary channel Park Si-young TV, prominent author and former liberal minister Rhyu Si-min issued a blistering assessment of the administration’s trajectory. While dismissing weekly poll fluctuations of one or two points as statistical noise, Rhyu characterized the multi-month slide as a systemic collapse of the ruling alliance.

Rhyu warned that President Lee had spent the past year choosing isolation, invoking the ancient Chinese adage gun-ju-min-su (君舟民水):

"The people are the water, and the ruler is the boat. The water can keep the boat afloat, but it can also overturn it and swallow it whole."

Rhyu pointedly cautioned that when a leader's approval drops below its foundational floor, extreme outcomes—such as the truncated terms suffered by Park Geun-hye or Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—cease to be unthinkable. He specifically singled out the president’s dismissive remarks on prosecutorial reform following the local elections, where Lee casually remarked that because authorities had been granted power, they would bear the responsibility. That, Rhyu observed, was an attitude "befitting a king, not an accountable democratic leader."

This growing dissatisfaction illustrates the principle of voter mobility. When political parties believe their supporters have nowhere else to go, they stop reforming. But South Korean voters have historically shown high mobility. In the 2021 presidential election, Lee squeaked through in an extraordinarily tight contest, securing 49.42 percent against the combined conservative vote of 49.49 percent. In an electorate balanced on a knife-edge, alienating even a 5 or 10 percent slice of swing progressives is political suicide.

The Rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party and a Fractured Left

As the Democratic Party drifted into insularity, the traditional progressive base began looking for alternatives. They found one in the Rebuilding Korea Party, the splinter progressive party originally launched by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk.

According to survey data compiled by Media Tomato, support for the Rebuilding Korea Party climbed steadily from 3.9 percent in early August to 6.2 percent in late August, 7.4 percent in September, and reached 9.0 percent by late October. During that exact timeframe, the Democratic Party’s approval dropped from 44.7 percent to 36.2 percent—an 8.5 percentage point drop that mirrored the Innovation Party's gains almost point-for-point. Pollsters Realmeter and EveryResearch confirmed the trend, showing the Rebuilding Korea Party capturing double-digit support among self-identified progressives (16.3 percent) and voters in their 40s (12.6 percent) and 50s (13.3 percent).

Under its newly elected leader, Shin Jang-sik, the Rebuilding Korea Party has abandoned its previous cooperative posture to pursue an assertive, independent course. During the 2024 general election, the party operated under the strategic pact of "district votes for the Democrats, proportional representation votes for the Innovation Party." Today, Shin portrays his party as the only genuine reform force, openly accusing the governing Democrats of growing conservative and dragging their feet on key pledges.

Shin has pressed the administration aggressively to completely strip state prosecutors of their supplementary investigative authority and has taken aim at the toxic online fandom culture within the ruling party. By offering a clearer, unapologetic progressive platform, the Rebuilding Korea Party has transformed from an auxiliary partner into a formidable rival for progressive votes.

This rivalry carries disastrous electoral implications for the Democratic Party in the capital region. In the densely populated Seoul metropolitan area, which accounts for roughly 120 legislative seats, parliamentary races are frequently decided by margins of 1 to 2 percentage points. The dangers of a split vote were already demonstrated in recent by-elections: while the Innovation Party managed to elect its candidate Jung Cheol-won as head of Damyang County, an uncoordinated multi-candidate race in Gyeonggi Province’s Pyeongtaek-B district divided the liberal vote and handed an unearned victory to the conservative opposition.

Democratic Party Representative Kim Young-hwan voiced the private dread felt by many lawmakers when he warned on the political broadcast 2-Minute News that if the Rebuilding Korea Party fields independent candidates in the capital with 10 percent polling support, the ruling party faces total wipeout across Seoul and Gyeonggi Province.

What to Watch Next

Voters who supported Lee Jae-myung did so largely because they needed a relentless political fighter capable of confronting the abuses and prosecutorial overreach of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s time in office. Yet delivering a change of government was a collective achievement won by civil society, parliamentary resistance, and allied political factions—not the exclusive trophy of a single faction. Loyalty among progressive voters is increasingly conditional, transactional, and performance-based.

Over the coming months, three specific milestones will determine whether the ruling Democratic Party can arrest its slide or face a crippling electoral reckoning:

  • The April 2027 By-Elections: Races in key battlegrounds, including the Gangneung parliamentary seat and a potential special election for the Seoul mayoralty, will test whether the Democrats and the Rebuilding Korea Party can negotiate unified candidacies or split the progressive vote.
  • Prosecution and Judicial Reform Legislation: Watch how the administration responds to Shin Jang-sik’s demand for a complete abolition of prosecutorial supplementary investigative powers. Continued compromise or delay from the Blue House will accelerate the flight of core progressives to the Innovation Party.
  • Inner-Circle Personnel Reshuffles: To restore credibility with moderates and disillusioned allies, President Lee will face mounting pressure to rein in the "Seongnam faction" media dominance, sideline polarizing loyalists, and open genuine channels of dialogue across the broader center-left coalition.
