The Mirage of Stability: Why Korea’s Real Estate Market is Facing an Irreversible Crisis

October 11, 2026

The South Korean real estate market is currently a landscape of lamentation. Prospective buyers have vanished, and homeowners are vocal about their deepening financial distress. It is a moment of profound confusion, but according to Lee Hyun-chul, head of the Apartment Cycle Research Institute, this chaos is not a random catastrophe. Instead, it is the inevitable consequence of systemic policy failures and a fundamental misunderstanding of market mechanics.

For years, the public has been fed a steady diet of conventional wisdom: "A surge in new apartment move-ins will stabilize prices," or "Strict regulations will kill demand." Lee argues that these are little more than parrot-like refrains that ignore the underlying reality. To understand why the market is in such a state, we must strip away the myths and confront the uncomfortable truths about how housing supply and demand actually function in South Korea.

The False Logic of "New Supply"

The most persistent myth in the Korean real estate discourse is the definition of "supply." Media outlets and pundits constantly warn that an influx of move-in ready apartments will drive down prices. Lee points out a critical flaw in this logic: apartments nearing completion are not "new supply" in any real economic sense.

South Korea operates on a unique "pre-sale" system, where buyers sign contracts and make payments while the building is still in the planning or construction phase. By the time the building is ready for move-in, the ownership has long been established. These units are not inventory that developers are trying to sell to the market; they are homes being occupied by their rightful owners. Conflating these completions with new market supply is a foundational error that has misled both the public and the government’s policy response.

True supply consists of only two things: "conversion supply," where existing homeowners put their homes on the market, and "new supply," which emerges through fresh subscription offerings. When the government confuses completed pre-sales with new supply, their interventions are bound to miss the mark.

The "Lock-in" Effect of Excessive Taxation

The government’s attempts to cool the market through aggressive taxation have also backfired spectacularly. Under the previous administration of the now-imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol, the government implemented brutal capital gains tax increases on multi-home owners. At its peak, the effective tax rate reached a staggering 82.5%. The logic was simple: make it too expensive to own multiple homes, and owners will be forced to sell, flooding the market with supply and lowering prices.

The reality, however, was a "lock-in" effect. Instead of selling, homeowners simply held onto their properties. Because the South Korean public has experienced decades of political shifts—from the Park Chung-hee era to the present day—they have learned that regulations are often temporary. Investors decided to "wait out" the current administration, betting that policies would eventually be relaxed. As a result, supply dried up, prices held firm, and the desperate wait for affordability only intensified the public’s anxiety.

This is the law of "irreversibility" in policy. Once a government loses the trust of the market and misreads the psychology of its citizens, it cannot simply "fix" the market with a new decree. The current government, led by President Lee Jae-myung, faces the unenviable task of trying to restore stability in a market that has already learned to adapt to—and survive—punitive government measures.

The Illusion of "Owner-Occupancy"

Another dangerous misconception involves the term "owner-occupancy" (sil-geoju). Many Koreans justify their real estate investments by claiming they are buying solely for the purpose of living in the home, not for speculation. While this may be true in name, it often masks a deeper desire for capital gains and social status.

Lee argues that when people hide behind the "owner-occupancy" shield, they fail to prepare for a downturn. They tell themselves they are safe because they "need a place to live," but when property values plummet and mortgage interest rates rise, the emotional comfort of that label provides no protection against bankruptcy. In the current economic climate, buying property—even for personal use—is a high-stakes entry into a volatile capitalistic market. Those who fail to acknowledge the speculative element of their decision-making process are the most vulnerable when the cycle turns.

What to Watch Next

The Korean real estate market is currently correcting, a process accelerated by the high-interest-rate environment, but one that was set in motion by years of policy mismanagement. As we look ahead, here is what to watch:

  • The Limits of Policy: Watch for whether the current administration moves beyond "band-aid" solutions. Any major shift in regulations will likely be met with skepticism by a public that has seen too many U-turns in policy over the last decade.
  • Liquidity and Purchasing Power: Real estate is only as valuable as the available purchasing power. Keep a close eye on household debt levels and loan regulations; if the government continues to restrict credit, the "buying power" of the average person will continue to shrink, further cooling the market.
  • The "FOMO" Correction: The era of blind, speculative buying—driven by the fear of missing out—has hit a hard ceiling. The market is now shifting toward a focus on fundamental value and asset protection.

Ultimately, the current state of the market is a harsh teacher. The era of assuming that housing prices will always go up is over. Those who survive this period will be the ones who stop looking to the government for magic solutions and start calculating their personal financial resilience with cold, hard numbers. The "pi-bada" (sea of blood) in the market is not a conspiracy; it is a mathematical reality for those who refused to look at the math before it was too late.
