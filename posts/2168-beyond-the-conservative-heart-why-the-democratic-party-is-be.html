Beyond the Conservative Heart: Why the Democratic Party is Betting Big on Gumi’s Industrial Future

November 10, 2026

Gumi, a city in the northern reaches of Gyeongsangbuk-do province, is often described as the "heart of Korean conservatism." For decades, it has served as a symbolic fortress for the right wing, largely due to its history as the birthplace of former President Park Chung-hee, the architect of Korea's rapid industrialization. To see the leadership of the Democratic Party (DP)—South Korea’s ruling liberal party—holding a high-level meeting here is a scene that would have been unthinkable just a few years ago. Yet, on a recent visit, the air was filled not with the familiar echoes of ideological warfare, but with the hum of talk regarding artificial intelligence, industrial robotics, and multi-billion dollar safety budgets.

The visit by Kim Min-seok, a prominent leader within the Democratic Party, signals a profound shift in the political geography of the peninsula. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the DP is no longer content to concede the Daegu-Gyeongbuk (TK) region to the People Power Party (PPP), the country’s main conservative opposition. Instead, the ruling party is attempting a "frontal breakthrough," promising to revitalize a region that many locals feel was left to stagnate during the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. By focusing on tangible economic benefits—specifically the "AI Transformation" of Gumi’s aging industrial complex—the DP is betting that the promise of a high-tech future can finally override the pull of historical regionalism.

Transforming the "Rust Belt" into an AI Hub

Gumi was once the engine of the "Miracle on the Han River." Its sprawling industrial complexes were the birthplace of the nation's electronics and textile industries. However, in recent years, the city has begun to resemble a Korean version of the American Rust Belt. As manufacturing moved overseas or consolidated in the Seoul metropolitan area, Gumi’s factories grew silent, and its youth began to migrate toward the capital in search of modern jobs. During the presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, critics argue that this centralization in Seoul only accelerated, leaving regional hubs like Gumi on the brink of collapse.

The DP’s strategy for Gumi is centered on what they call AX, or "AI Transformation." The plan is to inject artificial intelligence and robotics into the existing manufacturing infrastructure, turning traditional "smokestack" factories into smart, automated facilities. During his visit, Kim Min-seok framed this not as a mere local project, but as a constitutional necessity for "balanced national development." The goal is to make Gumi the national center for the robotics industry, ensuring that the city’s manufacturing heritage becomes its competitive edge rather than a relic of the past.

For the residents of Gumi, this is more than just political rhetoric. The promise of high-paying jobs in the tech sector is a direct response to the brain drain that has hollowed out the region. By pledging legislative support and dedicated budget lines for these industries, the DP is attempting to prove that they are the party of "utility" and "competence." They are moving the conversation away from the ideological ghosts of the 1970s and toward the practical realities of the 2030s.

Closing the Safety Gap with a $1.4 Billion Pledge

Beyond high-tech industry, the DP leadership brought another significant carrot to the table: a massive expansion of fire department funding. The party is currently reviewing a plan to provide approximately 2 trillion won (about $1.44 billion) in national support to modernize fire services across the country, with a particular focus on regions like Gyeongsangbuk-do.

In South Korea, fire services have historically been a mixed responsibility between the central and local governments. This has led to a "safety gap," where wealthy cities like Seoul enjoy the latest equipment and ample staffing, while rural provinces struggle with aging fire trucks and undermanned stations. Gyeongsangbuk-do, with its vast mountainous terrain and susceptibility to large-scale wildfires, has been particularly vulnerable. The DP’s proposal to shift the financial burden of fire services to the national treasury is a strategic move designed to provide an immediate "efficacy" to voters who feel neglected by the state.

"Safety is the most basic form of welfare and the primary reason for the state's existence," Kim Min-seok emphasized during the meeting.

By framing safety as a national responsibility, the DP is subtly critiquing the perceived failures of the previous administration. Under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, many regional leaders felt that public safety infrastructure was sidelined in favor of other priorities. By contrast, the Lee Jae-myung government is positioning itself as a "safety-first" administration, using the budget to stitch together the disparities between the capital and the provinces. For a conservative voter in Gumi, the promise of a modern, well-funded fire department is a powerful argument for looking beyond traditional party lines.

The Road to 100,000: Dismantling Regionalism

The DP's ultimate goal in the TK region is reflected in a specific, ambitious number: 100,000 registered party members. In a province where the liberal party has historically struggled to even field candidates in some districts, this target is a declaration of war against the status quo. The DP’s provincial leadership, led by figures like Gyeongbuk Provincial Chair Oh Jung-gi, believes that the tide is turning. They argue that the "TK Passing"—the feeling that the conservative party takes their votes for granted while the liberal party ignores them—has created a vacuum that the DP can fill.

This organizational push is aimed squarely at the 2028 general elections. The DP is no longer viewing Gyeongsangbuk-do as a "lost cause" but as a "frontier." By building a robust grassroots organization of 100,000 members, the party hopes to create a permanent infrastructure for liberal politics in the heart of the conservative south. Kim Min-seok’s praise for the local members, calling them the "vision of the Democratic Party," serves to validate those who have often felt like political pariahs in their own hometowns.

This strategy also benefits from a broader national context. With Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol now out of office and facing legal proceedings, the conservative opposition is in a state of flux. The DP is capitalizing on this moment of weakness to offer a stable, development-oriented alternative. The message to the voters of Gumi is simple: "The old guard failed you, and we are here to build your future." If the DP can successfully increase its membership in this region, it will fundamentally alter the math of South Korean presidential and general elections for a generation.

What to Watch Next

While the DP’s visit to Gumi was a successful piece of political theater, the real test will be in the follow-through. Here are the key developments to watch in the coming months:

  • Budget Deliberations: Watch how the proposed 2 trillion won ($1.44 billion) for fire services fares in the National Assembly. If the DP can push this through, it will be a major "win" they can point to during regional campaigning.
  • Legislation for the AI-Robot Industry: Look for the introduction of "Special Zone" status or tax incentive bills specifically targeting the Gumi industrial complex. The speed at which these bills move will indicate the government's true level of commitment.
  • The 100,000 Member Drive: Monitor the growth of the DP’s membership in Gyeongsangbuk-do. If the party approaches this milestone, it will force the conservative People Power Party to radically rethink its own regional strategy to avoid losing its "safe" seats.
  • Local Reaction: Pay attention to Gumi's local media and business associations. If the "AX strategy" gains traction among local business owners—who are traditionally conservative—it could signal a permanent shift in the city's political alignment.

The Democratic Party is attempting one of the most difficult maneuvers in Korean politics: convincing the "heart of conservatism" that its future lies with the liberals. By replacing slogans with smart factories and regional neglect with national budgets, they are testing whether economic pragmatism can finally dismantle the walls of regionalism that have defined the nation for half a century.
