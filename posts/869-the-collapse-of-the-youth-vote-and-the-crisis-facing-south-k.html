The Collapse of the Youth Vote and the Crisis Facing South Korea's Ruling Party

September 21, 2026

For decades, South Korea's progressive camp built a towering reputation on the promise of fairness, reform, and moral clarity. Today, watching the country's political landscape feels like watching that tower crumble from the inside. When the ruling party's approval ratings begin to crater and key demographics abandon ship en masse, it is no longer a minor weekly fluctuation. It is a blinking red warning light. With the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the center-left party currently leading the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—suddenly losing its lead to the conservative opposition, the leadership's refusal to face reality has turned a political downturn into a full-scale crisis.

Poll Numbers Slide as the Crucial Youth Vote Evaporates

Recent data from major pollsters paints a grim picture for the administration. Surveys by research firms like Realmeter and New Daily place President Lee's approval ratings hovering in the low-to-mid 30s, with disapproval ratings shooting past 60%. More alarmingly, the political momentum has shifted: the conservative People Power Party has pulled ahead of the Democratic Party, capturing roughly 42% of support compared to the ruling party's 36% in recent weekly tracking.

The epicenter of this collapse is the 20-something demographic. Among voters in their twenties, support for the ruling party plunged by double digits in a single week, hitting an unprecedented low of around 18%. Young voters, who were once a vital energy source for the progressive movement, are not merely changing their minds; they are staging a furious retreat driven by accumulated disappointment. When the party leadership stumbles through legislative hearings, dodges controversial legal reforms until public outrage boils over, and treats core principles as negotiable, young voters notice. The leadership's inner circle continues to brush off these numbers as temporary noise, risking a complete alienation of the generation that once powered their electoral victories.

The Specific Backlash from Young Women and Leadership's Blind Spot

Perhaps the most startling aspect of the current downward spiral is the absolute hemorrhage of support among women in their twenties. While male voters in the same age bracket showed only modest shifts, approval among young women crashed by nearly 20 percentage points in a single survey period, leaving it in the high teens with an 81% negative rating.

The immediate catalyst for this female voter revolt was a series of tone-deaf personnel appointments. Nominations meant to appeal to reform-minded voters instead sparked intense criticism over alleged favoritism and legal baggage, including figures tied to controversial lobbying allegations and repeated proportional representation perks. When questioned about the backlash, nominees offered rigid legalistic defenses rather than political accountability, alienating a demographic that places a high premium on systemic fairness.

Compounding the anger was the deafening silence of the party's own lawmakers. When opposition politicians hurled deeply inappropriate, gendered remarks during parliamentary hearings, the ruling party's female representatives largely stayed quiet. For a voting bloc that expected the progressive camp to act as a shield against indignity, watching the party look the other way while its supporters were mocked was the final straw. Young voters are realizing that the party leadership expects their votes as an unconditional right rather than a responsibility to be earned.

Controversial Appointments and the Deepening Intra-Party Rift

Rather than seeking to rebuild trust, the party leadership has doubled down on internal factionalism. The recent appointment of Kang Doo-gu—a two-term lawmaker known for previous infighting—to head the Democratic Research Institute, the party's major policy think tank and a recipient of substantial state funding, has infuriated reformists within the party. Critics have slammed the move as turning a crucial policy engine into a factional spoils system, warning that the institute is being hijacked to serve personal political ambitions rather than long-term national strategy.

At the same time, party leader Kim Min-seok has pursued a series of unpredictable political maneuvers, including high-profile visits to conservative figures who recently lost local elections, framing these moves as an outreach to moderate conservatives. However, for the progressive base that fought hard to win power, these tactical pivots look less like grand coalition-building and more like a betrayal of core values. By alienating their most dedicated supporters in a quixotic chase for hypothetical moderates, the leadership has triggered deep divisions at the worst possible moment.

Rhetorical Missteps and the Trap of "Hostage Politics"

The party leadership's communication strategy has only accelerated the friction. In an effort to defend the administration against internal criticism from progressive heavyweights like Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae—who pointed out sluggishness in the government's reform agenda—party chief Kim Min-seok invoked the historical trauma of former President Roh Moo-hyun's impeachment. Brandishing the word "impeachment" against an internal ally triggered swift rebukes from allied minor parties, who publicly blasted the comparison as reckless and inflammatory.

Rather than acting as a stabilizing bridge for diverse internal voices, the top leadership has increasingly relied on aggressive rhetoric and factional dominance. Critics both inside and outside the party argue that key party leaders are essentially using the presidential administration as political collateral, making moves calculated to secure their own post-presidency influence rather than ensuring the government's success.

The crisis facing South Korea's ruling party is not merely a dip in the approval charts; it is a fracture in the fundamental social contract between the political elite and the citizens who brought them to power.

What to Watch Next

As the ruling party navigates this severe test of public confidence, several concrete developments will indicate whether the leadership is capable of course-correction:

  • Demographic Recovery Attempts: Watch for any substantive policy shifts or youth-oriented engagement programs aimed specifically at regaining the trust of voters in their twenties, moving beyond symbolic rhetoric.
  • Intra-Party Pushback: Monitor how reform-minded and non-mainstream lawmakers respond to controversial appointments within the party apparatus and think tanks.
  • Coalition Dynamics: Pay attention to relations between the Democratic Party and smaller progressive allies like the Rebuilding Korea Party, as fracturing within the broader reform block will dictate electoral math moving forward.
  • Upcoming Polling Trends: Track whether the approval ratings stabilize near the current floor or continue to slide, which would force emergency leadership changes ahead of upcoming political milestones.
