Packing for the Camino de Santiago: How to Keep Your Backpack Under 5 Kilograms

September 07, 2026

The backpack is packed, unpacked, packed, and unpacked one final time. Tomorrow is the day, and the reality of what lies ahead is setting in. Preparing for the Camino de Santiago—the ancient pilgrimage network stretching across Europe to the shrine of Apostle James in northwestern Spain—is entirely different from packing for a standard vacation. Walkers traversing the trails between August and September know that every extra gram carried directly dictates the lifespan of their ankles and knees. Yet, knowing this rule doesn't make it any easier to leave things behind.

Statistics show that roughly eight out of ten pilgrims experience back or ankle pain within the first three days of their trek, a figure that jumps to 90 percent for those carrying packs heavier than 7 kilograms (about 15 pounds). Armed with warnings from seasoned walkers about how an extra 10 or 20 grams can eventually feel like a ton of bricks, the journey to achieve an ultra-light setup requires ruthless editing.

The Core Sleeping Setup: Slicing Weight Where It Counts

Choosing a backpack is the alpha and omega of pilgrimage preparation. Among popular outdoor brands, the Deuter Futra Pro 34-liter pack emerges as a reliable companion after thorough fittings. The golden rule dictates that a fully loaded pack should not exceed 10 percent of your body weight, setting a strict ceiling for the endeavor. Ventilation along the back panel is paramount under the blazing late-summer sun. Though an initial target of 4 kilograms (about 9 pounds) proves overly ambitious, relentless trimming brings the final dry weight down to 5.2 kilograms (about 11.5 pounds) once disposable items meant for an initial stop in Paris are stripped away.

Carrying this weight for eight hours a day is daunting, but every superfluous case, pocket, and accessory must be discarded. In rural public pilgrim hostels—known as *albergues*—which charge nominal fees around 10 euros per night, traveling light translates directly to physical advantage. Arriving earlier secures a better bed and crucial rest. Slimming down the pack is not about sacrificing comfort; it is a practical investment in reaching the destination.

When it comes to bedding, heavy winter sleeping bags are a clear liability during late summer. While budget-friendly down bags popular among hikers offer warmth, they weigh around 600 grams (1.3 pounds). Swapping the traditional sleeping bag for a Decathlon sleeping bag liner weighing just 275 grams (about 10 ounces) drastically cuts the load. Spread out like a simple sheet inside hostel bunks, the liner wards off questionable mattress hygiene while keeping warmth managed through strategic clothing layers—long sleeves, a windbreaker, and a lightweight down jacket worn together if temperatures plummet.

Wardrobe Minimalism: The Rule of Three

The core principle of pilgrim apparel is brutal reduction. Because the journey spans late summer into early autumn, conditions shift from blistering heat to crisp mornings, requiring quick-drying fabrics that can be washed daily. The clothing strategy relies on a strict formula: the clothes worn on the body, plus just two spare sets packed away.

Quick-drying synthetic shirts paired with UV-blocking arm sleeves protect against the Spanish sun, while versatile running shorts double as sleepwear. A pair of lightweight hiking trousers, a breathable windbreaker, and a compact Uniqlo ultra-light down jacket complete a modular layering system. Past travelers confirm that packing more than three sets of clothes is a fast track to back pain. Since most hostels feature laundry facilities or drying lines, washing garments during evening showers makes an extensive wardrobe entirely redundant.

Toiletries and cosmetics are notorious weight traps. Heavy body sunscreens are abandoned in favor of facial sunblock and a portable sun stick, while skincare is pared down to a small toner and a single moisturizer. To tackle cleansing without heavy liquid bottles, single-use enzyme washing powders provide an ultralight solution. Meanwhile, a solid shampoo bar cut strictly in half serves a dual purpose: washing hair and doing laundry. This kind of micro-optimization is the true essence of ultralight travel.

Foot Protection and Analog Survival Gear

On the Camino, socks are just as vital as footwear. The kit relies on two pairs of Darn Tough Merino wool socks and a single pair of toe socks as an emergency backup. Because ankle supports often make shoes uncomfortably tight, athletic tape cut to shape provides targeted joint reinforcement instead.

Foot blisters and plantar fasciitis are the most common hazards on the trail. Carrying a small medical kit with alcohol swabs, needles, thread, and antiseptic powder is non-negotiable. Small touches, like a mesh laundry bag with a clip, double as convenient shower caddies and practical tools for managing wet gear.

Analog fixes often beat modern tech conveniences. Trading heavy carabiners for a handful of safety pins allows damp laundry to be pinned directly to the outside of the backpack to dry on the move. Relying on wired earphones eliminates the stress of charging cables, while a sleek fanny pack worn close to the body manages daily cash, transit cards, and passports securely through initial city stops before hitting the rural trails.

Reflections on the Road Ahead

Packing for a long-distance trek inevitably brings internal anxieties to the surface—the persistent urge to pack for every imaginable worst-case scenario. Yet, laptops, hair styling tools, and bulky cosmetic jars rarely survive past the third day before being shipped home or abandoned. Shedding physical weight is fundamentally an exercise in mastering internal fears.

The 5.2-kilometer bag sitting by the door may still feel heavy once the walking begins, but enduring that weight is part of the transformation. As the journey unfolds along the yellow arrows pointing toward Santiago de Compostela, the physical load lightens while the mind grows resilient. The true gift of the trail is learning how to let go of everything that is not essential.
