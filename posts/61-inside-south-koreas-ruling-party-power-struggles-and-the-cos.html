Inside South Korea’s Ruling Party Power Struggles and the Cost of Political Loyalty

September 26, 2026

As South Korea prepares for the autumn Chuseok holiday—the traditional harvest festival when families gather and politicians desperately try to read the public mood—the country’s political arena is caught in a familiar yet exhausting cycle of infighting. On a recent political commentary broadcast, senior politicians and analysts dissected the fragile state of the liberal Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal opposition-turned-ruling political force under President Lee Jae-myung. Watching the discussion unfold, the underlying reality of Korean politics becomes glaringly obvious: beneath the fluctuating approval ratings and the theatrical displays of party unity lies a grueling war of survival among factions, driven by career preservation and profound political fatigue.

Winning an election and capturing executive power is never the finish line in South Korea. Instead, it merely sets off a high-stakes internal scramble over who gets to hold power, who gets pushed aside, and how those alignments will dictate the outcome of the next national vote. The candid admissions from party insiders on recent political broadcasts are not just isolated soap operas; they serve as a mirror reflecting the deeper fault lines running through contemporary South Korean society. To understand why these internal fractures matter so much, we have to look past the superficial headlines and examine the structural anxieties gnawing at the country's ruling bloc.

The Human Cost of Factional Warfare: Weight Loss, Bruised Egos, and the "Anti-Lee" Label

The physical toll of factional politics in Seoul is rarely discussed openly, making a recent revelation by Democratic Party lawmaker Choi Min-hee particularly striking. Choi disclosed that she had lost 4 kilograms (about 9 lbs) during a grueling party convention due to intense stress. While co-panelists expressed concern for her well-being, the remark highlights the immense mental and physical attrition required simply to stay afloat inside major South Korean political organizations.

Even more corrosive than the physical stress is the media's relentless reliance on toxic political framing. Outlets frequently slap reductive labels like "anti-Lee Jae-myung" onto figures to manufacture division where none may exist. Choi pushed back sharply against this tactic, noting that if independent thought is automatically categorized as opposition to the leader, basic political discourse becomes impossible. The weaponization of factional tags by parts of the media and opportunistic commentators creates an environment where politicians are constantly forced to prove their absolute loyalty, turning political parties into paranoid echo chambers.

At the same time, President Lee Jae-myung's recent efforts to bridge historical divides within the liberal movement—famously declaring that whether one’s roots lie with former presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, or Moon Jae-in, the entire party is now united under a single banner—have offered a glimmer of hope. Veteran activists who lived through the triumphs and heartbreaks of past liberal administrations are appealing for mutual acceptance between older party loyalists and newer waves of supporters. Yet, the party's leadership remains sluggish in institutionalizing this harmony, leaving space for radical online commentators to undermine reconciliation efforts from the outside.

The Machinery of Power: Personnel Reshuffles and the Rulebook of Vetting

As the administration navigates these political tremors, the perennial headache of presidential office personnel changes has resurfaced. With key secretarial posts up in the air following resignations, the debate over how to vet and select new staff has reached a boiling point. Insiders argue that appointments must strictly follow established institutional systems. During past progressive administrations under Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in, rigid verification protocols were meant to screen candidates thoroughly, down to minor property or tax issues, before any final appointment.

The recurring crisis in the presidential office is rarely due to a lack of formal rules; rather, it stems from leaders bypassing those rules to reward political allies. As the administration looks toward upcoming legislative by-elections, the criteria for key positions like the Chief of Staff must be rigorous. Analysts argue that opportunists who publicly attacked President Lee in the past, only to pivot toward him when political winds shifted, must be systematically excluded.

Furthermore, a successful Chief of Staff must possess not only baseline administrative competence but also the courage to deliver unwelcome truths to the president. In a political culture prone to sycophancy, the ability of aides to act as an internal "red team"—challenging groupthink and questioning executive decisions without fear of retribution—is the ultimate test of an administration's maturity.

The Erosion of the Core: Reading the Shifts in Public Sentiment

Public opinion in South Korea is famously volatile, but recent grassroots feedback points to a structural shift that should alarm party strategists. Observers tracking local neighborhood meetings note a distinct cooling of enthusiasm. Earlier in the year, criticisms of President Lee at grassroots gatherings were instantly shouted down by fierce loyalists. As economic and political pressures mounted, however, those defenses grew quiet, replaced by growing numbers of vocal critics and silent observers.

Polling data mirrors this on-the-ground cooling. While fresh supporters have cushioned some of the blow, a significant portion of traditional soft supporters have drifted away, alienated by the toxic behavior of extreme digital fandoms that frequently attack moderate voices. Rebuilding that bridge requires more than just appealing to swing voters during election season; it requires constructing a reliable, permanent base—what political professionals call a "structural majority"—by maintaining institutional trust and punishing bad actors within the party ranks.

The Dilemma of Reform and the Need for Radical Transparency

Nowhere is the cost of internal hesitation more evident than in the stalled push for prosecution and legal reforms. Controversies surrounding high-profile appointments within investigative bodies have exposed deep anxieties among lawmakers who suffered under previous conservative administrations. If the administration compromises on reform principles to appease short-term political pressures, those compromises run the risk of turning into devastating political boomerangs later on.

Critics argue that the ruling party has lost its narrative momentum because leadership is too often trapped in a defensive crouch, paralyzed by the fear of internal backlash. To break this cycle, analysts suggest reviving the format of the marathon, all-night televised debates that characterized past eras of fierce intra-party soul-searching, such as the splits and debates within the Uri Party in the early 2000s. Rather than hiding behind closed doors, bringing factional disagreements into the open through transparent debate could force the party to confront its internal contradictions directly.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves past the Chuseok holiday, several concrete developments will indicate whether the ruling bloc can stabilize its footing or slide deeper into division:
  • Presidential Office Appointments: Watch whether upcoming selections for senior presidential secretaries adhere to strict, transparent vetting systems or rely on closed-door factional patronage.
  • Handling of Party Factions: Pay attention to how leadership deals with extreme online influencers and whether they successfully integrate older mainstream loyalists with newer political bases.
  • Prosecution and Institutional Reform Bills: Track upcoming parliamentary committee hearings and legislative votes to see if the Democratic Party can project a unified, coherent strategy on judicial reform without triggering internal revolts.
