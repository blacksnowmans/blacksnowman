Walking 800 Kilometers with Less: A Pragmatist’s Guide to Packing for the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Every year, thousands of travelers find themselves standing at a crossroads that has little to do with European geography and everything to do with personal psychology. For those who lean heavily toward planning—the kind of travelers who lose sleep over unbooked reservations and unticked checklists—preparing for the Camino de Santiago, the historic network of walking trails leading to the shrine of Saint James in northwestern Spain, can feel like a high-stakes exercise in anxiety. When you commit to walking roughly 800 kilometers (about 500 miles) across Spain under your own power, rather than sliding through a windowed tour bus, gear ceases to be a lifestyle hobby. It becomes a matter of survival.

There is a persistent temptation among first-time long-distance trekkers to equate safety with expenditure. Walk into any outdoor outfitter, and you will find an array of high-end, premium-branded packs, technical shells, and ultra-lightweight accessories that can easily run into thousands of dollars. Yet, veterans of the French Way and other popular Camino routes often offer advice that runs counter to this retail instinct. Flashy brand logos do nothing to prevent blisters on a steep descent into Galicia, and expensive waterproof membranes cannot carry your weight for you.

The Golden Rule of Backpack Weight and the Myth of More

The single most important piece of advice for anyone preparing for a long trek can be summarized in a simple truth: the brand of your backpack matters far less than the weight of the contents inside it. When shopping for a pack, it is easy to be seduced by top-tier names like Gregory or Osprey, which often retail for well over $150 (about 200,000 won). However, a pragmatic approach often leads elsewhere—such as a 40-liter pack from Decathlon, priced closer to $75 (about 100,000 won).

The attraction of budget-friendly gear is not merely financial; it is functional. A proper Camino pack must offer adequate back ventilation, easy access points for essentials, and a harness that conforms tightly to your torso. More importantly, seasoned pilgrims adhere to a strict golden rule: your fully loaded pack should never exceed 10% of your body weight. If you weigh 60 kilograms (about 130 pounds), your total load, including water and snacks, should stay under 6 kilograms (about 13 pounds).

Achieving this threshold requires scrutinizing the weight of the pack itself. Many budget options come with integrated rain covers, saving you both money and the hassle of carrying a separate accessory. When packing, heavy items should sit high and close to the spine to ensure the center of gravity rests against your pelvis, preventing the strain from pulling you backward over a 25-kilometer daily march. Small modifications—such as rigging a lightweight bottle holder to your shoulder strap so you do not have to shrug off your bag every time you need a sip—transform minor annoyances into structural efficiencies.

Digital Minimalism and the Trap of the Just-In-Case Bag

One of the hardest psychological hurdles for modern travelers is letting go of digital security blankets. It is common to pack a heavy laptop or tablet "just in case" remote work calls or a blog needs updating. Yet, the reality of staying in *albergues*—the traditional pilgrim hostels scattered along the route—is far less accommodating to electronics than people realize. Once you plug in your phone and find a rare outlet in a crowded dormitory, your focus should be on recovery, not screen time.

A smartphone is generally all the navigation, communication, and journal-keeping equipment required. For those capturing high-resolution video, a compact portable SSD, such as a 1TB model that connects directly to a phone without requiring a laptop, can shave crucial pounds from your shoulders. Organizing your cables, SIM ejectors, and adapters into a single, dedicated pouch is equally vital; in the dim, chaotic environment of a shared hostel room, unorganized tech gear has a way of disappearing.

The primary trap of the Camino is the psychological friction of over-preparation. Packing a 40-liter bag to its absolute bursting point is an upfront reservation for physical misery. Gear should be chosen with a readiness to discard or downsize, because 800 kilometers of wind, rain, and gravel will quickly teach you what is truly indispensable.

Optimizing Apparel for Rain, Sweat, and Fast Drying

May weather in Spain is famously fickle, capable of shifting from blistering sunshine to driving rainstorms within the span of a single hour. In these conditions, fashion is an expensive distraction. While a traditional poncho with short sleeves might look more fashionable, a coat-style poncho that is large enough to drape entirely over both your body and a 40-liter pack is essential. Looking slightly unusual is a minor price to pay compared to suffering from hypothermia.

When it comes to clothing, three complete sets are more than enough. You wash what you wore that day, and it dries by morning. Convertible trekking trousers, which zip off into shorts or adjust into jogger styles, eliminate the need to pack multiple bottoms. Crucially, cotton is the enemy on a long trek; once soaked in sweat, it stays heavy and chafes the skin. Synthetic, moisture-wicking materials paired with an understanding of hostel drying conditions will serve you better than an overflowing wardrobe.

Physical support gear requires similar pragmatism. Trekking poles relieve immense pressure on the knees during long descents, but they come with a strict code of trail etiquette: rubber tips must be used to protect ancient stone paths, minimize noise, and preserve the natural terrain. Similarly, basic knee sleeves and sports gloves protect against joint strain and hand blisters caused by hours of gripping pole handles under a relentless sun.

What to Watch Next

As you finalize your packing list, keep a close eye on a few practical benchmarks before stepping onto the trail:

    Weigh your packed bag on a bathroom scale: If it crosses the 7-kilogram mark without water, begin ruthlessly editing out items. Test your footwear and socks on local terrain: Do not break in new boots on the first day in the Pyrenees. Check your financial redundancy: Ensure you carry multiple payment methods, such as specialized travel cards with foreign transaction fee waivers, stored separately from your cash. Embrace the mindset of subtraction: Remember that every item you leave behind is a gift to your future joints.

Ultimately, the Camino de Santiago is not a test of consumer gear or outdoor endurance sports. It is an exercise in stripping away the excess of daily life until only what is necessary remains. The frantic research, the debate over grams, and the nightly anxiety over what to bring will all dissolve the moment you take your first steps on the yellow-arrowed path.
