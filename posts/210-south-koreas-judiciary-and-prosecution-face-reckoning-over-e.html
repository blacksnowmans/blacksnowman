South Korea's Judiciary and Prosecution Face Reckoning Over Expense Scandals and Reform Lulls

September 29, 2026

For an outsider watching the evening news in South Korea, the headlines often present a dizzying array of grand political concepts—judicial independence, prosecutorial reform, and constitutional checks and balances. Yet beneath these lofty terms lies a more grinding reality: the persistent, unpleasant sensation that public tax money is being treated as a private slush fund by the very institutions sworn to uphold the law. Recent controversies involving the Supreme Court's official expense accounts and the vetting of candidates for a new anti-corruption investigative body have once again stripped bare the darker side of South Korea's legal establishment, forcing a hard look at what "reform" and "independence" actually mean in practice.

The Supreme Court's Dining Habits and Fictional Guest Lists

The controversy began when details emerged regarding how the Supreme Court and the National Court Administration—the body that manages administrative affairs for South Korea's court system—utilized their official expense budgets. According to investigative reports discussed widely in political commentary circles, lavish meals at high-end restaurants in affluent neighborhoods like Bangbae-dong in Seoul were routinely billed to taxpayers, with individual tabs reaching 150,000 to 300,000 won (roughly $110 to $220) per person.

The scandal, however, is not merely about expensive food; it is about outright fabrication. In one notable instance at a Bangbae-dong restaurant, the official expense report claimed 38 people were in attendance. Investigative checks revealed that the restaurant's entire seating capacity for private groups maxed out at 19, and even renting out the entire venue could only accommodate 33 people at most. Court officials have offered muddled explanations, suggesting the numbers were inflated simply to lower the apparent cost-per-head to meet administrative limits. To the South Korean public, which faces tightening household budgets and dutifully pays taxes, this explanation falls flat. When the final bastion of the law engages in clerical fakery to fund high-end dining on the taxpayer's dime, it crosses the line from administrative oversight into moral bankruptcy.

Shielding Privilege Behind the Banner of Judicial Independence

In South Korea, the independence of the judiciary is treated as a sacred constitutional principle, designed to protect judges from political interference when making rulings. However, critics argue that this principle has been weaponized as an all-access pass to avoid external scrutiny over administrative budgets and institutional management.

Power must always be accompanied by accountability and transparency. Yet the National Court Administration has historically operated behind closed doors, treating judicial independence as an impenetrable shield against public audits. When an institution's leadership treats its budget like a private bank account while wrapping itself in the flag of constitutional autonomy, public trust collapses. Genuine reform requires that the judiciary's financial operations be subjected to the same rigorous, unsparing oversight applied to any other government agency, and that any misuse of public funds face strict investigation.

The Looming Question of the New Investigation Agency's Leadership

While the judiciary faces a reckoning over its expense accounts, South Korea's prosecutorial reform movement is stumbling over its own transitional hurdles. Plans to launch the Serious Crime Investigation Agency (often referred to as the Jungsucheong)—a brand-new body designed to strip investigative powers from the powerful prosecution service and separate investigation from indictment—have been overshadowed by the shortlist of candidates running to lead it.

To the dismay of reform advocates, the names floated for the agency's inaugural chief include several career prosecutors, such as Kim Kwan-jung, Kim Ji-yong, Moon Hong-ju, and Lee Yoon-je. For a public that endured years of political turmoil specifically to curb the unchecked power of the prosecution, the prospect of installing a lifelong prosecutor—and specifically figures suspected of ties to conservative factions—at the head of a reform-oriented agency feels like a cruel irony. Entrusting a newly minted anti-corruption watchdog to someone steeped in the very institutional culture the agency was created to check risks turning the reform into a hollow exercise.

Defenders of these appointments argue that individual background matters less than robust institutional frameworks, pointing out that even among prosecutors, there are those who support separating investigative and prosecutorial powers. Yet this argument underestimates the weight of institutional culture. Lifelong prosecutors shaped by the hierarchical, loyalty-driven norms of South Korea's prosecution service may struggle to wield the reformist scalpel required to dismantle their old home's privileges. When building a new house to keep out the wolves, handing the keys to a veteran wolf—no matter how skillfully trained—is bound to make the neighbors nervous.

What to Watch Next

As these dual crises unfold across South Korea's legal landscape, several concrete developments will signal whether genuine accountability is taking root or if the scandals will simply fade into procedural cover-ups:

  • Audits and Investigations into Court Budgets: Watch for whether opposition lawmakers and civic groups can force a formal, transparent audit of the National Court Administration's expense accounts, or if the judiciary's internal resistance successfully blocks independent probes.
  • The Final Selection of the Investigation Agency Chief: Pay close attention to the final appointment for the head of the Serious Crime Investigation Agency. Choosing a career prosecutor will serve as an immediate litmus test for the administration's commitment to genuine structural reform, whereas selecting an outsider with a clean break from the prosecution service could signal a willingness to change course.
  • Legislative Pushes to Abolish the Court Administration: Momentum is building among progressive lawmakers to radically restructure or abolish the National Court Administration. Monitor floor debates in the National Assembly for concrete legislative proposals aimed at stripping administrative overreach from the courts.

Power rarely surrenders its privileges voluntarily. Unless South Korea's political and legal leadership faces unyielding pressure from an observant public, the cycle of institutional entitlement and superficial reform is destined to repeat itself.
