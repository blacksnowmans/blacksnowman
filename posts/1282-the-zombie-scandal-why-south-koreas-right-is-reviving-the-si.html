The Zombie Scandal: Why South Korea’s Right Is Reviving the Sillajen Conspiracy Against Rhyu Si-min

October 18, 2026

Across South Korean political YouTube channels and partisan message boards, an old phantom has begun rattling its chains once again. The comments appear with coordinated timing, demanding that Rhyu Si-min—one of South Korea's most prominent progressive public intellectuals, best-selling authors, and a former health minister—break his silence and answer for the catastrophic 2019 collapse of the biotech firm Sillajen. To anyone unfamiliar with South Korea’s cutthroat political ecosystem, the sudden resurgence of this five-year-old financial trauma looks like breaking news. To those who watched the scandal unfold under the aggressive investigatory apparatus of then-Prosecutor General Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, it is the return of a thoroughly debunked political hit job.

The campaign to pin the demise of a private pharmaceutical trial on progressive icons is not merely a fringe online obsession. It illustrates the enduring vulnerability of South Korean political discourse to weaponized prosecutorial leaks, retail financial grief, and guilt by association. With the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea pushing to dismantle prosecutorial overreach, looking back at the Sillajen saga reveals why institutional reform remains one of the most explosive battlegrounds in Seoul.

Anatomy of a Collapse: From Kosdaq Darling to Clinical Disaster

To understand why this conspiracy refuses to die, one must first look at the meteoric rise and agonizing fall of Sillajen itself. Founded as an ambitious domestic biotechnology venture, Sillajen became the toast of the tech-heavy Kosdaq exchange after listing in December 2016. Its flagship asset was Pexa-Vec, a genetically modified oncolytic virus designed to selectively infect and destroy cancer cells while leaving healthy tissue intact. To tens of thousands of South Korean retail investors, the company represented the frontier of modern domestic medicine—a potential cure for advanced liver cancer.

In the high-stakes world of global clinical trials, however, promising laboratory science often collides with brutal clinical realities. On August 2, 2019, Sillajen released a regulatory filing that sent shockwaves through the market: an independent Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) had formally recommended halting the company’s global Phase 3 clinical trial. The interim data revealed that Pexa-Vec failed to show a statistically significant improvement in overall patient survival compared to the control group. The stock plunged immediately, wiping out billions of dollars in market capitalization and devastating mom-and-pop investors who had poured their savings into the biotech hopeful.

Clinical failure is an occupational hazard in drug development worldwide, but in the hyper-charged hothouse of Yeouido—Seoul’s financial and political district—tragedy was swiftly reframed as grand conspiracy. In late 2019 and through 2020, the Seoul Southern District Prosecutors' Office launched an expansive, high-profile raid into Sillajen's executive suite. In June 2020, prosecutors indicted nine current and former executives, including former CEO Moon Eun-sang, on explosive allegations of breach of trust and trading on undisclosed material insider information.

Yet when prosecutors took these sensational charges before the bench, their marquee theories collapsed. The core breach-of-trust allegations stemmed from the issuance of bonds with warrants in 2014—years before the clinical crisis. More importantly, South Korean courts at every level—from the district court to the appellate bench and ultimately the Supreme Court—handed down full acquittals regarding the charge that executives had used insider knowledge of the failed trial to dump their shares early. The sensational narrative of orchestrated, state-backed market manipulation dissolved under judicial scrutiny. Following a painful two-year-and-five-month trading suspension, Sillajen's shares finally resumed trading on the Kosdaq in October 2022.

Anatomy of a Smear: Speeches, Informants, and Judicial Exoneration

If the courts found no evidence of insider trading around the clinical trial, how did a literary figure and progressive commentator like Rhyu Si-min become entangled in the wreckage? The right-wing rumor mill relied on two tenuous data points to construct an elaborate scaffolding of guilt.

First, Rhyu had delivered a generic, congratulatory speech at an informational technology briefing hosted by Sillajen back in 2015 in his capacity as a well-known public lecturer and former cabinet member. Second, an early venture capital firm that invested heavily in Sillajen—Value Invest Korea (VIK), which poured roughly 45 billion won (about $34 million) into the company in 2013—was headed by Lee Chul, a businessman who had once participated in progressive civic groups and political campaigns.

From these two benign threads, conservative provocateurs spun a narrative claiming Rhyu was the kingpin of an illicit lobbying ring trading political favors for biotech fortunes. Yet the actual prosecutorial record told the exact opposite story. Even as elite special-investigation units under then-Prosecutor General Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol combed through the company's financial records looking for dirt on political opponents, they came up empty-handed. In 2020, the Seoul Southern District Prosecutors' Office took the unusual step of stating officially and publicly that they had never investigated Rhyu Si-min because they found zero clues or evidence indicating that he had ever received money, engaged in lobbying, or committed any wrongdoing.

What the investigation did uncover was far uglier: an aggressive effort to engineer a confession. During that period, an infamous collusion scandal erupted involving a reporter from the conservative cable network Channel A, who attempted to pressure and cajole the imprisoned VIK chief Lee Chul into fabricating incriminating testimony against Rhyu, brandishing connections to high-ranking prosecutors. When the coercion scheme fell apart, the case against Rhyu collapsed with it. There was no secret bank account, no hidden bribe, and no insider stock transfer.

The "Poisoned Chalice" and South Korea’s Broken Political Marketplace

The persistence of the Sillajen trope exposes a recurring pathology in South Korean political culture: the refusal of political partisans to accept formal judicial reality when a rumor serves factional utility. When the biotech stock originally tanked in August 2019, figures like then-independent lawmaker Lee Un-ju jumped on microphones to declare Sillajen the "ultimate financial scam of the Moon Jae-in era," painting it as state-sponsored graft without providing a shred of documentary evidence. In an ironic twist emblematic of modern Korean political wanderers, Lee would later switch camps, rejoin the Democratic Party of Korea, and align herself with its mainstream leadership—leaving behind the shattered reputations and wild accusations she once championed.

In past public dialogues, Rhyu Si-min offered a chilling critique of why these dynamics persist and how the prosecutorial elite maintain their grip over both sides of the aisle. He warned of what he called the "poisoned chalice": whenever a new government takes power, the prosecution service discreetly approaches the victorious camp with an intoxicating offer:

"We will crush your political rivals even more brutally than we harassed you."

Rhyu warned that the moment a progressive administration accepts that blade to exact revenge against its enemies, it becomes a permanent hostage to the prosecutorial apparatus. The tragic suicide of former President Roh Moo-hyun in 2009, hounded by selective prosecutorial leaks of unverified claims, remains an indelible scar on the progressive psyche. The Sillajen smear was forged in the exact same furnace: leaking dark innuendo to friendly media outlets to destroy a public intellectual's moral standing before a single charge could be proven.

What to Watch Next

As conservative commentators and hyper-partisan YouTubers attempt to breathe fresh life into the Sillajen controversy, observers of South Korean domestic affairs should keep an eye on several concrete developments:

  • Legislative Reform of Investigative Powers: The ruling Democratic Party of Korea under President Lee Jae-myung is moving forward with institutional measures to curtail prosecutorial discretion, including strengthening oversight bodies like the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO). Watch whether conservative opposition figures attempt to use revived financial conspiracy theories to argue that curbs on prosecutorial reach will shield progressive elites from scrutiny.
  • The Battle Over the Partisan YouTube Ecosystem: In South Korea, high-subscriber YouTube channels often set the daily news agenda for political parties. Monitor whether establishment lawmakers from the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, formally adopt these renewed online talking points in parliamentary committee hearings or press briefings, or whether they keep the discredited theory at arm's length.
  • Judicial Retribution and Media Accountability: With the public increasingly fatigued by unsubstantiated financial gate allegations, civil defamation actions and regulatory scrutiny against coordinated disinformation campaigns could set new legal precedents for online commentators recycling debunked investigative files.

South Korea’s democracy has demonstrated remarkable institutional resilience, navigating financial crises, street protests, and the constitutional removal of leaders like Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who now sits in prison following his impeachment. Yet the lingering ghost of the Sillajen conspiracy proves that until facts consistently triumph over factional grievances, no buried smear is ever truly safe in its grave.
