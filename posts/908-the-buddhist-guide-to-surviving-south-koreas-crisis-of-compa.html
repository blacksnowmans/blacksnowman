The Buddhist Guide to Surviving South Korea’s Crisis of Comparison

October 10, 2026

For South Korea’s younger generation, the feeling of falling behind is no longer a temporary phase; it is a permanent state of existence. On social media, the "life gap"—the widening divide between those flaunting extravagant success and those struggling to land a decent job—is constantly pushed to the forefront. It is a hyper-connected misery where one’s own quiet isolation is measured daily against the curated victories of others. This has transformed simple envy into a paralyzing, existential dread.

It is against this backdrop that we are seeing an unlikely trend: young South Koreans turning to Buddhist temples and mindfulness practices to find footing in an unstable world. In a recent discussion, Boman, a deputy head monk at the Ban-A Seonwon temple in Yongin, and Han Seok-jun, a well-known South Korean announcer, dissected the specific burdens placed upon today’s youth. Their conversation suggests that the current epidemic of youth isolation is not a sign of individual weakness, but a logical result of a society that has forgotten how to teach its children to fail.

The Paradox of Overprotective Education

Monk Boman and Han Seok-jun argue that the modern South Korean upbringing—a high-pressure environment focused entirely on test scores—has left young adults fundamentally unequipped for the realities of life. Children are often treated like subjects in an intensive, two-decade-long exam-prep factory. Because their lives are so tightly managed, they never get the chance to stumble, fall, and pick themselves back up.

When these individuals enter the workforce, they encounter a world that does not prioritize them. Academic research supports this, showing that excessive parental intervention severely erodes autonomy and independence. Young adults who have never been allowed to make their own mistakes develop low self-efficacy. They are terrified of rejection and paralyzed by a perfectionism that often manifests as social anxiety or depression. The "good intentions" of parents have, in effect, stripped away the resilience these young people need to survive as adults.

As Monk Boman points out, the problem lies in the "glasses" through which these young people view the world—lenses distorted by their parents' rigid expectations. If a parent forces a child to wear their own narrow, fearful view of reality, that child will grow up unable to see the world as it truly is. The "life gap" isn't just about money; it’s about the structural inability to process life outside of the narrow, binary path of success and failure.

Buddhism as a "User Manual" for Life

Interestingly, the appeal of Buddhist philosophy is cutting across religious lines. On social media, people identifying as Christian, Catholic, or non-religious are finding deep resonance in the monk’s teachings. They aren't looking for a new faith; they are looking for a survival guide.

Boman describes Buddhism not as a religion in the traditional sense, but as a practical "user manual" for sentient beings. He breaks this manual down into four stages:

  • Product Construction: Analyzing the anatomy of the mind when it feels pain.
  • Usage Instructions: Learning how to live with joy and freedom.
  • Precautions: Understanding how the mind "breaks" when we succumb to anger or hatred.
  • After-Sales Service: Knowing when and how to seek help from mentors and teachers.

For a generation feeling adrift, this structured approach offers a sense of stability. The trend of visiting temples is fueled by what Boman calls the "logic of buoyancy": the heavier the burden of life, the greater the need for spiritual support. However, he warns that these experiences must not just be trendy "content" for social media. If the teachings are treated as mere entertainment, the underlying existential void will remain.

The Reality of Interpersonal Pain

Statistical data from Seoul highlights that for those in their 20s and 30s, the greatest source of stress is not money, but interpersonal relationships—specifically, the friction of workplace and academic environments. Many young people are desperate to find a way to escape the people who drain their energy.

Monk Boman’s advice is blunt: stop trying to escape. In Buddhist tradition, Won-jeung-hoe-go (怨憎會苦) refers to the inevitable pain of having to encounter people you dislike. You cannot curate the people around you in the real world. Instead of asking, "How can I avoid this person?", one should ask, "How can I remain unaffected even when I am around someone I dislike?"

The suffering in human relationships often stems from the futile effort to force others to conform to our own standards. Whether in marriage or at work, the desire to "fix" someone or make them act the way we want turns relationships into a hell of our own making. True peace comes from the ability to let go of the obsession to change others, accepting them as they are—moving through the world like the wind, which does not get caught in a net.

What to Watch Next

The conversation between Boman and Han highlights a critical shift in the Korean zeitgeist. As the country grapples with the fallout of the policies of the previous administration—specifically the fallout from the era of former President Yoon (who was removed from office and is currently imprisoned) and the ongoing recovery under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—the younger generation is looking inward for stability.

Moving forward, keep an eye on how these "mental health" trends manifest in public policy. As the ruling Democratic Party continues to navigate the economic and social challenges left behind by the previous conservative leadership, the focus on "quality of life" and mental resilience is becoming a political necessity. The true measure of the nation’s health may not be found in GDP growth, but in whether these young people can actually learn to remove their "glasses of the past" and face the world with their own eyes.
