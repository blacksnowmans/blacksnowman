The Digital Sales Floor: How AI and Black Friday are Reshaping South Korean Commerce

November 18, 2026

Imagine a smartphone screen glowing in the late-night silence of a Seoul apartment. On it, a charismatic host is rapidly demonstrating the texture of a puffer jacket, responding to a flurry of scrolling comments in real-time. This is live commerce—a multi-billion dollar industry that has become the heartbeat of South Korean retail. But if you look closer at the broadcasts occurring this November, the person behind the screen might not be a person at all, and the data being fed to the viewer is being synthesized in milliseconds by an algorithm. As the global "Black Friday" season descends upon the peninsula, the industry is undergoing a high-stakes transformation that pits cutting-edge artificial intelligence against the harsh realities of government-funded inefficiency.

South Korea has long been the world’s laboratory for e-commerce. With the highest smartphone penetration and some of the fastest internet speeds on earth, the transition from traditional television home shopping to mobile-first "Live Commerce" happened almost overnight. However, the current phase is no longer just about moving the shop to the phone; it is about automating the entire experience. From AI-generated product briefings to the rise of virtual influencers, the "human" element of the "human-to-human" sales model is being stress-tested by a drive for absolute efficiency.

The Algorithm as the Showrunner: KT Alpha’s AI Leap

KT Alpha Shopping, the T-commerce subsidiary of KT (Korea’s leading telecommunications giant), recently announced a total overhaul of its broadcasting interface. While a casual observer might just see a cleaner screen, the back-end represents a fundamental shift in how shopping information is delivered. The company has introduced an "AI Briefing" and "Live Talk" system. In the past, if a viewer wanted to know the specific measurements of a frying pan or the material of a rug, they had to type a question and wait for the show host to notice it. Now, an AI scans the product database and viewer queries to provide instant, summarized data overlays on the screen.

This is a strategic move to capture the "MZ Generation"—a Korean portmanteau for Millennials and Gen Z—who are notoriously impatient and prefer text-based information over long-winded verbal explanations. By turning a live broadcast into a "data stream," KT Alpha is effectively removing the friction of the sales process. However, the innovation goes deeper than just text summaries. The company is significantly increasing its reliance on virtual show hosts. These digital avatars don't get tired, don't demand raises, and can broadcast 24 hours a day without a single slip-up.

For the corporate giants, this is a survival tactic. The traditional home shopping industry in Korea has been struggling with rising transmission fees and a dwindling audience of older viewers. Automation offers a way to slash overhead. Yet, there is a cultural risk. The soul of live commerce has always been "jeong"—a Korean concept of affinity and connection. If the host is a script-reading bot and the interaction is handled by an algorithm, the broadcast risks becoming just another static vending machine. The industry is currently searching for the "Golden Ratio": a balance where AI handles the tedious data while humans provide the emotional "vibe" that actually triggers a purchase.

The November Feeding Frenzy: 93% Discounts and Psychological Warfare

While the tech giants refine their AI, the platforms are engaged in a brutal price war as "Black Friday" approaches. In Korea, this season has been localized into a month-long event often referred to as "K-Black Friday." Ably, a leading "vertical" fashion platform (meaning it specializes in a specific niche rather than selling everything), has rebranded its event as "Ab-Bl-Fr" (Ably Black Friday). They are offering discounts of up to 93%, involving over 2,000 individual brands.

A 93% discount is rarely about making a profit on the item itself; it is a customer acquisition cost. In the hyper-competitive Korean market, platforms like Ably and Gmarket—the massive e-commerce site owned by the Shinsegae retail conglomerate—use these "Live Time Deals" to spike their traffic and engagement metrics. During these broadcasts, influencers like the popular comedian and YouTuber Lim Lara are brought in to create a sense of urgency. For 70 minutes, viewers are bombarded with "only now" coupons and gift-with-purchase offers. It is a form of high-speed psychological warfare designed to turn a "want" into an "immediate need."

This trend highlights the growing importance of "channel-specific" strategies. For example, the feminine hygiene brand Secret Day has begun launching "large-capacity packs" exclusively for Naver Shopping Live—the live commerce arm of Naver, South Korea's equivalent to Google. By creating products that can only be bought during a specific live stream on a specific platform, brands are rewarding "platform loyalty." This allows them to bypass the general price wars of the open market and build a direct relationship with a captive audience.

Bridging the Digital Divide in Local Markets

Live commerce is also being touted as a lifeline for those left behind by the digital revolution: traditional market vendors and rural farmers. In Seoul’s Jung-gu district (the historic center of the city), the local government is facilitating "Live Shopping" debuts for traditional market merchants. This is a significant cultural shift. For decades, these markets relied on foot traffic in narrow alleys; now, a fishmonger or a hanbok (traditional clothing) maker can reach a nationwide audience via Naver’s platform.

Similarly, in rural areas like Bonghwa-gun in the mountainous north, the local government is training village leaders in "AI Administration" and live commerce techniques. The goal is to allow farmers to sell their specialty crops—like apples or mushrooms—directly to urban consumers, cutting out the middlemen who usually take a large cut of the profits. When it works, it is a beautiful synergy of traditional quality and modern distribution. It brings the "authenticity" of the countryside to the sterilized screens of city dwellers.

However, the transition is not always smooth. For an elderly farmer or a market stall owner, the technical barriers—lighting, sound, platform algorithms, and real-time customer service—are immense. This has led to a reliance on government-funded support centers, which brings us to the more controversial side of the Korean live commerce boom.

When State Funding Meets Market Reality: The Jeonju Scandal

While the private sector moves at lightning speed, the public sector’s attempts to "help" have often resulted in spectacular failures. A prime example currently making headlines is "Sodam Square" in Jeonju, a city famous for its traditional culture. This government-funded project was designed to support local small businesses in their transition to live commerce. According to reports from the Jeonju City Council, the project was granted about 5.8 billion KRW (approximately $4.1 million) in taxpayer money.

The return on this investment has been described as "disastrous." Critics point out that for every 10,000 KRW (about $7) spent on the program, it generated only 3,000 KRW (about $2) in actual sales for the businesses. In the world of retail, an ROI of negative 70% is a death sentence. Shin Dong-yi, a member of the Jeonju City Council, has raised sharp questions about "private contract" (su-ui-gye-yak) allegations—essentially accusing the administration of handing out lucrative production contracts to specific companies without fair competition, resulting in expensive, low-quality broadcasts that nobody watched.

This failure highlights a recurring problem in South Korea’s "Digital New Deal" era: "Exhibition Administration" (jeon-si-haeng-jeong). This is a term used to describe government projects that look good in a press release or a ribbon-cutting ceremony but lack the market expertise to actually function. Live commerce is a cutthroat, entertainment-driven market. When a government agency tries to run it with a "civil servant" mindset—focusing on filling out forms and hitting "broadcast hours" quotas rather than engaging an audience—the result is a ghost town of empty live streams and wasted taxes.

The stakes are high because the old ways of selling are dying. Major companies like LG Household & Health Care have seen their "multi-level" or "door-to-door" sales revenue plummet by nearly 50%, with the total market size shrinking below 1 trillion KRW (about $715 million). The people who used to sell cosmetics and health supplements by visiting homes are being forced to adapt or disappear. If the government’s support systems fail, these vulnerable workers have nowhere to go.

What to Watch Next

As the "Black Friday" dust settles and we move toward the end of the year, several key indicators will reveal the future of this industry. First, keep an eye on the "AI Adoption Rate" in consumer sentiment. If KT Alpha’s virtual hosts and AI summaries lead to a drop in engagement, we may see a "Return to Human" movement where platforms market "100% Human-Hosted" shows as a premium experience.

Second, watch for a consolidation of public commerce projects. Following the scandal in Jeonju, there is likely to be a nationwide audit of how local governments spend their "Digital Transformation" budgets. We may see a shift away from building expensive, state-owned studios and toward providing direct digital marketing vouchers that small businesses can use to hire their own professional creators.

Finally, observe the Global Pivot. With companies like APR (a Korean beauty tech firm) looking to acquire brands that dominate TikTok Shop in North America, the lessons learned in the hyper-competitive Korean "Live Lab" are about to be exported. The question is no longer whether you will buy your next jacket through a live stream, but whether the "person" selling it to you will be an AI trained on the streets of Seoul.
