Power Struggles and Shattered Alliances: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is at War With Itself

November 04, 2026

When a senior politician chokes back tears on camera and offers a public apology, the gesture is usually designed to lower the political temperature. Inside South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea, however, a recent confession by Representative Kim Min-seok achieved precisely the opposite effect. Rather than clearing the air, Kim’s tearful admission that he had leveled groundless conspiracy theories against his own colleagues blew open a bitter factional feud, shattered the fragile truce within the party’s leadership, and provoked a harsh backlash from the party’s closest parliamentary ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party.

For casual observers of South Korean politics, the spectacle of a governing party turning its knives on itself might seem puzzling. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, the liberal Democratic Party holds the executive and commands a formidable legislative presence. Yet the discipline that once united the progressives during their years fighting against the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ultimately impeached, removed from office, and is now imprisoned—appears to be evaporating. With their common conservative rival pushed into opposition, the battle for control over the ruling camp’s machinery, ideological direction, and future ticket has begun in earnest.

A Tearful Apology Ignites a Factional Firestorm

The immediate spark for the crisis occurred when Representative Kim Min-seok publicly bowed his head and confessed that long-running allegations he had amplified—accusing internal party rivals of factional divisiveness, harboring an "anti-Lee" agenda, and even colluding with Shincheonji, a secretive and controversial religious sect often used as political radioactive waste in South Korea—had no factual basis whatsoever. Kim visibly choked up, uttering a blunt "I am sorry" in front of voters and party members.

If Kim hoped the admission would be received as high-minded contrition, he miscalculated. The response from Representative Jung Chung-rae, another influential heavyweight inside the party’s top echelon, was swift and unsparing. Jung did not welcome the apology; he treated it as an open confession of political sabotage. Jung publicly blasted Kim’s past maneuvers as a "successful political smear operation," arguing that Kim had weaponized fabricated rumors to seize the party leadership in the first place.

For Jung and his loyalists, a tearful apology does not erase the damage inflicted on party cohesion. Jung explicitly categorized Kim’s actions as an anti-party offense—a severe label in South Korean politics that typically precedes disciplinary action or expulsion—and demanded clear accountability rather than empty words. The exchange immediately transformed what might have been an internal grievance into a full-scale leadership feud, dominating the domestic news cycle where more than 15 out of 19 leading political headlines in a single day were consumed by the Kim-Jung confrontation and its fallout.

"Fabricating groundless accusations to build political factions and then attempting to wipe away the slate with a single word of apology fundamentally undermines the democratic legitimacy of a party."

This fierce clash highlights a systemic hazard for the ruling party: when key lieutenants consume their political capital trading accusations of covert political sabotage, the Lee Jae-myung administration inevitably pays the price in lost governing momentum.

The Merger Push That Backfired: Friction With the Rebuilding Korea Party

Compounding the Democratic Party’s internal disarray is an escalating public row with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the hardline progressive outfit that has served as the Democrats' most vital legislative partner. Rumors floated by figures within the Democratic Party suggesting an impending formal merger between the two organizations—specifically slated for this coming January—were meant to signal unity and fortify the ruling bloc’s legislative numbers against the conservative opposition People Power Party. Instead, the proposal was met with open indignation.

Leaders of the Rebuilding Korea Party saw the January merger talk not as an olive branch, but as an arrogant attempt at an absorption merger, driven by Democratic Party spin doctors seeking to neutralize an independent progressive rival. Lawmakers from the Rebuilding Korea Party wasted no time pushing back:

  • Representative Kim Joon-hyung dismissed the leaks as unilateral media manipulation, telling the Democratic Party bluntly to "clean up your own house first" before presuming to talk about mergers with external parties.
  • Representative Shin Jang-sik warned that the Democratic Party had squandered its credibility through its handling of the merger issue. Shin countered by formally proposing that the two parties bypass backroom mergers altogether and instead field candidates in head-to-head competitive primaries, signaling that his party is fully prepared to challenge the ruling party directly in future general elections.

The rift exposes a deep strategic dilemma. The Rebuilding Korea Party built its political brand on uncompromising progressive sharpness and an aggressive pursuit of institutional reform. For its leadership, dissolving into a larger, faction-ridden Democratic Party would mean diluting their hard-won identity and submitting to the Democratic Party's internal hierarchy. By pushing too hard and too casually for an absorption, the Democratic Party leadership has managed to alienate its most essential coalition partner.

Losing the Reform Edge: The Retreat on the Chief Justice Inquiry

The friction between the two allied parties is not merely about candidate nominations and party logos; it is ideological. The Rebuilding Korea Party’s growing exasperation with the Democratic Party boiled over following the ruling party’s sudden decision to postpone a planned parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-de.

Judicial reform has long been a core rallying cry for South Korea’s progressive base, which viewed the judiciary under the previous political establishment with intense suspicion. Pursuing an official probe into Chief Justice Jo was framed as a litmus test of reformist resolve. However, from the perspective of the Democratic Party leadership, navigating the responsibilities of a governing majority requires avoiding an immediate, scorched-earth constitutional showdown with the judicial branch, especially when bread-and-butter economic legislation demands legislative focus.

To its progressive flank, however, the Democratic Party's retreat looked like cowardice. Spokespeople for the Rebuilding Korea Party launched a scathing linguistic critique, mocking the ruling party by declaring that "both the 'T' of impeachment and the 'G' of the state probe have vanished into thin air." In Korean political parlance, the play on initial consonants referred to the DPK abandoning both talk of impeachment (tanhaek) and the parliamentary inquiry (gukjeong josa).

The Innovation Party accused the Democrats of adopting a convenient "blind eye" to difficult fights the moment they took the reins of power. This critique strikes at a vulnerable nerve for the ruling party: if the Democratic Party continues to compromise to manage the state apparatus, it risks hemorrhaging its most dedicated activist base to the Rebuilding Korea Party, which remains unburdened by the practical compromises of running government ministries.

What to Watch Next

The simultaneous eruption of the Kim-Jung confrontation and the breakdown in coalition harmony with the Rebuilding Korea Party present President Lee Jae-myung’s governing coalition with its first structural stress test. As this factional battle unfolds, several key developments will indicate whether the party can stabilize its ranks or descend deeper into internecine conflict:

  • The fate of party disciplinary action: Watch whether Representative Jung Chung-rae and his allies formally push the issue of Representative Kim Min-seok’s Shincheonji smears to the party’s ethics committee. If the leadership attempts to quietly bury the issue, Jung’s faction could escalate, deepening the divide between party headquarters and rank-and-file activists.
  • The abandonment or revival of the January merger: Pay attention to whether Democratic Party figures continue to talk up a merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party or quietly shelf the initiative. If Representative Shin Jang-sik’s call for direct electoral competition gains traction, expect the two progressive camps to begin attacking each other openly on legislative priorities.
  • Legislative positioning on judicial reform: Observe how the Democratic Party handles Chief Justice Jo Hee-de moving forward. Rescheduling the parliamentary investigation would signal a surrender to the party's hardline base, while indefinitely shelving it could cement a long-term split with the Rebuilding Korea Party.

Transitioning from an insurgent opposition to a mature governing party is historically the most dangerous phase for South Korean political movements. With the conservative People Power Party waiting to capitalize on any sign of ruling-camp dysfunction, the Democratic Party must decide whether its immediate priority is consolidating state governance or settling personal scores among its own leadership.
