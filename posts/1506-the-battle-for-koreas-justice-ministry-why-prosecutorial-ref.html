The Battle for Korea's Justice Ministry: Why Prosecutorial Reform Hinges on Stripping Every Last Investigative Power

October 26, 2026

Inside the main hearing room of the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee in Seoul, the atmosphere is charged with partisan hostility. For months, the top cabinet post at the Ministry of Justice has sat empty. Conservative opposition lawmakers are hammering the liberal administration, seizing on parliamentary audits to fire broadsides at potential ministerial candidates. Liberal lawmakers push back, arguing that filling the cabinet vacancy is not an administrative formality—it is the indispensable final step in dismantling one of South Korea's most entrenched institutions: the unchecked power of the state prosecution service.

At the heart of this confrontation lies a fiercely contested question that has defined South Korean politics for years: Should state prosecutors retain any right to conduct investigations, or must their authority be strictly limited to bringing cases to court?

Korea's Judicial Crisis and the Race to Fill the Cabinet Vacancy

To understand why the appointment of a single cabinet minister carries such immense political weight in Seoul, one must understand the unique role the Ministry of Justice plays in South Korea's governing architecture. Unlike in many Western democracies where prosecutors operate largely as legal technicians reviewing police work, South Korean prosecutors historically possessed a dual monopoly: the sole authority to indict suspects and expansive power to direct and conduct criminal investigations directly.

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), sweeping prosecutorial reform stands at the absolute top of the domestic agenda. Reformers argue that past abuses—most glaringly observed during the tenure of Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later impeached and is currently imprisoned—demonstrated how easily prosecutorial authority could be weaponized against political rivals through targeted investigations and secondary probes.

However, the prolonged vacancy at the helm of the Ministry of Justice has created a dangerous operational vacuum. Without a confirmed minister to lead executive oversight and drive legislative implementation, the momentum for structural reform risks stalling. For the ruling DPK, appointing a resolute minister with an unyielding reform philosophy is essential to secure state stability and close loopholes that entrenched interests might exploit to preserve their privileges.

Partisan Warfare in the National Assembly: The "Defense Attorney" Frame

As speculation grows over who will take the reins at the ministry, the National Assembly has devolved into a theater of political combat. During recent committee audits, lawmakers from the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP)—led by figures such as Representative Na Kyung-won—launched concentrated attacks against top reform-minded candidates.

The opposition's main line of attack centers on framing key DPK lawmakers under consideration for cabinet or leadership roles, such as Representatives Park Gyun-taek and Yang Bu-nam, as "former defense attorneys for President Lee Jae-myung." Conservative lawmakers have sought to cast their legal backgrounds as a conflict of interest, alleging that appointing figures who once represented Lee in court amounts to political payback or an effort to build a personal shield for the presidency. PPP members have also dragged collateral names into committee hearings, including presidential aide Kim Hyun-ji and figures tied to the Daejang-dong land development controversy, turning policy audits into shouting matches.

Supporters of reform argue that this strategy is a transparent attempt to derail institutional change. In their view, serving as defense counsel is a standard professional activity for legal experts, and using it as a scarlet letter ignores candidates' actual qualifications and reform track records. By fixating on personal attacks and sensational political framing rather than evaluating policy competence, the opposition aims to sap public enthusiasm for curbing prosecutorial authority.

"Not Even a Single Drop of Broth": The Battle Over Supplementary Investigation Rights

The core legal dispute surrounding prosecutorial reform boils down to what is known in Korean criminal procedure as "supplementary investigative power" (보완수사권). Historically, even when primary investigative authority was transferred to the police, prosecutors retained the right to demand supplementary investigations or conduct direct follow-up investigations themselves before deciding whether to indict.

Reform advocates insist that as long as prosecutors retain even a fraction of investigative authority, the complete separation of powers remains an illusion. Representative Park Gyun-taek, a former senior prosecutor who is now a prominent DPK lawmaker advocating for reform, exemplifies this shift in perspective.

Early in his legal career, Park held the conventional view within the legal establishment: that prosecutors needed at least a minimal level of supplementary investigative authority to maintain case continuity and ensure effective indictments. However, after witnessing the aggressive expansion of prosecutorial overreach during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office—where minor procedural openings were routinely expanded into sweeping political target investigations—Park's stance reversed entirely.

During recent parliamentary discussions, Park captured the uncompromising mood of the reform movement with a vivid declaration, stating that when observing prosecutorial behavior, he realized "not even a single drop of broth should be left" when it comes to prosecutors' supplementary investigative powers.

This principle reflects a growing conviction among liberal reformers:

  • Zero Tolerance for Overlap: Allowing prosecutors even 1% of investigative discretion grants them the leverage to re-open direct investigations, launch secondary probes on unrelated charges, and bypass police findings.
  • Complete Structural Separation: True reform requires restricting the prosecution service exclusively to its constitutional function as an indicting agency that reviews evidence gathered by police and specialized investigative bodies.
  • Preventing Privatized Power: Without total structural severance between investigation and indictment, state prosecutorial power will inevitably expand back to its former overbearing state whenever political conditions allow.

What to Watch Next in Seoul's Judicial Overhaul

As the battle over the Ministry of Justice reaches a critical juncture, several key developments will determine whether South Korea can finally achieve a complete separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers:

  • The Official Cabinet Nomination: All eyes are on President Lee Jae-myung's imminent selection for Justice Minister. The choice will signal whether the administration intends to push through complete prosecutorial divestment immediately or navigate a protracted confirmation fight with the PPP.
  • Confirmation Hearing Battles: Expect the National Assembly Legislation and Judiciary Committee to become a major political battleground. The conservative opposition will double down on the "presidential defense team" rhetoric, while the liberal majority will seek to highlight the nominee's commitment to judicial equity.
  • Legislative Package on Investigative Bodies: Watch for parliamentary movement on enabling legislation that will establish standalone investigative agencies—such as a Major Crimes Investigation Agency—to take over remaining investigative duties from the prosecution service once supplementary powers are revoked.
  • Public Sentiment vs. Reform Fatigue: Years of intense political clashes over judicial reform have left many voters weary. The government's ability to frame complete prosecutorial reform as a fundamental restoration of civic fairness, rather than partisan retribution, will be vital to sustaining political momentum.
