From 60-Second Clips to AI Chatbots: How Live Commerce is Reshaping Korean Retail and Global Markets

November 13, 2026

Picture a living room in Seoul, though the television is barely holding anyone's attention anymore. Instead, the real action is happening on a smartphone screen, where a one-minute clip of a local farmer holding up freshly harvested pomegranates is deciding whether a small business survives the week. For merchants, creators, and platform giants across South Korea, the rules of retail are being rewritten at a dizzying pace—and if you blink, your profit margins might vanish into a platform’s sudden fee hike.

The global retail landscape has entered a precarious era defined by two conflicting forces: hyper-efficient automation and total platform dependency. While traditional TV home shopping networks in South Korea scramble to reinvent themselves around short-form video creators, cross-border sellers find themselves at the mercy of algorithms and financial policies that shift almost daily. To understand where commerce is heading next, we have to look past the glitz of viral videos and examine the fragile mechanics holding the modern digital marketplace together.

The Shrinking Screen: How Traditional Home Shopping is Pivoting to Short-Form Video

For decades, legacy home shopping channels in South Korea relied on a familiar format: hour-long, unhurried broadcasts beamed directly into living rooms. But as consumer attention spans fracture under the weight of mobile feeds, that model has reached its expiration date. Major Korean home shopping operators are rapidly restructuring, realizing that survival now depends on conquering the 60-second vertical video format pioneered by platforms like TikTok and YouTube Shorts.

A prime example is Hom&Shopping's recent recruitment initiative targeting ten specialized creator teams. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in South Korea have long struggled with a cruel paradox: they can manufacture exceptional products, but they lack the digital media infrastructure required to make them pop on a smartphone screen within a single minute. By stepping in to curate and deploy professional creators, the platform is effectively turning these influencers into outsourced digital sales reps and branding directors.

Yet, putting creators in front of cameras is only half the battle. Behind the scenes, legacy broadcasters are quietly integrating artificial intelligence deep into their operations—handling everything from customer service and predictive inventory management to real-time product recommendations. In an industry where data is currency, a home shopping company’s historical sales database becomes a powerful weapon when paired with AI. Even so, technology cannot rescue a fundamentally boring video. In an ecosystem where viewers swipe away in half a second, content quality remains king, and channels lacking a distinct identity are being weeded out without mercy.

The Global Seller's Nightmare: Navigating Whimsical Platform Policies

While domestic home shopping networks scramble to adapt, sellers venturing into international marketplaces face a different kind of crisis. According to a recent policy tracking report by payment solutions firm PortOne, five major global marketplaces altered their financial rules a staggering 135 times over a span of just nine months. That translates to a policy shift roughly every single day and a half.

For independent merchants operating on thin margins, this volatility is paralyzing. When a fee structure or settlement timeline changes overnight, a meticulously calculated business plan collapses instantly. These unannounced shifts function less like standard business adjustments and more like corporate coercion, forcing smaller sellers to spend their mornings deciphering complex policy notices instead of focusing on product development or marketing. When platforms hold total monopoly power, the merchant's bargaining leverage drops to zero.

Compounding this administrative fatigue are chronic settlement delays—a death sentence for retail businesses where cash flow is oxygen. As global e-commerce giants battle fiercely for market share, the mounting financial pressures of that war are increasingly being offloaded onto third-party vendors. For brands looking to break out of South Korea's saturated domestic market, avoiding total platform dependency has become an existential necessity. Diversifying sales channels and strengthening direct-to-consumer websites are no longer optional strategies; they are defensive armor against the whims of digital landlords.

K-Beauty's Global Surge Meets the Rise of AI-Driven Shopping

Despite the structural hazards of international platforms, Korean brands are achieving unprecedented milestones abroad. K-beauty exports to the United States have surged by 62.7%, fueled by a potent mix of online live streams and aggressive offline retail placement. Brands like d'Alba Global reported soaring quarterly operating profits, a testament to what happens when a company successfully leverages short-form video and live commerce to cultivate an international fandom rather than relying solely on low prices.

Today's K-beauty success stories are built on high-functionality derma-cosmetics and active real-time engagement. Streamers do not just push products; they break down chemical ingredients scientifically and demonstrate visible results live on camera, building deep trust with global consumers who expect transparency.

Meanwhile, neighboring China is already looking past human-hosted broadcasts entirely. AI-driven shopping has crossed a significant threshold, with AI chatbots now driving roughly 5% of total transactions on major platforms. Recent live-streaming highlights show AI agents successfully executing massive flash sales autonomously, managing customer inquiries, distributing targeted discount codes, and closing transactions around the clock. As this technology inevitably migrates to other markets, human creators will face a stark reality: to survive the AI onslaught, they must offer a level of authentic human connection and creative spontaneity that code cannot replicate.

The numbers behind this global shift tell a clear story:

  • 62.7%: The year-over-year surge in South Korea's cosmetic exports to the United States.
  • 135: The number of financial and operational policy revisions enacted by five major global marketplaces over a nine-month period.
  • 5%: The transaction volume share captured by AI chatbots in China's rapidly evolving e-commerce ecosystem.

From Regional Farms to Fandom Comedy: The Human Core of Live Commerce

While algorithmic efficiency and global conglomerates dominate headlines, live commerce retains a deeply grassroots appeal in local markets. Regional authorities in places like Goheung County are rolling out practical live-streaming training programs for local farmers, teaching agricultural producers how to pitch their own locally grown yuzus and pomegranates directly to smartphone users. By cutting out complex multi-tiered distribution chains, these digital workshops give farmers a rare chance at true economic self-reliance.

The magic of these local broadcasts lies entirely in their raw authenticity. A farmer standing in a field, stumbling over words while holding up a freshly picked vegetable, carries far more emotional weight than a polished corporate advertisement. Consumers are increasingly drawn to the human story behind what they consume.

We see a similar dynamic in the processed food and snack sectors, where entertainment creators and brand owners team up for live shopping events. When popular comedy acts or brand founders host unscripted live broadcasts—bantering with viewers, offering bundle deals, and sharing behind-the-scenes stories—shopping transforms into interactive entertainment. These broadcasts succeed not because they are the cheapest option available, but because they build a genuine sense of community between the buyer and the seller.

What to Watch Next

As the live commerce and retail sectors continue to evolve, several key developments will determine where the industry goes from here:

  • The Expansion of AI Automation: Monitor how quickly domestic Korean platforms begin adopting automated AI chat and sales agents to cut operational costs, and watch how human creators adapt their content to retain viewer loyalty.
  • Regulatory Scrutiny on Platform Policies: Keep an eye on potential legal or governmental pushes to establish fairer guidelines for cross-border marketplaces, particularly concerning sudden fee adjustments and delayed merchant settlements.
  • SME Digital Adoption Rates: Track the ongoing success of regional government initiatives—such as creator-matching programs and digital farming workshops—to see if smaller merchants can successfully insulate themselves against platform volatility through independent direct-to-consumer strategies.
