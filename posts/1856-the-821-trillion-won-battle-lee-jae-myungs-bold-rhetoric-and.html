The 821 Trillion Won Battle: Lee Jae-myung’s Bold Rhetoric and Korea’s High-Stakes Budget War

November 02, 2026

The air in Seoul’s Yeouido district, the heart of South Korean politics, has turned sharply cold—and not just because of the autumn breeze. On November 1st, the National Assembly became a theater of extreme confrontation as the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the current ruling party, and the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition, clashed over everything from diplomatic etiquette to a record-breaking national budget. At the center of the storm is President Lee Jae-myung, whose blunt, uncompromising style is redefining the presidency after the tumultuous years that led to the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

For an international observer, the current political landscape in South Korea can seem dizzying. The country is still navigating the aftermath of the "Prosecutor Republic" era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who is currently serving time in prison following his removal from office. In his wake, President Lee Jae-myung has stepped in with a mandate to rebuild the economy and restore Korea's standing on the global stage. However, as the latest headlines suggest, the transition is anything but smooth. With a staggering 821 trillion won budget (roughly $615 billion) on the table, the stakes have moved beyond mere policy; they have become a battle over the very vision of Korea’s future.

"Pigs See Only Pigs": The President’s Gloves-Off Diplomacy

President Lee Jae-myung has never been one to mince words. When the conservative opposition began a concerted attack on his recent overseas diplomatic tours, labeling them "wasteful" and "ineffective," the President did not retreat into prepared bureaucratic statements. Instead, he took to social media with a stinging proverb: "To the eyes of a pig, only pigs are visible."

This phrase, rooted in Buddhist philosophy, essentially suggests that one’s perception of the world is a reflection of their own character. By using it, President Lee was not just defending his itinerary; he was suggesting that the PPP’s criticism of his diplomacy as "money-wasting" reflects their own narrow-minded, transactional view of international relations. The President’s supporters cheered the "refreshing" honesty, while the opposition scrambled to frame it as a "distorted perception of state affairs."

The core of the dispute lies in the value of presidential diplomacy. The Lee administration argues that in an era of shifting global supply chains and heightened security risks, "sales diplomacy" is a necessity, not a luxury. Reports emerged that the President maintained such a grueling schedule during his trip that he required intravenous (IV) drips and suffered from frequent nosebleeds. To the DPK, this is a sign of a leader "bleeding for the national interest." To the PPP, specifically Representative Jeong Jeom-sik, it was an opening for a personal jab: "Is he bleeding because he’s staying up late on social media?"

"The essence of diplomacy is the practical benefit we gain for the nation. To turn the process itself into an object of ridicule is to ask whose side the opposition is actually on."

This rhetorical escalation is significant because it highlights the deep scars left by the previous administration. The DPK frequently points out the contrast between President Lee’s active engagement and the "diplomatic disasters" of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, during which the former leader was criticized for various gaffes and a perceived lack of preparation. By framing the current criticism as "petty," the Lee administration is attempting to solidify its image as the "adult in the room" regarding foreign policy.

The $615 Billion Gambit: Investing in a Post-Crisis Future

While the verbal sparring makes for good headlines, the real war is being fought over the 821 trillion won (about $615 billion) budget proposal for the coming year. This is a historic sum, and it represents President Lee’s "bold investment" strategy. For a foreign investor or market analyst, this budget is the clearest roadmap of where the Lee administration wants to take South Korea.

The DPK’s budget is centered on several key pillars:

  • R&D Restoration: Reversing the controversial cuts made during the previous era to ensure Korea remains a leader in semiconductors and AI.
  • Energy Transition: Funding the shift toward renewable energy and the "Green New Deal" initiatives.
  • The "Basic Society" Framework: Implementing foundational welfare programs designed to provide a stronger safety net in an aging society.

The People Power Party, however, has pledged to take a "scalpel" to this proposal, aiming to cut at least 30 trillion won (approximately $22.5 billion). They have branded the budget as "populist" and a "blank check" that will burden future generations with debt. The PPP’s stance is a classic conservative appeal to fiscal responsibility, but in the current climate, it is also a strategic attempt to hamstring President Lee’s signature policy wins before the next election cycle.

The conflict over the diplomacy budget is a microcosm of this larger fight. The government is seeking to increase the budget for international cooperation, arguing that Korea’s influence is a "hidden asset" that pays dividends in trade and security. The opposition demands "immediate proof of results," a demand that ignores the long-term nature of geopolitical relationships. As the budget review process begins in the National Assembly, expect a "war of attrition" that could last until the end of the year, with the livelihood of ordinary Koreans caught in the middle.

Internal Fractures: The PPP’s Struggle to Find a Post-Yoon Identity

While the opposition is united in its hatred of the Lee administration’s budget, it is deeply divided within itself. A significant development on November 1st was the firm stance taken by Representative Jang Dong-hyeok against the return of Han Dong-hoon to the party leadership. Han, who served as Justice Minister under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, was once seen as the "crown prince" of the conservative movement. Now, he appears to be a political pariah.

"As long as I am in leadership, the conditions for Han Dong-hoon's return don't even exist," Jang stated. This is a powerful signal that the PPP is trying to excise the "pro-Yoon" or "pro-prosecutor" elements that led the party to its current weakened state. Instead of looking back toward the imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s inner circle, figures like Jang are looking toward a different kind of alliance.

Jang has openly floated the idea of a partnership with Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul, and Lee Jun-seok, the former PPP chairman who famously broke away to form his own movement. This "New Conservative" coalition would theoretically combine Oh’s administrative experience and middle-class appeal with Lee Jun-seok’s popularity among younger voters. The goal is to move from "Prosecutor Conservatism" to "Policy Conservatism."

However, this transition is fraught with contradictions. Even as they talk about "moderate expansion," the PPP continues to flirt with its far-right base. In a recent gathering in the Chungcheong region—a critical "swing" area in Korean elections—over 1,000 party members gathered to shout slogans for a "Conservative Rebirth." Yet, the rhetoric at such events often leans into conspiracy theories, such as the abolition of early voting and claims of election fraud.

Threats to the Democratic Process: The Early Voting Controversy

Perhaps the most concerning trend for those watching Korean democracy is the persistent claim from a wing of the PPP that the recent elections were rigged. On November 1st, calls to abolish early voting were again raised, based on unverified claims that the digital counting systems were compromised.

Early voting was introduced in South Korea to increase participation and convenience, and it has been widely successful. Attempts to roll it back are seen by many analysts as a "sore loser" narrative—an attempt to delegitimize the Lee administration’s mandate rather than addressing why the conservative platform failed to resonate with voters. When senior figures like Jang Dong-hyeok refer to certain protest sites as "new sanctuaries of democracy" while standing alongside election deniers, it raises questions about the PPP’s commitment to constitutional norms.

Meanwhile, at the local level, there is a more constructive movement toward "Local Council Acts." Leaders of provincial and municipal councils, largely from the PPP, are pushing for more independence from local governors. They want their own power over personnel and budgets to ensure they can effectively check the executive branch at the local level. This "grassroots democracy" movement is a rare area of bipartisan potential, but it remains overshadowed by the high-decibel shouting matches in the central government.

What to Watch Next

As the "Budget War" enters its most intense phase, here are the concrete developments to monitor:

  • The 30 Trillion Won Cut: Watch which specific programs the PPP targets. If they focus on R&D or semiconductor support, it could trigger a backlash from the tech industry and markets.
  • The Oh Se-hoon Factor: As the PPP distances itself from Han Dong-hoon, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s profile as the "rational alternative" will likely grow. His policy moves on housing and urban development will be key indicators of the party's new direction.
  • Legislative Deadlock: If the budget is not passed by the legal deadline, we could see a "stop-gap" budget situation, which would create significant uncertainty for government agencies and contractors.
  • Diplomatic Outcomes: President Lee is likely to continue his "sales diplomacy." Tangible results—such as large-scale energy contracts or supply chain agreements—will be his best defense against the "pigs see only pigs" controversy.

South Korea is currently a laboratory for how a "strongman" progressive government handles a fractured, conspiracy-laden opposition in a post-crisis era. The results of the next few months will determine whether the 821 trillion won is a bridge to the future or just more fuel for the political fire.
