The Cracks in the Fortress: Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval is Plummeting

October 26, 2026

In the quiet corridors of the government complex in Seoul, the mood has shifted from the triumphant energy of the inauguration to a palpable sense of siege. For months, President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea (DP)—the country’s main liberal party—have relied on a "direct communication" strategy to bypass traditional media and speak straight to the people. But the latest data suggests that the people are no longer listening with the same enthusiasm. Instead, a growing number of Koreans are turning away, frustrated by what they perceive as a breakdown in administrative order and an increasingly heavy-handed approach to dissent.

The release of the latest Realmeter poll for the fourth week of October 2026 has sent shockwaves through the ruling camp. President Lee’s approval rating has slid to 35.3%, a drop of 2.0 percentage points from the previous week. Meanwhile, negative assessments have surged to 62.3%. This creates a "dead cross" gap of 27.0 points—a margin that political analysts in Seoul often describe as the threshold for "psychological impeachment," where a government loses the functional trust required to push through major legislative agendas.

For a foreign observer, these numbers might look like standard political volatility. In South Korea, however, they represent a fundamental fracturing of the coalition that brought Lee to power. The decline is not merely a product of economic headwinds; it is a reaction to the very style of governance that President Lee has championed since taking office after the tumultuous removal and imprisonment of his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

The Exodus of the "House Rabbits"

In Korean political parlance, loyal base voters are known as "house rabbits" (jip-tokki), while swing voters are "mountain rabbits" (san-tokki). The most alarming aspect of the recent poll for the Lee administration is that the house rabbits are fleeing. Support among those who identify as liberals dropped by 5.6 percentage points, falling from 57.7% to 52.1%. When a president’s own ideological base begins to waver, it usually signals a crisis of identity within the administration.

The demographic breakdown tells an even grimmer story. Support among retirees and the unemployed—groups highly sensitive to the immediate "feel" of the economy and social stability—plummeted by 9.0 percentage points. Among those aged 70 and older, traditionally a demographic that values institutional order, approval dropped by 7.0 points. In the capital city of Seoul, a critical barometer for national sentiment, the President’s approval is barely clinging to the 30% mark.

Why are these groups leaving? For the elderly and the middle class in Seoul, the chaos of "SNS politics" appears to be the primary culprit. President Lee has made a point of using social media to announce policy shifts and criticize opponents in real-time. While this was seen as refreshing during the campaign, it is now being viewed as a source of instability. When the President bypasses his own ministers and career bureaucrats to post late-night thoughts on complex judicial reforms or housing taxes, it creates a vacuum of professional expertise. The result is a government that feels reactive and impulsive rather than steady and planned.

The Great Reversal: People Power Party Takes the Lead

Perhaps the most significant psychological blow to the ruling Democratic Party is the reversal in party approval ratings. For the first time in four months, the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—has surged ahead of the DP by a margin outside the polling’s error range. The PPP now stands at 42.6%, while the DP has stagnated at 36.0%.

This 6.6 percentage point gap is a dramatic turnaround. Just a few months ago, the PPP was struggling to find its footing as it dealt with the fallout of the previous administration. However, the opposition has successfully consolidated its base by capitalizing on the DP’s "slip of the tongue" risks. Recent gaffes by DP leadership, such as controversial comments regarding the "reusing of salt" in public welfare programs, have painted the ruling party as out of touch with the struggles of ordinary citizens.

The PPP’s rise is also a result of "reflexive benefit." As Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president currently serving a prison sentence, has seen some of his secondary legal risks ease through recent court rulings, the conservative base has felt emboldened to mobilize. Jung Chung-rae, a high-ranking member of the Democratic Party, summarized the fear within his own ranks: "No matter how many mountain rabbits you catch, if the house rabbits leave the house, the elections are doomed."

SNS Politics: Democratic Innovation or System Failure?

At the heart of the Lee Jae-myung presidency is a belief in unmediated access. During a recent briefing, the President defended his heavy use of social media, stating that without it, he would not be in the position he is today. He described SNS as a "breathing hole" that allows him to hear the "cries of the alleyways," comparing himself to ancient kings who would dress as commoners to listen to gossip in taverns.

However, this "tavern-style" governance is clashing violently with South Korea’s highly trained professional bureaucracy. The Presidential office has pointed to global examples, such as Mexico’s President Claudia Sheinbaum and her "Mañanera" daily press conferences, to justify Lee’s constant digital presence. But critics argue there is a world of difference between a scheduled, transparent briefing and a flurry of unfiltered social media posts.

The danger of this approach was laid bare in a recent security lapse. In an attempt to boast about a successful crackdown on voice phishing, the President posted a photo to his SNS account. Sharp-eyed observers quickly noticed that the image contained documents clearly marked as "confidential" (daeoebi), including sensitive information regarding an ongoing special prosecution. This was not just a PR blunder; it was a breach of state protocol that suggests the President’s personal social media activities are not being properly vetted by the National Security Office or the Digital Communications Secretary.

"When the President speaks, the world moves. If that speech is unrefined, the system breaks."

This bypass of the system has led to what some call "policy by tweet." When the President posts a definitive stance on the unconstitutionality of a new investigative agency before his own Ministry of Justice has finished its legal review, he effectively muzzles his own experts. Career bureaucrats, afraid of contradicting the Commander-in-Chief, simply fall in line, leading to poorly vetted policies that often require embarrassing "clarifications" days later.

The Return of the "Muzzle": Press Raids and Dissent

For many liberal voters, the most painful betrayal has been the administration’s treatment of the press. During his campaign, Lee Jae-myung promised a new era of transparency, contrasting himself with the "authoritarian" tendencies of the past. Yet, the recent surge in search and seizure warrants against media outlets has led to accusations of "ibtulmak"—a Korean term meaning "stuffing a gag in someone's mouth."

The flashpoint of this controversy is the raid on Sisa Tapa, a liberal-leaning media outlet, and its representative Lee Jong-won. The investigation follows Lee’s hints that he possessed evidence of the administration’s interference in party affairs. The government’s decision to use the heavy machinery of the state—prosecutors and police raids—to respond to these claims has left even DP supporters uneasy. They ask: How is this different from the tactics used by the previous administration?

Jang In-su, a prominent journalist, has been vocal about the absurdity of these raids. "If a report is false, the article itself is the evidence. It’s on the servers, it’s on YouTube. Why do you need to raid an office and seize a reporter’s phone?" he asked. The prevailing theory among critics is that the raids are not about gathering evidence for a libel case, but about "hunting for the source." By seizing phones and laptops, the government can identify the whistleblowers within the administration or the party who are feeding information to the press.

This climate of fear has a chilling effect. When the state treats a journalist’s contact list as a map for a "conspiracy hunt," sources dry up, and the media begins to self-censor. For a president who claims to rely on the "breath of the people," cutting off the oxygen of a free press is a dangerous irony. As the "moral high ground" of the DP erodes, so too does the support of the middle-class swing voters who value democratic norms above partisan loyalty.

What to Watch Next

The Lee Jae-myung administration is at a crossroads. To regain the trust of the "house rabbits" and stabilize the ship of state, several key developments will be worth watching in the coming weeks:

  • A Cabinet Reshuffle: Pressure is mounting for a major shake-up of the Prime Minister’s office and the senior secretaries at the Blue House. If President Lee replaces his "loyalist" inner circle with more traditional, technocratic figures, it could signal a return to systematic governance.
  • The "SNS Exit Strategy": Watch for whether the President scales back his direct social media posts in favor of formal briefings. Any move to empower the Digital Communications Office to "filter" the President’s output will be seen as a win for the bureaucracy.
  • The 30% Floor: If the President’s approval rating falls below 30% in the next two weeks, he enters the "lame duck" zone. This would likely trigger a rebellion within the Democratic Party as lawmakers begin to distance themselves from the President to save their own seats in upcoming local elections.
  • Judicial Outcomes: Several high-profile cases involving both the President’s aides and opposition figures are reaching critical stages. The courts’ decisions on the legality of recent press raids could either embolden the administration or force a humiliating retreat.

President Lee Jae-myung once said that power is like a "plantain leaf fan"—a small movement can create a wind that changes the world. Right now, the wind he is generating seems to be blowing his own supporters away. Whether he can change the direction of that wind will determine the fate of his remaining term.
