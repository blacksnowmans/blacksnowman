A Gridlocked October: South Korea’s Political Wrangling Intensifies

October 22, 2026

On October 21, the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido felt more like a battleground than a legislature. As the annual parliamentary audit—a critical period where lawmakers scrutinize government administration—approached its final days, the tension between South Korea's ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the main opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), reached a boiling point. From attempts to impeach the head of the judiciary to fierce disagreements over who should testify about the inner workings of the presidential office, the day highlighted the deep, structural divisions currently paralyzing South Korean politics.

While President Lee Jae-myung spent his day visiting a third-generation "new town" housing project in Incheon and local markets to signal a focus on the economy, the political elite in Seoul remained locked in a zero-sum game of strategy and attrition. Here is a breakdown of the critical power struggles unfolding in the capital.

The Judiciary Under Fire: The Push to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae

The most explosive development of the day was the Rebuilding Korea Party’s—a progressive minor party—official delivery of an impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae to the DPK leadership. The Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Cho Kuk, has accused the Chief Justice of colluding with other Supreme Court justices to interfere in elections. Their demand is aggressive: they want the motion cleared by October 31, citing it as the deadline for the detention of Kim Keon-hee (the wife of ousted former leader Yoon Suk-yeol, who was impeached and is currently imprisoned).

The argument from the Rebuilding Korea Party is that the evidence is clear enough for the Constitutional Court to reach a decision within two months. This represents a rare and dangerous escalation: a direct attempt by legislative power to unseat the head of the third branch of government. While the move resonates with supporters who demand a total "cleansing" of judicial power structures left over from previous conservative administrations, it is a high-stakes gamble that risks a massive political backlash.

In contrast, the DPK leadership, led by Floor Leader Han Byeong-do, remained visibly cautious. Accepting the motion without immediate commitment, Han signaled that the party would discuss it "thoroughly." The DPK is clearly wary of the political fallout; they prefer a more methodical approach, favoring a parliamentary investigation after the current audit concludes to build a solid legal foundation before pursuing such a nuclear option. The PPP, meanwhile, has hit back hard, accusing the DPK of attempting to "tame" the judiciary to shield President Lee Jae-myung from potential legal scrutiny.

The Battle for Accountability: Who Speaks at the Audit?

The National Assembly’s Steering Committee, which oversees the presidential office, saw a complete breakdown in cooperation on October 21. During a plenary session, the DPK pushed through a motion for witness testimonies for the upcoming audit of the Blue House (the presidential office). However, they conspicuously excluded key figures demanded by the opposition, including Presidential Senior Secretary for Political Affairs Kang Hoon-sik and the Director of Policy, Kim Yong-beom.

The PPP reacted with fury, with its committee representative, Lee Sang-hwi, labeling the move a deliberate cover-up. The opposition argues that the core function of the parliamentary audit is to monitor the "core of power," and that excluding the President's closest aides renders the exercise toothless. The debate devolved into a shouting match, with accusations of "pathetic language" and demands for resignations flying across the room, effectively burying any chance of meaningful policy review for the day.

Cracks in the Progressive Bloc: Cho Kuk vs. The DPK

Beyond the partisan divide, a new fault line has emerged within the broader progressive coalition. Cho Kuk, leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, has launched a stinging critique of the ruling DPK’s recent policy shifts. Specifically, Cho has publicly attacked the DPK’s consideration of delaying taxes on cryptocurrency gains and its inclusion of exemptions to the "52-hour work week" in proposed regional development legislation.

Cho argues that these moves are "political engineering"—sacrificing core progressive principles of tax justice and labor rights simply to win over voters or appease corporate interests. By positioning his party as the guardian of ideological purity, Cho has forced the DPK into an uncomfortable corner. The ruling party, currently juggling the responsibilities of governance and economic management, now faces internal pressure to prove that it hasn’t abandoned its reformist roots for the sake of electoral convenience.

Looking Ahead: The Cost of Stagnation

As the audit period draws to a close, the question remains: is this political infighting actually serving the public? President Lee Jae-myung’s focus on housing supply and market stability is intended to provide a counter-narrative of a "government that works." Yet, as long as the National Assembly remains trapped in a cycle of impeachment threats, witness boycotts, and ideological infighting, the legislative output necessary to sustain that growth remains uncertain.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on whether the DPK moves forward with an official "parliamentary investigation" into the judiciary. If they do, it will signal that the party has decided the political risk of attacking the Chief Justice is worth the potential gain. Additionally, monitor the DPK’s response to the labor and tax critiques from the Rebuilding Korea Party; if they fold under the pressure of their smaller ally, it could lead to significant legislative instability for the Lee Jae-myung administration moving into the end of the year.

Politics is the art of managing the weight of reality, but in Seoul, the weight of the past and the maneuvering for the future seem to be eclipsing the needs of the present. Whether this intense period of oversight results in genuine reform or simply more gridlock will define the political landscape for the coming months.
