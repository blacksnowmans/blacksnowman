The End of Traditional Media Hegemony: How YouTube Overtook South Korea’s Airwaves

September 24, 2026

The media landscape in South Korea has undergone a seismic shift. For decades, the nation’s airwaves were dominated by a handful of powerful terrestrial broadcasters—the traditional gatekeepers of public discourse. Today, that hierarchy has crumbled, replaced by a digital-first reality where a YouTube channel, operating out of a private studio, commands more public trust than the long-standing giants of the industry.

The 2026 Media Trust Survey, a joint study by SisaIN (a prominent current affairs magazine) and Gallup Korea, provided the definitive proof of this power shift. In this influential poll, now in its 20th year, Kim Ou-joon’s “Humility is Hard, News Factory”—a digital program—claimed the top spot with a 6.3% trust rating. It outperformed legacy titans like MBC’s Newsdesk (5.9%) and KBS’s News9 (4.9%). This result sends a clear message: in the modern era, the size of a broadcasting platform no longer guarantees authority.

The Move of Media Power: When YouTube Swallows Terrestrial TV

This achievement, reached just three years and nine months after the channel's launch, is not merely a "viral success" story. Kim Ou-joon, the creator and host, designed his program specifically to function as "terrestrial television without the transmission tower," intentionally positioning it to compete head-to-head with the likes of MBC and KBS, South Korea’s major public broadcasters. While there are countless YouTube channels globally with larger subscriber bases, the "News Factory" is arguably unique in successfully challenging, and ultimately eclipsing, the national news incumbents in a direct battle for credibility.

This underscores how vulnerable the once-fortified walls of traditional media have become when faced with the weapon of viewer "trust." While some industry analysts view this as a symbolic changing of the guard, the true driver is a deep, unmet public craving for a different kind of "public" discourse. The team behind the channel aims to define itself as the "public broadcaster of the YouTube era," a goal they have carefully cultivated by leveraging years of experience—dating back to the alternative news site Ddanjilbo in 1998.

Meanwhile, the market is scrambling to adapt. Regional MBC affiliates, for instance, are fighting for survival by pivoting to YouTube, often reorganizing their internal structures to report directly to company presidents and scraping by on modest budgets—often less than $70,000 (100 million won). Yet, while most regional outlets struggle to gain traction with new channels, the "News Factory" has mastered the art of narrative-driven content, proving that success requires more than just moving to a digital platform; it requires the "power of truth" and the efficacy of an alternative press.

Trapped in Tribalism: The Erosion of Shared Reality

Beneath these shifting statistics lies a more troubling reality. According to the 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer, 74% of South Koreans admit they do not trust individuals whose values differ from their own. In this environment, the question "Is this media outlet honest or competent?" has been replaced by the more divisive: "Is this media outlet on our side?" Political tribalism, reinforced by consumption algorithms, has created a society where citizens live under the same sky but inhabit completely different realities.

This has led to the paradoxical rise of both trust and distrust toward the same institutions. MBC, for example, is simultaneously ranked as both the most trusted and the most distrusted news organization in the country. Political parties have mastered the art of weaponizing this divide, preferring to fuel anger to consolidate their base rather than seek consensus. Media outlets often serve as the speakers for this rage, leaving citizens trapped in echo chambers of their own making. A society where we refuse to acknowledge the legitimacy of the "other's" truth is a society where democratic collaboration becomes a biological impossibility.

The Price of Wrongdoing: Accountability in the Courts

The crisis of trust extends beyond media into the heart of the judicial system. Currently, the Seoul Central District Court is handling the trials of military figures accused of involvement in an insurrection. The prosecution has recommended 30-year sentences for figures like Yeo In-hyung and Lee Jin-woo. However, the case of former Special Warfare Commander Kwak Jong-geun stands in stark contrast. By admitting his charges and apologizing to the public, he has prompted an unusual show of leniency from the families of the victims of the 1980 Gwangju Uprising (the 5.18 movement), who petitioned for his mercy, suggesting that if more officers had acted like him, the tragic loss of life could have been prevented.

This highlights a shift in how society views accountability: it is not just the crime, but the refusal to acknowledge the truth that earns the most severe social condemnation.

The Ethics of Expense Reports

The judiciary itself is currently facing a self-inflicted blow to its credibility. Revelations regarding the professional expenses of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae have caused public outcry. Reports indicate questionable expenditures, including about $70,000 (100 million won) spent on wine, and instances of "inflating" guest numbers for restaurant visits—such as claiming 38 people attended a meeting in a space that physically could not accommodate them. When a total of roughly $180,000 (260 million won) in taxpayer funds is managed with such "accounting errors," it raises a fundamental question: how can the judiciary demand that the public respect the law when its leaders treat public funds with such structural negligence?

🔥 Where Has the Judiciary's Trust Gone?

The law is meant to be equal for all, but if those who wield it view expense reporting as an opportunity to "game the system," the authority of the law itself dies. "Inflating guest numbers" is not a simple accounting glitch; it is an act of breach of trust that misappropriates taxpayer money. When the head of the judiciary displays such a disconnect from the public’s moral standards, the expectation that the courts will deliver justice begins to wither.

The Path Forward: Political Reset and the Burden of Proof

President Lee Jae-myung’s recent 100-minute press conference was seen by many as a successful, if unexpected, performance. By avoiding excuses and acknowledging his responsibilities, he managed to stop the bleeding in his approval ratings, which rebounded to 34.8% after ten weeks of decline. However, the real challenge lies in winning back the 25% of voters who remain skeptical, saying they liked him in the past but not now. Success for this administration will not come from rhetorical flair, but from tangible progress in reviving the economy and establishing a fair, consistent rule of law.

Furthermore, as experts like Professor Han Moon-do have warned, the government’s diplomatic strategy needs an upgrade. Moving from a defensive, "wait-and-see" posture to a proactive stance in relations with the U.S. and in the push toward a peace regime on the Korean Peninsula is critical. Diplomacy is an art form; it requires the courage to put one's cards on the table, not just the willingness to follow the lead of others.

Editor’s Reflection: The Courage to Listen

Watching the press conference, I felt both the crushing weight of the isolation a leader must feel and the absolute necessity of reaching out across the political divide. Our society is splintering, and the conviction that "only my side is truthful" is choking the life out of our community. We must move beyond the obsession with being "right" and focus on the messy, difficult process of compromise.

The rise of YouTube over terrestrial media is a phenomenon, but it carries a warning. If these new platforms simply become the next generation of partisan echo chambers, we have gained nothing. The democratic process is not about one side winning; it is about different "truths" clashing and breaking until they form a broader, shared reality. The President’s decisions matter, but the real work starts with us: the willingness to open our ears and accept that a healthy democracy requires us to live alongside those we disagree with.

Trust is not something to be seized; it is something to be built together.
