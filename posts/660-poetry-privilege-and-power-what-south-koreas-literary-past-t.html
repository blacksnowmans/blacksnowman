Poetry, Privilege, and Power: What South Korea's Literary Past Tells Us About the Psychology of Elites

October 04, 2026

Staring at a utility bill or stressing over a few dollars in parking fees is an unglamorous, universally humbling experience. It is the quiet, grinding anxiety of ordinary life that keeps most people tethered to survival, worrying about petty expenses while grand political dramas play out on television screens far above them. Yet, in South Korea, a recent resurgence of interest in mid-20th-century literature has sparked a much deeper conversation about how these very anxieties connect to the mechanics of power, inequality, and human corruption.

By pairing the works of iconic South Korean poet Kim Soo-young with modern sociological experiments, commentators have been re-examining a persistent question: What happens to human morality when people are given wealth or authority? Far from being just an academic literary review, the discussion touches raw nerves in a society intensely familiar with rapid modernization, fierce economic competition, and the corrupting pull of political office.

Poetry for the Petty and the Powerful

Kim Soo-young, who lived through the tumultuous upheaval of the April 19 Revolution of 1960—the mass student-led uprisings that toppled South Korea’s autocratic First Republic—did not write untouchable, sterile verse. His work confronted hypocrisy, cowardice, and the small-minded compromises of everyday survival. In poems like Exiting the Ancient Palace One Day, Kim famously skewered the intellectual who rages against abstract historical injustices while remaining safely silent in the face of immediate, petty grievances, yet breaks down in anger over trivial personal inconveniences.

This stark honesty strikes a chord because it reflects the universal guilt of the struggling citizen. People want to see themselves as noble fighters for justice, but the daily pressure of household debt, inflation, and bills often reduces them to exhausted pragmatists worrying about the next paycheck. Kim's genius, and the insight of those reflecting on his work today, lies in the paradox that grand historical changes are rarely driven by flawless, towering heroes. Instead, the great turning points—such as the April 19 Revolution—were fueled by ordinary, terrified, petty people who simply reached a breaking point against injustice and stepped out onto the streets with shaking hands.

In a society that often cycles between cynicism about politics and despair over economic inequality, looking back at these ordinary revolutionaries offers a grounding perspective. The daily anxieties of making ends meet do not invalidate a person; rather, the collective conscience of ordinary citizens remains the true anchor of society.

The Wealth Paradox: Luxury Cars and the Rise of the 'Social Sociopath'

If ordinary people are grounded by their vulnerabilities, privilege often operates in the exact opposite direction. Discussing the book Humankind: A Hopeful History, commentators have pointed to real-world behavioral experiments conducted in the United States that examine the relationship between class and basic empathy.

In these studies, researchers observed drivers at pedestrian crossings. When participants drove modest, older vehicles—such as an affordable compact car—they reliably stopped to yield to pedestrians and followed traffic regulations. However, when the very same individuals were placed behind the wheel of luxury vehicles, their behavior shifted dramatically, with a marked increase in reckless driving and a disregard for right-of-way rules. Notably, repeat studies in the U.S. found that luxury car owners, particularly drivers of high-end imports like BMWs, consistently ranked among the worst offenders for ignoring basic traffic courtesy.

This phenomenon points directly to the concept of the "social sociopath." Sociologists use the term to describe individuals who appear perfectly charming, reasonable, and kind when they are in subordinate positions or ordinary circumstances, but transform entirely the moment they are handed even a fraction of authority or status. The accumulation of wealth and privilege dulls the capacity for empathy, fostering an illusion that one's success is entirely self-made and that everyone else is merely an obstacle on the road.

This dynamic is visible far beyond the highway. In workplaces, local organizations, and national politics, even the smallest title or badge can trigger an innate desire to control and dominate others. The tragedy of modern capitalism and concentrated power is how systematically it strips away the rich's ability to relate to the struggles of ordinary people.

Retaining Humility at the Top

This brings the discussion to the heavy burden of political leadership in South Korea, specifically reflecting on the presidency of Moon Jae-in, who served from 2017 to 2022. Governing a democracy after years of authoritarian trauma and acute polarization requires an almost superhuman restraint. In a political landscape where factions demand total victory, compromise is often treated as treason, and the temptations of executive overreach are immense, maintaining democratic norms is an exhausting, lonely path.

Moon's administration navigated a deeply fractured political environment, attempting to adhere to procedural legitimacy despite relentless pressure from entrenched prosecutorial and media cartels. While no government is without flaws, the deliberate choice not to weaponize state power for personal or factional vengeance stood as a rare exercise in restraint. In an era where political power is frequently treated as private property to be exploited for tribal gain, the quiet dignity of a leader trying to remain tethered to the common sense of ordinary citizens serves as a stark contrast.

True competence in a democracy is not merely about achieving aggressive, top-down outcomes through brute force. It is found in the humility to limit one's own power and listen to the muffled voices of the marginalized.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea continues to navigate intense political polarization, corporate-dominated markets, and widening social divides, the lessons from Kim Soo-young's poetry and modern behavioral sociology remain intensely relevant. Observers should monitor several key developments:

  • Shifts in Political Accountability: Watch how current and emerging political figures handle institutional checks and balances, particularly regarding the use of prosecutorial and investigative powers against political rivals.
  • Public Discourse on Inequality: Pay attention to how cultural and literary themes addressing wealth, class divide, and elite empathy resonate in mainstream media and best-selling non-fiction.
  • Grassroots Civic Engagement: Observe whether public frustration with institutional corruption translates into constructive civic movements or deepens societal cynicism and apathy.

Ultimately, the enduring relevance of Korea's literary heritage is its reminder that societies are not sustained by the flashy leaves at the top of the tree, but by the deep, unglamorous roots buried in the soil. As long as ordinary people retain their capacity for empathy and refuse to let power turn them into monsters, the foundation remains intact.
