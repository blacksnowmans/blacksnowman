The "Korea Discount": Why Record Profits Aren't Saving the Seoul Stock Market

October 12, 2026

For the average South Korean retail investor—often called "ants" (gaemi)—there is a special kind of frustration that comes from watching your portfolio bleed red while the companies you own report record-breaking profits. It is a scene playing out across trading screens nationwide: blue-chip giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the bedrock of the nation's economy, are seeing their stock prices crater despite impressive earnings. The common sentiment among retail investors is one of betrayal: Why is the market turning on companies that are performing at the top of their game?

The "Korea Discount"—a long-standing phenomenon where South Korean stocks trade at significantly lower valuations than their global peers—has persisted through the tenure of the disgraced former leader, the incarcerated Yoon Suk-yeol, and remains a crushing weight under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung. To understand why, one must look past the headlines and into the structural rot that defines the South Korean capital market.

The Great Disconnect: Profits Up, Valuations Down

In mid-October, the KOSPI (the Korea Composite Stock Price Index) suffered a staggering 3.65% drop, wiping out massive amounts of value as foreign investors dumped over $1.5 billion (2 trillion KRW) worth of shares in a single session. The KOSDAQ, which typically houses smaller, tech-heavy firms, fared even worse, with investors feeling the sting of a 5% decline.

Even companies posting stellar earnings, such as Celltrion, are barely holding their ground, while titans like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix have seen their share prices plummet by over 4%. This is not a reaction to a singular corporate scandal or a massive economic crisis, but a symptom of a systemic loss of confidence. Global financial outlets like Bloomberg have begun to question the very relevance of the South Korean market, noting that despite a total market capitalization of roughly $4.3 trillion (5,700 trillion KRW), the daily trading volume has evaporated—dropping by 70% compared to the highs of last spring.

The core of the issue is that South Korea is viewed by global investors not as a growth engine, but as a "playground" for short-term speculation. While international firms like Nvidia or Micron have seen rallies, Korean semiconductor giants remain stagnant. Foreign investors are increasingly wary of the extreme volatility, high barriers to entry, and a market structure that seems designed to prioritize insiders over shareholders.

The "Predatory" Corporate Governance Model

The true culprit behind the Korea Discount is an entrenched, systemic bias that favors controlling families—the chaebols—at the expense of minority shareholders. A recurring pattern in the Korean market is the "high-price sale of management control, low-price issuance of new shares" strategy.

When a majority shareholder wants to offload their position, they often demand a high "management premium," selling their stake at a significant markup. Once the new buyer assumes control, they often move to dilute the shares of existing minority investors by issuing new shares to themselves at heavily discounted, "below-market" prices. This is a double hit for the retail investor: their original investment is devalued by the shift in control, and then further diluted by the new share issuance.

Recent cases have highlighted this predatory cycle:

  • Lotte Rental: The parent company sold a 56% stake at $58 per share (77,000 KRW), but the very next day, the company issued new shares to the buyer at just $22 per share (29,000 KRW).
  • Kakao Games: Similar tactics were used to transfer control while leaving minority shareholders holding the bag with significantly diluted equity.

These practices, while technically operating within the current legal framework, are widely viewed as a form of state-sanctioned theft. It is no wonder that international investors view the South Korean market with cynicism; in a system where the "owners" treat the company as their private piggy bank and treat public shareholders as short-term targets, long-term institutional investment is impossible.

The Path Forward: Reform or Irrelevance

The current state of the market is not a fate set in stone, but a policy failure that can be corrected. The most frequently cited success story is Japan, where the Tokyo Stock Exchange worked with the government and pension funds to enforce rigorous corporate governance reforms. By mandating transparency and focusing on shareholder value, Japan successfully lured back foreign capital.

For South Korea, the path to recovery rests on three pillars:

  • Amending the Commercial Code: Currently, corporate directors owe a fiduciary duty only to the "company," which is often interpreted as the controlling family. Expanding this duty to include "the company and its shareholders" is a vital first step.
  • Mandatory Tender Offers: To stop the predatory practice of bypassing minority shareholders during management changes, South Korea must introduce mandatory tender offer rules that ensure all shareholders are treated equally.
  • Long-term Stewardship: Domestic institutional investors and pension funds must move away from short-term profit chasing and hold companies accountable for long-term growth and governance.

The Lee Jae-myung administration faces a critical test. As AI and global platform giants redefine the global economy, the underlying value of a market still depends on the integrity of its rules. If the government fails to protect the "ants" from the predatory practices of the few, the Korea Discount will remain, and the capital market will continue to wither.

What to Watch Next: Keep a close eye on the National Assembly's debates regarding the Commercial Code reform. If the bill to expand the fiduciary duty of directors moves forward, it will be the strongest signal yet that the government is serious about dismantling the structures that have held the Korean market back for decades. Without meaningful legislative change, the market will likely remain trapped in a cycle of volatility and stagnation.
