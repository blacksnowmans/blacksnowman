Beyond the Mudfight: How Political Feuds and Tax Tweaks Overshadow South Korea's Real Crises

September 05, 2026

Turn on the news in South Korea, and you are immediately plunged into a theater of perpetual outrage. Step back just a little bit, and the sheer pettiness of it all becomes suffocating. For outside observers, following South Korean politics often feels like trying to decipher a fast-moving, high-stakes soap opera where the plot changes daily, yet the underlying theme never does: rival factions locked in total war, weaponizing scandals, media outlets chasing clicks with sensational headlines, and the actual, pressing concerns of everyday citizens entirely lost in the shuffle. But beneath the noise lies a deeper institutional sickness—one where governance has been replaced by psychological warfare, and where tax policies and judicial processes are bent to serve partisan survival rather than the public good.

Text Messages and Manufactured Scandals: The Weaponization of Routine Communication

Consider the recent firestorm surrounding a text message sent by Kim Seung-won, a lawmaker from the main opposition Democratic Party, to Kim Gang-lip, former head of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. Conservative critics and select media outlets quickly seized upon the exchange, framing it as undeniable evidence of a backroom deal or inappropriate political lobbying on behalf of pharmaceutical interests. The narrative was pushed aggressively: powerful politicians leaning on regulators to fast-track favors.

Yet, the reality—as is so often the case in these manufactured dramas—tells a vastly different story. During prosecutorial questioning, the former food and drug safety chief himself drew a clear line, stating that the communication was well within the bounds of normal duties for a member of parliament. Furthermore, actual data showed that the clinical trial approval process in question was actually delayed compared to the national average. Facts, however, matter very little in a mudfight. The mere existence of a text message was weaponized to demonize a lawmaker, chilling legitimate policy communication and turning routine legislative oversight into a toxic political trap.

This tactics-first approach is hardly new, but it has grown increasingly vicious. Figures like Han Dong-hoon, a prominent conservative politician and former justice minister, have frequently leaned into sensationalist framing—such as invoking lurid barroom tropes—to drag political debates away from policy substance and down into the gutter of tabloid gossip. Mainstream conservative papers like the Chosun Ilbo and DongA-Ilbo eagerly amplify these narratives because outrage guarantees readership. In South Korea's hyper-polarized media ecosystem, who can throw the lowest punch is far more profitable than who can offer the best solution.

Real Estate and Tax Cuts: Who Benefits When the Rules Are Relaxed?

While politicians and media outlets trade insults over text messages, consequential policy shifts are happening in the background—often with troubling implications for economic equality. The ruling bloc’s recent push for property tax reform is a prime example. The government proposed raising the threshold for the comprehensive real estate holding tax (often called the comprehensive real estate tax) on single-home owners from 1.2 billion won (about $900,000) to 1.4 billion won (about $1.05 million).

To an outside observer, this might sound like a simple technical adjustment to keep pace with inflation or housing price growth. But in South Korea’s hyper-sensitive housing market, real estate is the ultimate flashpoint of social mobility and inequality. The comprehensive real estate tax was originally introduced to curb asset polarization and enforce tax equity among ultra-wealthy property owners. By relaxing these standards, the government is effectively sending a signal that asset holders deserve relief, inviting fierce criticism that the policy is a giveaway to the wealthy that risks reigniting speculative demand.

The optics of the move are particularly painful given recent economic history. South Korea has repeatedly learned—through multiple boom-and-bust cycles across successive administrations—that treating the economy simply as a matter of cutting taxes for asset holders only widens the gap between the haves and have-nots. When the state prioritizes protecting high-value real estate over the economic stability of the broader working public, it breeds a deep sense of alienation. Moving the tax threshold by 200 million won is not just a fiscal adjustment; it acts as a psychological shield for speculators, widening the chasm between ordinary citizens and the property-owning elite.

Internal Fractures and the Cost of Unquestioning Loyalty

The dysfunction is not limited to inter-party warfare; it is eating away at the internal structures of the political parties themselves. Recent tensions within the Democratic Party—highlighted by the resignation of Lee Seok-yeon, a former chairman of the National Integration Committee—have dragged complex internal power dynamics into the open. The fierce factional battles surrounding the leadership of party head Lee Jae-myung illustrate a party increasingly consumed by internal turf wars rather than forward-looking reform.

When Lee Seok-yeon stepped down and lamented that he had become a "stumbling block," it laid bare a troubling reality: the party apparatus is becoming increasingly closed off to internal dissent. Healthy political parties require internal debate and ideological friction. But when integration is demanded as absolute conformity—a rigid "follow my lead" mentality—it shuts out alternative perspectives and alienates moderate voters.

On the other side of the debate, academic and progressive defenders view figures like Lee Jae-myung not merely as politicians, but as instruments for broader systemic reform. This fundamental divide—between seeing a leader as an indispensable vehicle for structural change versus viewing them as a lightning rod for factional self-preservation—defines the current left-leaning camp. Yet, as the energy of the party is funneled into "protecting the leader" and purging internal critics, the core promises of reform fade away, leaving supporters exhausted by endless defensive posture.

The Criminalization of Politics: The prosecution's handling of these investigations reveals an unsettling trend. Even when prosecutors conclude that bribery charges lack merit, they frequently attach political funds violations or other suspended indictments. This is not about pursuing strict legal justice; it is about leaving a permanent political scar—a tool of systemic neutralization disguised as the rule of law.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this turbulent political landscape, several concrete developments will signal whether the country is headed toward deeper polarization or a course correction:

  • The Progress of Property Legislation: Watch how opposition parties handle the ruling bloc's real estate tax amendments in the National Assembly. Will they use it as leverage for broader welfare concessions, or will it pass with quiet compromises behind closed doors?
  • Internal Party Realignment Ahead of Local Elections: Pay close attention to how figures critical of the Democratic Party leadership navigate their exit or attempts at internal reform. Their ability to organize—or lack thereof—will dictate whether a viable moderate-reform alternative emerges.
  • Judicial Rulings and Political Fallout: Several ongoing investigations and trials involving high-profile political figures are reaching critical junctures. How the courts rule—and how political actors spin those rulings—will set the tone for the national conversation leading into future election cycles.

Ultimately, the tragedy of contemporary South Korean politics is not merely that politicians fight dirty, but that the public is constantly invited to cheer for one side of the mudslinging. Until voters and politicians alike demand a return to substantive policy debates over sensationalist theater, the country will remain trapped in a cycle where everyone fights for power, and nobody governs.
