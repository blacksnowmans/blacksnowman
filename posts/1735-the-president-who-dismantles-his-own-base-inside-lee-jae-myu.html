The President Who Dismantles His Own Base: Inside Lee Jae-myung’s War for the Ruling Party

October 30, 2026

When Rhyu Si-min, South Korea’s preeminent progressive author, veteran intellectual, and former cabinet minister, recently raised a startling hypothesis on political airwaves, it sent shockwaves through Yeouido, Seoul’s political district. Rhyu warned that President Lee Jae-myung is methodically executing what amounts to the dismantling of his own progressive camp. More intriguingly, Rhyu remarked that while he had figured out why the president was doing this, he lacked the definitive smoking-gun evidence required to declare it publicly. In the coded language of high-stakes Korean factional warfare, an intellectual of Rhyu’s stature claiming he cannot speak for lack of hard proof does not signify doubt. It means the battle lines have already hardened behind closed doors.

For outside observers, the spectacle of a sitting head of state actively undermining the traditional identity of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) looks baffling. Typically, presidents consolidate their founding coalition to weather legislative friction and public discontent. Yet Lee’s political maneuvers reveal a vastly different calculus: a calculated drive to cleanse the party of its historic loyalties, silence internal dissent, and remake the ruling organization around his personal survival and an ideological cohort forged decades ago outside the mainstream establishment.

The Ghosts of Seongnam: The Radical Fringe at the Democratic Core

To grasp why President Lee seems so alienated from the mainstream traditions of his own party, one must revisit the early 2010s. Lee did not rise through the traditional ranks of South Korea’s student democracy movement of the 1980s, nor was he nurtured within the genteel reformist circles surrounding the late former President Roh Moo-hyun or former President Moon Jae-in. Instead, Lee’s springboard was local government in Seongnam, a satellite city south of Seoul, where he secured the mayoralty through an electoral coalition with radical leftist organizations.

Chief among these forces was the so-called Eastern Gyeonggi Coalition (Gyeonggi Dongbu Yeonhap), a notoriously secretive faction with roots in the National Liberation faction of student activism, once epitomized by figures like Lee Seok-ki inside the now-dissolved Unified Progressive Party (UPP). Mainstream progressives still remember the 2012 UPP crisis, when this faction engaged in blatant primary vote-rigging, leveled accusations of internal sabotage against any critics, and physically assaulted party leaders—including Rhyu Si-min himself on a public stage—to retain control of the party’s steering apparatus.

For years, mainstream liberal voters tolerated Lee Jae-myung’s abrasive edge because they had grown frustrated with the perceived timidity of Roh and Moon, whose administrations repeatedly promised sweeping judicial and social reforms only to retreat under conservative pushback. Lee presented himself as the anti-establishment brawler: a direct, unforgiving "cider" politician who promised instant gratification. Accompanied by flagship policy slogans like basic income, Lee provided the radical faction an institutional vehicle to enter the main bloodstream of the Democratic Party. What began as a transactional alliance in Seongnam gradually metastasized into a complete takeover bid for the nation's dominant liberal apparatus.

Purging the Old Guard: Why the Pro-Moon Base is Viewed as the Ultimate Threat

The deepest animosity inside the ruling party today is not between President Lee and the conservative opposition. Rather, it is the subterranean feud between Lee’s inner circle and the historic base of the party: the loyalists of former President Moon Jae-in and the late Roh Moo-hyun. This division was laid bare during previous primary battles, where deep mutual distrust pushed some pro-Moon voters to break ranks and support conservative candidates, including during the turbulent political era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol before his eventual impeachment and imprisonment.

For President Lee, this internal resentment is compounded by acute anxiety over his post-presidential future. Haunted by long-running legal battles and corruption investigations, Lee and his strategists operate under an existential dread: the conviction that if pro-Moon moderates retain their organizational grip on the Democratic Party, they will willingly hand him over to prosecutors the moment his presidential term concludes. In Lee’s eyes, the party’s original core represents a far more treacherous threat than the conservative People Power Party.

Consequently, Lee’s apparatus has turned the tactics of factional elimination against its own colleagues:

  • Targeting independent popular figures: Lawmaker Jung Chung-rae, a fiery grassroots favorite, has been subjected to coordinated demonization by hardline pro-Lee online networks. Yet during the party convention, Jung stunned the leadership by capturing 46% of the vote—proof that nearly half the party’s active membership remains deeply skeptical of Lee's total dominance.
  • Sidelining veteran mediators: Former party leaders and senior statesmen such as Choo Mi-ae—who once provided Lee crucial institutional cover when primary challenges threatened his political viability—have been systematically shunted aside or pushed toward provincial perches to dilute their influence.
  • Floating erratic mergers: Faced with stiff resistance from party chairman Kim Min-seok and independent power brokers, presidential operatives floated the prospect of a snap merger with the minor left-wing Rebuilding Korea Party, attempting to dilute traditional party members with outside hardliners.
"The factional tactics that once shattered the far-left Unified Progressive Party—labeling any dissenter a saboteur or a traitor—are being re-enacted inside the ruling Democratic Party today."

A Toxic Sugar Rush: Courting the Online Right at Progressivism’s Expense

When ideological control fails, political strategists often reach for populism. To offset his fraying standing among traditional progressive voters, President Lee has embarked on a bizarre tactical flirtation with young, anti-feminist, and anti-immigrant internet spaces, such as the male-dominated online forum FM Korea (Femco).

This strategy was put on stark display when Lee openly questioned state support for multicultural families and declared that foreign agricultural workers "do not contribute significantly to the Korean economy." The remarks sparked immediate outrage across civil society and progressive civic groups. Presidential spokespersons hurried to spin the statements as an inquiry into the fair allocation of post-natal care subsidies, but the damage was done. The president had deployed the blunt language of right-wing grievance politics from the Blue House podium.

A fitting metaphor for this approach is mixing two packets of cheap, ultra-sweet instant coffee into a bottle of refined cold brew. Add one packet, and the sweetness is lost against the bitterness of the roast; add two, and the overwhelming blast of refined sugar punches straight through, delivering what critics call an "expensive cheap taste." That initial sugar high produces an immediate rush of online engagement, but over time it ruins the brew and poisons the constitution. By chasing viral, anti-immigrant talking points to peel away young conservative voters, the administration is discarding decades of progressive human rights and labor values for a fleeting blip in the polls.

Even staunch confidants are showing signs of exhaustion. Senior lawmaker Jung Sung-ho, a longtime friend and ally dating back to their days at the Judicial Research and Training Institute, has publicly counseled the president to stop venting his unvarnished personal thoughts on social media platforms like X. When figures who know the president intimately begin advising him to put down his phone and step back from impulsive public interventions, it signals a deeper institutional fraying within the executive branch.

What to Watch Next

President Lee Jae-myung’s bid to replace the Democratic Party’s founding soul with a personal defense mechanism is approaching a critical juncture. The friction between an unyielding presidential faction and a resilient party base cannot remain quiet indefinitely. Observers should track three critical indicators over the coming months:

  • The 46% Resistance: Watch how Lawmaker Jung Chung-rae and non-aligned lawmakers organize inside the National Assembly. If nearly half the party base is willing to defy the presidential faction in internal votes, any sign of executive weakness could trigger an open rebellion.
  • Rhyu Si-min’s Escalation: Rhyu remains a bellwether for traditional progressive conscience. If he chooses to present the "evidence" he hinted at regarding the systematic dismantling of the party, it could grant mainstream liberals the moral authorization to split cleanly from the administration.
  • The Limits of Reactionary Populism: Pay close attention to how the presidential office navigates cultural and labor policy. Further rhetoric targeting migrant workers or social minorities in pursuit of internet applause will widen the breach with civic organizations, labor unions, and religious progressives who historically formed the backbone of the party's election machinery.

South Korea’s ruling party is not an easily digestible activist cadre; it is a sprawling, deeply rooted political institution built over decades of democratic struggle. By attempting to dismantle that legacy to secure personal impunity, President Lee may find that breaking the vessel that carried him to power leaves him entirely exposed when the political weather turns.
