Inside the People Power Party's Rupture: Why South Korea's Main Opposition Is Turning Its Fire Inward

November 28, 2026

On November 27, thirty-two separate news headlines in South Korea converged on a single story: the country's main conservative opposition party had stopped talking to itself. Inside the People Power Party (PPP), which holds more than 100 seats in the 300-member National Assembly, party leader Jang Dong-hyeok drew a hard line against his predecessor, Han Dong-hoon, who has been seeking to rejoin the party after being hit with disciplinary sanctions. According to a November 27 political briefing compiling reports from South Korean outlets including Yonhap News TV, Dailian, and OhmyNews, Jang told Han to "resolve it legally" and warned that even if a court suspended the original penalty to allow Han's return, the leadership could open fresh disciplinary proceedings against him.

Hours later, Jang went further, declaring he would boycott the party's scheduled December 2 general assembly of lawmakers—known in Korean politics as an uichong—saying there was "no reason to go and listen to remarks." For international observers trying to follow South Korean politics under President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the clash looks less like a routine policy dispute and more like an existential battle over who controls the conservative movement ahead of future party leadership and presidential contests. As the Dong-A Ilbo noted in an editorial cited in the briefing, a party consumed by pointing fingers both inside and outside its ranks risks losing its basic function as a political counterweight in parliament.

A Legal Wall Against Han Dong-hoon's Return

To understand why a single reinstatement request has paralyzed South Korea's largest opposition party, it helps to look at the figures involved. Han Dong-hoon, the former PPP leader, retains a substantial following among party members and aligned lawmakers—referred to in South Korean media as the "pro-Han faction," which includes more than 10 vocal parliamentarians. Jang Dong-hyeok, the current party chair, leads the mainstream camp now steering the PPP's organizational machinery.

As reported by the Korea Daily (Miju JoongAng Ilbo) and summarized in the November 27 briefing, Jang has stated that his opposition to Han's reinstatement remains unchanged. His specific legal argument is striking: even if a court of first instance or another judicial body suspends the effect of Han's existing disciplinary punishment on procedural grounds, Jang contends that the underlying grounds for discipline would not disappear, leaving the leadership free to pursue a separate round of sanctions.

Critics within the party and media commentators describe this approach as erecting "multiple locked doors" around the PPP—combining Ethics Committee penalties with restrictive interpretations of party rules to delay or condition any review of Han's return indefinitely. Political analysts cited by KBC Gwangju Broadcasting have gone so far as to suggest that under these conditions, Han might find it more realistic to prepare an independent new party than to fight his way back into the PPP. Supporters of Jang's firm stance, by contrast, frame the dispute around legal and procedural finality rather than political compromise, insisting that party discipline cannot simply be waved away by factional pressure.

The Caucus Boycott and Allegations of "Fabricated Discipline"

The standoff is set to collide directly with the PPP's internal legislative forum on December 2. In South Korea's National Assembly, a party's general assembly of lawmakers serves as its primary democratic venue for debating strategy, airing dissent, and building consensus among elected representatives. Jang's decision to skip the December 2 meeting—captured by OhmyNews as creating an atmosphere of looming confrontation—has been seized upon by pro-Han lawmakers as proof that internal communication has broken down.

"Make sure to attend and take responsibility." — Representative Bae Hyun-jin, urging PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok to appear at the December 2 lawmakers' assembly, as cited in South Korean media reports.

According to Yonhap News TV, pro-Han lawmakers have labeled the December 2 gathering Jang's "last chance," with some going so far as to characterize his leadership style as reckless and unprincipled. At the same time, the rhetoric on the anti-Jang side has drawn criticism of its own: Representative Bae Hyun-jin sparked controversy by making personal remarks about Jang's demeanor, commenting on what she described as his unusual gaze. As the briefing notes, exchanges of that kind risk reducing a serious debate over procedural legitimacy and party direction into personal mudslinging.

Ethics Committee Disclosures and the Nominations Fight

Beneath the war of words lies a potentially more damaging institutional crisis. Reports in Dailian have highlighted an escalating series of mutual disclosures between Jang Dong-hyeok and members of the party's Ethics Committee over allegations of "fabricated discipline"—claims that specific political intent or procedural distortion tainted the disciplinary action against Han Dong-hoon. Because a party's ethics body is meant to serve as an impartial arbiter, accusations that it functioned as an instrument to remove a political rival strike at the credibility of the PPP's internal governance. While PPP Secretary-General Jeong Hee-yong and other party officials have moved to mediate the dispute, the trust deficit has already widened.

The conflict has also spilled into the sensitive territory of future parliamentary nominations. Jang Ye-chan, a former Youth Supreme Council member, explicitly targeted 10 sitting PPP lawmakers for exclusion from party nominations, describing the current state of affairs as "sickening." In Korean party politics, control over candidate nominations determines political survival, making threats of nomination purges one of the sharpest weapons in factional warfare. According to commentary in the briefing, opposition observers have begun asking whether the current leadership has any coherent electoral strategy at all, as internal battles overshadow efforts to rebuild public support and keep approval ratings from stagnating.

Street Politics, President Lee Jae-myung, and the Insurrection Anniversary

As internal pressure has mounted, Jang Dong-hyeok has sought to pivot outward against President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. Jang declared that "where Lee Jae-myung belongs is prison" and urged South Korean courts to resume trials involving the president. He has also signaled plans to take the party outside parliament for street mobilization on December 3, coinciding with the second anniversary of the December 3 insurrection.

An editorial in the Kyunghyang Shinmun questioned that pivot, arguing that a leader struggling to manage turmoil inside his own party is unlikely to win broader public sympathy by launching street protests. Other voices inside the PPP have tried to split the difference. Lawmakers Yoon Sang-hyun and Woo Jae-joon have publicly raised "Jang Dong-hyeok accountability"—arguing that the party chair bears responsibility for the current crisis—while simultaneously maintaining that President Lee remains the party's "primary enemy." Critics of that framing argue that treating domestic political opponents as enemies to be eliminated leaves little room for legislative negotiation or policy competition.

Meanwhile, the legislative cost of the infighting is mounting. With more than 100 bills awaiting review in the National Assembly, the briefing notes that South Korea's main opposition has been largely absent from debates over inflation and household economic pressures. For ordinary voters—and for outside observers assessing policy predictability in Asia's fourth-largest economy—the concern is that partisan gridlock and factional warfare inside the 300-seat parliament are crowding out substantive governance. With speculation emerging about potential defections or swing votes involving more than 50 seats between Jang's hardline allies and the pro-Han camp, the PPP's internal arithmetic has become directly relevant to national legislative stability.

What to Watch Next

The next few days will determine whether the People Power Party can contain its fracture or whether the conservative bloc splits more formally. Four concrete developments will be critical to watch:

  • The December 2 general assembly of lawmakers: Whether Jang Dong-hyeok reverses his boycott under pressure from Bae Hyun-jin and other lawmakers, and how many of the PPP's roughly 100-plus legislators openly align with the pro-Han faction or echo Yoon Sang-hyun and Woo Jae-joon's calls for leadership accountability.
  • The December 3 mobilization: How Jang executes his planned extra-parliamentary rally on the second anniversary of the December 3 insurrection, and whether rank-and-file conservative lawmakers join him in the streets or distance themselves from hardline rhetoric.
  • The courts and Han Dong-hoon's next move: Whether a court suspends Han's disciplinary penalty, whether Jang's team follows through on its threat to launch a second round of discipline, and whether Han ultimately moves toward forming an independent new party.
  • Further Ethics Committee revelations: Whether Secretary-General Jeong Hee-yong's mediation efforts calm the dispute, or whether additional disclosures confirm or refute the allegations of "fabricated discipline" and deepen calls to overhaul the party leadership.

How these flashpoints resolve will shape not only the future of South Korean conservatism, but also whether the National Assembly can function with a coherent opposition capable of engaging President Lee Jae-myung's administration on legislation and the economy.
