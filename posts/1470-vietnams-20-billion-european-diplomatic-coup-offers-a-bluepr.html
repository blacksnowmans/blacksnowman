Vietnam’s €20 Billion European Diplomatic Coup Offers a Blueprint for South Korea’s Pragmatic Foreign Policy

October 26, 2026

When To Lam, Vietnam’s General Secretary and State President, stepped off his aircraft in Western Europe recently, he was not simply conducting a routine series of courtesy calls. He was executing a carefully calculated diplomatic offensive aimed at reshaping how South East Asia’s fastest-growing manufacturing hub interacts with Western industrial powerhouses. By the end of his tour through Copenhagen, Brussels, and the headquarters of the European Union, Vietnam had systematically elevated its relationships with Denmark, Belgium, and the EU to strategic partnerships—unlocking an estimated €20 billion (roughly $21.7 billion USD) in potential investments, technology transfers, and resource commitments.

For international observers watching East Asian geopolitics, this trip represents more than a victory for Hanoi. It provides a masterclass in how middle-weight industrial nations can leverage strategic autonomy amid worsening superpower friction between Washington and Beijing. In South Korea, policy analysts and market watchers are paying intense attention to Hanoi's maneuvering. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, Seoul has been actively recalibrating its foreign policy toward multi-vector pragmatism. Seeing a neighboring Asian power secure massive high-tech and green capital from Europe offers both a challenge and a validation for South Korea's own diplomatic direction.

Elevating Ties in Brussels and Copenhagen: The Mechanics of To Lam’s European Tour

To fully grasp the magnitude of Vietnam’s diplomatic haul, one must examine the specific stops along To Lam’s itinerary. In Denmark, discussions centered heavily on renewable energy and maritime infrastructure. Denmark home to world-leading offshore wind and green energy developers, sees Vietnam’s thousands of kilometers of coastline as a prime testbed for clean power transition. In Belgium and at EU headquarters in Brussels, the focus shifted toward high-tech logistics, intellectual property protection, and integrating Vietnam into European supply chains.

Diplomatic vocabulary often treats a "relationship upgrade" as mere symbolic fluff, but in international political economy, it functions as a regulatory green light. Formalizing a strategic partnership with the EU means:

  • Deeper regulatory alignment: Streamlining customs, lowering non-tariff barriers, and establishing formal frameworks for foreign direct investment.
  • Secured institutional protection: Providing European capital with legal backing when entering Southeast Asian industrial infrastructure.
  • High-level technology sharing: Facilitating structured joint research and development rather than simple assembly-line offshoring.

Vietnam has long carried geopolitical vulnerabilities, sharing a land and sea border with China while relying on Western markets for export revenue. To Lam’s direct engagement with European leadership demonstrates how a country can build a third pillar of economic security. Rather than forcing itself into a binary choice between American military guarantees and Chinese economic dominance, Hanoi built a robust bridge to Europe's deep capital reserves and technical standards.

Beyond Handshakes: Converting €20 Billion in Capital into Industrial Modernization

The headline figure from the tour—€20 billion—is substantial by any metric. For a developing nation navigating the transition from low-wage assembly to advanced manufacturing, capital of this scale targeted at green infrastructure and technological research is transformative. Vietnam has spent the last decade absorbing low-end manufacturing displaced from China, but its leadership is acutely aware of the "middle-income trap." To sustain long-term growth, it needs high-value industries like semiconductors, clean energy generation, and advanced logistics.

The European agreements focus precisely on these bottlenecks. Partnering with Belgian port operators promises to modernize Vietnam’s maritime shipping networks into regional trade hubs. Simultaneously, Danish green energy investments aim to decarbonize the country’s industrial power grid. This decarbonization is crucial: as the European Union rolls out its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), non-EU exporters will face stiff tariffs unless their manufacturing processes meet strict environmental standards. By bringing Danish green technology straight to its factories, Vietnam is pre-emptively insulating its export economy against future European carbon taxes.

Crucially, Hanoi’s leadership treats these diplomatic wins with operational urgency. Le Hoai Trung, Minister of Foreign Affairs and head of the Party's Central External Relations Commission, stressed that vague diplomatic declarations must immediately translate into concrete local executive action plans. Regulatory overhauls, land-use approvals, and local administrative streamlining are already being updated to turn European capital pledges into physical factories and power grids without delay.

Bamboo Diplomacy Meets Pragmatism: Why Seoul is Paying Close Attention

To understand why Korean political analysts are reading Vietnam's European tour so closely, one must look at South Korea's internal political pivot. Foreign observers often view South Korea through the lens of its domestic disputes, but the shift in foreign policy over the past year has been profound.

During the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached following a historic political crisis and currently remains in prison—South Korea’s foreign policy relied heavily on rigid, ideological alignment with specific Western blocs. Critics argue this value-based strategy needlessly alienated key regional trade partners, diminished South Korea's diplomatic flexibility, and resulted in lost economic opportunities across emerging markets.

In contrast, the administration of President Lee Jae-myung has championed a return to "pragmatic, interest-based diplomacy." President Lee’s doctrine emphasizes that foreign policy must serve a direct economic purpose: securing supply chain resilience, opening export markets, and protecting the economic livelihoods of Korean citizens. From Seoul's perspective, Vietnam’s strategy—famously dubbed "Bamboo Diplomacy" for having deep, unyielding roots combined with flexible branches that bend with global winds—is a practical demonstration of multi-vector balancing.

"In international relations, ideology should remain a tool, not a trap. National interest, economic security, and pragmatic flexibility must always sit at the center of statecraft."

Vietnam is a single-party state that has successfully forged high-level strategic ties with democratic European nations without sacrificing its sovereign autonomy. It did so by offering what European markets desperately need: a reliable, greening, high-capacity manufacturing base outside of traditional geopolitical flashpoints. For South Korea, which hosts global tech titans like Samsung and LG that maintain massive industrial footprints inside Vietnam, Hanoi's rising international status is both an opportunity and a wake-up call.

What to Watch Next in the Korea-Vietnam-EU Dynamics

As the diplomatic fanfare subsides, several key indicators will reveal how this geopolitical shifting reshapes Asian supply chains and South Korea's economic landscape:

  • Implementation of the €20 Billion Framework: Watch how quickly European green energy projects achieve regulatory approval in Vietnam. The speed at which Danish offshore wind developments break ground will test whether Vietnamese bureaucracy can match its diplomatic ambition.
  • Korean Corporate Realignment in Vietnam: South Korean conglomerates maintain immense manufacturing assets in Vietnam. As Hanoi shifts toward European environmental and technological standards, Korean firms operating locally will need to accelerate their own local RE100 (renewable energy 100%) transition to stay aligned with host-country infrastructure.
  • President Lee Jae-myung’s ASEAN Policy Initiatives: Keep an eye on how the Lee administration builds upon South Korea's Southern diplomatic policy. Expect Seoul to propose high-tech triangular cooperation models linking South Korean advanced technology (ICT, batteries, chips) with Vietnam’s upgraded industrial capacity and Europe’s green capital.

To Lam’s European tour proved that middle powers do not need to sit passively on the sidelines of superpower rivalries. By executing a sophisticated foreign policy grounded in economic pragmatism, Vietnam secured its industrial future. For South Korea under President Lee Jae-myung, Hanoi’s success serves as compelling evidence that flexible, interest-driven diplomacy remains the most effective tool for navigating an unpredictable world.
