Black Snowman

Why We Can't Stop Caring What Others Think—And How to Stop

September 11, 2026

Picture yourself standing in front of a mirror, adjusting a collar that is already fine, rehearsing a casual laugh for a gathering you are not even sure you want to attend. Every day, millions of people move through life braced against an invisible wall: the gaze of strangers, colleagues, and friends. We worry that our clothes look wrong, that our words sound careless, or that our silence will be misread. For anyone particularly sensitive to social judgment, this constant calculation turns the self rigid, like a robot programmed never to make a misstep. But the exhausting business of worrying about what others think is rarely about them. It is an ongoing negotiation with how much of our true selves we are willing to accept.

To understand why this happens and how to break the cycle, it helps to look at the teachings of Pomnyun Sunim, a prominent South Korean Buddhist monk and philosopher known for his direct, practical advice on modern mental distress. Through his public lectures and counseling sessions, Pomnyun has spent decades dissecting why modern people feel so chronically surveilled and exhausted by their own social anxiety.

Two Kinds of Social Awareness: Genuine Care Versus Deception

Pomnyun argues that not all social awareness is toxic. To untangle why other people's opinions weigh us down so heavily, we first have to separate healthy consideration from manipulative performance.

The first type of social awareness comes from a place of inner peace. It is the quiet check-in with oneself: "How is the person across from me feeling right now?" or "Am I accidentally making things difficult for them?" This kind of awareness springs from warmth and empathy, and it does not make us miserable. It forms the bedrock of healthy human relationships because it is offered from a place of stability rather than panic. Complete apathetic disregard for others shades easily into arrogance, so noticing those around us is not inherently wrong.

The problem begins with the second type: the desperate desire to look better than we are. This is not consideration; it is deception. We want to appear kinder, smarter, or more capable than our actual baseline. Pomnyun offers a blunt comparison to illustrate this, noting that even putting on makeup is essentially a form of small-scale fabrication designed to bypass reality. We curate our social media profiles, inflate our achievements in job interviews, and sand down our rough edges not to connect, but to pass inspection.

The trap is that these curated versions of ourselves do not just fool the audience—they eventually fool the author. As Pomnyun warns, if you pretend to be kind when you are not, and people continually praise your kindness, you eventually start believing your own press release. That dissonance creates a low-grade, 24-hour panic. When your entire social standing rests on a performance, you cannot afford to drop character for a single second.

The False Advertising of Relationships

This performative impulse is perhaps most visible in romance and dating. We show up to first dates wearing shoes that make us taller, clothes we normally wouldn't touch, and a polished, docile demeanor that bears little resemblance to our actual Sunday morning selves. Relationships that begin with inflated claims almost always run into trouble later.

Imagine two people who meet while each projecting an idealized version of themselves—let us call it a score of 150. Eventually, the daily grind of living together strips away the varnish, leaving the baseline reality of 100. The partner feels cheated, experiencing a sudden shock of betrayal because the person they married seems fundamentally different from the person they courted. Conversely, relationships that start from a humble or even slightly undervalued place tend to age remarkably well. When people discover over time that you are actually steadier, kinder, and more reliable than your modest introduction suggested, trust deepens organically.

Modern youth culture intensifies this pressure. Surrounded by digital feeds that demand instant validation, younger generations are particularly vulnerable to keeping up appearances. When self-worth is tied to real-time metrics of approval, stepping off the treadmill feels terrifying. Yet the antidote to burnout is not better performance; it is a willingness to start from the bottom.

If your actual academic standing or skill level puts you at fifth place in a room, pretending to be third place is an exhausting waste of energy. It is far more comfortable to accept being seventh and quietly build your foundation from there. When expectations are low, you have room to breathe, experiment, and surprise people later. Stripping away the packaging is the only way to build a relationship that can survive real life.

The Trap of the Approval Economy

The hunger for external validation does not just strain personal relationships; it distorts public life. Even figures widely respected for their wisdom, like Pomnyun himself, express unease at how easily public admiration curdles into an illusion. When people look at a public teacher from a distance, they often project an image of flawlessness. When they get close enough to see ordinary human mistakes, disappointment follows, sometimes turning into bitterness.

This dynamic plays out daily with influencers, celebrities, and public figures who crack under the weight of expectations they never actually agreed to fulfill. The desire to be seen as good makes people fragile. Social psychologists note a strong correlation between obsessive concern over others' opinions and a lack of internal self-efficacy. When your internal compass is weak, you try to stuff your pockets with other people's compliments to keep from floating away.

This is why treating everyday life like an endless job interview drains the joy right out of it. Students frequently report spending hours calculating how every facial expression will land, essentially treating themselves like corporate PR bots. Walking through life with a 24-hour surveillance camera pointed at your own behavior guarantees chronic anxiety.

Breaking out of this loop does not mean becoming rude or indifferent to others. It means recognizing the limits of mind-reading. You cannot preemptively manage everyone else's comfort without losing your own mind. Most conflicts can be handled as they arrive, without spending hours burning nervous energy on hypothetical disasters.

What to Watch Next

As this dynamic plays out across digital spaces and daily life, watch for a few key shifts in how people manage public visibility:

  • The "Anti-Curation" Trend: Look for whether younger digital creators continue pushing toward hyper-polished aesthetics, or if fatigue accelerates a counter-movement toward unedited, low-stakes sharing.
  • Corporate and Institutional Transparency: Notice how public figures and brands handle the inevitable backlash when their curated facades slip. The organizations and individuals that survive scandals intact are increasingly those that admit error quickly rather than doubling down on perfection.
  • Changes in Workplace Boundaries: Observe how remote and hybrid work environments alter social performance. Without the physical office theater, many workers are quietly recalibrating how much energy they spend on impression management versus actual output.

Ultimately, the opinions of others are just weather—passing noise that cannot alter the core architecture of your life unless you let it inside. The quietest, most radical thing you can do is simply to show up as you are, and let the performance finally come to an end.
