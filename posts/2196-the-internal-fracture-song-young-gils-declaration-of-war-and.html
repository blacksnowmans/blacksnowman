The Internal Fracture: Song Young-gil’s "Declaration of War" and the Religious Shadow Over South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 11, 2026

The atmosphere within the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation's ruling party under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, shifted from uneasy silence to open hostility over a single weekend. What was initially expected to be a standard internal apology regarding procedural grievances has instead erupted into what is being called the "Song Young-gil Declaration of War." Song, a heavyweight five-term lawmaker and former party chairman, stood before cameras to declare a "resolute war against systematic illegal political intervention disguised as religion." This aggressive pivot has effectively ignited a fuse beneath the party's already simmering factional tensions.

For those observing from outside South Korea, the spectacle might look like standard political infighting. However, the stakes involve the intersection of religious influence, the sanctity of private data, and the legitimacy of the DPK’s internal democratic processes. At the heart of the storm is the allegation that the Shincheonji Church of Jesus—a controversial religious group often accused of secretive political maneuvering—has infiltrated the ruling party’s ranks to manipulate internal elections. Whether these claims are backed by hard evidence or are the desperate gambit of a politician facing waning relevance is the question currently paralyzing the DPK leadership.

A Heavyweight at the Edge: The Motivations Behind the War

To understand why Song Young-gil is raising the stakes so high, one must look at his current political standing. Song is a veteran of the DPK, a man who has held the highest positions in the party and served as a crucial ally to the current administration. However, his recent trajectory has been turbulent. After a period of self-reflection following a "cash-for-votes" scandal during a previous party convention, Song attempted a political comeback by founding the "Pine Tree Party." When that venture failed to gain traction, he unilaterally declared the party dissolved and sought to return to the DPK fold, a move that many rank-and-file members viewed as an entitled overreach.

The immediate catalyst for Song’s "declaration of war" appears to be a recent poll that served as a political death sentence. In a survey conducted by "Poll Flower" (Yeon-ron-jo-sa-ggot), a prominent polling organization, Song’s support as a future presidential candidate plummeted to a staggering 3.1% in automated response (ARS) surveys, placing him last among seven potential candidates. Even in telephone interview polls, he hovered at 7.0%, significantly trailing rivals like Kim Min-seok, the current DPK chairman. In politics, being forgotten is more terrifying than being hated. For a man who has spent decades at the center of power, the 3.1% figure represents an existential threat.

By framing the internal party struggle as a crusade against religious infiltration, Song is attempting to pivot from a disgraced veteran to a "protector of the party." He claims that a significant number of Shincheonji followers have registered as party members to distort the "will of the party" during primaries. However, critics argue this is "noise marketing" at its most destructive. Song’s evidence remains largely anecdotal—hearsay from former religious members rather than a "smoking gun" of coordinated interference. By attacking the current leadership under Chairman Kim Min-seok, Song is carving out a space for himself, even if it means setting the party house on fire to stay warm.

The Shincheonji Frame and the Legal Powder Keg

The term "Shincheonji" carries immense weight in South Korean discourse. The group is often used as a political "third rail"; any association with it can be lethal to a politician’s career. Song’s team has compiled what they call a "report" on the group’s infiltration, alleging a "secret alliance" between the religious group and certain party factions. They characterize Shincheonji’s tactics as "infiltration and takeover," labeling them a "divisionist" force aimed at undermining President Lee Jae-myung’s hold on the party.

However, the methods used to "verify" this infiltration have created a massive legal and ethical crisis. To prove that Shincheonji members are in the party, Song’s camp reportedly compared the DPK’s internal member list with lists of known religious members. This is a potential violation of the Personal Information Protection Act and the Political Parties Act. In South Korea, a party’s member list is its most sensitive asset. Accessing and sharing this data for cross-referencing with outside groups without consent is a criminal offense, with precedents from the Seoul High Court suggesting severe penalties for party officials who leak such data.

The DPK finds itself in a paradoxical trap. If the party investigates Song’s claims, it must admit that its internal data was handled insecurely. If it ignores them, it leaves itself open to accusations of being "captured" by a cult-like entity. Moreover, the "evidence" Song has presented is being mocked by legal experts as "a collection of old newspaper clippings and conspiracy theories." By treating tens of thousands of party members as potential infiltrators without concrete proof, Song is practicing a form of modern McCarthyism that threatens to alienate the very base the DPK needs to maintain its governing mandate.

A Leadership Under Fire: The Crisis of Kim Min-seok

The fallout has landed squarely on the shoulders of DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok. The party’s base is increasingly frustrated with what they perceive as a tepid response to these allegations. Recent polling indicates that over 60% of DPK supporters believe Chairman Kim needs to take responsibility and offer a formal, public apology for the chaos surrounding the "secret alliance" rumors. This sentiment is particularly strong among the party’s core supporters, who feel that the leadership has allowed the party’s reputation to be dragged through the mud.

Adding fuel to the fire is the perceived insult to the Honam region—the southwestern provinces that serve as the traditional heartland and electoral stronghold of the DPK. Some proponents of the Shincheonji theory have pointed to lower-than-average turnout in places like Jeju and high religious membership in Honam as "proof" of an organized takeover. To Honam voters, who have long been the backbone of South Korean democracy, being labeled a "breeding ground for Shincheonji" is a profound regional insult. The failure of the leadership to decisively defend these members has led to a sense of betrayal.

"Is a successful political conspiracy exempt from punishment?"

This question, posed by DPK Supreme Council member Lee Sung-yoon, captures the growing anger within the party. A group of eight grassroots organizations, including the "Union for Democratic Party Members," has begun legal proceedings to suspend Chairman Kim Min-seok’s authority. These members are crowd-funding the legal costs, with thousands of individuals contributing about $25 (33,000 KRW) each to demand accountability. They argue that if the current leadership was elected through a flawed or "manipulated" process involving religious interference, then the entire leadership structure is illegitimate. This internal rebellion suggests that the DPK is facing a crisis of confidence that cannot be solved with a simple press release.

Falling Approval and the Move Toward Judicial Reform

While the DPK fights internally, the broader political landscape is shifting. The administration of President Lee Jae-myung is seeing a dip in approval ratings, recently falling to around 44.1% amid economic concerns and a volatile stock market. In South Korean politics, an approval rating below 40% is considered the danger zone for a sitting president. The internal "war" declared by Song Young-gil is seen by many as a distraction that the government cannot afford while trying to navigate a difficult legislative agenda against the People Power Party (PPP), the main conservative opposition.

In response to these pressures, the DPK’s active base is turning its attention toward the judiciary. There is a growing movement among party members to petition for the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. The argument from the pro-government base is that the judiciary has become a "political actor," stalling reforms and targeting the current administration. A petition to impeach the Chief Justice is currently gathering thousands of signatures, reflecting a desire to shift the narrative from internal bickering to a "grand struggle" for judicial reform. Large-scale rallies are already being planned for late November to coincide with Chairman Kim’s 100th day in office.

The situation remains precarious. If the DPK cannot resolve the Song Young-gil "war" through its internal disciplinary systems, it risks a full-scale forensic audit of its membership by state prosecutors—a nightmare scenario for any political party. The conservative opposition, the PPP, is already calling for a special counsel investigation into the religious infiltration claims, relishing the opportunity to watch the ruling party dismantle itself from within.

What to Watch Next

  • The 50,000 Signature Threshold: Watch if the petition to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae reaches the required 50,000 signatures in the next 30 days, which would force a formal legislative response and likely escalate tensions between the Blue House and the courts.
  • The Injunction Ruling: The court’s decision on the injunction to suspend Chairman Kim Min-seok’s duties will be a turning point. A ruling in favor of the disgruntled members would effectively decapitate the party leadership.
  • The 100-Day Rally: On November 24, the DPK base plans a massive demonstration. The turnout and the primary targets of the protesters' ire—whether they focus on the opposition, the judiciary, or their own party leadership—will signal the DPK’s direction for the remainder of the year.
  • Song Young-gil’s Legal Status: As Song continues his "war," any movement in the ongoing investigations into his previous "cash-for-votes" scandal could either bolster his "persecuted hero" narrative or finally end his political career.
