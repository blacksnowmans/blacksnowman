Black Snowman

The Silent Crisis of South Korea’s 30- and 40-Something Men

September 11, 2026

When men in their 30s and 40s open their phones late at night, they are often met with a flood of anonymous posts on online community forums—digital confessionals filled with a heavy, collective sigh. Consider a typical case: a 35-year-old man, a graduate of a local four-year university working at a mid-sized firm. He earns an annual salary of 50 million won (about $37,500) and has scraped together a net worth of 100 million won (about $75,000) through relentless, grinding frugality. Yet, sitting in a counseling office, he confesses with a hollow look, “I’ve been living so hard, but I don’t know if I’m actually living a good life.”

These are not men who have suffered catastrophic failures. By all outward appearances, they have stable jobs and have lived upright, diligent lives. Why, then, are they sinking into such profound skepticism? The issue is not absolute poverty, but a crushing sense of relative deprivation—a relentless, invisible scale upon which they weigh themselves against others, slowly eroding their sense of self-worth.

The Trap of Comparison in a Zero-Sum Society

The first thing these men reveal in counseling is a deep, agonizing void born from financial comparison. To a 35-year-old, that 100 million won ($75,000)—the fruit of ten years of skipping meals and denying himself small pleasures—feels like a significant achievement. That is, until he hears news of a peer who bought an apartment early with parental help or hit the jackpot on an investment. In an instant, his own decade of grit feels pathetic.

This is the root of their self-derision: “I spent ten years barely eating or spending a dime to save 100 million won, while others already have homes and assets? What was all that hard work for?” While there is nothing inherently wrong with parental support or investment success, for those who have had to navigate the brutal South Korean economy entirely on their own, the success of others feels like an act of violence that renders their own efforts worthless.

Data from the Korean Statistical Information Service (KOSIS) confirms the reality of this divide. The wealth and income gap between the top 20% and bottom 20% of households is widening dramatically. In a structural environment where the wealthy leverage debt to buy real estate and multiply their assets, the 3040 generation—whose wealth accumulation is often delayed—experiences this gap not just as a statistical difference, but as a direct threat to their survival and social standing.

The Illusion of the Marriage Market and Social Media

This financial deprivation peaks at one of life’s most critical junctures: the marriage market. In casual matchmaking meetings or blind dates, questions like “What is your annual salary?”, “How much have you saved?”, or “Do you own a home?” act as cold, transactional blades that dismantle a man’s life narrative.

Even a man who prides himself on being a productive, upright member of society suddenly feels small when faced with these standards. When you combine this with the curated, glittering lives displayed on social media—which users scroll through incessantly—the result is a constant, distorted comparison. The mental toll is not caused by physical hunger, but by the "illusion of backwardness." It is the gnawing feeling that despite working themselves to the bone, they are fundamentally falling behind. The economic benchmark expected of men in this era has long since surpassed what an individual can reasonably bear.

The Invisibility of Effort and the Weight of Isolation

The isolation is compounded by a lack of recognition. These men return home from intense, high-pressure jobs only to find no place of true rest. At the office, their performance is often treated as the bare minimum. A manager’s remark like, “Well, at your seniority, you’re supposed to be doing that for the pay you get,” can extinguish the last embers of professional pride. In a performance-review culture that treats employees like disposable parts—ignoring successes as “expected” but branding a single minor error as incompetence—it is impossible to feel valued.

They cannot even seek solace at home. If they attempt to voice their exhaustion, they are often met with dismissive “comfort”: “At least you have a job. Think of how many people are unemployed right now; you should be grateful.” Knowing that others are suffering more, they suppress their pain, telling themselves they have no right to complain. As they swallow their emotions in silence, their mental health quietly deteriorates.

The real crisis hits when the front door closes behind them at night. The tension maintained throughout the day as a “social mask” suddenly snaps. Without someone to tell, “I really had a hard time today,” the solitude becomes absolute. They realize that even if they have friends, they can only exchange superficial platitudes about how “that’s just work life.”

Turning the Arrow Inward

Initially, this anger is directed outward at the system: “The housing prices are insane,” or “The company’s evaluation system is broken.” But such anger is unsustainable when everyone around them seems to be navigating the system just fine. Eventually, the arrow of blame turns inward. “Is it just me?” they wonder. “Am I just not good enough?”

Every rejection—whether a failed promotion or a dead-end date—is internalized. They stop seeing the objective circumstances or the incompatibility of personalities and instead conclude, “If I had a higher salary, would she have looked at me twice?” This self-blame is a toxic cycle. It destroys self-esteem, leading these men to avoid new challenges entirely. They adopt a defensive mechanism: before a chance even arises, they reject it, telling themselves, “Forget it, who would want someone like me?”

This is not just a personal failure but a consequence of the patriarchal "breadwinner" model that still permeates South Korean society. Long after the rapid industrialization of the 20th century, the cultural expectation that a man’s humanity is tied solely to his economic provision remains. When that expectation is not met, the result is a profound, paralyzing sense of worthlessness.

A Call to Break the Cycle

The most important thing to realize is that the "relative deprivation" these men feel is a distorted image reflected in the mirror of other people’s standards. Everyone starts from a different place and moves at a different speed. The habit of cutting oneself down against a uniform, societal yardstick must be broken.

Just because you started later, or currently have fewer assets than your peers, does not mean your effort is fake. The sweat and the hours you put in, from dawn until night, are a record of a life that no one has the right to diminish.

What to watch next: As South Korea faces a record-low birth rate and a shrinking workforce, look for policy shifts regarding mental health support for middle-aged men and potential labor market reforms aimed at decoupling salary from seniority. However, the true change must be cultural: a shift away from measuring a man's value by his bank account alone. Until then, these men must learn to practice the most radical act of all—giving themselves the grace to exist without the need for constant, quantifiable success.
