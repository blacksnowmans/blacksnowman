Global Upheaval and the Digital Shift: What Korea Can Learn from Israel, Brazil, and France

October 28, 2026

In the high-tech newsrooms of Seoul, where screens flicker with real-time data from every corner of the globe, the atmosphere has grown increasingly heavy. From the razor-thin margins of the Israeli Knesset elections to the smoke-filled streets of Paris and the digital battlegrounds of Brazil, a wave of popular discontent is reshaping the world. For observers in South Korea—a nation currently navigating its own complex political landscape under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party—these events are not merely distant headlines. they are mirrors reflecting the precarious state of modern democracy, the evolution of political warfare, and the volatile energy of a new generation.

The source of this global friction is multifaceted, but the common thread is a profound disconnect between institutional politics and the lived reality of citizens. Whether it is the existential anxiety of a nation at war or the economic frustration of youth facing a jobless future, the "old ways" of governing and reporting are being tested. To understand why this matters to a reader interested in Korea, one must look past the sensationalist clips of burning cars or shouting politicians and examine the underlying shifts in how power is won, lost, and challenged in the 21st century.

The Israeli Standoff: A Nation Divided by War and the Ballot

Israel’s recent general election serves as a stark reminder of how external conflict can paralyze internal politics. According to exit polls from KAN, Israel’s public broadcasting platform, the political landscape is split almost exactly down the middle. The anti-Netanyahu bloc is projected to hold roughly 52 seats, while the pro-Netanyahu camp trails slightly with 50. In a parliamentary system, this two-seat gap represents a chasm of uncertainty that could dictate the future of Middle Eastern security for years to come.

For the Korean observer, the Israeli situation is particularly poignant. While Korea’s own domestic politics often center on the divide between the Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party, the Israeli experience shows what happens when political polarization is superimposed onto a state of perpetual military conflict. The tension in Israel is palpable not just because of the numbers, but because of the transparency—or lack thereof—in the process. Unlike the lightning-fast, highly synchronized ballot counting seen in South Korea, the Israeli process is fragmented, with updates released in bursts that only serve to heighten the national anxiety.

The stakes here transcend the borders of the Middle East. As global supply chains and energy markets remain sensitive to any tremor in the Levant, the political stability of Israel is a core component of international security. If the election results fail to produce a decisive mandate, the resulting vacuum could lead to further escalation or a breakdown in diplomatic efforts. For a country like South Korea, which relies heavily on global stability for its export-driven economy, the Israeli "political melting pot" is a cautionary tale of how internal fractures can weaken a nation’s standing on the world stage.

Lula’s Digital Resurrection: The New Mechanics of Political Power

If Israel represents the paralysis of traditional politics, Brazil’s recent presidential election represents its digital evolution. The victory of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva was nothing short of a political miracle, orchestrated in a mere three-week window between the first and second rounds of voting. What is most striking about Lula’s comeback is not just the ideology he represents, but the methodology he employed—a strategy some have termed a "political coup of personal skill."

Lula, an octogenarian politician often associated with an older, analog style of labor activism, managed to bridge the generational divide by embracing the tools of the youth. By partnering with younger members of the Chamber of Deputies (Brazil's lower house of parliament) and leveraging platforms like WhatsApp, Lula’s campaign rapidly built a following of over ten million users. This was not just about broadcasting messages; it was about "mission-based" campaigning. Supporters were given specific tasks, creating a decentralized but highly coordinated digital army.

The brilliance of the strategy lay in its focus. While his opponent, Flávio, attempted to trap Lula in a vague "corruption" narrative, Lula pivoted to "life-centric" messaging. He moved away from abstract debates and spoke directly to the fears and hopes of the populace: "If my opponent wins, you will lose the benefits you rely on," and "I will secure the economy and your safety." This shift from the moralistic to the pragmatic is a lesson that resonates globally. It suggests that in an era of information overload, the candidate who can most effectively link their policy to the voter’s daily survival—using the digital tools the voter already uses—will ultimately prevail. This digital mobilization is something the Democratic Party in Korea has watched closely, as the influence of "fandom politics" and SNS-driven campaigning continues to grow in Seoul.

Beyond the Flames: Misreading the French Social Safety Net

Turning to Europe, the reporting on recent protests in France highlights a significant failure in modern media literacy, particularly within the Korean press. Many outlets have focused exclusively on images of looting, arson, and "urban collapse," painting a picture of a nation on the brink of anarchy. However, this sensationalist lens obscures the nuanced reality of French civil society. To understand the protests, one must distinguish between three distinct groups: the students and ordinary citizens advocating for specific reforms, the "Black Bloc" (far-left groups that target the capitalist system itself), and the opportunistic criminals who exploit the chaos.

The Korean media has often conflated these groups, presenting a distorted narrative that views the unrest as a simple byproduct of "excessive welfare" and "fiscal deficit." Critics point to the fact that under President Macron, the fiscal deficit rose from 2.6% to 5.8%. However, framing this purely as a failure of the welfare state is a fundamental misunderstanding of the European social model. In Europe, welfare is not viewed as a "handout" or a "cost," but as a vital social safety net—the very thing that prevents the kind of "Rust Belt" desperation seen in parts of the United States.

This debate over the "social safety net" is highly relevant in Korea today. Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, there has been a renewed focus on expanding social services and ensuring that the government acts as a buffer against economic shocks. When Korean media outlets dismiss the French protests as mere "rioting" caused by "lazy welfare," they miss the deeper point: people are taking to the streets because they feel the safety net—the very foundation of their social contract—is being shredded. Treating these events as a form of "voyeuristic news" rather than a serious analysis of social stability does a disservice to the public’s understanding of how a modern state functions.

The Return of Student Activism: Gen Z and the One Piece Flag

Perhaps the most unexpected development in recent months is the resurgence of student-led protests, starting in Indonesia and spreading through parts of Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe, and France. The symbol of this movement is surprising: the pirate flag from the Japanese animation One Piece. In Indonesia, university students have taken the lead in criticizing political privileges and government failures, using pop culture symbols to unify a generation that feels robbed of its future.

For many older Koreans, this evokes memories of the 1980s democratization movement, when students were the vanguard of social change. For decades, it seemed that the era of the "student activist" had passed in Korea, replaced by a focus on individual career advancement and economic stability. However, the global trend suggests that Gen Z is reaching a breaking point. With high education levels but a lack of quality jobs, young people across the globe see unemployment not as a personal failure, but as a systemic political betrayal.

In Indonesia, a nation of 290 million people with a massive youth population, these protests are a barometer for democracy. They represent a digital-native generation that is using its connectivity to share inspiration and tactics across borders. They are not just protesting for "democracy" in the abstract; they are protesting for the right to a livable life. This is a crucial takeaway for the current political establishment in Seoul. While the Lee Jae-myung government has made efforts to address youth unemployment, the global energy of Gen Z suggests that if the gap between political rhetoric and economic reality remains too wide, the "One Piece" flags could very well appear in Gwanghwamun Square.

What to Watch Next

As we look toward the coming months, the intersection of digital technology, social welfare, and youth frustration will continue to define the global political landscape. The events in Israel, Brazil, and France are not isolated incidents but part of a larger, interconnected struggle to redefine the relationship between the state and the citizen.

Specifically, observers should keep an eye on:

  • Digital Campaigning Norms: Watch whether other nations adopt Lula’s "mission-based" WhatsApp strategy, and how governments attempt to regulate this kind of decentralized political mobilization.
  • The Welfare Debate in Korea: As the Lee Jae-myung administration continues its policy rollouts, pay attention to how the "French model" of the social safety net is used as both a goal and a warning in domestic political discourse.
  • Youth Unemployment Metrics: The stability of governments in Southeast Asia and beyond will depend on their ability to create actual jobs for the Z-generation, rather than just offering temporary subsidies.
  • Media Accountability: Look for a push toward better fact-checking in Korean international reporting, especially regarding the use of AI-generated images or sensationalist framing of foreign protests.

The world is currently in a state of "unstable equilibrium." The old political playbooks—relying on traditional media or vague moral attacks—are failing. Whether it is the digital savvy of a Brazilian veteran or the street-level passion of an Indonesian student, the message is clear: politics only matters when it becomes "my problem." For Korea, staying ahead of these trends is not just a matter of foreign policy; it is a matter of domestic survival.
