South Korea’s Ruling Party Moves to Impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae: A Historic Showdown Over Judicial Power

November 21, 2026

Behind closed doors on November 20, South Korea’s political landscape underwent a seismic shift. Lawmakers from the ruling liberal Democratic Party gathered for a tense general caucus to debate a decision that threatens to upend the delicate balance between the legislative and judicial branches. By the end of the meeting, the party leadership had officially codified an unprecedented stance as binding party policy: moving to impeach Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Korea.

For a ruling party to launch impeachment proceedings against the head of the nation's highest court is a moment without parallel in South Korea’s democratic history. The trigger was immediate: just one day earlier, the Supreme Court issued a contentious ruling remanding a major political case back to a lower court. Viewing the decision as a direct betrayal of judicial integrity, the Democratic Party decided that quiet endurance was no longer viable. The move has plunged Seoul into a fierce constitutional collision, drawing sharp condemnation from the conservative opposition and raising critical questions about the independence of the judiciary.

To understand why this showdown matters, one must look past the immediate political rhetoric to the institutional stakes. In South Korea's high-stakes presidential system, judicial rulings routinely shape political fortunes. By taking the nuclear option of impeaching the Chief Justice, the Democratic Party is signaling its willingness to rewrite the rules of political combat, setting up a clash whose consequences will echo far beyond the halls of the National Assembly.

Inside the Closed-Door Decision: How the Democratic Party Made Impeachment Official

The decision to seek Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae’s removal was not made lightly. During the secretive caucus on November 20, Democratic Party lawmakers engaged in heated debates over the strategy and potential political fallout. Party leaders argued that recent rulings from the Supreme Court had crossed a critical threshold, effectively eroding public trust in the judiciary and leaving the party with no choice but to take drastic action.

Rather than relying on a symbolic political resolution, the Democratic Party immediately moved to turn its declaration into concrete action. The party formally established a dedicated Impeachment Task Force, appointing Representative Yang Bu-nam to head the initiative. The mandate given to the task force is clear: complete legal reviews, draft the formal impeachment motion, and push the bill through the floor before the calendar year comes to a close.

This rapid timeline reflects a deliberate strategy of momentum. Party insiders emphasize that delaying the vote into the new year would only allow opposition forces time to regroup and build a counter-narrative. By treating the impeachment as a fast-track priority, the task force intends to keep the pressure firmly on the Supreme Court while laying out a detailed constitutional case against Cho.

The Legal Spark: The Han Duck-soo Ruling and the Four Articles of Impeachment

The immediate catalyst for this constitutional crisis was the Supreme Court’s decision to overturn a lower court ruling regarding former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo and send the case back for rehearing. The verdict sent shockwaves through the ruling party. Senior Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Min-seok publicly denounced the Supreme Court's ruling as an "anti-historical decision that grants immunity to historical opportunists," accusing the bench of substituting political calculation for sound jurisprudence.

The Impeachment Task Force is building its case around four core allegations of unconstitutional and unlawful conduct by Chief Justice Cho. Beyond the Han Duck-soo decision, the task force is also scrutinizing procedural irregularities and alleged bias in lower court remand proceedings connected to President Lee Jae-myung. Democratic Party legal strategists contend that Cho committed serious constitutional violations through unfair trial procedures, biased application of the law, and a failure to observe due process.

The Ruling Party's Legal Perspective: "The Supreme Court's remand decision regarding Han Duck-soo represents more than a mere legal disagreement; it marks a historical regression for our judiciary. When the highest court loses its neutrality to grant impunity, it must be held strictly accountable under the Constitution."

However, drafting an impeachment motion is only the first step. The true hurdle lies in the Constitutional Court, which must ultimately evaluate whether the Chief Justice’s actions constitute a grave violation of the law sufficient to justify removal from office. Democratic Party lawyers are currently refining the legal arguments to ensure the charges can withstand intense judicial scrutiny during constitutional review.

Opposition Fury: Han Dong-hoon and the Conservative Resistance

The response from the conservative opposition was swift and blistering. The People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition party, condemned the Democratic Party’s decision as an act of pure political retaliation and an unconstitutional attempt to subjugate the judiciary. Opposition lawmakers argued that attempting to remove the head of the judiciary simply because a ruling did not favor the government amounts to a direct assault on the rule of law.

Han Dong-hoon, leader of the People Power Party, launched a scathing verbal assault on the ruling party’s leadership. Criticizing the Democratic Party in unusually harsh terms, Han remarked, "If you are going to lose your mind, at least do it with some dignity." He went on to assert that the chance of the Constitutional Court actually upholding Cho’s impeachment was "exactly zero percent," challenging Representative Kim Min-seok to stake his legislative seat on the outcome of the trial.

Conservative leaders have framed the impeachment push as a form of state subversion. In a visible show of resistance, senior People Power Party officials, including Representative Jeong Jeom-sig, took to the streets in Seoul's Hongdae shopping district. Marching before the public, they called for the scrapping of special prosecutor acts pushed by the majority and denounced what they termed the ruling party's "trial cancellations and impeachment madness." Adding to the fiery rhetoric, conservative legal commentator Kang Dae-gyu suggested that if the impeachment motion is ultimately rejected by the Constitutional Court, the lawmakers who voted for it should have their salaries docked as punishment for abusing legislative authority.

Dissent and Alliances: Internal Doubts and Scramble for Broader Support

As the Democratic Party prepares for a legislative vote, it is also navigating complex political dynamics both within its own ranks and across the broader political spectrum. To maximize pressure, the ruling party is exploring cooperation with minor liberal parties, such as the Rebuilding Korea Party (also known as the Cho Kuk Innovation Party). A united coalition among progressive and anti-establishment factions could amplify calls for judicial reform and lend broader political weight to the impeachment motion.

Yet, unity within the ruling party itself is not absolute. During the internal discussions, Representative Gwak Sang-eon—a Democratic Party lawmaker who is also the son-in-law of the late former President Roh Moo-hyun—publicly voiced opposition to making the impeachment an official party mandate. Gwak cautioned his colleagues against impeaching the Chief Justice merely out of dissatisfaction with court verdicts, warning that such action could alienate moderate voters and trigger severe political backlash.

Gwak's rare public dissent highlights the strategic dilemma facing the ruling party. While the party leadership is committed to an aggressive stance to enforce accountability on the court, moderate members worry that being seen as punishing judges over trial outcomes could undermine the party's own credibility and damage public trust in democratic institutions.

What to Watch Next

With the Democratic Party setting a tight deadline to complete the process before the end of the year, South Korean politics is entering uncharted territory. The immediate focus turns to several key developments:

  • Drafting the Formal Motion: The Impeachment Task Force led by Representative Yang Bu-nam will finalize the specific constitutional charges against Chief Justice Cho, focusing on trial procedure violations and political bias.
  • The National Assembly Floor Vote: As the majority party, the Democratic Party possesses the legislative numbers required to pass an impeachment motion through the single-chamber parliament. Watching whether internal dissidents like Gwak Sang-eon break party ranks during a vote will be a key indicator of internal cohesion.
  • Constitutional Court Referral: If the impeachment motion passes the Assembly, Chief Justice Cho’s duties will be immediately suspended pending a trial at the Constitutional Court. How the court approaches this unprecedented case will test the boundaries of judicial independence and executive-legislative balance in South Korea.
  • Public and Opposition Escalation: The People Power Party has pledged all-out resistance, including continued street demonstrations and legal challenges. Public reaction to street protests and parliamentary fights will determine which side carries the political momentum into the coming year.
