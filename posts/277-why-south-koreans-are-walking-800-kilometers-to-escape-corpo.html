Why South Koreans Are Walking 800 Kilometers to Escape Corporate Burnout

September 07, 2026

Every morning in Seoul, millions of workers step out into a society engineered for maximum velocity. From elite university admissions to brutal corporate hierarchies, the rules of achievement are rigid, heavily scrutinized, and relentlessly demanding. In an environment where personal worth is constantly benchmarked against standardized milestones, the pressure to conform can feel suffocating. For a growing number of South Koreans, the antidote to this modern fatigue is not a quiet weekend getaway or a beach resort, but an 800-kilometer trek across northern Spain.

South Korea consistently ranks among the top nationalities for completing the Camino de Santiago, the ancient network of pilgrimage routes terminating at the shrine of the apostle Saint James in Santiago de Compostela. While Western travelers have long treated the trail as a spiritual or cultural journey, its massive popularity in South Korea speaks to a distinct social malaise. Escaping the relentless weight of expectations, thousands of Koreans fly to Europe each year to subject themselves to agonizing physical exhaustion, trading the corporate desk for a heavy backpack and a pair of hiking boots.

From Seoul’s High-Rise Towers to the Slopes of the Pyrenees

The journey for most Korean pilgrims begins in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, a small mountain village on the French side of the Pyrenees. Reaching this starting point requires a grueling journey of nearly 20 hours from Seoul, followed by a lengthy seven-hour drive from Madrid. Stepping out into the mountain air, travelers receive their pilgrim's credential—a small passport that must be stamped at checkpoints along the way. In that moment of intake, the visitor sheds the identity of a standard tourist and assumes the formal status of a pilgrim.

The sheer scale of this migration is striking. Every year, roughly 60,000 people begin their trek from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, drawing participants from some 150 countries. The trail, once the exclusive domain of medieval religious devotees and later a niche historical route, has transformed into a secular sanctuary for modern workers. For citizens of a hyper-digitized, hyper-competitive society, the appeal is precisely the contraction of life's choices. In normal daily life, an individual navigates an exhausting labyrinth of micro-decisions and social expectations. On the Camino, however, the existential burden is brutally simplified: the only objective is to walk forward and reach the day's designated shelter before sunset.

The Paradox of the Modern Pilgrim and Overtourism

As the Camino de Santiago has evolved into a global phenomenon, it has collided with the realities of modern mass tourism, creating a friction that local residents know all too well. The traditional way stations known as *albergues*—communal hostels spaced 10 to 20 kilometers apart—form the lifeblood of the route. Unlike profit-driven hotels governed purely by capitalist logic, these shelters have historically fostered a communal atmosphere where strangers share kitchens and dormitories.

Yet, this institutional ecosystem is straining under the weight of its own success. With the volume of travelers surging sixfold compared to two decades ago, rural Spanish villages along the route are increasingly overwhelmed. Local residents now contend with early-morning noise, crowded paths, and the general disruption of quiet provincial life by groups of travelers. Pilgrims arrive seeking spiritual renewal and peace, yet their sheer numbers threaten to transform the historic trail into just another consumer-driven itinerary. This contradiction forces a difficult reckoning: when the pursuit of personal mindfulness disrupts the daily peace of local communities, the modern pilgrimage risks becoming an extractive exercise rather than a respectful journey.

The Radical Solitude of Physical Pain

Despite the communal nature of the hostels, the core experience of the Camino remains fiercely solitary. While families or friends sometimes walk together, the management of physical suffering is entirely internal. No companion can share the throbbing agony of a burst blister on the sole of a foot or ease the deep-seated muscle fatigue of climbing mountain passes in freezing winter conditions.

This intense physical strain strips away social facades. In the crucible of daily exhaustion, the trivial anxieties of urban life dissolve, replaced by a hyper-focus on immediate survival and basic logistics: Did I walk the 25 kilometers safely? Are my socks dry? What will the weather be tomorrow? By forcing the mind to anchor itself entirely in the present moment, the trail offers a practical lesson in mindfulness. It replaces abstract, future-oriented anxiety with the tangible rhythm of a single footstep.

What to Watch Next

As the popularity of long-distance trekking continues to surge across East Asia, several key developments merit close attention:

  • Overtourism Regulations: Watch for potential caps on daily pilgrim numbers or stricter regulations on commercial tour groups implemented by local authorities in popular European pilgrimage regions.
  • Shifts in Corporate Wellness: Observe whether South Korean companies begin incorporating extended sabbatical leave or outdoor endurance programs into corporate culture to address severe white-collar burnout.
  • Infrastructure Evolution: Monitor how historical European trail towns adapt their local housing and municipal services to balance the economic benefits of international tourism against the preservation of resident quality of life.

Ultimately, the enduring appeal of the Camino for South Koreans lies in its absolute lack of standardized scoring. There are no corporate performance reviews on the mountain trail, no competitive university entrance exams, and no social penalties for slowing down. For a generation exhausted by the pressure to prove their worth at every turn, walking 800 kilometers is not merely a vacation—it is an aggressive reclamation of time, space, and self-ownership.
