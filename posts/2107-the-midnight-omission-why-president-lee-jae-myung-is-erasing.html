The Midnight Omission: Why President Lee Jae-myung is Erasing His Predecessor

November 08, 2026

At 1:00 AM, while most of Seoul was asleep, the blue light of a smartphone illuminated the face of the man at the center of South Korean power. President Lee Jae-myung, the current leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) administration, was composing a message that would send ripples through the nation’s political landscape by sunrise. On the surface, the post was a meditation on the nature of democracy. "Praise but do not worship; support but do not deify," the President wrote. It sounded like a statesman-like warning against the dangers of personality cults. Yet, to those who know the intricate, often blood-soaked internal hierarchies of Korean liberal politics, the message was less about philosophy and more about a cold, calculated erasure.

In his late-night missive, President Lee invoked the legacies of the two "saints" of the Korean left: Kim Dae-jung, the Nobel Peace Prize winner who led the country through the 1997 financial crisis, and Roh Moo-hyun, the firebrand populist whose tragic death in 2009 turned him into a permanent icon of the progressive movement. But there was a glaring, deafening silence where a third name should have been. Moon Jae-in, the immediate predecessor and the man who led the liberal camp for a decade, was nowhere to be found. In the world of Korean political signaling, what is left unsaid is often louder than what is shouted from the rooftops. This "Moon Jae-in Passing"—a term now trending in Seoul's political circles—reveals a presidency grappling with deep-seated insecurity and a base that is beginning to fracture.

The Echoes of a Vanishing Predecessor

To understand why President Lee’s omission of Moon Jae-in matters, one must understand the current state of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's ruling party. For years, the party was synonymous with the "Moon Jae-in era." Even after leaving office, Moon has remained an unusually popular figure for a retired Korean president, often greeted by cheering crowds and maintaining a robust following that transcends typical party lines. Just days before the President’s midnight post, a massive gathering hosted by Lee Jong-won, the head of the influential pro-liberal media outlet Sisa Tapa, saw an outpouring of affection for the former president. For a sitting leader like Lee Jae-myung, who thrives on the energy of his own hardcore fandom, seeing his predecessor command such heat is more than a nuisance—it is a threat.

President Lee’s selective memory in his SNS post was a surgical strike. By quoting Kim Dae-jung’s famous plea for "acting conscience" and Roh Moo-hyun’s "organized power of awakened citizens," Lee was attempting to stitch himself directly onto the mantle of the party’s historical greats. He was effectively saying: I am the rightful heir to the giants, and we don't need the man who came in between. By telling his supporters not to "worship," he wasn't just offering a democratic lesson; he was subtly criticizing the "Moon-pa" (hardcore Moon supporters) and signaling that the era of Moon Jae-in is officially over.

However, this erasure comes at a peculiar time. Usually, a sitting president ignores a predecessor when they are strong and want to move forward. Lee, however, is acting from a position of perceived weakness. Within the party, the silence is being read as a "warning shot" to those who still look toward Moon's residence in the southern village of Yangsan for guidance. It is an attempt to consolidate the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction by dismantling the emotional ties that still bind the party to its previous leader.

The Cold Reality of the 38% Approval Rating

The psychological tension behind the President’s midnight post is fueled by a sobering set of data. According to the latest figures from Gallup Korea, one of the nation's most respected polling firms, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dipped to 38%. Meanwhile, his disapproval rating has climbed to 51%. For any administration, falling below the 40% mark is the psychological "danger zone" where the ability to push through major legislation begins to evaporate.

Perhaps most alarming for the Blue House—the presidential office—is the collapse of support in what was once considered the "concrete" base: voters in their 40s and 50s. For the first time since Lee took office, a "dead cross" has occurred in these demographics—a term used in Korea when negative sentiment overtakes positive sentiment. In their 40s, disapproval hit 46% against 45% approval; in their 50s, the gap widened to 50% disapproval. Even within his own party's supporters, 22% now view his performance negatively. One in five of his own voters has effectively checked out.

The reasons for this slide are multifaceted. The administration has struggled with a series of high-profile personnel failures. Kim Seung-won, the nominee for Minister of Justice, faced a public backlash with 43% of the public deeming him unfit. Even more damaging was the case of Yong Hye-in, the nominee for Minister of Gender Equality and Family, who was forced to withdraw her candidacy on September 13 after a polling disaster showed a staggering 61% disapproval rating. When a government cannot even clear the bar for its own cabinet appointments, the public's patience wears thin. In Seoul, the heart of the nation’s wealth and middle-class sentiment, the President’s disapproval rating has hit 60%. The "midnight post" was an attempt to change the subject from these failures to a battle for the "soul" of the party.

The Psychology of Power: Anger Over Loneliness

Critics and analysts have been quick to dissect the President's state of mind. Ryu Si-min, a prominent liberal intellectual, author, and former minister who has often been a sympathetic voice for the party, offered a cutting psychological profile of what happens to leaders in this position. Ryu argues that the common trope of the "lonely leader" is a myth. According to his analysis, top power holders don't feel loneliness in the way ordinary citizens do. Instead, they feel "anger" and "irritation."

"Power is the ability to enforce one's will even against the will of others," Ryu noted in a recent commentary. "When that will is thwarted, when the public doesn't follow, or when approval ratings collapse, the leader doesn't feel solitary grief. They feel a sense of fury that the world is not bending to their command."

This perspective reframes the President’s "do not worship" message. It wasn't an act of humble self-reflection, but rather a manifestation of this irritation. If the public is unhappy, the leader's psychological defense mechanism is to blame the "culture" of the supporters or the lingering influence of the previous administration. By instructing his base to stop "deifying" leaders, Lee is subtly suggesting that the current dissatisfaction is a result of irrational expectations or misplaced loyalties to the "old" guard of Moon Jae-in.

This stands in stark contrast to the historical figure Lee invoked: Roh Moo-hyun. When Roh’s approval ratings plummeted during his term, he was known for agonizing over whether his very existence was a burden to the country. He possessed a rare "meta-cognition"—an ability to see himself through the eyes of the public and feel genuine shame for failing their expectations. Analysts suggest that President Lee is currently missing this trait. Instead of the "sincere reflection" seen in Roh, Lee is using "distancing tactics," trying to place the blame for the current malaise on the "worship culture" of the very fans he helped cultivate.

The Irony of the "New Lee Jae-myung" Fandom

There is a profound irony in President Lee's warning against deification. His rise to power was fueled by a hyper-active, digitally savvy, and often aggressive fandom known as the "New Lee Jae-myung" (or Gaeddal) movement. This group has been criticized by the opposition—the People Power Party—and even moderate liberals for their uncompromising stance and their tendency to "worship" Lee as a singular savior who can cleanse the country of the "corrupt" elements of the past.

For the President to now preach against "religious praise" feels, to many, like a classic case of what Koreans call "I-nam-bul"—a popular slang term meaning "if I do it, it's a romance; if you do it, it's an affair." He seems perfectly comfortable with a fandom that protects him from the legal troubles surrounding the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former president who was impeached and is currently serving time in prison), but he finds the "worship" of Moon Jae-in to be a threat to democratic health.

In an attempt to regain the initiative, the administration and the DPK have begun pushing for a "Special Prosecution" into what they call "fabricated indictments" from the previous era. This is a clear attempt to rally the core base by refocusing anger on the remnants of the old prosecution-led power structure that existed under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. However, this move risks further alienating the moderate "swing" voters in Seoul who are more concerned with their mortgage rates and the rising cost of living than with re-litigating the legal battles of the past three years. The data shows that the people leaving Lee's side aren't the "radical" progressives, but the pragmatic middle class who feel the government has lost its way on the economy.

What to Watch Next

As the "Moon Jae-in Passing" controversy continues to simmer, several key indicators will determine whether President Lee can stabilize his administration or if he will continue to retreat into a bunker of ideological purity.

  • The "Special Prosecution" Gambit: Watch how aggressively the DPK pushes the special probe into "fabricated indictments." If this becomes the central theme of the autumn legislative session, it signals that the President has given up on winning back the middle ground and is focusing solely on "trench warfare" with his core supporters.
  • The Yangsan Pilgrimage: Keep an eye on how many DPK lawmakers continue to visit former President Moon Jae-in’s residence. If high-ranking party members continue to seek Moon's counsel despite the "midnight warning," it suggests that Lee’s grip on the party’s internal hierarchy is weaker than it appears.
  • Economic Policy Pivot: With approval ratings in Seoul at 29%, the administration desperately needs a "win" on the economic front. Watch for any shifts in real estate or tax policy aimed at the capital's asset-owning class. If no pivot occurs, the 4050 "dead cross" may become a permanent feature of this presidency.
  • Cabinet Shake-ups: Following the withdrawal of Yong Hye-in, the next round of appointments will be a test of whether President Lee is willing to nominate moderate, bipartisan figures or if he will double down on "New Lee Jae-myung" loyalists.

President Lee Jae-myung’s midnight post was intended to show strength and philosophical depth. Instead, it exposed the fraying edges of a liberal coalition that is struggling to define itself without the shadow of its predecessor. You can erase a name from an SNS post, but erasing a political legacy from the hearts of the voters is a much more difficult task.
