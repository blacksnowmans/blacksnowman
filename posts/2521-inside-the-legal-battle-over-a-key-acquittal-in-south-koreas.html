Inside the Legal Battle Over a Key Acquittal in South Korea's Martial Law Trials

November 19, 2026

Just after 11:00 p.m. on December 3, South Korean lawmakers received urgent text messages summoning them to the National Assembly as emergency martial law took effect. Within six minutes, a follow-up message redirected members of the People Power Party—then the country's ruling party, now the main opposition—away from parliament and toward the party's central headquarters. Over the next hour, the meeting location flipped back to the National Assembly and then back to party headquarters once more, leaving lawmakers scrambling in the dark as a historic vote to overturn martial law approached.

That chaotic 60-minute window now sits at the center of a fierce legal and political dispute following a first-instance court's decision to acquit Rep. Choo Kyung-ho, who served as the People Power Party's floor leader on the night of December 3. The trial court cleared Choo of charges that he obstructed the parliamentary vote to lift martial law after speaking by phone with Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later removed from office and imprisoned. While the court cited strict criminal standards of proof and the absence of a phone recording, prominent commentators on the progressive YouTube current-affairs program Kim Ou-joon's Humility Is Difficult News Factory—including journalists Hong Sa-hoon and Joo Jin-woo, ruling Democratic Party of Korea lawmaker Yoon Kun-young, and Cho Kuk, head of the Innovation Policy Research Institute at the minor Rebuilding Korea Party—have sharply criticized the verdict's logic, its factual framing, and what they suspect is a broader judicial effort to shrink the scope of South Korea's insurrection trials.

Sixty Minutes, Four Venue Changes, and One Missing Recording

To understand why a single floor leader's text messages carry such weight, it helps to recall how South Korea's constitution checks emergency powers. When martial law is declared, only a majority vote by lawmakers inside the National Assembly plenary chamber can legally force it to be lifted. On the night of December 3, every minute and every lawmaker physically present inside the parliament complex mattered.

According to the timeline examined in court, Choo Kyung-ho sent four separate assembly notices to People Power Party lawmakers in the span of one hour:

  • 11:03 p.m. (December 3): A mass text instructed lawmakers to gather at the National Assembly.
  • 11:09 p.m.: Six minutes later, the venue was changed from the National Assembly to the People Power Party central headquarters.
  • 11:22 p.m.: Yoon Suk-yeol and Choo held a phone call lasting roughly one minute.
  • 11:33 p.m.: Following the call, the meeting point shifted back from party headquarters to the National Assembly's Special Committee on Budget and Accounts conference room.
  • 12:03 a.m. (December 4): With the parliamentary vote imminent, the venue was switched yet again from the National Assembly back to party headquarters.

For the first-instance court, the decisive legal question centered on that 11:22 p.m. phone conversation. In its ruling, the bench stated that it was "hard to see that any request" to block the gathering of lawmakers or obstruct the vote was included in the call. Crucially, no audio recording of the conversation exists. With no direct recording to verify what was actually said, and with Choo testifying that he neither received nor acted on any instruction to sabotage the vote, the court concluded there was insufficient objective evidence to reject the defendant's account. Applying the criminal-law standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, the judges acquitted him.

Supporters of strict due process note that criminal courts are bound by the presumption of innocence and cannot convict on suspicion alone. Yet critics on Kim Ou-joon's Humility Is Difficult News Factory argued that the ruling carried judicial skepticism too far. Hong Sa-hoon, Joo Jin-woo, Yoon Kun-young, and Cho Kuk contended that the court effectively accepted the defendant's testimony at face value while severing the circumstantial link between the 11:22 p.m. phone call and the midnight order redirecting lawmakers away from parliament. Participants in sensitive conversations at the highest levels of power rarely record themselves, critics noted, warning that treating the lack of a recording as an automatic shield against surrounding timeline evidence risks creating an escape hatch for closed-door collusion.

Disputes Over the Verdict's Facts and Logic

The backlash against the ruling goes beyond how the judges weighed the phone call. Panelists on the broadcast also challenged the accuracy of basic facts written into the verdict itself, arguing that errors in the court's narrative were used to justify Choo's decisions.

Specifically, the critics pointed to sections of the ruling describing how opposition parties—including the Democratic Party, then in opposition and now South Korea's ruling party, as well as the Rebuilding Korea Party—responded that night. According to the panelists, the written verdict depicted the Rebuilding Korea Party as having convened its lawmakers' meeting at party headquarters rather than at parliament. They also criticized a framing in the ruling that suggested the Democratic Party had prior knowledge of a martial law scenario while the People Power Party knew nothing. In reality, the commentators argued, lawmakers across opposition benches immediately rushed to the National Assembly upon hearing the martial law announcement in a desperate bid to secure the plenary hall.

Even more contentious was the court's reasoning regarding the vote's final outcome. In acquitting Choo, the first-instance judges added that because other lawmakers successfully convened at the National Assembly and passed the resolution lifting martial law, Choo's venue changes had no actual effect on the result. The court further reasoned that the required quorum had already been met even without votes from People Power Party lawmakers.

On the broadcast, critics condemned this line of thinking as one that "reversed the order of cause and effect."

"Only because other lawmakers from the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party climbed walls and fought to defend the plenary hall did the lifting of martial law succeed—and paradoxically, that success was used to acquit Choo Kyung-ho."

From the critics' perspective, nobody inside the National Assembly knew until the gavel fell whether enough lawmakers would reach the chamber in time. Judging the legality of an alleged attempt to scatter lawmakers based on the eventual success of those who defied the chaos, they argued, substitutes hindsight for an assessment of intent and action at the moment the crisis unfolded.

Are Insurrection Trials Being Narrowed?

Rather than viewing Choo's acquittal in isolation, commentators on the program placed it alongside an earlier court decision involving former agency chief Cho Tae-yong. Together, they argued, the verdicts point toward a consistent narrowing of both who qualifies as a "key participant in insurrection" and how long the insurrection itself is legally defined to have lasted.

Under South Korean criminal law, raising the legal threshold for "key participant" status and trimming the timeline of an insurrection carries major consequences. If the events of December 3 are treated as a brief, tightly bounded episode driven solely by a handful of top figures, officials across government ministries, intelligence agencies, the military, and parliament who are accused of cooperating or failing to act face far lower legal exposure.

Hong Sa-hoon, Joo Jin-woo, Yoon Kun-young, and Cho Kuk went further, floating a speculative theory about judicial strategy. In their view, courts may be moving toward convicting a small core of figures facing overwhelming public scrutiny—specifically Yoon Suk-yeol, Kim Keon-hee, and Kim Yong-hyun—while clearing surrounding cabinet members, officers, and politicians. The panelists suggested this pattern could be designed with Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae in mind, arguing on air that "only by narrowing the scope of the insurrection can it be avoided even if it reaches Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae."

That assertion remains an interpretation advanced by political and media critics rather than an established judicial fact. In any multi-defendant criminal proceeding, courts are required to evaluate individual guilt separately rather than sweep peripheral figures into collective liability. Still, the perception among critics that the judiciary is systematically trimming away the outer rings of the December 3 case highlights a widening trust gap between parts of the public and the courts.

What to Watch Next: The Supreme Court and Political Reckoning

Because first-instance verdicts are only the opening round in South Korea's three-tier court system, attention is already turning to how higher courts—and the ruling Democratic Party—will respond in the months ahead.

  • Supreme Court divisions: Observers are watching closely for upcoming Supreme Court rulings on insurrection-related cases. Broadcast commentators predicted fierce internal debate among the justices, expecting any majority ruling that continues narrowing liability to trigger sharp, detailed dissenting or separate opinions that will expose the judiciary's internal divide to public view.
  • Public reaction to top-court outcomes: If the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae appears to insulate surrounding figures from accountability, critics warn of intense public backlash over what they view as a disconnect between legal technicalities and the lived memory of December 3.
  • Democratic Party strategy: On the political front, debate is sharpening over how the ruling Democratic Party will navigate ongoing impeachment pushes and judicial reform demands. Panelists on the broadcast expressed split views—some arguing party leadership has effectively stepped back from impeachment efforts, others insisting a narrow window for decisive action remains—while criticizing leadership for perceived hesitation in the face of voter demands for clear accountability.

The timestamps from 11:03 p.m. to 12:03 a.m. are fixed in the record. Whether higher courts treat that hour as an inconclusive logistical scramble or as an actionable piece of a broader constitutional crisis will define not only Choo Kyung-ho's legal fate, but also how South Korea's judiciary records one of the most turbulent nights in its modern democratic history.
