The New "Major Crime Investigation Agency": Reform or a Trojan Horse for Prosecutors?

September 10, 2026

South Korea’s long-standing debate over curbing the power of the Prosecution Service—an institution long criticized for its "imperial" reach—is approaching a critical climax. The government is moving ahead with plans to split the agency’s investigative and indictment powers, creating a new body called the Major Crime Investigation Agency (MCIA). However, as the legislative details emerge, the mood on the ground has shifted from hopeful anticipation to deep cynicism. Many observers are asking: is this a genuine structural reform, or are we simply witnessing the rebranding of the old guard?

The Personnel Problem: Old Faces in New Uniforms

The skepticism began in earnest when the committee responsible for selecting the first chief of the MCIA was unveiled. The committee consists of nine members, yet three are former prosecutors. Crucially, there is not a single former police officer represented. In the context of South Korean politics, this composition speaks volumes.

The backgrounds of the committee members have sparked outrage among critics. Several are closely tied to the current administration and the "pro-Yoon" faction (referring to President Yoon Suk-yeol, a former Prosecutor General). Critics argue that the selection process is being used to place loyalists in key positions, ensuring that the new agency remains under the influence of the very same prosecutorial culture that the reform was supposed to dismantle.

Park Eun-jung, a lawmaker from the opposition Rebuilding Korea Party, hit the nail on the head during parliamentary questioning: "We cannot expect individuals deeply embedded in the 'pro-Yoon' prosecutorial network to conduct investigations in the spirit of reform." The fundamental contradiction here is stark: the agency was designed to break the prosecution's monopoly on power, yet it appears poised to be staffed by the very people who wielded that power for years.

The Risk of "Zombie" Prosecutorial Culture

The concerns are not merely about the committee members, but also about the potential candidates for the MCIA chief position. Names being floated in the media include figures who were part of the command structure during controversial past investigations, such as the high-profile disputes involving the former Channel A reporter and the alleged illegal exit ban of former Vice Justice Minister Kim Hak-ui.

While the government defends these potential appointments by arguing that the agency needs seasoned investigators and that "prosecutorial background should not be a disqualifier," critics view this as a superficial excuse. If the leaders of the new agency harbor the same deep-seated, pro-authority habits as the old Prosecution Service, the MCIA risks becoming a "second prosecution"—or worse, a more sophisticated tool for political leverage.

Police Under Siege: Drowning in Caseloads

While the political drama unfolds at the top, the reality on the front lines of law enforcement is far grimmer. As the Prosecution Service prepares to shed its investigative duties, the police are bracing for a massive influx of cases. Already, the police are grappling with a backlog of over 100,000 pending cases.

Despite the immense increase in workload, the government’s commitment to funding this transition has been lackluster. The proposed budget increase for police investigative costs for the coming year is roughly $225,000 (300 million won). Once inflation is accounted for, this is essentially a budget cut. Lawmaker Kim Dong-ah of the Democratic Party of Korea highlighted the absurdity of this during a recent audit, noting that police are expected to absorb tens of thousands of cases without the necessary financial or human resources.

The structural issues are equally concerning. Only about 20% of police personnel are dedicated exclusively to investigations; the rest are rotational staff who move between traffic patrol, community policing, and security details. Expecting this system to suddenly excel at complex white-collar or high-level corruption investigations—without a massive overhaul in training and funding—is a recipe for failure.

A New Era of "Self-Reliance" for Citizens

By October 2026, South Korea’s legal landscape will undergo a massive shift. The Prosecution Service will effectively lose its direct investigative function, replaced by the new MCIA and a separate office for indictments. While the goal is to prevent the concentration of power, the transition creates significant risks for the average citizen.

The process for challenging "no-indictment" decisions by the police or the MCIA is becoming increasingly complex. Citizens now have a strict three-month window to appeal a decision. If a case stalls for more than six months, the bureaucratic hurdles for a secondary appeal become even steeper. Experts warn that the era of relying on state agencies to independently "find the truth" is fading. Instead, we are entering an era of "legal self-reliance," where citizens must arm themselves with exhaustive evidence and logical arguments from the very start of a legal dispute.

What to Watch Next

  • Final Confirmation of the MCIA Chief: Watch to see if the selection committee pivots to a neutral candidate or cements the current "pro-Yoon" trend. This will set the tone for the agency’s independence.
  • Budget Adjustments: Keep an eye on upcoming parliamentary budget sessions. If the police do not receive a significant increase in investigative funding, the "reform" will likely result in lower-quality investigations and increased public frustration.
  • Legislative Tweaks: As the 2026 deadline approaches, watch for any amendments to the law regarding the coordination between the new prosecution office and the MCIA. Any loophole that allows for "customary" prosecutorial interference in investigations will be a major indicator of whether the reform has truly succeeded or failed.

Ultimately, this reform is being sold as a way to disperse power. But without the proper resources and a genuine shift in the culture of those holding the reins, the change may be nothing more than a change of nameplate. For now, citizens should remain vigilant—because in this new, fragmented legal landscape, protecting your own rights is becoming an increasingly personal responsibility.
