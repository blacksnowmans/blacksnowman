The Price of Politics: Why South Korea’s "Slap-on-the-Wrist" Culture Endures

September 06, 2026

For most of us, the rules of society are rigid. If you break the law, you face consequences. But in the world of high-stakes Korean politics, time and justice seem to flow differently. A headline recently rippled through Seoul’s political circles: Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul and a prominent figure in the conservative People Power Party (the current ruling party), saw his inner circle face a court verdict regarding the illegal payment of opinion poll costs. The sentence? A fine of 10 million won (about $7,500).

To the political establishment, this was a sigh of relief. It is a "keep-your-job" fine—not large enough to trigger an automatic disqualification from office or trigger a fresh election. But for the average voter watching from the sidelines, the ruling feels less like justice and more like a routine transaction in a "league of their own." It raises an uncomfortable question: Is the law a fair arbiter, or is it merely a toll booth that political elites pay to continue their business as usual?

The Hidden Hand Behind the Polls

The court’s verdict was clear: the defendants did, in fact, have others pay for opinion polls on their behalf. This was not a simple, innocent debt between friends. The evidence, including KakaoTalk messages and links to media outlets, painted a picture of a campaign team working in lockstep to manipulate their public image. The defense’s attempt to claim they were "deceived" or that the polls were "rigged" by third parties fell flat. They knew the intricacies of the Political Funds Act—the law governing how money can be raised and spent in elections—better than anyone, yet they chose to walk a razor-thin line to tip the scales in their favor.

The core issue here is that opinion polls are not just data; in South Korean politics, they are the primary currency of momentum. When a campaign finds a poll result unfavorable, using illicit funds to bypass standard campaign financing to "fix" that image is a direct insult to the voter. It treats the electorate as a variable to be managed rather than a constituency to be served.

"The Fine" as a Political Safety Net

The court also ruled that it was highly improbable that Mayor Oh was unaware of the actions taken by his closest aides, including key defendant Kang Cheol-on. The specific sum of 21 million won (about $15,700) was officially categorized as a prohibited political donation. The law is designed to prevent this because third-party payments act as a back-door for lobbying, effectively eroding the political neutrality required for a fair election. While the court didn't find enough evidence for every single cent, the ruling established a clear pattern of systemic, intentional rule-breaking.

This brings us to a chronic ailment in South Korean democracy: the reliance on the "fine" as a safety net. Historically, violations of the Political Funds Act rarely lead to prison time or career-ending consequences for top-tier politicians. In a 2009 scandal, over 2,300 people were indicted, yet the vast majority of first-instance rulings resulted in nothing more than fines. When statistics show that 70% of such cases end in a simple monetary penalty, the law stops acting as a deterrent and starts acting as an operating expense. For many, it has become a "cost of doing business."

The Art of the Legal Loophole

Whenever a high-ranking politician faces these charges, the script is predictable. The defense will appeal to the Supreme Court, citing complex legal technicalities—arguing that the lower court "failed to perform its duty" or "exceeded the bounds of judicial discretion." These aren't just legal arguments; they are smoke screens. By dragging the process out and focusing on procedural minutiae, they ensure that the "substantial truth"—the actual abuse of power—gets lost in a mountain of paperwork.

This creates a dangerous "learning effect." When politicians see that their peers can circumvent the spirit of the law by mastering its letters, they become more emboldened, not less. The law is meant to be the minimum standard of morality, but in this system, politicians are becoming experts at skating just along the edge of the ice without falling through. When the prosecution cannot perfectly map every flow of illicit money, defendants exploit these gaps, claiming the funds were used for "necessary political activities" rather than illicit ends. For the voter, this confirms a cynical, but increasingly common, suspicion: the game was never fair to begin with.

Why We Should Be Angry

This case is not just about one mayor or one campaign team. It is a mirror reflecting how lightly we, as a society, have treated the transparency of election camps. Behind the 21 million won and the web of political brokers lies a massive attempt to blind the voters. We are often told that the "quality of the crime" is severe, yet the punishment remains light. As long as relationships built on money can be settled with a fine, and those fines can be paid and forgotten, the pyramid of power will remain unthreatened.

The Political Funds Act is intended to be the bedrock of our democracy. If the flow of money is not clean, the direction of politics can never be straight. If we continue to view these crimes as "unavoidable mistakes" or "regrettable technicalities," we are effectively granting amnesty to the corrupt. True justice isn't found in the narrow, technical door of a courtroom; it is found in the persistent, watchful eye of the electorate.

What to Watch Next

  • The Appeal Process: Watch to see if the prosecution or the defense pushes for a higher-level review. Often, these cases enter a "legal marathon" designed to exhaust public interest until the issue fades from the headlines.
  • Legislative Reform: Pay attention to whether there is any genuine movement in the National Assembly to revise the Political Funds Act. Without closing the "fine as a penalty" loophole, future violations are virtually guaranteed.
  • Public Sentiment: The real test will be how the electorate responds in future local and general elections. Until voters treat campaign integrity as a non-negotiable metric for their support, the "business of politics" will continue to operate under the same shadow.

Ultimately, when the law fails to act as a barrier to corruption, the final verdict must come from the ballot box. We must remember that a 10 million won fine does not wash away the act of subverting the democratic process. The responsibility to demand a cleaner, more transparent system rests with us.
