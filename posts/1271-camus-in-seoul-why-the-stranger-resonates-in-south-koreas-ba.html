Camus in Seoul: Why ‘The Stranger’ Resonates in South Korea’s Battle Over Justice and Conformity

October 18, 2026

Step into a modern South Korean courtroom, or scroll through the country’s trending YouTube feeds, and you will quickly notice a peculiar obsession: the trial is rarely confined to physical evidence or statutes of law. Instead, the public and the legal establishment alike dissect a defendant’s micro-expressions. Did they bow at the expected forty-five-degree angle? Did their voice break with the requisite cadence of remorse? In the algorithmic arena of South Korean social media, creators produce minute-long videos dissecting the suspect’s gait, past diary entries, and facial stiffness, confidently branding them a sociopath because they failed to perform the scripted choreography of public repentance.

For decades, readers across the globe have studied Albert Camus’s 1942 masterpiece The Stranger (L’Étranger) as an existential parable of the absurd. Yet in contemporary South Korea, the story of Meursault—condemned to the guillotine less for discharging a firearm under a blinding Algerian sun than for refusing to weep at his mother’s funeral—reads less like an abstract philosophical allegory and more like an unsparing critique of modern Korean society. Between the suffocating pressures of social conformity and an entrenched legal apparatus now at the heart of the country's fiercest political struggles, Camus’s indictment of institutional hypocrisy hits uncomfortably close to home.

The Tyranny of the Emotional Script

The tragedy of Meursault begins not on the sun-scorched beach, but with one of the most provocative opening lines in world literature: "Mother died today. Or maybe yesterday; I can't be sure." In that detached telegraphic dispatch, Meursault marks his perilous distance from the social conventions of his world. At the elderly care home in Marengo, he declines to view his mother’s body. He sips white coffee, smokes a cigarette during the vigil, and the very next day attends a comedy film starring Fernandel before going swimming and starting a romance with Marie. To the magistrate, the jury, and the press, these mundane reactions are assembled into an airtight indictment of monstrous unfilial behavior.

This dynamic is painfully familiar to anyone who has navigated the social landscape of South Korea. Korean social life operates under intense communal scrutiny, where grief, filial duty, and social deference are strictly codified. To mourn improperly is to invite immediate suspicion. When someone experiences profound grief and finds themselves numb, frozen, or unable to shed tears for days, the surrounding whispers quickly question their sanity or empathy. The underlying presumption is that grief exists only in approved, standardized quantities.

As the French philosopher Élisabeth Badinter observed, cultural sanctities such as maternal instinct are largely modern historical constructs rather than immutable natural laws. Prior to the eighteenth century, French aristocratic and bourgeois infants were routinely handed off to rural wet nurses with little emotional intimacy; it was only through thinkers like Jean-Jacques Rousseau and the rise of industrial nation-states—analyzed later by Michel Foucault—that motherhood was moralized and disciplined to nurture productive labor. Similar emotional realignments occurred across modern Korean history, notably when independence activist and educator Bang Jeong-hwan pioneered Children’s Day in 1923, transforming children from familial labor into respected individuals. Yet once institutions canonize these sentiments, they weaponize them into coercive standards of normality. Anyone whose genuine emotional rhythm diverges from the state-sanctioned script is categorized as a deviant.

From the Blinding Sun to the Digital Guillotine

When Meursault stands before the magistrate and blurts out that he pulled the trigger "because of the sun," the courtroom erupts in ridicule. Outside the physical courtroom, the modern equivalent of this incomprehension plays out daily across South Korea’s digital landscape. When an arrest is made or a controversy erupts, digital platforms such as YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels transform into 24-hour moral tribunals. Rather than evaluating systemic failure or concrete facts, the public conversation descends into ruthless character assassination.

Online vigilante channels—often referred to locally as "cyber wreckers"—dig up high school yearbooks, analyze casual body language, and generate millions of views with captions proclaiming that an individual "always gave off an ominous aura." Simultaneously, the justice system and popular commentary demand performative stereotypes: suspects must grovel with theatrical self-loathing to earn sentencing leniency, while victims are expected to present spotless records of helpless purity to be taken seriously. Had Meursault collapsed to his knees, wept theatrically, and blamed his actions on the disorientation of grief, the colonial court would likely have commuted his sentence. Because he chose unvarnished truth over artificial repentance, the system demanded his head.

"The court was interested not in the physical circumstances of the homicide, but in whether the defendant conformed to the moral fiction invented by the community."

The Legal Cartel and the Fight for Systemic Reform

The second half of The Stranger shifts into an incisive courtroom drama, where the most unsettling feature is Meursault’s complete erasure from his own trial. The judge, the prosecutor, and even his own defense attorney form an exclusive linguistic and professional guild. Meursault’s lawyer delivers his defense in the first person, declaring, "It is true I killed... but I loved my mother," reducing the defendant in the dock to a mute prop while elite professionals stage a play designed entirely for their own career advancement and institutional vanity.

This estrangement mirrors the deep crisis of trust surrounding the South Korean judiciary and prosecutorial elite. For years, the phrase "legal cartel" has dominated South Korean domestic politics. Citizens have long pushed back against jeon-gwan ye-u—the deeply entrenched, lucrative convention of granting preferential treatment to former judges and senior prosecutors who move to private practice at prestigious law firms. Through this revolving door, retired judicial figures have routinely commanded hundreds of thousands of dollars in retainer fees for single cases, securing leniency for corporate titans and political insiders while ordinary citizens face the unbending weight of the law.

These systemic distortions reached a breaking point during the contentious presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, a former prosecutor general who staffed key state offices with former investigative colleagues and utilized selective indictment practices before being impeached and ousted from office. The legacy of weaponized investigations sparked massive candlelight rallies outside prosecutors' offices across Seoul, driving widespread demands to strip the prosecution service of its unchecked monopoly over indictments and warrants. Today, under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, reforming these entrenched judicial hierarchies remains one of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea's most volatile legislative mandates, resisted fiercely by the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. The outrage that fuels these reform efforts stems directly from the dynamic Camus documented eighty years ago: an insular legal caste wielding state power to impose moral judgments disconnected from the citizens they claim to serve.

The Unnamed Arab and What to Watch Next

Rereading Camus today also requires confronting the text's own profound blind spot. In The Stranger, the man Meursault kills on the beach never receives a name; he remains simply "the Arab," an anonymous colonial extra sacrificed to serve a European existential crisis. Just as Meursault was silenced by the French colonial court, the indigenous population of Algeria was rendered invisible by the colonial order. Modern interpretations, including recent cinematic projects by French directors like François Ozon, have sought to restore agency to this silenced history, demonstrating that classic literature must be read with critical awareness rather than blind reverence.

For young South Koreans enduring hyper-competitive job markets, soaring urban housing costs, and an exhausting culture of mandatory positivity, Camus’s critique offers an unexpected refuge. When society insists that exhaustion is merely a lack of effort and demands smiling conformity, Meursault’s refusal to lie about his feelings becomes a poignant defense of human dignity. Facing execution, he rejects the prison chaplain's religious platitudes and opens his heart to the "benign indifference of the universe," preserving his integrity by refusing to pretend.

As South Korea continues to wrestle with its legal institutions, several key developments will signal whether the structural absurdities Camus highlighted can be corrected:

  • Legislative momentum on prosecutorial reform: Watch how President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the ruling Democratic Party negotiate restructuring bills to dismantle the investigative and prosecutorial monopolies traditionally concentrated in the central investigative offices.
  • Crackdowns on revolving-door practices: Monitor incoming judicial appointments and Bar Association ethics regulations aimed at curbing jeon-gwan ye-u retainer windfalls for retired top prosecutors and judges.
  • Policy and legal responses to digital vigilantism: Observe parliamentary hearings on regulating algorithmic mob justice and the spread of unverified defamation by online creators, balancing constitutional protections of free speech with defenses against digital character assassination.

Whether in colonial Algiers or contemporary Seoul, the ultimate test of a society’s maturity lies in how it treats those who refuse to recite the consensus script. When institutions demand emotional hypocrisy as the price of admission, recognizing the absurdity of the performance is the first indispensable step toward genuine reform.
