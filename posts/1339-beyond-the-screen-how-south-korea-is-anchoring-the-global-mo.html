Beyond the Screen: How South Korea is Anchoring the Global Move Toward Physical AI

October 20, 2026

In a bustling corner of Seoul, developers are preparing for a visit that signals a major shift in the global tech hierarchy. On October 22, OpenAI, the creator of ChatGPT, is set to host a major developer event in the South Korean capital. But the conversation is no longer just about chatbots or clever prose. The chatter in the "Teheran-ro" tech district—Seoul’s equivalent to Silicon Valley—is about something much more tangible: the transition from AI that lives in a browser to AI that walks, talks, and cleans our homes. This is the era of "Physical AI," and South Korea, with its unique blend of high-tech infrastructure and heavy manufacturing, is becoming the world's most important laboratory for it.

The news cycle on October 19 highlighted a profound tension in the industry. While companies like Anthropic are pledging billions to social causes to mitigate the job losses AI will inevitably cause, they are simultaneously taking on massive, high-interest debt to fund the physical hardware required to keep the revolution alive. Meanwhile, Korean giants like LG Electronics and local government agencies are moving fast to put "brains" into everything from vacuum cleaners to four-legged patrol robots. For the international observer, this isn't just a technical update; it is a glimpse into how the next decade of labor, security, and daily life will be restructured.

The Rise of Physical AI and OpenAI’s Seoul Strategy

For the past two years, AI has been a "monitor-bound" phenomenon. We typed into a box, and the AI replied. However, the latest industry movements suggest that the "brain" is finally finding its "body." Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, recently declared that the company aims to develop humanoid robots by 2027. This is a pivot from pure software to the physical realm—a move that explains why OpenAI is increasingly focused on the Korean market.

South Korea is a global leader in robotics and hardware. By bringing its developer ecosystem to Seoul, OpenAI is positioning itself to partner with the companies that will build the physical shells for its next generation of models. We are already seeing the software bridge this gap. ChatGPT can now navigate Chrome tabs, fill out forms, and handle repetitive "screen labor" that previously required human fingers. The goal is to move this capability from the screen to the real world: an AI agent that doesn't just book your flight on a website, but a robot that physically checks you into your hotel.

This shift was highlighted prominently by LG Electronics, one of South Korea’s largest "chaebols" (family-run conglomerates). In a massive display at New York’s Times Square, LG showcased its 68-year history of innovation, culminating in a vision for "Physical AI." For LG, AI isn't just a feature in a smartphone; it is an ambient intelligence that understands the layout of a home, manages energy consumption, and interacts with users through household appliances. This vision of the "AI-native home" is where South Korea intends to lead the global market, leveraging its dominant position in the global appliance industry.

The Heavy Price of Progress: Debt, Costs, and Social Responsibility

While the technological vision is grand, the financial reality behind it is increasingly grim. Anthropic, the AI startup founded by former OpenAI executives, recently made headlines with a dual-pronged announcement that illustrates the precarious state of the industry. On one hand, the company announced it would donate 5% of its equity (about $7.5 billion based on recent valuations) to a public interest fund. This fund is designed to prepare society for the "job shock" caused by AI.

The data justifying this donation is startling. Anthropic’s internal hiring trends show that the proportion of entry-level technical hires has plummeted from 27% to just 9%. AI is already doing the work of junior coders and researchers, effectively pulling the ladder up behind the current generation of tech workers. By pledging this equity, Anthropic is attempting to build a social safety net before the IPO (Initial Public Offering) market demands more aggressive profit-seeking.

However, this altruism is set against a backdrop of desperate capital raising. Reports indicate that Anthropic is seeking to issue subordinated bonds worth about $9 billion (12 trillion KRW) at interest rates exceeding 10%. This is an extraordinarily high cost of capital for a tech firm, reflecting the "compute war" currently raging. To stay competitive, these firms must buy tens of thousands of Nvidia chips and build massive data centers. The "burn rate"—the speed at which they spend cash—is now so high that even multibillion-dollar investments from Google and Amazon aren't enough.

This financial pressure is trickling down to the companies that use these AI models. Businesses in Korea and abroad are reporting "AI cost bombs." Developers and employees have been using premium, high-cost models (like GPT-4 or Claude 3 Opus) for simple tasks like summarizing short emails—tasks that could be handled by models that are 100 times cheaper. As a result, corporate Korea is shifting toward a "pragmatic AI" approach, deploying "Small Large Language Models" (sLLMs) for daily tasks and saving the expensive "super-brains" for complex reasoning. The era of unlimited, free-flowing AI experimentation is ending, replaced by strict cost-benefit analysis.

National Security and the Domestic Semiconductor Push

Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea, there has been a significant push to ensure that South Korea’s "Physical AI" revolution is powered by domestic technology. The government’s Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) has recently initiated several "World Model" research projects. These are intended to provide the "brain" for autonomous military assets, ensuring that South Korea does not remain entirely dependent on American software for its national defense.

The semiconductor ecosystem is responding in kind. KAI (Korea Aerospace Industries) and Gaonchips, a prominent Korean design house, have announced a partnership to develop domestic AI chips for next-generation unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). These chips will be manufactured using Samsung Electronics' 8-nanometer process. By moving away from general-purpose GPUs (Graphics Processing Units) made by Nvidia and toward specialized, domestic AI silicon, Korea is attempting to insulate its defense and industrial sectors from global supply chain shocks.

This push for "On-device AI"—AI that runs locally on a chip without needing a cloud connection—is also reaching the consumer market. Nvidia recently released "RTX Spark" for Windows ARM PCs, and Lenovo has already launched compatible laptops in the Korean market. This allows users to run massive 32GB AI models directly on their desks. For the Lee Jae-myung government, this technological sovereignty is a top priority, as it combines economic growth with national security. Local governments are also jumping in: the city of Cheonan has deployed four-legged patrol robots to enhance citizen safety, while provinces like North Jeolla and Daegu are racing to become hubs for Physical AI manufacturing.

The "Shadow AI" Dilemma and New Social Realities

As AI becomes ubiquitous, it is creating new headaches for management and fascinating shifts in Korean society. One of the most pressing issues for Korean firms like the Korea Investment Corporation (KIC) is "Shadow AI"—the unauthorized use of external AI tools by employees. When an employee pastes sensitive corporate data into a public chatbot to get a summary, that data can potentially be used to train the next version of the model, leading to massive intellectual property leaks.

To combat this, Korean companies are beginning to issue "Digital Employee IDs" for AI agents. Rather than banning the technology, they are treating AI software as a member of the staff, with specific access permissions and security protocols. This allows the Democratic Party-led government’s push for digital productivity to continue without compromising the security of the nation's "key technologies."

Culturally, the AI wave is hitting every demographic. At a recent "AI Golden Bell" event (a popular quiz show format in Korea) hosted by Kakao—the operator of Korea’s ubiquitous messaging app—the average age of participants was nearly 72. These senior citizens weren't just curious onlookers; they were using ChatGPT to solve complex problems, proving that the generational digital divide is closing faster than anticipated. On the other end of the social spectrum, some reports have highlighted a "Silicon Valley rush," where high-achieving individuals are specifically seeking out AI entrepreneurs and engineers as partners, viewing them as the new elite of the global economy.

What to Watch Next

The developments of this week suggest that we are moving past the "hype" phase of generative AI and into a period of hard-nosed implementation. For those following the Korean market, three key indicators will determine the direction of the next few months:

  • OpenAI’s Local Partnerships: Watch the outcomes of the October 22 Seoul event. Any formal agreements between OpenAI and Korean hardware giants like Samsung or LG regarding humanoid robotics will be a massive market signal.
  • The "Token Budget" Shift: As companies move away from expensive "frontier" models toward cheaper, specialized local models (sLLMs), we will see if the revenue growth of companies like Anthropic and OpenAI can be sustained.
  • Physical AI Security: As robots move into factories and public spaces, the first major "Physical AI" security breach will likely lead to a wave of new regulations from the MSIT. The transition from data theft to physical safety risk is the next great challenge for the Lee Jae-myung administration.

South Korea is no longer just a consumer of global tech trends; it is the place where the world’s most advanced software meets the world’s most advanced hardware. Whether it's a 70-year-old winning a quiz with a chatbot or a domestic AI chip powering a drone, the "Physical AI" revolution is being built here, one chip and one robot at a time.
