The Revolving Door of Korean Justice: Inside the V Global Crypto Scandal Audit

October 16, 2026

Imagine sitting in a wood-paneled hearing room in Yeouido, the heart of Seoul’s political district. Outside, the autumn air is crisp, but inside the National Assembly, the atmosphere is stifling. High-ranking officials from the National Police Agency (NPA) sit at long tables, facing a barrage of questions from lawmakers. This is the Gukjeong-gamsa, the annual National Audit, a month-long marathon where the legislature grills every corner of the executive branch.

For the average global observer, these hearings might look like mere political theater. But for the victims of one of South Korea’s largest cryptocurrency scams, and for a public weary of institutional corruption, the recent audit of the police was a moment of reckoning. At the center of the storm is a concept known as Jeongwan-ye-u—the "privileged treatment of former officials"—and a high-profile lawyer who once stood at the pinnacle of the prosecution service.

The debate isn't just about legal technicalities; it is about the "Legal Cartel" that many believe still operates in the shadows of the post-impeachment era. It is about whether the police, now operating under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung and his Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), have truly broken free from the habits of the past, or if the "shadows of collapsed justice" from the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—currently serving time following his removal from office—still linger within the investigative ranks.

The Shadow of 'Jeongwan-ye-u' and the Legal Cartel

To understand why the recent audit was so explosive, one must first understand Jeongwan-ye-u. Literally translating to "precedent-official treatment," it refers to an informal, systemic corruption where retired judges and prosecutors are hired as defense attorneys to exert undue influence on their former colleagues still in office. It is a persistent "monster" in the Korean judicial system, one that breeds deep public cynicism.

During the audit by the National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee, lawmakers zeroed in on Lee Jong-geun, a former high-ranking prosecutor who served as the Head of the Criminal Department at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office—a "big fish" by any standard. The controversy involves his decision to represent key figures in the V Global case shortly after leaving public service.

The V Global scandal was a massive multi-level marketing (MLM) cryptocurrency fraud. At its peak, the scam siphoned off trillions of won—estimated at roughly $2 billion to $3 billion—from over 50,000 victims. For many in their 20s and 30s, who saw crypto as one of the few remaining paths to financial stability, the collapse of V Global wasn't just a loss of money; it was a destruction of their future.

The sting becomes even more painful when the "legal technicians" defending the perpetrators are the very people who were supposed to be protecting the public. As Representative Chae Hyun-il of the DPK pointed out during the audit, Lee Jong-geun was in a position of oversight regarding the V Global investigation while he was a prosecutor. For him to then turn around and accept a massive legal fee to defend those same individuals as a private lawyer is, in the eyes of many, a direct betrayal of public trust. It raises a chilling question: Was the investigation handled properly while he was in office, or was he already preparing his "soft landing" into a lucrative private practice?

The Suseo Police Investigation and the 'Pre-Booking' Escape

The focus of the audit then shifted to the Seoul Suseo Police Station, which handled the complaint against Lee Jong-geun for allegedly violating the Attorney Act. Article 31 of the South Korean Attorney Act is clear: a lawyer is strictly prohibited from taking on a case that they handled as a public official. This is the minimum safety mechanism designed to ensure that public power isn't sold for private gain.

However, in February of this year, the Suseo Police Station quietly closed the case. They used a procedure known as "closing the investigation before booking" (ipgeon-jeon-josa-jonggyeol). In the Korean legal system, this means the police decided there wasn't even enough evidence to formally register the individual as a suspect. They effectively smothered the case in its crib.

Representative Chae Hyun-il’s questioning of Woo Jong-soo, the head of the National Investigation Bureau (the police's primary investigative arm), was a masterclass in probing institutional opacity. Chae noted that even the Legal Ethics Committee—a body not known for radical activism—had recognized the seriousness of the case and requested an investigation. Why, then, did the police dismiss it so readily?

"The public’s anger is immense," Chae stated during the hearing. "A high-ranking prosecutor who supervised a massive fraud case becomes the lawyer for the perpetrators right after retiring. When the police close this without even a formal booking, it looks like you are protecting one of your own—or at least one of the elite circle."

The response from the police leadership was noticeably cautious. Commissioner Woo Jong-soo defended the decision by citing technicalities regarding "the timing of the retention" and the "identity of the specific case." But he also left a door open, admitting that whether the investigation was "sufficient" could be a "separate matter." This hedge suggests that even within the police leadership, there is an awareness that the optics of the Suseo decision are disastrous.

A Trillion-Won Crime and the Ethics of Defense

To put the stakes in perspective, we must look at the victims of the V Global scam. These were not just institutional investors; they were retirees who invested their pensions and young workers who invested their savings, lured by promises of 300% returns. When the scam collapsed, the human cost was devastating, leading to broken families and, in some tragic instances, suicides.

The V Global case is a prime example of the "legal technicians" at work. When a crime involves billions of dollars, the perpetrators can afford the most expensive "shields"—former prosecutors who know exactly how the system works, where the blind spots are, and who to call. This creates a two-tiered system of justice: one for those who can afford the "former official" premium, and one for everyone else.

The audit revealed that the case has now been referred to the Bureau of Major Crimes Investigation (BMCI), an agency often likened to a specialized anti-corruption unit. This move signals that the initial police dismissal was not the end of the story. If the BMCI finds that the Suseo Police Station overlooked obvious evidence or was influenced by Lee Jong-geun’s former status, it will trigger a crisis of confidence for the National Police Agency, which has fought hard for greater investigative autonomy from the prosecution.

For the Lee Jae-myung administration, which campaigned on the promise of "uprooting deep-seated evils" and curbing the power of the "Legal Cartel," this case is a litmus test. If a former prosecutor-turned-lawyer can successfully bypass the Attorney Act in a case involving 50,000 victims, then the reforms promised after the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol will be seen as hollow. The public is watching to see if the police will act as a "cane for the people" or a "shield for the powerful."

What to Watch Next: The Re-Investigation and Legislative Fixes

The fallout from this audit is likely to ripple through the legal and political landscape for months. Here are the concrete developments to monitor:

  • The BMCI Re-investigation: Watch for whether the Bureau of Major Crimes Investigation formally books Lee Jong-geun as a suspect. This would be a significant blow to the "former official" culture and a win for the DPK’s reform agenda.
  • Internal Audit of Suseo Police: Pressure is mounting for the National Police Agency to conduct an internal review of the officers who initially cleared the case in February. Any signs of "external pressure" or "negligence" could lead to disciplinary actions.
  • Legislative Amendments to the Attorney Act: Lawmakers like Chae Hyun-il are likely to propose tightening the Attorney Act to close the "technicality loopholes" that allow former officials to claim they are defending "different but related" cases.
  • Victim Advocacy: The 50,000 victims of V Global are becoming increasingly organized. Their presence at future hearings or protests will keep the pressure on the government to ensure that the legal fees paid to former officials—often suspected to be "criminal proceeds"—are scrutinized.

The National Audit reminded us that while the faces at the top of the government have changed, the structures that protect the elite are remarkably resilient. The Lee Jong-geun controversy is more than a legal dispute; it is a battle for the soul of the Korean justice system. In a country where the President himself rose from the ranks of human rights law to fight against entrenched interests, the expectation for a "fair society" has never been higher. Whether the police can meet that expectation remains the most pressing question in Yeouido today.
