A Party at War with Itself: How Leaked Tapes and Factional Feuds Plunged South Korea’s Ruling Democrats into Crisis

November 17, 2026

Inside the private, invitation-only Telegram channels of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), political discussions are supposed to stay behind closed doors. Access requires strict real-name verification, creating what was intended to be a secure domain for senior party insiders and lawmakers aligned with President Lee Jae-myung. Instead, those digital walls recently collapsed into a toxic battlefield of explicit personal abuse, leaked audio recordings, and ruthless factional warfare.

As internal bickering eclipses national governance, South Korea’s ruling party finds itself engulfed in a self-inflicted political crisis. What began as private disagreements and tactical maneuvers by party leadership has erupted into an all-out civil war, dragging down public approval ratings, alienating the party's core voters, and threatening the legislative momentum of President Lee’s administration.

Toxic Chatrooms and Press Feuds: The Ethics Crisis Inside the Ruling Party

The controversy first erupted inside a highly exclusive Telegram group chat. Populated by key lawmakers from the pro-Lee faction—and previously attended by Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok—the encrypted room was meant to be a secure space for internal coordination. However, an unmasked user operating under the alias "Hangang" posted graphic sexual insults and explicit death threats directed at senior party figure Choi Min-hee.

In response to what amounted to character assassination within her own party's rank-and-file, Choi filed a criminal complaint for insult and intimidation at the Yeongdeungpo Police Station in Seoul. Yet, despite the severity of the online violence, major media outlets gave the incident sparse coverage, and the DPK leadership alongside "Demincho"—an association of first-term Democratic lawmakers—remained noticeably silent. Supporters quickly voiced outrage, questioning why party leaders refused to launch an immediate internal investigation into an anonymous user who was almost certainly an internal lawmaker or party member.

The friction surrounding Choi quickly expanded into a broader conflict with the press. On November 5, Choi posted photos of news photographers covering a DPK legislative meeting into a KakaoTalk group chat of over 500 local supporters in Namyangju, Gyeonggi Province. Accompanying the images was a caption describing the press as a "pack of hyenas" trying to capture images of party division.

The post triggered immediate condemnation from major media bodies. The Korea Photojournalists Association and the Korea Video Journalists Association held an emergency press conference at the National Assembly, calling the remarks a direct attack on press freedom. Although Choi issued an apology on Facebook and held a formal briefing to express regret, her defensive posture during media questioning—and a subsequent public attack on an online political journalist whom she accused of speaking for rival party factions—only escalated the confrontation.

Calculated Timing: How a One-Month-Old Recording Became a Political Weapon

The crisis deepened significantly when Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok deployed a leaked audio file targeting Choi. The recording originated from a closed-door constituent meeting in Namyangju in mid-October, during which Choi made a minor verbal slip while explaining local misunderstandings regarding President Lee Jae-myung. Choi had already apologized to the local party members present, resolving the issue at the time.

However, Kim Min-seok held onto the audio file for nearly a month, releasing it in mid-November to initiate a sudden, high-profile internal ethics inspection. The timing raised immediate suspicions of political calculation. Kim was approaching his 100th day in leadership while facing dipping personal approval ratings and growing criticism over his command. Simultaneously, key political allies within Kim's faction were embroiled in their own scandals—including Rep. Park Sung-joon destabilizing the party's official real estate policy platform and Rep. Park Hee-seung drawing fierce public backlash over inappropriate verbal remarks.

By releasing the month-old recording, Kim effectively redirected media scrutiny away from his faction’s missteps and onto an internal rival. On November 15, just three days after the inspection was ordered, the DPK spokesperson announced that Kim had accepted the ethics report and formally referred Choi to the party’s Central Ethics Tribunal. Under party regulations, potential sanctions range from warnings and suspension of party roles to outright expulsion.

Critics within the party pointed out a glaring double standard. While Choi was targeted with maximum disciplinary pressure, lawmakers from the dominant leadership faction faced no such ethical referrals for their policy blunders or public misstatements. Furthermore, past primary controversies—including unexamined allegations of Shincheonji religious group intervention involving Kim Min-seok himself—were quietly swept aside. This perceived weaponization of the disciplinary process has drawn widespread accusations of selective enforcement and factional bias.

A Disaster in the Polls: Core 40s and 50s Voters Abandon the Democrats

The public fallout from the leadership's internal warfare arrived swiftly in national polling data. A survey conducted by Every Research on behalf of EveryNews (conducted November 13–14, 2026, sampling 1,000 adults nationwide via 100% wireless ARS, with a margin of error of ±3.1 percentage points at a 95% confidence level) painted a grim picture for the ruling party.

President Lee Jae-myung’s job performance rating fell to 35% positive against 61.9% negative—the lowest approval figure recorded by the polling firm during his presidency. More critically, overall party support figures revealed a dramatic reversal between the ruling party and the main conservative opposition:

  • People Power Party (PPP): 41.5% (main conservative opposition party)
  • Democratic Party of Korea (DPK): 27.2% (ruling party, falling into the 20s)
  • Cho Kuk Innovation Party: 10.1% (minor progressive party)
  • Reform Party: 2.3%
  • Progressive Party: 1.6%

The most devastating metric for the Democrats was the sudden breakdown of their traditionally reliable core voter base among Koreans in their 40s and 50s. Historically the bedrock of Democratic electoral victories, these demographics showed a sharp shift toward the opposition:

  • Voters in their 40s: PPP led with 42.1%, while DPK support plummeted to 27.0% (with 12.0% shifting to the Cho Kuk Innovation Party).
  • Voters in their 50s: PPP recorded 34.5%, DPK dropped to 26.7%, and the Cho Kuk Innovation Party captured 17.5%.

This structural shift indicates that both long-standing party loyalists and newly joined voters are abandoning the ruling party out of frustration with internal infighting. Meanwhile, President Lee’s apparent reluctance to actively intervene and restore order has left many supporters disillusioned, weakening the administration's political leverage just as key legislative battles loom.

The 55 First-Timers and the Betrayal of Reformist Values

The internal conflict reached a flashpoint with the collective action of "Demincho," an association representing first-term Democratic lawmakers. Traditionally expected to challenge party hierarchy and champion political reform, 55 first-term lawmakers instead signed a joint petition backing the leadership's aggressive stance against internal critics.

Angry party members and online activists quickly compiled and circulated the list of signatories, labeling them the "55 Enemies of Reform." The list includes lawmakers Ahn Tae-jun, Lee Kang-il, Park Min-kyu, Cho In-chul, Kim Jun-hyuk, Lee Gi-heon, Kim Nam-hee, Baek Seung-ah, Lee Geon-tae, Yoon Jong-gun, Lee Kang-hee, Kim Tae-sun, Jeon Yong-gi, Han Jung-ae, Song Jae-bong, Park Hye-chul, Jang Jong-tae, Kim Nam-joon, Kim Moon-soo, Cho Gye-won, Lee Yong-woo, Kim Woo-young, Moon Geum-ju, Kim Yoon, Go Gyeong-jong, Lee Jae-gang, Park Jung-hyun, Kim Hyun-jung, Lim Mi-ae, Kim Dong-ah, Park Hong-bae, Kim Nam-geun, Oh Se-hee, Lee Jae-gwan, Lee Yeon-hee, Lee Jeong-hyun, Park Ji-hye, Yeom Tae-young, Jeon Jin-sook, Choi Hyun-il, Huh Seong-moo, Ahn Do-geol, Hwang Myeong-seon, Park Sun-won, Kim Yong-man, Park Hee-seung, Kim Jun-hwan, Park Jun-taek, Lee Ju-hee, Kim Yong-gap, Jung Jin-wook, Kim Seong-beom, and Lim Moon-hyuk.

For many rank-and-file members, seeing fresh legislative faces act as enforcers for factional leadership represented a deep betrayal of party values. The disconnect between elected officials and grassroots supporters was further highlighted by an incident in which a DPK lawmaker verbally berated ordinary citizens participating in an early morning vigil demanding the impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol (who was subsequently impeached and imprisoned). The lawmaker reportedly yelled, "Are you crazy?" at citizens standing in the rain—a moment that quickly became a symbol of political arrogance.

"When politicians who were elevated by the people start treating ordinary citizens with contempt, the founding principles of the party are effectively dead."

What to Watch Next

With public support cratering and factional divisions out in the open, the coming weeks will determine whether the Democratic Party can contain the damage or slip further into crisis. Readers tracking South Korean politics should keep an eye on several key indicators:

  • The Central Ethics Tribunal Decision: Watch whether the party's disciplinary body enforces a severe sanction against Choi Min-hee or backs down under public scrutiny. A harsh penalty will confirm fears of a factional purge, while a lighter sentence could provoke further backlash from leadership allies.
  • Presidential Leadership: Track whether President Lee Jae-myung breaks his neutrality to rein in Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok and restore party discipline, or remains on the sidelines as his legislative power base deteriorates.
  • Police Investigation into Telegram Abuse: Follow the Yeongdeungpo Police Station's investigation into the user "Hangang." If law enforcement unmasks the perpetrator as a sitting lawmaker or high-ranking party official, the revelation will shatter the leadership's remaining moral authority.
  • Opposition Realignment: Monitor whether the main opposition People Power Party can consolidate its polling lead (41.5% to 27.2%) and translate the ruling party's self-destruction into lasting legislative momentum.
