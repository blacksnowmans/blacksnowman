The Camino de Santiago Reality Check: Why Everything You Pack is Too Heavy

September 07, 2026

The Camino de Santiago is one of those bucket-list journeys that occupies a strange space in the global travel imagination. It promises quiet introspection, sweeping Spanish vistas, and a chance to strip away the complexities of modern life. Yet, for anyone who has spent an evening scrolling through travel forums or watching first-person accounts, the reality often looks rather different. You see triumphant photos at the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela, but you also read frantic accounts of travelers weeping on the first day under 20-kilogram (about 44-pound) packs, wondering if they signed up for a spiritual retreat or a medieval penal colony. Packing for the Camino is a masterclass in self-deception. Armed with romantic notions, we toss in extra layers, luxury toiletries, and "just in case" items until our luggage weighs us down physically and emotionally.

To put it bluntly, the Camino is an exercise in minimal survival and a relentless negotiation with your own physical limits. The items that matter are not glamorous travel accessories; they are purely functional tools designed to keep your body moving forward one agonizing step at a time. Stripping away the fantasy reveals a very practical truth about what you actually need to survive the trail—and what you will bitterly regret bringing.

The Golden Rule of Packing: Subtraction is a Skill

When prospective pilgrims first look at backpacks, the conversation inevitably drifts toward capacity. Should it be 40 liters? 50 liters? The mistake lies in assuming that a larger bag gives you flexibility. In reality, on a long-distance walking trail, weight does not add up arithmetically; it multiplies geometrically with every kilometer. The core principle of packing light is simple: the pack itself must be featherlight, and your clothing strategy should revolve around exactly three outfits.

Establishing a daily laundry routine is not optional; it is the cornerstone of survival on the trail. For men, a pair of quick-drying boardshorts or athletic swim trunks can double as casual wear and inner lining, eliminating the need for bulky extra underwear while slashing drying times. Preparing for the Camino is ultimately a process of subtraction—removing items, and then removing them again until nothing is left but the absolute essentials.

💡 Summary Checklist

  • Smaller backpacks are always better. Carrying 20kg is a self-inflicted punishment.
  • Stick to the three-outfit rule. Doing daily laundry is the secret to reducing load.
  • Sturdy hiking boots are non-negotiable survival gear. Look for thick, supportive soles.
  • A sleeping bag is essential. Public hostels often lack blankets or maintain questionable hygiene standards.

Footwear and First Aid: Your Fate is Tied to Your Feet

On the Camino, your shoes are not merely gear; they are temporary extensions of your anatomy. As the terrain shifts unpredictably between jagged stones, loose dirt tracks, and asphalt, thin fashion sneakers will pulverize the soles of your feet within hours. Sturdy hiking boots are mandatory, paired with a lightweight pair of sandals or sliders to give your feet a chance to breathe in the evenings.

Blisters are the inevitable uninvited guest of every pilgrim. A proper medical pouch must contain more than basic painkillers and indigestion tablets; it needs a generous supply of specialized blister plasters, anti-inflammatory gels, and bandages. As anyone who has limped through a Spanish village searching for a rural pharmacy will tell you, wasting precious daylight hunting for medical supplies is a miserable experience.

Navigating the Albergue Ecosystem

Many first-time travelers assume that accommodation along the Camino can be booked well in advance the way one might reserve a chain hotel. The reality of the traditional municipal *albergues* (pilgrim hostels) is quite different. Many operate on a strict first-come, first-served basis with no online booking systems whatsoever. Staying in these public shelters is remarkably cheap, usually costing between €5 and €8 (about $5.50 to $9) per night, but the facilities are intentionally basic.

Communal living demands a strict adherence to unwritten etiquette. Noise discipline, respect for shared sleeping quarters, and basic cleanliness are paramount. Knowing how to navigate this network—distinguishing between public and private hostels, and understanding which ones accept reservations—is the mark of an experienced traveler.

🔥 The Injury Reality Check

While people set off chasing romance, historical data shows that a significant percentage of pilgrims are forced to abandon their journeys or seek medical treatment due to preventable injuries. Walking hundreds of kilometers without physical preparation is not a spiritual pilgrimage; it is an aggressive physical gamble.

Knee and ankle injuries remain the ultimate enemy of a successful completion. Spending at least a month prior to departure training with a weighted pack on local hiking trails is virtually the only way to insulate yourself against the physical shock awaiting you in rural Spain.

The Economics of Letting Go: What to Leave Behind

Clothespins, multi-plugs, and laundry soap do not need to be hauled across continents from your home country. Spanish supermarkets and village shops sell these mundane supplies cheaply and abundantly. The same goes for wet wipes and trekking poles; you can easily acquire them locally the moment you determine they are genuinely necessary.

Countless travelers make the mistake of packing for every conceivable hypothetical scenario, only to end up paying local post offices halfway through the trip to ship their excess baggage home. The comforting thought of "I might need this eventually" turns out to be the heaviest item in your pack. Embracing local infrastructure and accepting that you do not need to be entirely self-sufficient from day one is the key to enjoying the journey.

📊 By the Numbers

A standard day on the Camino involves walking between 20 and 30 kilometers (about 12 to 18 miles). Slinging a 20kg pack over your shoulders is functionally equivalent to carrying a heavy barbell and performing tens of thousands of squats over undulating terrain. That cumulative mechanical pressure is the single biggest determinant of whether you reach Santiago.

Trimming your pack weight down below 10kg dramatically reduces your risk of a season-ending injury.

What the Trail Actually Teaches You

💭 An Editor's Reflection

It is worth asking a fundamental question: out of all the invisible baggage we carry through our everyday lives, how much of it could we possibly squeeze into a backpack?

Bank balances, corporate titles, and the complicated social dynamics of the modern workplace suddenly lose all meaning out on the gravel paths of northern Spain. Your daily responsibilities shrink to a wonderfully uncomplicated checklist: wash your clothes, check the route for tomorrow, and tend to the blisters on your feet.

Many travelers set off for Santiago convinced they will unearth some profound existential revelation. Yet, looking at the faces of fellow walkers, what you see is not sudden enlightenment, but a quiet, hard-earned peace.

That peace comes from an empty pack and the simple, rhythmic act of walking day after day. Rather than agonizing over expensive technical gear, the best preparation you can do is lace up an old pair of sneakers today and walk out the door for thirty minutes.

The Camino does not begin with a grand itinerary or a masterclass in equipment selection. It begins with the movement of your feet against the earth.

Finishing a packing list always stirs up an irresistible urge to throw on the backpack and head straight for the airport. But next time, the philosophy has to be different: pack light, travel lean, and let the physical lightness translate into freedom of movement. What are you carrying in your bag right now, and what are you finally ready to leave behind?

Before you step out the door, what is the first thing you will empty from your life?
