Seoul’s Redevelopment Turf War: Should Borough Mayors Get the Power to Approve New Housing?

October 19, 2026

Step into an old neighborhood in Seoul away from the gleaming glass towers of Gangnam, and you will find a maze of narrow alleyways barely wide enough for a delivery scooter, lined with crumbling brick villas built in the 1980s. Emergency fire trucks cannot enter these lanes, and residents have spent decades waiting for modern apartment complexes to replace their decaying homes. But in South Korea's densely populated capital, turning an aging neighborhood into high-rise housing requires navigating a brutal maze of urban planning rules. Now, a quiet administrative revolt among Seoul’s local boroughs threatens to upend how the city grows—and who gets to decide its future skyline.

At the center of the storm is a proposal passed by the Seoul Council of District Mayors to wrest zone designation authority away from the Mayor of Seoul and hand it to the city's 25 autonomous borough heads (gu-cheong-jang). The proposed power shift applies to small- and medium-sized redevelopment sites under 30,000 square meters (roughly 323,000 square feet, or under 800 residential units). Proponents promise to slash approval wait times from years down to six months. Critics, however, warn of haphazard urban sprawl, political pork-barrel development, and severe administrative incompetence.

With housing supply shortages evolving from a real estate issue into a national priority under President Lee Jae-myung's administration, this administrative tug-of-war highlights a deeper struggle: Can local boroughs be trusted with the keys to city planning, or will decentralization trigger a wave of predatory, fragmented development?

A Bold Decentralization Push—or a Political Power Grab?

Under current South Korean law, the Mayor of Seoul holds exclusive authority to designate official urban renewal and redevelopment zones. This centralization was designed as a crucial safeguard to preserve a unified master plan for a metropolis of nearly 10 million people. However, as housing shortages have intensified, government leaders across the political spectrum have demanded aggressive "speed battles" to streamline zoning and environmental approvals.

Capitalizing on this pressure, a coalition of borough offices spearheaded by Dongjak District formed a dedicated task force. The group rallied working-level officials from 15 to 16 boroughs—including Gangbuk, Gangdong, Gangseo, Guro, Geumcheon, Nowon, Seodaemun, Seocho, Seongdong, Seongbuk, Yongsan, Eunpyeong, Jung-gu, and Jungnang. Their goal was to force a revision of the Enforcement Decree of the Act on the Improvement of Urban Areas and Dwelling Conditions for Residents.

Rather than using household counts (which fluctuate constantly during architectural design revisions), the coalition agreed on an objective geographic boundary: sites of 30,000 square meters or less. Furthermore, the district mayors demanded that the threshold for "minor revisions" to urban plans—changes that district heads can execute without Seoul City Hall’s approval—be doubled from less than 10% to less than 20%. This would allow local borough heads to alter building coverage ratios, floor area ratios (FAR), base infrastructure scales, and public facility uses entirely on their own authority.

For borough mayors, obtaining this power is politically irresistible. Local borough heads are elected officials whose reelection prospects depend heavily on delivering tangible local construction projects. If the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport reviews the amendment and the Cabinet approves it, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—which holds a legislative majority—could swiftly codify the change to support its national housing drive. But beneath the rhetoric of local autonomy lies a calculated political play to capture control over lucrative redevelopment projects.

The Promise of Speed Versus the Trap of Fragmented Development

From a purely administrative perspective, granting borough mayors direct designation rights offers clear operational advantages:

  • Explosive Activation of Small Sites: Elected borough heads, eager to build a visible track record before the next local elections, will actively champion neighborhood renewals that previously lingered in bureaucratic limbo.
  • Elimination of Administrative "Ping-Pong": Currently, redevelopment plans bounce back and forth between borough offices and Seoul City Hall. A single paperwork submission can spend six weeks in transit, repeating three or four times across seven to eight distinct approval gates. Consolidating the process under a single borough window eliminates years of friction.
  • Hyper-Local Planning Expertise: City Hall bureaucrats evaluate neighborhood requests using top-down maps. Borough staff, who know every dead-end alley, parking shortage, and fire-hazard block in their jurisdiction, are better positioned to tailor micro-level urban plans.

However, real estate analysts warn that the policy carries five severe structural flaws that could backfire on residents:

The most dangerous risk is artificial zone splitting. Consider a decaying 50,000-square-meter neighborhood that needs integrated development to accommodate proper schools, wider roads, and large public parks. To avoid Seoul City Hall’s rigorous oversight and fast-track a project within their electoral term, borough officials will face an overwhelming temptation to carve that land into two smaller 25,000-square-meter zones. Slicing land into small, disconnected plots strips neighborhoods of essential civic infrastructure.

Furthermore, local project continuity would become vulnerable to local political cycles. Whenever a local election brings in a new borough mayor, ongoing projects spearheaded by the predecessor could be stalled or scrapped. Other major risks include an absolute shortage of qualified urban planning personnel inside local borough offices, hyper-localized border disputes between adjacent property owners, and intense local political lobbying by special interest groups.

Seoul City Strikes Back: Exposing the Real Bottlenecks

Faced with a loss of municipal control, the Seoul Metropolitan Government (SMG) launched an aggressive counterattack. In an official briefing, Kim Dong-gu, SMG’s Director General of Architectural Planning, systematically dismantled the district mayors' arguments.

City Hall points out that of the nine major administrative authorization gates required for a redevelopment project—ranging from committee approval and union establishment to business execution and management disposition—borough heads already control seven. Seoul City Hall controls only two: the initial Urban Planning Committee review for zone designation, and the final integrated review.

More importantly, data published by the city refutes the claim that City Hall is the bottleneck. A full redevelopment project takes an average of 12 years from conception to ground-breaking. Seoul City’s two review stages take an average of just four months combined—accounting for a mere 2.7% of the total project timeline. Over the past three years, Seoul’s Urban Planning Committee processed applications in an average of 84 days with a 90% pass rate, while integrated reviews took just 32 days with a 97% pass rate.

Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon strongly criticized the proposal during a City Council session, calling it a "politically motivated demand by those who do not understand what actually happens on the ground." Oh argued that piecemeal developments under 800 units undermine broad regional master plans, leaving neighborhoods with severe infrastructure imbalances.

The Data Behind the Delays: Out of 193 active small-scale redevelopment sites (under 500 households) in Seoul, only 31 sites (16%) are currently waiting at the zone designation stage. The remaining 84% are stalled inside borough-level administrative procedures.

An internal survey by Seoul City revealed a startling lack of institutional capacity: 17 out of 25 local borough offices do not have a single employee with experience handling Urban Planning Committee reviews. Rotational civil service assignments mean borough staff lack the specialized legal expertise required to navigate complex urban renewal laws. In districts like Changsin 9 and 10, projects that met all legal criteria were delayed for months by borough offices under the guise of "conflict resolution."

This reality prompted the Seoul Redevelopment Association (an alliance representing approximately 65 redevelopment project leaders) to protest outside City Hall, demanding that the authority transfer be scrapped. Project leaders argue that shifting power to borough mayors will create inconsistent local standards, exacerbate internal union feuds, and subject projects to local political interference.

Market Realities: High Costs, Extreme Polarization, and Picking Winners

While politicians argue over authority, property investors are focused on basic economics. Small-scale redevelopment projects face an inherent disadvantage: high per-unit construction costs and low profit margins due to limited floor area for commercial sales.

In Seoul's prime redevelopment zones, early entry has already become prohibitively expensive. In Jayang 4-dong (A-Zone), located near Jayang Station in Gwangjin District, acquiring a modest, aging villa requires an upfront premium (웃돈) of 1.2 billion to 1.4 billion KRW (approximately $880,000 to $1.02 million USD) on top of the property's base value. In Yongsan District's Wonhyo-ro 1-ga zone, premiums range from 600 million to 1 billion KRW ($440,000 to $730,000 USD), pricing out everyday buyers.

If designation authority is transferred to local boroughs, speculative capital will likely chase early-stage "pre-designation" properties, hoping to flip them for quick profits once borough mayors fast-track approvals. However, market analysts warn against blind speculation. Because small developments lack scale, only sites with exceptional locational advantages will generate meaningful returns.

Two prime examples highlight where small-scale developments can succeed:

  • Yongsan Cheongpa Transit-Oriented Zone: Spanning just 19,772 square meters (well below the 30,000 m² limit), this site behind Seoul Station leverages high-density transit incentives to secure a floor area ratio (FAR) of over 400%. This allows the small plot to yield 700 units, overcoming small-scale cost penalties through vertical density.
  • Gwangjin-gu Guui-dong Moa Town: Covering 22,427 square meters, this site qualifies for small-scale designation while planning a high-density complex of 600 units. Situated three subway stops from Gangnam and adjacent to Konkuk University's expansive, low-rise campus, the site guarantees permanent, unblockable open views—a rarity that Commands premium valuations in Seoul.

Without high FAR incentives or permanent open views, small redevelopment sites risk heavy financial losses for property owners when construction cost overruns wipe out margins.

Reflecting on these market dynamics, the rush toward deregulation raises difficult questions about urban policy. During Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, unaddressed real estate project financing (PF) risks and aggressive market-floating measures contributed to severe market distress, leaving many young buyers trapped in high-interest debt and rental fraud schemes. While expanding housing supply is critical to stabilizing home prices for Seoul's younger generation, deregulating urban planning without strong safeguards risks prioritizing election-driven political wins over sustainable urban growth.

What to Watch Next

As the debate moves from City Hall to the national legislature, several key milestones will determine whether Seoul's urban landscape undergoes a historic decentralization:

  • MOLIT and Cabinet Review: The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport must evaluate the Council of District Mayors' proposed amendment to the Enforcement Decree. Watch whether the ministry adopts the 30,000-square-meter standard or attempts to reintroduce tighter household limits.
  • City Hall’s Legislative Strategy: Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon is lobbying the national government and city council to block the revision, arguing that master-plan controls are vital for citywide infrastructure.
  • Borough Office Hiring and Training: If the decree passes, monitor whether the 17 boroughs currently lacking experienced urban planning personnel launch specialized hiring initiatives, or if administrative bottlenecks simply migrate from City Hall down to district offices.
  • Early "Pre-Designation" Market Shifts: Real estate capital flows into low-rise villa clusters in districts like Dongjak, Mapo, and Yongsan will indicate whether retail investors believe borough mayors can actually deliver on their fast-track promises.
