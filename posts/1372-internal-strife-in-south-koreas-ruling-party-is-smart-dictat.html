Internal Strife in South Korea's Ruling Party: Is "Smart Dictatorship" Fracturing the Democratic Party?

October 21, 2026

For those watching South Korean politics, the current atmosphere inside the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the ruling party led by President Lee Jae-myung—has shifted from vibrant debate to a tense, suffocating silence. Despite the party’s massive legislative control and the successful inauguration of the Lee administration, which promised a society of "suppressing the strong and supporting the weak" (a core tenet of the President’s political philosophy), the mood on the ground is grim.

The party is currently embroiled in a bitter internal power struggle, characterized by a growing backlash against the leadership of Representative Kim Min-seok and a surprising, nostalgic longing for the leadership style of former party leader Jung Chung-rae. This is more than a simple factional feud; it is a fundamental debate over what democratic governance should look like in a party that once prided itself on diversity and inclusion.

The Crisis of Leadership: "Speaking Freely is Risky"

Recent reports suggest that the DPK has become a place where senior lawmakers fear to tread. According to reports from the Seoul Economic Daily, one veteran lawmaker noted, "There is an atmosphere where we all have to watch what we say. People are afraid that if they speak out, they might be warned or subjected to official investigations."

This "chilling effect" marks a significant departure from the DPK’s historical culture of dynamic, democratic discourse. The leadership’s tendency to reach for disciplinary measures and internal investigations whenever dissent arises has earned it a reputation for what critics call "smart dictatorship"—a style of management where decisions are standardized and dissenting voices are suppressed under the guise of unity. When the demand for a "single line of command" to support the President morphs into "listen only to me," the party’s vitality begins to wither.

In contrast, there is a growing yearning for the leadership of former party leader Jung Chung-rae. Supporters and party members alike recall his ability to be a "cider" (a Korean slang term for a refreshing, straight-talking person)—someone who could fight hard against opponents while remaining willing to apologize for his own party's missteps. His leadership was built on direct, bottom-up communication with party members, not the wielding of disciplinary blades.

The Fall of Kim Min-seok and the "House Rabbit" Warning

The sentiment toward Representative Kim Min-seok, by contrast, has turned frigid. Within the party, there is open talk that his leadership has been defined by a series of strategic missteps and an inability to reconcile with other factions. The controversy over his unilateral decision-making—particularly regarding the appointment of unelected supreme council members—has alienated even his colleagues.

This internal friction is causing alarm among the party’s core supporters, often referred to as the "house rabbits" (the loyal base). Jung Chung-rae once famously warned: "Even if we catch the 'mountain rabbits' (swing voters), if the 'house rabbits' leave the house, the general and presidential elections are hopeless."

The current leadership appears to be ignoring this warning, viewing internal criticism as a nuisance rather than a vital component of party health. If the DPK continues to dismiss its core base as merely "extreme fandom," it risks losing the very foundation that allowed it to rise to power. Supporters who once felt empowered by the party now feel alienated, wondering if the DPK has lost the democratic spirit that was once its greatest asset.

The "Smart Dictatorship" and the Struggle for Reform

The DPK finds itself in an ironic position: it holds a "structural majority" in the National Assembly, yet many supporters are asking, "Why hasn't the world changed, even with this much power?" The party’s ambitious reform agenda—covering areas like judicial and media reform—is stalling, caught in the gears of internal infighting rather than external progress.

The "smart dictatorship" mentioned by critics is not about brute force, but rather the sophisticated use of public relations and the deliberate cutting off of communication channels to neutralize internal opposition. By framing legitimate concerns as "division" or "factionalism," the leadership is effectively insulating itself from the very people it claims to represent.

Furthermore, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces a relentless campaign of "demonization" by the prosecution and conservative media. While the President secured a remarkable 49% of the vote despite an onslaught of investigations, the party’s failure to consolidate internally makes it harder to defend against these external attacks. The public, especially swing voters, values competence and efficacy. When the DPK appears as a house divided, it fails to project the image of a party capable of managing the nation.

What to Watch Next

As the DPK moves forward, three key indicators will determine whether it can recover its democratic soul:

  • Resolution of the Supreme Council Conflict: Will the leadership retract or justify its unilateral personnel decisions, or will it double down? Further alienation of the "pro-Jung Chung-rae" faction could lead to a permanent fracture.
  • Management of External Investigations: How the party responds to ongoing pressures, such as the recent search-and-seizure operations involving progressive media outlets, will show whether it can regain its role as a unified, resilient opposition to the conservative forces currently out of power (and, in the case of former President-turned-prisoner, Yun Seok-yeol, behind bars).
  • Reclaiming the "Middle": The party’s ability to prove its competence in implementing real-life changes for citizens will be the final test. If it continues to prioritize internal discipline over policy results, its lead with swing voters will almost certainly evaporate.

The DPK is at a crossroads. The current leadership must decide if it wants to lead a party of yes-men or a party of partners. If it continues to stifle internal dissent, it may find that the "smart" path to control leads directly to political obsolescence.
