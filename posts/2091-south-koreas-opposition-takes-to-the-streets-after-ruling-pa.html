South Korea’s Opposition Takes to the Streets After Ruling Party Rams Through Special Counsel Bill

November 07, 2026

On November 6, the plaza in front of South Korea’s National Assembly in Seoul turned into a battleground of loud megaphones, red banners, and intense political theater. Moments after the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) used its parliamentary power to pass a contentious piece of legislation known as the Fabricated Prosecution Special Counsel Act, lawmakers from the main opposition People Power Party (PPP) walked out of the plenary chamber in unison and assembled directly on the building’s front steps.

Surrounded by party staff and media crews, the opposition leadership declared an immediate move to extra-parliamentary rallies, effectively breaking off routine legislative cooperation with the government. "Today Lee Jae-myung lives and South Korea dies," opposition lawmakers shouted in unison, aiming their attacks directly at the sitting president. The fierce rally signaled a complete breakdown of inter-party dialogue in the National Assembly, plunging South Korean politics into a deep freeze at a time when major legislative and economic decisions hang in the balance.

To understand why a single special counsel bill has sparked such an intense confrontation, one must look at the structural fault lines of South Korea’s current political landscape. Under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling DPK, the government has pushed aggressively to reform the nation’s legal and investigative institutions. The DPK’s central argument for the new law rests on addressing what it describes as systematic overreach and political weaponization by prosecutors during the tenure of former president Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was subsequently impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned. From the ruling party’s perspective, appointing a special counsel to investigate past instances of "fabricated prosecutions" is a necessary mechanism to correct historical miscarriages of justice and hold rogue prosecutors accountable.

For the conservative opposition People Power Party, however, the bill is seen in a dramatically different light. PPP leaders view the legislation not as a genuine effort at judicial reform, but as an unconstitutional attempt to rewrite history and shield President Lee Jae-myung from legal scrutiny. By creating a special counsel mechanism aimed at reviewing past indictments and dropped charges, the PPP argues that the ruling majority is effectively attempting to override the judiciary’s constitutional authority and erase the legal record of the sitting administration’s key figures.

A Legislative Flashpoint at the National Assembly

The immediate catalyst for the crisis came on the afternoon of November 6, when the DPK brought the Fabricated Prosecution Special Counsel Act to the floor of the National Assembly. Refusing to participate in what they characterized as a unilateral exercise of power, members of the People Power Party boycotted the final vote and staged a dramatic walkout.

Outside the main hall, PPP leaders held a mass condemnation rally to formally launch their out-of-assembly campaign. Taking the microphone, PPP Leader Jang Dong-hyeok declared November 6 to be "the day constitutional rule of law died" in South Korea. Delivering an unusually aggressive speech, Jang launched an explicit assault on the sitting government, declaring that President Lee Jae-myung's administration possessed no intention of preserving the constitution. Jang went so far as to call for President Lee to be removed from office, framing the opposition’s newly declared street struggle as a fight to bring down what he described as an authoritarian leadership.

Joining him at the podium, PPP Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik directed his criticism at the substance of the legislation itself. Jeong denounced the special counsel bill as an outrageously partisan measure designed explicitly to grant immunity to the president and dismantle the independence of the prosecution. Following the speeches outside parliament, opposition members and supporters marched beyond the Assembly grounds toward key public areas in Seoul, including routes leading toward Gwanghwamun and the presidential area, making clear that their resistance would no longer be confined to committee rooms and parliamentary debate.

The Framing War: Shielding the President vs. Reforming Political Prosecution

The clash over the special counsel bill highlights a fundamental divide over the nature of South Korean democracy and the rule of law. The two major political camps are operating under entirely incompatible narratives, creating a rhetorical void where compromise is virtually impossible.

  • The Ruling Party Perspective: The Democratic Party maintains that past investigative practices—particularly during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure—corrupted the prosecution service into a political weapon. For the DPK, establishing a special counsel to re-examine suspicious or fabricated indictments is an essential step toward restoring public trust in law enforcement and protecting citizens from abusive state power.
  • The Opposition Perspective: The People Power Party argues that using a legislative majority to mandate special counsel investigations into past prosecutorial decisions undermines the judicial branch's independence. They view the law as a "bulletproof shield" crafted to retroactively clean the legal slate for President Lee Jae-myung and his political allies.

This framing war is not limited to legal theory; it is spilling over into broader policy and budgetary fights. Following the passage of the special counsel bill, the PPP announced that it would also seek to completely scrap major government initiatives, including the administration’s proposed Future Response Fund. By threatening to block key fiscal programs and leveraging every procedural tool—from filibusters and parliamentary audits to street rallies—the opposition is signaling its intent to cripple the ruling party's policy agenda across the board.

The intensity of the dispute was reflected across the domestic media landscape on November 6, when over 70 distinct news headlines dominated major Korean news outlets within a single day. Reports across major newspapers and broadcasters underscored a shared observation: formal legislative process has given way to raw political warfare.

From Parliament to the Streets: The Opposition’s High-Stakes Gamble

By taking its fight out of the National Assembly and onto the streets, the People Power Party is embarking on a high-stakes strategy with significant political risks. Extra-parliamentary rallies (known locally as jangwae tujiang) are a long-standing tactic in South Korean political history, often deployed by minority parties when they feel completely outmaneuvered by a ruling majority inside the legislature.

In the short term, rallying on the streets can energize the conservative base, signaling uncompromising ideological resistance against the DPK government. It provides a platform for party leaders like Jang Dong-hyeok and Jeong Jeom-sik to rally core supporters and project strength. However, extended street politics carries clear long-term vulnerabilities. When an opposition party abandons parliamentary negotiations to rely primarily on street rallies and calls for presidential impeachment, it risks alienating moderate voters who expect elected officials to work within constitutional institutions to solve pressing national issues.

When political disputes shift from committee rooms to megaphones and street marches, the primary casualty is governance itself. South Korea faces complex domestic economic pressures and shifting international dynamics, yet the institutional machinery required to address these challenges is currently gridlocked by partisan hostility. As both major parties treat each other as adversaries to be defeated rather than partners in governance, the public space for pragmatic policymaking continues to shrink.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters a period of heightened political volatility following the November 6 vote, several key developments will determine how this crisis unfolds over the coming weeks:

  • Budgetary Deadlock: Watch how the PPP’s pledge to scrap the administration’s Future Response Fund impacts upcoming legislative negotiations. With the opposition in full-combat mode, routine budget reviews could easily stall, threatening government spending plans.
  • Momentum of Street Demonstrations: Pay attention to whether the PPP can sustain its out-of-assembly rallies around Seoul, and whether its aggressive rhetoric demanding the removal of President Lee Jae-myung succeeds in mobilizing broader public participation or triggers a backlash from moderate voters.
  • Implementation of the Special Counsel: Track the formal steps to appoint and launch the Special Counsel for Fabricated Prosecution. How the newly appointed special counsel defines its scope—and how judicial bodies react—will be a crucial test for the stability of South Korea's legal institutions.
