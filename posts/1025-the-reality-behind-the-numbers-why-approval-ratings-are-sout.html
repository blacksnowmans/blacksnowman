The Reality Behind the Numbers: Why Approval Ratings are South Korea’s True Scorecard

September 23, 2026

Every Monday in South Korea, the media cycle is dominated by the latest presidential approval ratings. Released by polling agencies like Gallup Korea and Realmeter, these numbers are treated with the intensity of stock market charts—a volatile barometer of the national mood. The prominent South Korean writer and former politician Rhyu Si-min once famously observed that "presidential approval ratings don't just appear out of thin air; they begin in the actual, lived reality of the people." This simple sentiment is the key to understanding the joys, sorrows, and tectonic shifts of South Korean politics.

It is easy to fall into the trap of viewing these ratings as mere products of media framing or calculated political events. We often blame the media for manipulating public opinion or critique YouTube algorithms for creating echo chambers that reinforce confirmation bias. While those factors certainly play a role, they are secondary to the "real world" that voters wake up to every morning. Before any headline hits, the collective reality of the citizenry acts as the massive engine driving these fluctuations. Ultimately, approval ratings are not just statistics; they are a report card on the quality of life.

The True Foundation of Approval: Life Beyond the Spreadsheet

The Cost of Living as the Ultimate Metric

Behind every percentage point released by pollsters lies the existential economic anxiety of millions. The price of groceries, the crushing burden of monthly mortgage interest, and the uncertainty of whether this month's paycheck can actually sustain a household—these are the "base physical condition" of a nation's political support. While high economic indicators do not always guarantee high approval, history shows that it is rare for a government to maintain high ratings when the basic sense of safety in daily life collapses.

For the average South Korean, employment statistics and household income are not academic concepts. They represent the desperate struggle to keep a job or the painful decision to cut back on a child’s private tutoring (a major expense in the Korean household budget). Politicians often forget that these individual experiences aggregate into the massive waves of public opinion. No matter how brilliantly a president markets their policies, those words cannot override the helplessness a citizen feels at the supermarket checkout counter.

In this sense, approval ratings serve as both a request and a warning: a demand for the government to protect the lives of its people. When the gap between the government's rhetoric and the citizen's reality manifests in the polls, it is an indictment of the administration's failures. Dismissing low numbers as "biased respondents" or "faulty polling methods" is merely a refusal to face the mirror.

Cracks in the Social Safety Net

Stability in public services—policing, healthcare, education, and infrastructure—is just as vital as economic health. These services are the primary measures by which citizens judge a government’s reliability. When the news is filled with daily crises and the government appears incompetent in its response, it erodes the public’s psychological safety net. When the feeling that "this government does not care about my life" takes hold, support withers rapidly.

Social stability is a baseline expectation of the state. When the education system feels disconnected from reality, or the medical system faces paralysis, it becomes a toxic force that destroys emotional support, even if macroeconomic figures look impressive. Citizens prioritize the safety of their neighborhoods and the futures of their children over grand political discourse. When that priority is ignored, the polls start to fluctuate violently.

📊 By the Numbers

A 30% or 40% approval rating is not just data; it is a collective response to the question: "Does the nation's current direction align with my daily reality?" As Rhyu Si-min suggested, power comes with responsibility. The current 30% range often seen in South Korean polls serves as a real-time feedback system, showing that the government has failed to create tangible improvements from the perspective of the average citizen.

The Distortion of Reality: Media and Political Framing

Truth Lost in the Frame Wars

Public opinion, born in the reality of everyday life, is often refracted through the prisms of media and partisan politics. Certain outlets inflate economic figures to praise government policy, while others amplify the suffering of citizens to criticize it. In this process, the fragments of truth vanish, and voters are forced to view the world through "frames" created by political parties. YouTube and social media only accelerate this bias.

This is why modern polling is rarely a simple summation of public will. Citizens trapped in ideological camps view the exact same economic situation through entirely different lenses. It has become common to see one group claim that inflation has decreased due to brilliant policy, while another insists they are suffering because of that same policy. The process of information distribution itself has become a battlefield for political struggle.

The Disconnect of Political Communication

The language used by politicians is often completely detached from the temperature of the streets. While citizens struggle with high costs of living, the political sphere remains fixated on judicial risks or partisan infighting. This lack of empathy breeds deep disillusionment. The moment the public perceives that politics provides no help to their actual lives, the government loses its momentum.

Political communication should be a process of winning hearts, not just transmitting information. When those in power use the language of authority rather than the language of the people, they inevitably become isolated. Approval ratings serve as the most accurate warning sign against such arrogance.

🔥 A Core Problem to Address

The greatest ailment in current Korean politics is the "taxidermy of public opinion through partisan logic." When belonging to a camp takes precedence over facts, social consensus becomes impossible. The clash between a "hardcore base" that won't abandon a leader regardless of scandals and an "opposition" that will never accept any success is the primary factor driving the regression of our politics.

The Rigidity of Support and the Middle-Class Dilemma

Unlike in the past, approval ratings in South Korea have become remarkably rigid. Driven by intense fandom and extreme polarization, core supporters often refuse to abandon their leaders even in the face of major mistakes. This is a symptom of the "religionization" of politics, where citizens equate their personal identity with a politician, turning polling into a tool for cheering rather than rational evaluation.

While this provides a temporary shield for the government, it ultimately leads to stagnation and incompetence. A culture that silences internal criticism in favor of blind following degrades the operational capacity of the state. However, even this rigid structure eventually faces the brutal reality of a collapsing quality of life. No amount of fandom can mask the pain when the foundations of daily life crumble.

In this landscape, the real victims are the moderate and independent voters. They crave practical change rather than partisan bickering, yet both sides focus only on inciting these groups to pick a side. When the middle ground becomes silent or disillusioned, the possibility of social consensus fades. The task for South Korean society is to move beyond the fluctuating numbers and listen to the diverse voices hidden beneath them. If the political establishment truly wants to serve the country, it must stop the "frame wars" and face the pain on the ground head-on.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on how the government addresses the "middle-class squeeze" in the coming months. If policymakers continue to prioritize partisan base-building over tangible cost-of-living relief, watch for the approval ratings of the moderate, independent demographic to drop further—a shift that historically precedes major electoral realignments in South Korea.
