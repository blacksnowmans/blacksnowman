A Hollow Apology and the Ghosts of 1980s Activism: Behind the Ruling Democratic Party’s Internal Clash

November 06, 2026

When a ruling party finds itself in the middle of a political storm, the words chosen by its leadership can either steady the ship or expose the fault lines beneath its deck. Inside South Korea's governing Democratic Party of Korea, an escalating internal dispute has laid bare a dynamic far uglier than a mere communications misstep. At the center of the furor stands party leader Kim Min-seok, whose response to simmering allegations involving the controversial religious group Shincheonji has ignited fierce pushback from party members and sparked an uncomfortable reckoning with the factional habits of South Korea’s political elite.

For observers outside South Korea, the episode might look like an ordinary intraparty squabble. Yet, set against the backdrop of the administration of President Lee Jae-myung—which relies heavily on legislative discipline to carry out its agenda on economic reform and public welfare—the clash reveals how deeply the culture of the 1980s student activist generation still shapes legislative behavior. Rather than demonstrating institutional accountability, party leaders have fallen back on decades-old factional playbooks: dismissive apologies, attempts to silence internal dissent, and cynical appeals to unity.

The 'Political Judgment' Excuse: A Defective Apology Over Shincheonji Ties

The controversy stems from unresolved questions surrounding ties to Shincheonji, a secretive, fringe religious organization in South Korea that has frequently drawn intense public scrutiny and political hostility. For any mainstream politician, particularly within the center-left Democratic Party, proximity to the sect is toxic. When public and internal pressure mounted on Kim Min-seok to address the allegations, party members expected a direct, humble accounting of what transpired.

Instead, Kim offered an explanation couched in the language of pragmatic calculation. Presenting his past decisions as mere exercises in "political judgment" (jeongmujeok pandan), the leadership attempted to frame the issue not as an ethical lapse or a breach of trust, but as a minor miscalculation of circumstances. To rank-and-file party members and external critics, this defense felt like an insult to their intelligence.

An authentic apology requires a clear statement of fault and a willingness to shoulder responsibility. By retreating behind the vague, technocratic shield of political judgment, Kim avoided acknowledging the underlying wrongdoing. Far from putting the issue to rest, this transactional approach to public trust provoked accusations that the leadership treats party ethics as a poker game. When leaders hide behind political jargon instead of offering genuine contrition, public faith collapses, leaving behind an impression of cynical self-preservation rather than statesmanship.

Weaponizing Sacrifice and Hiding Behind the Public Welfare Shield

As anger inside the party mounted, senior figures aligned with the leadership sought to shut down the debate. Lawmakers such as Kim Young-jin stepped into the fray, publicly urging the party to move past the Shincheonji controversy and redirect all energy toward bread-and-butter economic issues, commonly referred to in South Korean politics as minsaeng (people's livelihoods). On the surface, the call sounded noble. A governing party ought to prioritize the economic wellbeing of ordinary citizens over internal wrangling.

In practice, however, this appeal functions as a time-tested diversionary tactic. Critics quickly pointed out the hypocrisy: the very figures who sparked the crisis were now invoking the public interest to shield themselves from accountability. Using public welfare as a rhetorical cudgel against internal critics turns a vital governing priority into a tool for self-defense. Anyone raising legitimate ethical concerns is instantly branded as an agitator engaging in destructive "friendly fire" while the nation struggles.

This dynamic reached a particularly sour note when voices within the leadership camp began citing the past sacrifice of former party leader Jung Cheong-rae. Years earlier, Jung was controversially dropped from the candidate nomination list—an exclusion known locally as a "cutoff"—yet chose not to leave the party. Instead, he embarked on what Koreans call baek-ui-jong-gun, an idiom derived from military history meaning to serve dedicatedly from the lowest ranks without an official title, campaigning tirelessly across the country to secure a general election victory for his colleagues.

Rather than honoring Jung’s historic loyalty as a model of selfless dedication, the current leadership has distorted the precedent. It is now being weaponized as a moral muzzle: a message to Jung and other critical voices that they, too, should swallow their objections, keep quiet, and fall in line. Conscripting another politician's past suffering to protect current leadership from scrutiny is a breach of basic political decency. When sacrificial loyalty is repurposed into an instrument of intimidation, party democracy gives way to authoritarian control.

This pattern of suppressing dissent under the guise of unity is not unprecedented. A similar dynamic took hold during the tenure of former party leader Lee Nak-yon, when legitimate internal criticisms were routinely dismissed as unhelpful friction. The Democratic Party today risks repeating those very mistakes, disabling its own internal checks and balances just when the Lee Jae-myung administration needs a healthy, responsive legislative partner.

The Undongkwon Playbook: Three Decades of Student Faction Tactics in Modern Power

To understand why this political culture persists, one must trace its lineage back to the 1980s and 1990s student democracy movement, known as the undongkwon. As pointed out by political commentator and journalist Jang Yong-jin, the current tactics used to marginalize rivals are direct descendants of the fierce factional clashes of that era, particularly the bitter sectarian divides between the National Liberation (NL) faction, which focused heavily on anti-imperialism and reunification, and the People's Democracy (PD) faction, which emphasized class struggle and labor rights.

During those campus election wars, victory was rarely secured through transparent policy debate alone. Instead, factional operatives relied on coordinated whisper campaigns, character assassination, and manufactured rumors to delegitimize their ideological opponents. Men who cut their teeth in those underground political struggles learned that power is won by annihilating rivals morally before defeating them numerically.

Today, that student activist style manifests in a recognizable three-step cycle:

  • Stage One: Neutralize intraparty opponents and alternative factions through smear campaigns, distorted narratives, and reputational damage.
  • Stage Two: Upon taking power or weathering a storm, refuse to issue a sincere, unreserved apology for any collateral damage or misdeeds committed along the way.
  • Stage Three: Demand immediate post-election reconciliation, insisting that all members join as "one team" under the banner of unity, while treating ongoing criticism as treachery.

This forced harmony is not reconciliation; it is coerced surrender. Demanding that victims of character assassination immediately unite with their detractors without any acknowledgment of wrongdoing is an act of political gaslighting. It strips the party of genuine intellectual vitality and replaces principled debate with top-down enforcement. The rigid, vertical hierarchy that once characterized revolutionary student committees has been imported directly into the governing halls of a modern democracy, where lawmakers are expected to keep their heads down, refrain from speaking out, and follow orders without question.

What to Watch Next

A ruling party that relies on obedience rather than consensus cannot indefinitely sustain governing momentum. For the administration of President Lee Jae-myung to execute its policy goals and preserve long-term public confidence, the party supporting it must demonstrate that it is capable of self-correction. Clinging to the antiquated survival tactics of the student activist era will only widen the distance between the Democratic Party and a voting public weary of political theater.

In the coming weeks and months, several concrete fault lines will reveal whether this factional culture can be dismantled or if it will further harden:

  • The Shincheonji explanations: Watch whether Kim Min-seok and his inner circle are compelled by grassroots party members to offer a substantive, transparent accounting of the allegations, moving beyond the dismissive "political judgment" talking point.
  • The pushback from critical factions: Pay close attention to how independent voices and senior figures like Jung Cheong-rae respond to the leadership’s pressure tactics. If they openly reject the demand to stay quiet, internal debate could force structural reforms in how the party manages dissent.
  • Legislative coordination with the Presidential Office: Monitor how smoothly the party apparatus processes the Lee Jae-myung administration's priority bills. If factional paranoia consumes leadership bandwidth, policy delivery on economic reform will inevitably stall, providing an opening for the conservative opposition People Power Party to capitalize on the governing coalition's internal fractures.
