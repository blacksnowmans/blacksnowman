Selective Justice and Power: The Controversy Over the Investigation into Han Dong-hoon's Daughter

October 12, 2026

When the scales of power tilt, the hidden face of inequality inevitably comes to light. South Korea’s legal system prides itself on a universal standard of justice, but recent parliamentary audits have dragged a deeply troubling case back into the spotlight. The police handling of allegations surrounding the daughter of Han Dong-hoon—a prominent lawmaker and former high-ranking official of the People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party—has laid bare just how selective the rule of law can be. What looks from the outside like a routine college admissions inquiry is actually a stress test for the country's entire system of fairness. For many ordinary citizens watching from the sidelines, the glaring disparity in how investigations are conducted depending on who holds power breeds a profound sense of disenfranchisement.

The Anatomy of an Investigation That Went Nowhere

Investigations are fundamentally acts of institutional will. When a law enforcement agency genuinely wants to uncover the truth, standard tools like search warrants and witness summonses are deployed without hesitation. Yet, the investigation into the alleged falsified credentials of Han Dong-hoon’s daughter followed a radically different script over an agonizing 20-month period. Lawmakers, including Representative Yoon Kun-young of the liberal ruling Democratic Party, pointed out during recent parliamentary audits that while the citizen who originally filed the complaint was summoned and interrogated six times, the primary subject of the accusation—the suspect herself—was subjected to a single written questionnaire.

This is far outside normal investigative practice. Even Kim Jong-chul, acting commissioner-general of the Korean National Police Agency, conceded during the audit that the procedure was "not standard." Raising the procedural hurdles for accusers while granting the child of a powerful figure the comfort of answering questions from home sends a chilling signal to the public. For nearly two years, this case appears to have been treated as untouchable, shelved quietly out of sight.

In assessing the integrity of any probe, the presence or absence of compulsory investigative measures is the ultimate litmus test. To spend 20 months without securing a single search warrant is tantamount to admitting a total lack of intent to investigate. The public is left asking a bitter question: Why do the same police and prosecutors who once poured 131 search warrants into a separate high-profile political case suddenly wield a dull, blunted knife when the target belongs to the political elite?

Excuses, Red Tape, and Dead Ends Abroad

Law enforcement agencies frequently hide behind the complexities of international mutual legal assistance when a case stalls overseas. Yet the police response in this instance plumbed new depths of institutional irresponsibility. When the anti-corruption unit of the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency sent an overseas assistance request to the United States, prosecutors rejected it, arguing that the request failed to clearly demonstrate why the information was strictly necessary. Instead of pushing back, refiling, or appealing the decision, the police simply folded their hands and gave up.

Worse still was the handling of unreturned records from overseas institutions. Out of 14 requested sets of foreign data, three never arrived. Rather than acknowledging that foreign authorities failed to reply, police officials conveniently reclassified the missing documents as cases where "no relevant regulations existed"—as if non-response and non-existence were interchangeable concepts. Even the acting police chief acknowledged under questioning that these concepts are fundamentally different. This semantic sleight-of-hand suggests an investigation engineered from the start to arrive at a predetermined conclusion.

By relying exclusively on domestic records while abandoning efforts to secure vital foreign evidence, investigators ensured the probe would remain toothless. It was a performance designed to look like work while guaranteeing that no inconvenient truths would emerge.

Two Cases, Two Realities: The Double Standard in South Korean Policing

To understand the depth of public cynicism, one must look at how starkly this investigation contrasts with other high-profile probes. Take the investigation into Kim Hye-kyung, wife of current President Lee Jae-myung. Over a roughly six-month period, that investigation saw authorities execute 131 search warrants, summon numerous witnesses, and ultimately forward the case for indictment. By contrast, the investigation into Han Dong-hoon’s daughter accumulated a pristine record of zero search warrants and zero witness interviews over 20 months.

It requires a suspension of disbelief to accept that these two vastly different outcomes were produced by the same national police force. As opposition lawmakers have asked, would an ordinary citizen facing identical allegations be granted such gentle, hands-off treatment? Sweeping aside mountains of missing evidence with the excuse that none could be found—while relentlessly combing through the lives of political opponents—destroys the foundational public trust upon which legal institutions depend.

The damage goes deeper still. During a media briefing, National Office of Investigation chief Woo Jong-soo confidently claimed that a substantial number of witness interviews had been conducted. Subsequent checks revealed the actual number was zero. For a top-tier law enforcement official to feed outright fabrications to the press and the parliament is not merely a bureaucratic error; it points directly toward an organized effort to cover up a preferential investigation.

Calls for a Complete Re-examination

Lawmakers are now demanding an intensive internal audit by the national police headquarters to root out past and present institutional biases. While acting chief Kim Jong-chul has attempted to buy time by waiting for separate administrative audit results, the pressure to act is mounting. If the police genuinely believe in law and order, they must audit their own decision to clear the case without charges.

"New facts, if brought to light, mean a full re-investigation remains on the table," Kim acknowledged under pressure—an admission that comes late, but is entirely necessary. Should the original complainants file a fresh accusation, authorities will have no choice but to review the case from scratch.

The irony is heavy. Lawmakers like Han Dong-hoon built their political brands on demanding strict adherence to rules and accountability from others, dismissing his daughter's alleged paper-padding and volunteer record inflation as mere high school "practice." Yet when those same uncompromising standards are turned toward his own family, the response is evasion and silence. For a political figure who climbed to prominence waving the banner of institutional fairness, applying a double standard to one's own household is the ultimate form of political hypocrisy.

What to Watch Next

As this controversy continues to churn through the political landscape, several concrete developments will determine whether accountability prevails or the issue is successfully buried:

  • The Re-filing of Complaints: Watch for civil society groups and opposition lawmakers to submit fresh legal filings, which will legally force the police to formally address whether new evidence warrants reopening the case.
  • Ministry of Justice Audits: Keep an eye on ongoing administrative reviews within the justice apparatus, which may uncover procedural violations by investigators that make it impossible for leadership to maintain the current non-prosecution stance.
  • Internal Disciplinary Actions: Monitor whether the National Police Agency follows through on identifying and penalizing officials responsible for false public briefings regarding the number of witness interviews conducted.
  • Parliamentary Follow-up: The Democratic Party retains majorities and committee leverage to keep dragging law enforcement chiefs back to the National Assembly for questioning, ensuring the issue will not quietly fade from the headlines.

The core purpose of any police force is to apply the law equally to all citizens, regardless of their family tree or political alignment. When investigators turn themselves into shields for the powerful, ordinary people lose faith in the possibility of a level playing field. The scales of justice are supposed to be held by a blindfolded figure who cannot see who is standing before her. Unless South Korea's law enforcement agencies summon the courage to wipe away the stain of preferential treatment and launch a thorough re-investigation, that blindness will look less like neutrality and more like willful ignorance.
