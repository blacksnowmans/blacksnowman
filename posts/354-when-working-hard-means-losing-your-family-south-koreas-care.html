When Working Hard Means Losing Your Family: South Korea's Care Crisis

September 07, 2026

Glancing down at a smartphone screen on a crowded evening subway ride home, a familiar tightness often settles in the chest. People try to comfort themselves with the thought that everyone else lives this way, too. Yet staring blankly at the passing city lights outside the window, a fundamental sense of doubt creeps in: What are we actually running this hard for? For dual-income parents in their thirties and forties, that emptiness cuts especially deep. While busy working to secure financial stability after having children, what has actually happened to the modern Korean household? A recent documentary series from South Korea's EBS broadcasting network, Documentary K, exposes a painful reality that goes far beyond simply being "busy"—it shows the frayed edges of the one sanctuary that should be completely safe: the family.

Ironically, children are growing up largely in the absence of their parents. The daily life of five-year-old Lee Ru-ri captures the exhausting portrait of South Korean working mothers and fathers. Every morning, her mother, Lee Sun-ah, wages a small war to wake the child, wash her, dress her, and get her onto the 8:08 AM shuttle. A round-trip commute of nearly two hours drains the parents' physical stamina. Her father, Lee Ki-hyun, a career soldier of 12 years, rushes back late from work just to pick up his daughter—and when he is occasionally delayed, the child is left behind to cry alone. Left by herself late into the evening at a taekwondo academy, the little girl sobs that she misses her mother, only to flash a brave smile in front of the camera and say, "I'm not lonely. I know how to play by myself." That mature expression cuts all the more deeply into the hearts of those watching.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Long working hours for dual-income couples have created an absolute lack of time spent with children and a severe crisis in childcare.
  • As academic competition and materialism fill the void left by genuine family connection, South Korean youth happiness scores languish near the bottom globally.
  • Ultimately, the breakdown of family functions feeds into a massive vicious cycle of young generations avoiding childbirth and harboring deep distrust toward others.

The Grind of Dual-Income Life and the Reality of the Care Void

A Clockwork Routine From Dawn Until Late at Night

By comparison, the daily schedule of Byun Ji-hyun, a public elementary school after-school teacher, and her husband Yoon Dong-ho, a private school teacher, might seem manageable. Because the mother starts work at 11 AM, getting the children off to kindergarten is relatively straightforward. But looking beneath the surface is suffocating. Once the children board the shuttle around 9:20 AM, the parents have a brief 30-to-40-minute window to fold laundry and rush through household chores. Having spent five years trying through seven rounds of in vitro fertilization before finally having children, they feel profoundly grateful every moment, yet the crushing weight of reality squeezes them tight.

Commutes drag on, and points of contact with the children steadily vanish. As a rookie head of academic affairs, the father is regularly delayed until 7:30 or 8:00 PM. The mother is completely drained by school administration and classes, leaving her burdened by guilt when she cannot give her children her best care if they fall sick. "We send them to academies because we think it's better for them to experience things and enjoy themselves while they're young," they admit, but pick-up times limit these lessons strictly to arts and physical education; English hagwons (private cram schools) remain out of reach.

To bridge the gap between kindergarten dismissal and the parents' 7 PM return, children are sent to late-night extended-care daycares. Yoon Si-woo, a young boy, describes the experience with flat resignation in an interview: "Sometimes I'm bored, or I just play with toys, or play with broken toys, or just sit there. I play a little, or lie down when I'm tired." Even when parents return late at night and rush through dinner preparation in 30 or 40 minutes, the children are too exhausted to wait. The simple happiness of sitting down together for a family meal during the workweek has become a relic of the past in modern South Korea.

The Bitter Gender Gap in Domestic Labor

This burden of care goes far beyond a mere shortage of time. Figures from the South Korean Ministry of Gender Equality and Family’s affiliated Korean Women's Development Institute starkly indict the inequality within dual-income households. As of 2024, married women in dual-income families spent an average of 2 hours and 27 minutes per day on household management, compared to just 1 hour and 11 minutes for men. Women spend 1 hour and 16 minutes more per day on domestic labor than men—a 2.1-fold difference. On weekdays, that gap widens further: working women spend 2 hours and 13 minutes, while working men spend 58 minutes.

Time spent caring for family members averaged 1 hour and 31 minutes daily for women, compared to 1 hour and 19 minutes for men. Conversely, men spent an average of 6 hours and 52 minutes working at their jobs, roughly 50 minutes longer than women (6 hours and 2 minutes). Even after sweating at a job all day, women step straight onto another treadmill of domestic chores and caregiving. Consequently, the cultural and leisure time of dual-income women averaged 2 hours and 49 minutes daily—48 minutes less than men (3 hours and 37 minutes)—with the gap widening to 1 hour and 26 minutes on Sundays.

The message behind these heavy statistics is unmistakable. Even as the proportion of dual-income couples with children under 18 has jumped significantly over the past decade, and male parental leave hits record highs, the quality of life and labor intensity experienced inside the home remain profoundly imbalanced. Both men and women return from work exhausted, but that accumulated fatigue and pressure for career interruption are absorbed through personal sacrifice. No matter how much policies improve, if the social safety net for daily life remains frayed, those gaps will inevitably be filled by mothers' guilt and children's loneliness.

Children Lost Between Cram Schools and Late Nights

The childcare vacuum of the preschool years evolves into a monstrous academic pressure-cooker once children reach middle school. The daily schedule of Baek Seo-hee, an eighth-grader at Eunyang Middle School, is suffocating. Although she lives with her mother, who runs a private business, even a mother working from home often finds herself trapped in a separate space within the same house. With high-stakes testing starting in earnest this year, academic stress has reached a breaking point.

Hearing Seo-hee's schedule firsthand is startling. "When academy makeup classes are scheduled, I go straight from school to math, then English, and stay at the hagwon for about seven hours. When I get home, it's 11 PM, and then I study and always sleep past midnight." The fact that she cannot remember the last time her family ate a meal together on a weekday illustrates how isolated contemporary teenagers are within the walls of their own homes. Her mother, Lee Ji-hyun, knows this isn't right. Yet she offers a bitter defense: "They have to live in this world, and go to middle and high schools in Korea, so even though I know it's wrong, I keep pressuring her and asking if she's studied and prepared..."

Meeting friends at a tteokbokki (spicy rice cake) shop, Seo-hee finally lets out pent-up frustration. Her friend Kim Eun-chae vents about a reality where parents chase only grades and money, while Seo-hee confesses, "I wish someone would walk by, tap me on the shoulder, and say, 'Here, take this,' handing me 10 billion won [about $7.3 million]." Though she secretly posts cooking videos on social media without parental consent, dreaming of a different path, children facing the massive barrier of South Korea's educational reality abandon their own dreams and yearn for monetary escape. Can this truly be called a normal landscape of growth?

🔥 The Heavy Burden of "Family" and Forgotten Children

We often call family the world's ultimate refuge, but for today's teenagers and young adults, home has morphed from a haven into an extension of endless evaluation and competition. When parents return exhausted from long work hours and their few words of conversation turn into cross-examinations about grades and test scores, the isolation children feel reaches its peak.

The moment efficiency and resume-building muscle out love and connection, the family ceases to be a bond to lean on and becomes a heavy burden they want to escape.

South Korea's Self-Portrait: Rock-Bottom Subjective Well-Being for Youth

The Painful Truth of OECD's Lowest Well-Being Scores

An analysis by Professor Yum Yoo-shik of Yonsei University's Department of Sociology lays bare the raw reality we face. Set against an OECD country average of 100, the subjective well-being score of South Korean children and adolescents sits at a dismal 65 points, leaving them stuck near the very bottom for decades. While the figure of 65 points in 2010 briefly rebounded to 74 points in 2014 and 80.4 in 2019, it slumped back to 79.5 in 2021. The critical point is not the fluctuation of numbers, but the structural despair of never having risen to the upper tier.

In the fourth grade of elementary school, children cite family relationships as the value most necessary for happiness (45%), while money ranks lowest at 21%. By the third year of middle school, however, those values completely flip. The value of material wealth (money) surges to 39%, while relationships (family) plunge to 34%. We must deeply reflect on what this reversal means. Growing up, children instinctively realize that the family they ought to depend on is failing to function, crushed under the weight of long working hours and the pressures of life.

Parents, too, are so consumed by making a living that they lose the leisure to share emotional communion with their children, while schools transform from places of learning into battlefields for ranking. The anxiety of knowing that family cannot provide emotional protection naturally leads to the materialistic conviction that "the only thing that can protect me is money." This tragic transition—where the condition for happiness is swapped from relationships to capital—gnaws at the souls of children and chills the temperature of South Korean society as a whole.

Dinner Tables Transformed into Arenas of Debate and Critique

The domestic landscapes remembered by young adults who have reached maturity are an extension of this tragedy. Through "balance game" exercises exploring young people's inner thoughts, the answers break the heart. Those who choose "returning to the past to play on the playground with my parents" express a deep deprivation stemming from a total lack of such memories. More shockingly, not a single person chose "having dinner with family five times a week during adolescence" over "attending five cram schools every week."

The reasoning is piercing. Family dinner was never a time of warm comfort; it had warped into "an arena for debate," "a site for career planning," and "a place to point out my flaws." Even when they wanted nothing more than to rest desperately upon returning home, a dining table forced to bear parental expectations and real-world pressures was not a sanctuary, but another kind of prison. While one can understand the exhausting lives of parents, the vicious cycle persists in which the weight of that sacrifice is passed on to the children as emotional debt.

As senior researcher Do Nam-hee of the Korea Institute of Child Care and Education diagnoses, the reason South Korean adults are unique among 17 surveyed nations in choosing material values over family is clear: their experience of family was not positive. The fundamental reason young people show waning desire for others and shun marriage and childbirth is that their first disappointment in human relationships began right inside the home. Lacking the confidence to subject their own children to the same family dynamic that hurt and pressured them, young people choose the path of remaining alone instead.

Young People Afraid to Connect and the Meaning of Sejong University's Dating Class

This emotional deficit profoundly harms how young adults form relationships with others when they enter society. A "dating class" taught by adjunct professor Bae Jung-won at Sejong University is a small attempt to heal the isolated inner lives of modern youth. Beyond simply teaching romance between men and women, the course aims to teach students how to acknowledge human differences and build healthy relationships. Assignments require students to ask someone out on a date over the phone or in person, face each other uninterrupted for at least four hours, and interview their parents about their marriages.

The professor assigns such demanding tasks for a clear reason: to give young people—who have encountered distorted views of love and relationships through the cracked mirror of the family—an opportunity to genuinely contemplate their own lives, marriages, and love. Amid a global trend of declining marriage and romance, the class strives to remind students that deep communion with others is the true force sustaining life. Yet changing a single university lecture hall cannot instantly dismantle a culture of radical self-preservation built over decades in South Korea.

Young people who never received sufficient love and unconditional support from parents during childhood define themselves as "people who don't know how to give love." Fearing childbirth because they feel unequal to the sacrifice of their mothers, and putting up defensive shields to avoid being hurt by opening up to others, their hesitation runs deep. Ultimately, the root of all this friction is not individual selfishness, but an inhumane social structure that puts work before people and grades before relationships.

Subjective well-being scores lingering near the bottom of the OECD at 65 points, paired with indicators of rising materialism by the third year of middle school, serve as painful warning lights showing how many precious emotional assets we have squandered behind a facade of economic achievement.

The Core of Birthrate Policy: Reforming a Society Where Work and Family Coexist

The French Example and the Real Solution We Are Missing

Every year, the South Korean government pours tens of trillions of won into budgets proclaiming anti-birthrate measures, yet young people still refuse to have children. The reason is simple: the core of the problem does not lie in a bit of financial support, but in a society structured around long working hours where raising children together remains impossible even if you have them. Looking at the policy trends of European welfare states—such as France, which thoroughly guarantees public childcare systems and flexible working hours—reveals the sharp limits of South Korea's approach. While Germany guarantees 67% of parental leave pay and France treats birth leave and public childcare as basic state responsibilities, South Korea has continued to dump all responsibility onto individual households and demand personal sacrifice.

Municipal emergency childcare services or expanded subsidies for childcare helpers up to 250% of the median income by 2026 are certainly better than nothing. Hearing reviews from dual-income parents who caught their breath using short-term services during sudden care emergencies makes the necessity of these systems clear. Yet if these programs remain one-off emergency prescriptions that fail to change the daily reality of parents whose workdays still end past 8 PM, the childcare gap will simply morph into new shapes.

We must confront the truth: it is not that there are too few places to leave children, but that parents lack any time of their own to eat dinner with them. As long as parents leave work after 7 PM and children wander hagwons until 11 PM, no amount of cash subsidies will make people want to have children. What the state must guarantee is not piecemeal cash handouts, but a life with evenings—where parents can leave work on time, look their children in the eye, and share a dinner together.

What Real Heritage Should We Leave Our Children?

The question posed by the documentary ultimately leads to a fundamental reflection on what kind of memory our society will pass down to children. While adults use bulky toys and cram-school enrollment certificates as absolution for their absence, children conclude in their loneliness that "I was not loved" or "The only thing that can protect me is money." When those children grow up to become parents themselves, the unyielding chains of a vicious cycle lock tight as they feel the exact same fear and dread, ultimately refusing childbirth.

Breaking this chain requires looking beyond individual willpower or parental sacrifice. We must boldly cut away corporate cultures that treat overtime as a virtue, test-obsessed educational systems that measure human worth by a single rank, and outdated conventions that dump housework and caregiving onto specific genders. A society where mothers don't feel guilty when they are sick, and fathers can leave work on time to hold their children's hands at the playground, is the only true advanced nation and the sole exit from the demographic cliff.

How much longer will we ignore the sighs of teenagers wishing for lottery windfalls of 10 billion won? What children wanted were not high-end luxury toys, but the warm gaze of parents sitting beside them on the swings. Unless that ordinary normalcy is restored, South Korea's population crisis will never be resolved. Children should no longer be left alone in empty rooms to comfort themselves with forced maturity. Now, the state and society must answer.

💭 Editor's Note: A Grand Transition Toward an Evening Life

The word echoing through my mind while organizing this piece was "deprivation." Though people say we are materially far more prosperous than in the past, the inner lives of our children and youth have fallen into an unprecedented spiritual famine. Just as parents did not choose to grow distant from their children, children did not invite their own loneliness. Everyone worked hard and believed they did their best in their respective places, but the reality that the reward for such diligence is isolation and distrust remains this era's most fatal pathology.

The heritage we must pass down to the next generation is not Gangnam apartment square footage or a thick bank account, but the human trust and warmth of knowing that "someone is by my side when times are hard." A society where subway doors open on the evening commute, and people can smile thinking of someone waiting at home instead of staring at a smartphone screen. If we fail to reclaim that natural, humble daily life, our growth will ultimately prove to be a boomerang targeting ourselves. Before it is too late, we must stop our headlong rush and look into each other's eyes.
