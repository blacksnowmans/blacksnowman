Beyond the School Gates: South Korea’s Nationwide Shift Toward Lifelong Education

October 26, 2026

Imagine a crisp October afternoon at Ilsan Lake Park in Goyang, a sprawling "satellite city" just northwest of Seoul. The air is filled not just with the scent of autumn leaves, but with the hum of thousands of citizens—ranging from young parents to retirees in their seventies—crowding around booths. They aren’t there for a typical food festival or a K-pop concert. They are there to showcase digital art created via Artificial Intelligence, to demonstrate new vocational skills, and to celebrate the "harvest" of a year spent in the classroom. This is the reality of South Korea’s "Lifelong Learning City" initiative, a movement that has transformed from a niche educational hobby into a core strategy for national survival.

In late October, a flurry of activity across South Korean municipalities highlighted a significant shift in the country’s social fabric. From Jangseong-gun, a rural county in South Jeolla Province, receiving its official designation as a "Lifelong Learning City," to Uiwang in Gyeonggi Province drawing a staggering 35,000 residents to its educational festival, the message is clear: in an aging, hyper-technological society, education no longer ends with a diploma. It is a lifelong bridge to social inclusion and economic relevance.

The Political Pivot: From "Every Man for Himself" to State-Led Welfare

To understand why these local festivals are making national headlines, one must look at the shifting political landscape in Seoul. Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea (South Korea’s main liberal party), there has been a concerted effort to frame education as a form of "universal welfare." This stands in sharp contrast to the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently imprisoned.

During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, regional education ecosystems often felt the strain of what critics described as "opaque budget execution" and a policy of "individual survival," where local governments were left to fend for themselves without a cohesive national vision. The current government has pivoted back toward a model where the state and local municipalities take active responsibility for the intellectual and social health of the citizenry. By designating specific areas as "Lifelong Learning Cities," the Ministry of Education provides both the brand and the structural support needed for small towns and large cities alike to build "educational safety nets."

For a country facing "regional extinction"—a term used in Korea to describe the death of rural towns as young people flock to Seoul—this is more than just a feel-good policy. For a place like Jangseong-gun, being a "Learning City" is a survival tactic. By offering high-quality education for all ages, the local government hopes to improve the "quality of life" enough to retain its population and perhaps even attract retirees back to the countryside.

Bridging the Silver Divide in the Age of AI

Perhaps the most modern frontier of this educational push is the focus on Artificial Intelligence (AI) and digital literacy. South Korea is one of the most digitally integrated societies on earth, yet it suffers from a profound "digital divide" where the elderly and the marginalized are increasingly locked out of basic services—from ordering food at a kiosk to accessing government benefits online.

In Goyang Special City, the municipal government has moved beyond teaching seniors how to use smartphones. They are now running courses at the "Baekma Hwasarang" learning center that teach residents how to use AI to generate images, produce videos, and even publish e-books. The goal is to turn "passive consumers" of technology into "active creators." When a 70-year-old resident learns to use a generative AI tool to write a memoir or illustrate a story for their grandchildren, the technology stops being an intimidating barrier and starts being a tool for self-realization.

Similarly, Anyang, another major city in the Gyeonggi provincial area surrounding Seoul, recently hosted the "Future Lifelong Learning Forum." The central theme was preventing "technological alienation." Experts and policymakers gathered to discuss how to ensure that the rapid advancement of AI does not become a new form of class warfare. The consensus is that digital literacy is no longer a luxury; it is a fundamental civil right that the local government is obligated to protect. By funding these programs, the city is effectively subsidizing the social re-entry of an entire generation that might otherwise be left behind by the Fourth Industrial Revolution.

Education as a Tool for Radical Inclusion

While the AI courses grab the headlines, the "Lifelong Learning" movement is also filling critical gaps in the traditional welfare system. This is best seen in the way local agencies are targeting the most vulnerable members of society. In Ansan, the city recently held its first-ever "Developmental Disability Lifelong Education Festival." For families of people with developmental disabilities, education often stops after they reach adulthood, leading to social isolation and a heavy burden of care for the parents.

"Education is the most powerful form of welfare. It is the promise that no single person will be left behind, regardless of their physical or mental condition," noted observers at the Ansan event.

The role of regional universities is also evolving. In the past, Korean universities were seen as "ivory towers" focused solely on degree-seeking students aged 18 to 24. However, with the sharp decline in the school-age population due to Korea’s record-low birth rate, universities like Baekseok University and Baekseok Culture University in Cheonan are reinventing themselves as community hubs. They are now partnering with local governments to provide vocational training and welfare-linked education for the general public. This "win-win" strategy allows universities to stay relevant while providing the community with high-level professional resources that a local government office couldn't provide on its own.

A Nationwide Network of Learning Festivals

The scale of public participation in these programs is often underestimated by those outside Korea. When Uiwang City held its "Edu-Uiwang Oulim Festival" at Wangsong Lake, it wasn't just a small gathering of hobbyists. Roughly 35,000 citizens attended. For a city of about 160,000 people, that means nearly one-fifth of the entire population turned out for an event centered on English learning, culture, and lifelong education.

Similar "Festa" events have been held in Gumi (an industrial powerhouse in the southeast) and Jeungpyeong. These festivals serve a psychological purpose: they turn the "private act" of studying into a "public act" of community building. In a society that has long been criticized for its "hyper-competitive" and "lonely" nature, these festivals offer a rare space for social cohesion. When residents see their neighbors’ artworks or listen to a peer's success story in a new trade, it fosters a sense of collective growth rather than individual competition.

This trend is set to continue through the end of the year. Taebaek, a former coal-mining city in the mountainous Gangwon Province, and Yeongdeungpo, a major district in Seoul, have both announced large-scale learning festivals for late October and November. The sheer geographic spread—from the northern capital to the southern tip of the peninsula—shows that "Lifelong Learning" has become a standardized expectation for local governance in South Korea.

What to Watch Next

As the "Lifelong Learning City" model matures, there are several key developments for observers to monitor:

  • Budgetary Sustainability: While the Lee Jae-myung administration has prioritized "education as welfare," the long-term funding of these programs will depend on whether local governments can prove a return on investment—either through increased employment for seniors or reduced social welfare costs.
  • The "Digital Safety Net": Watch for whether other cities follow Anyang and Goyang in making AI literacy a standard part of the curriculum. If successful, this could provide a global blueprint for how aging societies can handle rapid technological shifts.
  • University Restructuring: Keep an eye on how many more "regional universities" pivot toward adult education. This could be the start of a fundamental change in the Korean higher education business model.
  • Legislative Support: There is growing talk in the National Assembly (Korea's parliament) about formalizing the "Right to Lifelong Education" into law, which would mandate certain levels of funding regardless of which party is in power.

In the end, the "harvest" of these programs isn't just about learning a new language or how to prompt an AI. It is about redefining what it means to be a citizen in a rapidly changing world. By moving education from the classroom to the city square, South Korea is attempting to build a society where the sunset of one’s career is not the sunset of one’s growth.
