The "Reform" Trap: How South Korea’s Prosecution is Consolidating Power

September 14, 2026

For four years, the mantra of "prosecution reform" has echoed through the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly. The goal was simple on paper: separate the agency’s investigative powers from its power to indict, effectively stripping the Prosecution Service of the unchecked authority that has made it the most powerful institution in the country. Yet, as the government moves to appoint the inaugural head of the new Major Crimes Investigation Agency—an organization originally conceived to replace the prosecution’s investigative reach—the reality has turned into a bitter irony. Instead of reform, the process has devolved into a mechanism for the prosecution to cement its own influence.

Establishing the Agency: A Reform or a Regressive Step?

The controversy surrounding the candidates for the new agency’s chief is not merely about their background as career prosecutors. It is about the specific histories of those being pushed for the role. Lawmakers like Park Eun-jung have pointed out that the candidates are not being selected for their investigative prowess in the service of justice, but rather as a reward for their past efforts in shielding the institution from accountability.

Consider the case of the controversial "Kim Hak-ui exit ban" investigation. Prosecutors who once faced internal calls for reform found themselves under investigation instead, a classic move to punish those who dared to challenge the status quo. When individuals who have spent their careers distorting facts—even in high-profile cases—are placed at the helm of the very agency meant to curtail prosecution power, the contradiction is absolute. It is not reform; it is a rebranding of the status quo. To place figures with such baggage in leadership roles is to betray the fundamental intent of institutional change, turning the agency into a shadow of the organization it was meant to replace.

The Internal Sabotage of Reform

The testimony of lawmaker Kim Yong-min highlights a deeper, more calculated strategy. The new investigative agency was originally designed as the third pillar of a comprehensive plan to dismantle the prosecution’s monopolistic power. Yet, the current candidates are largely drawn from the very ranks that fought tooth and nail against the agency's creation four years ago.

Effective reform requires a leader who believes in the institution's mission—someone to secure budgets, organize personnel, and establish genuine independence. If the head of this agency is someone who fundamentally denied its necessity, the organization risks becoming nothing more than a puppet. It becomes a tool used to maintain the prosecution's grip on power from within. This "failed reform" scenario is unfolding in real-time, as lawmakers watch their legislative vision for a balanced system be hollowed out and filled with political loyalists.

The "Pro-Yoon" Fortress and the Personnel Crisis

The heart of the problem lies in the recruitment process within the presidential office, specifically the civil affairs line, which has become a stronghold for former prosecutors. The committee tasked with recommending the new agency’s chief is dominated by prosecution insiders, including former aides to President Yoon Suk-yeol. This is a "League of their own," where those loyal to the President ("Pro-Yoon" figures) appoint their peers to the highest offices.

This is not a matter of simple personnel management; it is a calculated restructuring. When these loyalists take charge of the new agency, they will inevitably fill its ranks with like-minded individuals. Even after previous political scandals forced high-ranking officials to resign, the prosecution’s drive to control all investigative agencies has remained relentless. Critics argue that the presidential office's claim of "not knowing" about these administrative maneuvers is either a confession of incompetence or a sign that the system is being deliberately shielded from oversight.

Public Distrust Reflected in the Numbers

The public is clearly skeptical. Recent polling data indicates that 53.6% of the public views the appointment of former prosecutors to these roles as "inappropriate," while only 37.9% see it as appropriate. This 5-to-3 ratio is not just a political preference; it is a clear message from the electorate. Years of institutional resistance, combined with the prosecution’s tendency to ignore reform mandates, have left the public with the firm belief that the current prosecution is the subject of reform, not its leader. Ignoring these numbers risks eroding the remaining legitimacy of the current administration’s legislative agenda.

Why Personnel Purges Are the Only Way Forward

The current struggle is exacerbated by "poison pills" buried in legislation—such as clauses that allow for individual discretion in personnel transfers, which the Ministry of Justice is now using as a shield to protect existing power structures. Even when lawmakers draft compromise bills, the details are often warped or undermined during the implementation phase.

The career trajectory of figures like Kim Ji-yong, a senior prosecutor currently in the spotlight, serves as a case study for why reform is stalling. From his involvement in past cases that effectively suppressed legitimate investigations to his current prominence, his rise is a signal of what the administration truly values. When those who have blocked reform are granted more power, the system will only move backward.

Without a genuine purge of the political prosecutors who have spent their careers protecting their own institutional interests, any new agency or law is essentially toothless. The structure of the institution is not the only problem; it is the human element—the network of loyalists that prevents any real change from taking root.

What to Watch Next

  • Legislative Oversight: Keep an eye on the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee. Will they push back against the "poison pill" clauses in the new agency's mandate, or will they continue to be sidelined by the presidential office?
  • Personnel Appointments: Watch who is named as deputy heads and department chiefs under the new agency's leader. If they are all drawn from the same "Pro-Yoon" circle, it will confirm the institutional capture that critics fear.
  • Public Sentiment: How the administration responds to the 53.6% disapproval rating will be telling. If they continue to disregard public skepticism, expect further political gridlock and deepening distrust in the judiciary.
