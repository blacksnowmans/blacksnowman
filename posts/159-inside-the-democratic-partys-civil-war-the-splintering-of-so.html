Inside the Democratic Party's Civil War: The Splintering of South Korea's Ruling Power

September 04, 2026

Politics is often described as a living organism, but in South Korea, alliances forged in the heat of an election victory can decay overnight. Following the Democratic Party’s dramatic triumph in the 2025 presidential election, the euphoria barely had time to settle before internal fractures began to show. What looked from the outside like an unstoppable political machine has, over the past year and a half, devolved into a bitter exercise in mutual exhaustion.

To understand what is happening inside the Democratic Party—South Korea's main liberal political force, currently governing under President Lee Jae-myung—is to understand the inherent contradictions of a party that won big only to realize its members had entirely different ideas about what to do with power. This is not merely a policy dispute; it is a clash of political survival, competing egos, and fundamentally conflicting visions of how a government should operate. The trajectory from the high-stakes optimism of mid-2025 to the grinding infighting of late 2026 offers a sobering case study in how quickly ruling coalitions can eat their own.

The Stalled Promise of Judicial Reform

When the new administration took office, its core supporters expected a lightning-fast dismantling of South Korea's entrenched judicial and prosecutorial establishment. For years, the party had campaigned on reining in the immense, unchecked powers of the prosecution service. Yet, the early appointment of former high-ranking prosecutors—veterans of the very investigative units the party had vowed to reform—to key presidential oversight roles sent shockwaves through the base.

Placing figures associated with the old regime at the vanguard of reform was billed by pragmatists as an exercise in broad-tent governance and administrative competence. To the party’s core supporters, however, it looked like a profound betrayal. The rationale of broad-tent politics swallowed the substance of reform whole, leaving activists wondering whether the government was genuinely interested in changing the system or simply staffing it with its own inner circle.

This dissonance quickly infected the legislative arena. While hardliners in the party demanded swift, sweeping changes to supplementary investigative powers, the executive branch urged caution and institutional stability. The resulting policy gridlock metastasized into a toxic proxy war over who could claim to speak for President Lee. In the halls of the National Assembly, policy debates were sidelined by a petty scramble to monopolize the president's brand.

Who Owns the Party? The Battle Over Internal Democracy

As the administration stumbled on reform, the party apparatus became consumed by structural warfare. Under the leadership of party heavyweights like Jung Chung-rae, an aggressive push was made to implement a "one person, one vote" system for both ordinary and delegate members—a reform framed by its proponents as the ultimate expansion of grassroots democracy.

Critics, however, viewed it as a transparent maneuver to cement the leadership’s internal control by bypassing traditional party structures. When the proposal was voted down in the party’s central committee, rational debate broke down entirely. One faction screamed about "party member sovereignty," while the other denounced the leadership for running self-serving fiefdoms.

The factionalism curdled further with the emergence of pressure groups like a special assembly organized to demand the dropping of criminal charges against President Lee. Participation in the group instantly became a crude litmus test, dividing lawmakers into loyalists and dissidents, or "true believers" versus the untrustworthy. Lawmakers found themselves huddled beneath the vast umbrella of Lee's political brand, only to spend most of their energy stepping on each other's toes.

The Local Elections and the Breaking Point

The breaking point arrived with the local elections. While party leaders publicly celebrated their performance as a resounding victory, President Lee’s camp offered a much harsher assessment. "If we win seats we should have easily secured, that is not a success," the president reportedly signaled, drawing a sharp dividing line between his own standards and the party apparatus's spin.

That subtle rebuke cleared the path for a rapid reshuffling of power dynamics. The definitive symbol of this shift occurred during an overseas presidential trip, when Jung Chung-rae—once a towering figure in the party leadership—was conspicuously absent from the presidential send-off line at the airport, replaced instead by rival party heavyweight Kim Min-seok. For political insiders, the "airport passing" was a blunt broadcast that the party's center of gravity had moved. For the base, it was an alarming signal that their champions were being systematically purged from the inner circle.

Reflections in a Shattered Mirror

South Korean political commentary often searches for external scapegoats—opportunists, traitors, hostile media, or saboteurs—to explain why ruling parties tear themselves apart. But the deeper pathology lies in the structural contradiction of the government's own making: the instinct of state power to preserve institutional continuity versus the base's revolutionary desire to burn the old system down.

Time and again, South Korean politics produces populist heroes only to watch them get dragged down by the competing ambitions of the people who rode their coattails. The splintering of the Democratic Party is not just a soap opera of oversized egos; it is a manifestation of a maturing electorate. Today’s voters are no longer easily swayed by tribalistic flag-waving. They watch the infighting, read the data, and register their disappointment at the ballot box.

Beating a mirror does not change the reflection; it only breaks the glass. As the Democratic Party stumbles through the back half of its term, the real question is whether it will realize that the crisis is coming from inside the frame.

What to Watch Next

  • The Legislative Agenda: Watch for any renewed compromise—or total paralysis—on judicial and prosecutorial bills in the National Assembly, which will signal whether the executive and legislative branches have found a working truce.
  • Factional Realignments: Pay attention to how sidelined heavyweights position themselves ahead of upcoming internal party conventions, indicating whether a formal splintering is underway.
  • Voter Sentiment and Polls: Monitor presidential and party approval ratings in upcoming regular surveys to see if the public disconnect translates into sustained electoral punishment.
