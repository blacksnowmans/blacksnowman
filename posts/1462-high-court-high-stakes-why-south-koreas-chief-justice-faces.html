High Court, High Stakes: Why South Korea’s Chief Justice Faces Impeachment

October 26, 2026

The air in Seocho-dong, the neighborhood that serves as the heart of South Korea’s judicial and legal world, has grown thick with a familiar, heavy tension. For decades, the granite facades of the Supreme Court and the Prosecutor’s Office have stood as symbols of a "sacred" independence—an independence that critics increasingly argue has curdled into a lack of accountability. Today, that tension has reached a breaking point. The target of this brewing storm is Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, who now faces the unprecedented prospect of a parliamentary investigation and, potentially, impeachment.

In the halls of the National Assembly in Yeouido, the rhetoric has shifted from legal disagreement to open political warfare. While the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation’s ruling party under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, weighs the strategic timing of a legislative probe, the more aggressive Rebuilding Korea Party is already sharpening the blade for impeachment. To an outside observer, this might look like a standard political skirmish. However, for those following the long arc of South Korean democracy, this is the latest chapter in a fundamental struggle to redefine the boundaries of judicial power in a post-authoritarian state.

The Verdict That Broke the Camel’s Back

The immediate catalyst for this crisis was a ruling handed down on October 25. The Seoul High Court acquitted Oh Se-hoon, the Mayor of Seoul and a prominent figure in the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, in his second-instance trial. For the liberal bloc, the acquittal was more than just a legal loss; it was a confirmation of a perceived double standard. They argue that while the judiciary applies a "harsh, microscopic" standard of law to liberal politicians, it offers a "generous shield" to conservative elites.

This sense of "judicial distrust" is not new, but it has reached an inflection point. In the eyes of the DPK and its supporters, the judiciary has become a political actor hiding behind the robes of neutrality. When identical sets of laws and precedents result in wildly different outcomes depending on the defendant’s political affiliation, the court’s moral authority evaporates. For many, the acquittal of Mayor Oh was the spark that landed in a massive powder keg of accumulated grievances regarding the fairness of the Korean legal system.

The anger currently boiling over onto social media and into political rallies reflects a deep-seated belief that judicial independence should not be used as a shield for individual judges to exercise arbitrary power. The demand now is for "judicial justice"—a demand that the common sense of the public, which has been forged through the fires of the "Candlelight Revolution" and previous presidential impeachments, be reflected in the courtrooms. The sentiment is clear: a judiciary that loses its impartiality has effectively abdicated its constitutional role.

The Legacy of the Previous Administration

To understand why Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is being targeted, one must look back at the circumstances of his appointment. Cho was elevated to his position during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol, the former president who was recently impeached and is currently imprisoned. The current ruling bloc views Cho’s leadership as a remnant of the "Yoon era," a period they characterize as a time when the judiciary and the prosecution moved in lockstep to suppress political rivals.

Beyond the political alignment, there is a mounting list of administrative failures. Chief among them is the chronic and severe delay in trials. Under Cho’s leadership, high-profile cases involving major corruption or power-peddling frequently stall for years, while the everyday legal needs of citizens are pushed to the back burner. In the legal world, there is a maxim: "Justice delayed is justice denied." By this metric, critics argue the South Korean court system is failing its most basic duty.

Rather than taking responsibility for this paralysis, the Chief Justice has been accused of focusing on preserving the privileges of the judicial elite. His refusal to step down despite the mounting administrative crisis is seen by the DPK as an act of defiance against the will of the sovereign people. If the court cannot or will not reform itself from within, the argument goes, then the National Assembly—as the representative body of the people—has a constitutional obligation to intervene. The authority of the judiciary is not divine; it is delegated by the public, and that delegation can be questioned when the institution fails to function.

A Strategic Divide: Impeachment vs. Investigation

Within the liberal coalition, there is a consensus that Cho Hee-dae must be held accountable, but there is a sharp disagreement on the "how." Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party and a former Justice Minister, has been the most vocal proponent of immediate action. He has characterized the current moment as the "last golden opportunity" for judicial reform, warning that if the legislature hesitates now, the judicial "cartel" will simply hunker down and wait for the political winds to shift.

Cho Kuk’s urgency is rooted in history. He and his supporters remember the failures of previous liberal administrations—specifically the Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in eras—where attempts at judicial and prosecutorial reform were often thwarted by institutional resistance and a lack of decisive legislative action. For the Rebuilding Korea Party, the large majority currently held by liberal forces in the National Assembly is a mandate that must not be squandered on "excessive caution."

"We cannot afford to waste the golden time for judicial reform. To leave the arbitrary rulings and trial delays hidden behind the name of 'judicial independence' is a breach of trust against democracy."

On the other side of the strategy debate is Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the DPK leadership. While not ruling out impeachment, Kim advocates for a more methodical, step-by-step approach. His plan starts with a formal parliamentary investigation (Gukjeong Josa). The logic is tactical: an immediate impeachment filing could backfire if it is perceived as purely political or if it lacks a mountain of undeniable evidence. By holding hearings and using the legislature’s investigative powers first, the DPK aims to build a public consensus and a solid legal record of the Chief Justice’s alleged misconduct.

This "dual-track" approach—where the Rebuilding Korea Party pushes the front line while the DPK builds the procedural foundation—may actually be more effective than a single unified strategy. It allows the liberal bloc to maintain pressure on the judiciary while ensuring that any final move toward impeachment is insulated against claims of procedural unfairness. The goal is to dismantle what they call the "judicial cartel" once and for all.

The "Imperial" Power of the Chief Justice

The structural issue at the heart of this conflict is the nature of the Chief Justice’s power in South Korea. The position is often described as "imperial." The Chief Justice holds the power to recommend Supreme Court justices, the power to appoint and assign all judges across the country, and the power to oversee the disciplinary process for the entire branch. In a system where a single individual holds such immense sway over the careers of every judge in the nation, the "independence" of an individual judge can easily be compromised by the desire to stay in the good graces of the top leadership.

The DPK’s push for a parliamentary investigation is, in many ways, an attempt to crack this monopoly on power. They are calling for the decentralization of judicial administration and a more robust system of checks and balances. The fact that several Supreme Court justice seats have remained vacant for over seven months is cited as a prime example of the Chief Justice’s negligence—using his appointment power as a political bargaining chip rather than fulfilling his constitutional duty to keep the highest court fully functional.

This administrative vacuum has real-world consequences. With the Supreme Court understaffed, the backlog of cases grows, and the "right to a speedy trial" guaranteed by the constitution becomes a hollow promise. For the DPK, this isn't just about Cho Hee-dae the man; it is about a system that allows one person to hold the entire legal system hostage to their personal or political whims.

The Opposition’s Defense: "Stalking the Judiciary"

The People Power Party (PPP) has reacted with predictable fury, framing the move as a direct assault on the separation of powers. Representative Chung Jum-sik of the PPP has labeled the proposed investigation as "stalking the Chief Justice" and a "test run for impeachment." The conservative narrative is that the DPK is simply trying to intimidate judges who might rule against their own leaders in ongoing legal cases.

The PPP argues that by threatening the head of the judiciary, the ruling party is attempting to "erase the crimes" of its own members. They frame the DPK’s talk of "judicial reform" as a cynical ploy to ensure that the courts remain subservient to the current administration. In their view, the liberal bloc is using its legislative majority to bypass the legal process whenever they receive a verdict they don't like.

However, the DPK has countered that they are simply reclaiming a constitutional right. They argue that the judiciary is not a "temple of perfection" that is immune to oversight. If there are signs of collusion between the prosecution and the courts—what they term "fabricated indictments"—then it is the duty of the National Assembly to investigate. They see the PPP’s defense of the Chief Justice as an attempt to protect the very same "establishment" that benefited from the actions of Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol’s administration.

What to Watch Next

As the "Judicial War" enters its next phase, several key developments will determine the outcome. First, keep an eye on the 'Special Counsel Act for Fabricated Prosecutions.' The DPK intends to push this through alongside the parliamentary investigation, targeting the alleged nexus between politically motivated prosecutors and the judges who facilitate them. This will likely be the primary tool for gathering the "evidence" needed for a potential impeachment.

Second, watch the Supreme Court vacancy issue. If the Chief Justice continues to delay appointments or if the National Assembly continues to reject his nominees, the highest court in the land will remain paralyzed. This "judicial vacancy" could become the primary justification for a "neglect of duty" charge in an impeachment trial.

Finally, the public mood will be the ultimate arbiter. In South Korea, political momentum is often driven by the "street." If the public perception of judicial unfairness continues to grow—driven by more controversial acquittals of conservative figures or further delays in trials for ordinary citizens—the pressure on the DPK to move from "investigation" to "impeachment" will become irresistible. The die is cast, and the countdown to a historic constitutional confrontation has begun.
