Crisis and Soul-Searching: Inside the Lee Jae-myung Administration’s Political Crossroads

September 10, 2026

On Tuesday, September 8, 2026, I found myself watching Kim Ou-joon’s Humility is Difficult: News Factory, a highly influential and often provocative political commentary show in South Korea. The program centered on two major pillars: a keynote speech by Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK, the country’s main liberal opposition party, which currently holds the presidency under Lee Jae-myung), and an interview with Hong Ik-pyo, the Senior Secretary to the President for Political Affairs.

Watching these two segments, it was impossible not to feel the weight of the current political moment. The Lee administration, now eight months into its term, is navigating a turbulent period defined by falling approval ratings and intense internal scrutiny. The dialogue within the studio wasn't merely political theater; it was a mirror reflecting the limitations and the harsh reality currently facing the South Korean government. As observers, we are forced to ask: are these proposed solutions merely rhetoric, or is there a genuine path forward for a society exhausted by perpetual internal conflict?

Beyond Industrialization: Kim Min-seok’s Vision for a 'Dignified Society'

In his keynote address to the National Assembly, Kim Min-seok set a somber but ambitious tone, stating, "Politics must view the future through the eyes of the vulnerable." His argument is that South Korea, having successfully navigated the twin historical milestones of rapid industrialization and democratization, must now transition into a "Dignified Society."

Central to this is Kim’s concept of a "Basic Society." This is not simply a proposal to increase welfare spending; it is an ideological shift aimed at ensuring the state provides a baseline for human dignity. In a society characterized by fierce competition and a "survival of the fittest" mentality, Kim argues that the state must act as more than just a safety net—it must be a shield for human worth. While skeptics will rightly point out that the DPK has often prioritized rhetoric over legislative substance, the directionality of this shift is arguably what Korean society currently lacks.

Furthermore, Kim addressed the precarious geopolitical landscape. He declared that "the era of unilaterally looking only to the United States is over." In an age of sharpening U.S.-China rivalry, he emphasized that Korea can no longer afford to be a passive bystander. This was a blunt warning against the country’s long-standing tradition of sycophantic diplomacy. Perhaps most tellingly, he proposed a radical change in National Assembly decorum: seating lawmakers in alphabetical order rather than by party affiliation. While symbolic, this proposal highlights a desperate need to break the "hostile symbiosis" of the two major parties.

The Internal Fissures: Hong Ik-pyo and the Crisis of Unity

When Hong Ik-pyo, the Senior Presidential Secretary, appeared on News Factory, the atmosphere grew noticeably tense. The show’s host, Kim Ou-joon, wasted no time in grilling him on rumors surrounding the administration’s alleged "blacklisting" of certain media outlets and the internal chaos within the DPK.

Hong’s defense of the administration was careful. When asked about the "ban" on appearing on certain political programs, he insisted it was a matter of scheduling and caution during a sensitive period rather than active censorship. However, his answers underscored just how fragile the administration feels it is. When pressed on past failures—such as the collapse of party merger plans and controversies surrounding inflammatory social media posts by high-ranking officials—Hong emphasized that President Lee’s singular mandate is "integration."

The most chilling part of the interview, however, was the discussion regarding the "internal war" within the liberal camp. Hong openly criticized the use of derogatory labels—a common practice among extreme factions of the party base who turn on their own colleagues with vitriol. He noted that such behavior is "undesirable" and lamented that the movement has lost the language of comradeship. This internal cannibalism is, as many fear, a clear symptom of a government that is struggling to maintain its cohesion while under constant attack from both the opposition and its own disillusioned supporters.

The Triple Threat: Politics, Policy, and Attitude

Perhaps the most honest moment of the broadcast was Hong’s breakdown of why the president’s approval ratings are in freefall. He attributed the decline to a "triple crisis" of political management, policy failure, and tone-deafness.

  • Political Management: The failure to integrate various factions within the DPK has turned the party into a battlefield of internal suspicion.
  • Policy Failure: Economic instability, particularly confusion over real estate tax policy and erratic financial regulations (including issues with stock-market-linked ETFs), has directly alienated the middle class.
  • Attitude: Hong admitted that both the Blue House (the executive office) and the ruling party have failed to maintain a "humble posture," opting instead to push forward with a "we know best" attitude that has deeply frustrated voters.

This admission of a "tone and manner" issue is telling. The administration has frequently been criticized for appearing isolated in a "glass tower." While there is a delicate balance to be struck between presidential dignity and accessibility, the current silence or hyper-filtered messaging is being read by the public as arrogance or avoidance. South Koreans, currently burdened by an unforgiving economic climate, are not looking for polished PR; they are looking for genuine, unfiltered engagement.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will be a crucible for the Lee Jae-myung administration. Watch for these three developments:

  • Legislative Follow-through: Will Kim Min-seok’s "Basic Society" rhetoric translate into concrete bills, or will it evaporate as the political wind changes?
  • Internal Discipline: Can the DPK muzzle its own fringe influencers and stop the "internal shooting" that is currently eroding its credibility?
  • Communication Shift: Will the President move away from his current reserved communication style toward a more direct, humble, and "raw" engagement with the public?

Politics in South Korea is a high-stakes, high-decibel affair. As the administration faces these harsh realities, the question is no longer just about survival—it is about whether the government can remember the promise it made to the voters at the beginning of its term. If they continue to prioritize internal power struggles over the daily struggles of the public, the mandate they won will likely remain a fleeting, wasted opportunity.
