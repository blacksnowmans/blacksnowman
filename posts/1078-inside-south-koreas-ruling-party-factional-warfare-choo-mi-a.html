Inside South Korea's Ruling Party: Factional Warfare, Choo Mi-ae, and the Battle for the Democratic Party's Soul

October 13, 2026

To watch South Korean politics right now is to watch a high-stakes, behind-the-scenes scramble for survival. Beneath the polished veneer of official press briefings and parliamentary decorum, the country's progressive camp is locked in a bitter internal war. A recent political commentary broadcast on the popular political YouTube channel Park Si-young TV laid bare a series of bruising factional clashes—centering on veteran politician Choo Mi-ae and a cryptic social media post from President Lee Jae-myung—that offer a rare window into the anxieties shaping the ruling Democratic Party of Korea ahead of the next general elections.

For outside observers, South Korean political drama often looks like theater. But these localized flare-ups—arguments over a vague social media phrase, historical backstories of growing up in a laundry shop, accusations of internal sabotage, and battles between moderate and hardline factions—are the vital tectonic plates moving underneath Asia's most dynamic democracy. They dictate policy, dictate who gets nominated to run for office, and ultimately determine whether the progressive agenda can survive.

The Cryptic Presidential Post and Choo Mi-ae’s Defense

The latest political storm began with a short, ambiguous post on social media by President Lee Jae-myung. Using the phrase "living in luxury as high-ranking officials," the president ignited an immediate frenzy across the political spectrum. Opposition politicians and ruling party insiders alike rushed to decipher who, exactly, the president was targeting.

When opposition lawmaker Joo Eo-young bluntly asked senior politician Choo Mi-ae during a session whether the presidential barb was aimed at her, Choo drew a sharp line. "No," she fired back, "I do not believe the president was targeting me."

This exchange exposes the exhausting, hyper-sensitive nature of South Korean political interpretation. In a party where proximity to power dictates everything, a single phrase from the top is treated like an oracle's omen. Every lawmaker reads the tea leaves to figure out where the winds of favor are blowing, and who might be pushed out in the next round of candidate nominations.

Choo, attempting to defuse the fallout, leaned heavily into her personal history. She pushed back against any notion that she is out of touch with ordinary citizens, recounting her childhood as the daughter of a dry cleaner, born into a struggling family where her mother could not even raise her directly. Her fiery defense—that she has never lived a life of luxury or harmed others while ignoring ordinary livelihoods—was heavy with a sense of grievance. When she lamented that being misunderstood by the media is simply an occupational hazard for a politician, it captured the bitter-sweet reality of political survival in Seoul.

Yet, this friction also highlights a peculiar dynamic with the press. Much like former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook, Choo has built a reputation for aggressively clashing with mainstream media outlets. In South Korean politics, this kind of institutional defiance is not a liability; it is rocket fuel for a politician's base. To frustrated voters tired of cautious, finger-in-the-wind politicians, a leader who refuses to back down and wears an unapologetic "anti-establishment" badge provides a powerful sense of catharsis. It is a strange, enduring feedback loop: the more media outlets push back against certain politicians, the tighter their core supporters rally around them.

Internal Sabotage and the Looming Threat of 'Candidate Slaughter'

If external opposition is a known quantity, the real terror for any South Korean political party always comes from within. The broadcast turned a harsh spotlight on the concept of haedang haengwi, or "acts harmful to the party"—a phrase thrown around when politicians actively undermine their own side.

The consensus voiced on the program was grim: there are simply too many internal saboteurs within the Democratic Party to count. While the party should ideally function as a unified vehicle for reform, it has increasingly resembled an ideological battleground where individual ambitions override collective goals. Even during the turbulent years of impeached former leader Yoon Seok-youl's administration—when progressives theoretically needed absolute unity to fight an aggressive conservative establishment—internal foot-dragging was a constant, frustrating reality.

This persistent internal friction has pushed the party to a breaking point. Political analysts inside Seoul now view a massive generational turnover—often grimly referred to in Korean political parlance as a "nomination massacre"—as an absolute necessity for the upcoming general elections. Incumbents who have spent years cozying up to the establishment to preserve their political lives are facing an exhausted electorate that has little patience left for self-preservation politics.

The logic is simple: if the party fails to weed out those who use reform as a slogan while protecting their own vested interests, the voters will do it for them at the ballot box. The upcoming candidate nomination process is shaping up to be the ultimate test of whether the Democratic Party has the stomach to cut out its own dead weight.

The Tug-of-War: Moderate Expansion vs. Hardline Core

At the strategic level, the Democratic Party is caught in a high-stakes identity crisis. When Kim Min-seok secured the party leadership over rival candidate Chung Cheong-rae, it signaled a calculated pivot toward a moderate expansion strategy rather than a purely hardline reformist path.

Yet, this pivot has come with a heavy political cost. While the party leadership chased the elusive moderate voter, its approval ratings—along with the president's—have drifted downward. The political reality laid out by analysts is stark: the party's hardline base, often aligned with figures like Chung, is tightly bonded, highly disciplined, and deeply loyal. Conversely, the loose coalition of centrist voters drawn in by promises of moderation are fickle; the moment a compromise is struck on core principles, they abandon ship.

Furthermore, condescension or outright exclusion directed toward the party's more radical, passionate grassroots supporters has only served to harden their resolve. As electoral consultants look ahead to the crucial Seoul and Gyeonggi capital region—the undisputed battleground that decides South Korean elections—the demographic weight within the party's internal structures is shifting. While centrist strategies look good on paper, alienating the bedrock base in pursuit of middle-of-the-road voters risks leaving the party empty-handed on both fronts.

"The crisis facing the Democratic Party is not coming from the outside; it is born of internal fatigue and self-interest. The base does not want lukewarm compromises; they want reforms that draw blood."

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward the next electoral cycle, outside observers should track a few concrete indicators to see which faction gains the upper hand:

    The Nomination Rules: Pay close attention to how strictly the Democratic Party implements its candidate screening process. If party leadership shields long-term incumbents to maintain stability, expect internal fury to boil over. If they wield the knife ruthlessly, expect dramatic party-switching and independent runs. Capital Region Polling: Watch the approval numbers specifically in Seoul and Gyeonggi Province. Because this region dictates national electoral outcomes, any sustained dip here will force party strategists to either double down on moderation or pivot back to energizing the hardline base. The Rhetoric of Independence: Notice whether prominent figures like Choo Mi-ae and other vocal lawmakers escalate or temper their anti-media and anti-establishment stances. Their tone is the most sensitive barometer of how nervous the party's leadership is about losing touch with ordinary voters.

South Korean politics is loud, messy, and constantly shifting, but its underlying logic is remarkably consistent. Parties that lose touch with the source of their power—and spend more time fighting internal turf wars than delivering on reform—inevitably face a harsh reckoning at the polls. For President Lee Jae-myung’s administration and the Democratic Party, the clock is ticking to prove they can cut away their own afflictions before the voters do it for them.
