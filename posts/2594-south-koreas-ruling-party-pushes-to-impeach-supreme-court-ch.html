South Korea’s Ruling Party Pushes to Impeach Supreme Court Chief Justice: Inside the Constitutional Crisis

November 21, 2026

Inside the National Assembly in Seoul, South Korea’s political arena has erupted into open war. On November 20, the ruling Democratic Party made a historic and explosive decision: it officially adopted the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, the head of South Korea’s Supreme Court, as its binding party platform. Setting a strict deadline to force the measure through the legislature before the end of the year, the ruling party has drawn an uncompromising line in the sand.

In South Korean constitutional history, taking direct aim at the head of the judiciary through a legislative impeachment vote is an extraordinary measure. By binding its lawmakers to remove the Chief Justice, the Democratic Party is asserting aggressive legislative oversight over a judicial branch it accuses of unfair rulings and failing to protect constitutional values. For the opposition, however, the move represents an existential threat to the rule of law. The main opposition People Power Party (PPP) immediately condemned the initiative as a "parliamentary coup" and an outright assault on the separation of powers.

To understand why this showdown matters—and how it threatens to paralyze governance in Seoul—one must look at the deep institutional friction currently tearing through the country's political establishment.

The Democratic Party’s Fast-Track Drive Against Chief Justice Cho

Why the Ruling Party Is Targeting the Bench

The Democratic Party’s decision on November 20 was not merely a warning shot; it was a formal declaration of institutional combat. By adopting the impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae as an official party platform (dangron), party leadership ensured that all ruling party lawmakers will be expected to vote in unison when the measure reaches the National Assembly floor.

The core of the ruling party’s justification rests on claims of judicial unfairness, controversial court rulings, and a perceived failure by Chief Justice Cho to uphold constitutional duties. From the perspective of President Lee Jae-myung’s party, the judicial system requires urgent structural reform. Ruling party strategists argue that holding the top official of the judicial branch legally and politically accountable is a necessary step to overcome systemic resistance and achieve thorough judicial reform.

The Year-End Deadline and Legislative Paralysis

Speed is central to the ruling party's playbook. Leadership announced plans to process the impeachment motion through parliamentary reporting and a final floor vote "as quickly as possible," establishing an explicit target to complete the process before the year ends. This aggressive timeline is designed to maintain political momentum and demonstrate decisive action against judicial roadblocks.

However, the rush to vote before the end of December has effectively frozen normal legislative work. Bipartisan negotiations on routine legislative matters and state affairs have ground to a halt. As the Democratic Party marshals its parliamentary majority to fast-track the motion, the National Assembly has transformed into a high-stakes arena where bipartisan compromise is no longer viable.

The Opposition Responds: "A Palace Coup and Political Retaliation"

Severe Rhetoric from Opposition Leaders

The People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative opposition party, responded to the announcement with unprecedented fury. Opposition figures framed the Democratic Party’s campaign not as a legitimate exercise of legislative oversight, but as an authoritarian power grab designed to subjugate the judiciary.

Han Dong-hoon, the former leader of the People Power Party, delivered a scathing critique, labeling the impeachment drive a "palace coup" and an "insurrection" aimed at destroying republic governance. Escalating his rhetoric, Han warned that the ruling party was "inviting a national catastrophe akin to the Imjin War"—referring to the devastating 16th-century conflict that ravaged the Korean Peninsula. Other key conservative leaders, including lawmakers Jang Dong-hyuk and Jeong Jeom-sik, similarly denounced the move as an "assembly coup" born of extreme legislative arrogance.

Framing the Push as Retaliation and Distraction

In the narrative built by the opposition, targeting Chief Justice Cho is directly tied to political self-interest. Conservative lawmaker Jeong Jeom-sik argued that the impeachment push is a clear act of "political retaliation" intended to punish the judiciary for past decisions unfavourable to the ruling bloc and to shield President Lee Jae-myung from ongoing legal scrutiny.

Furthermore, the People Power Party claims the motion is a tactical maneuver designed to divert public attention away from internal political troubles—such as ongoing factional friction referred to in political circles as the "Myeong-Cheong War." By opening a dramatic front against the Supreme Court, the opposition argues, the ruling party hopes to unite its supporters and shift the national conversation away from domestic controversies.

Judicial Independence and the Escalating Constitutional Risks

The Threat to the Separation of Powers

Beyond the immediate partisan crossfire, legal experts and observers are voicing deep anxiety over the long-term health of South Korea's constitutional framework. Democracy in South Korea relies on a system of checks and balances among the executive, legislative, and judicial branches. When a ruling legislative majority moves to remove the nation's top judge over dissatisfaction with judicial administration and rulings, it risks creating a dangerous precedent.

If judges fear that issuing rulings contrary to the preferences of the ruling party could result in retaliatory impeachment, the independence of the entire court system is placed under immense pressure. Conservative lawmakers staging protests in the National Assembly rotonda emphasized this warning, arguing that eroding judicial neutrality ultimately deprives citizens of an impartial legal system.

Institutional Backlash and Operational Freeze

The potential overuse of impeachment carries severe administrative costs. Legal commentator Kang Dae-gyu noted in public commentary that if the Constitutional Court ultimately rejects the impeachment motion, those who weaponized the constitutional mechanism should face strict political accountability—even suggesting that lawmakers should face pay cuts for causing state disruption.

Crucially, if the National Assembly passes the impeachment bill, Chief Justice Cho will be immediately suspended from his duties pending a final judgment by the Constitutional Court. A prolonged suspension would leave the Supreme Court without its leader, paralyzing highest-level judicial administration and leaving major appeals stalled during a period of acute political volatility.

What to Watch Next in Seoul

As South Korea enters a period of intense constitutional instability, several concrete benchmarks will determine how this battle unfolds over the coming weeks:

  • The National Assembly Vote: Monitor whether the Democratic Party can maintain party discipline and force the impeachment motion through a plenary vote before the December 31 deadline.
  • Judicial Branch Statements: Watch for formal responses from lower court judges, legal associations, or Supreme Court justices regarding the preservation of judicial independence.
  • The Constitutional Court Review: If the motion passes parliament, Chief Justice Cho’s immediate suspension will shift the battlefield to the Constitutional Court, where judges will deliberate on the legal validity of the impeachment.
  • Opposition Resistance: Observe how Han Dong-hoon and the People Power Party organize counter-strategies, including potential legislative filibusters, street protests, or emergency legal filings to halt the proceedings.
