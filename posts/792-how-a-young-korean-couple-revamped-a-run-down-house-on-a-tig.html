How a Young Korean Couple Revamped a Run-Down House on a Tight Budget

October 07, 2026

The quest for a home with a private yard—a rarity in South Korea’s hyper-dense, apartment-dominated cities—is about much more than simply owning property. It is a massive lifestyle project that changes the very texture of daily life. For one couple, that dream started with a weathered, dilapidated house and a strict budget of about $70,000 (98 million won). Peeling floral wallpaper, heavy archaic boilers, and decades of accumulated wear greeted them at the door. Yet beneath the decay, they saw the potential for their own sanctuary.

The Aesthetics of Tight Spaces: Preserving Flow and Structure

When buying an older home, the most common beginner's mistake is the urge to tear everything down. Smashing walls indiscriminately and altering the flow of a house only inflates construction timelines and often destroys the inherent efficiency of a compact footprint. Rather than gutting the interior, the couple chose to respect the original skeleton of the building. In older houses, altering the core structure often shrinks the usable interior space, so the focus instead turned to clever spatial management.

In narrow quarters, a clear line of movement connecting the living room to the kitchen is essential for daily convenience. The weathered wooden staircase leading to the second floor, too, was preserved and repaired rather than aggressively demolished. This preserved the house's unique narrative while naturally keeping unexpected structural modification costs at bay. A small bedroom at the far end of the living room barely accommodates a large king-sized bed, but rather than feeling cramped, its high density turns it into an intense, focused zone dedicated purely to rest. Small spaces thrive on the art of strategic placement and intentional emptiness.

Harvesting Light and Warmth from Existing Windows

Natural light and ventilation are frequently overlooked in home renovations. Older Korean houses often feature small windows designed to maximize heating efficiency, but this house fortunately possessed large, south-facing windows. These windows required zero construction and instantly doubled as interior centerpieces and free energy sources. In the winter, deep afternoon sunlight floods the living room with warmth.

Cutting a brand-new window into a load-bearing wall is a massive engineering undertaking that risks exterior waterproofing failures and steep costs. Working with what was already there completely bypassed these headaches. Ultimately, a house must breathe with nature. A large window is not merely a hole for light; it acts as a live picture frame pulling the courtyard outside directly into the living room.

Confronting the Attic and the Realities of DIY Labor

An attic on the second floor sounds whimsical in theory, but execution proved punishing. Because irregular lines and steep angles intersect under high ceilings, the second-floor renovation demanded painstaking, high-risk labor where a margin of error of a single inch could ruin the finish. Structural reinforcement and insulation had to occur simultaneously, frequently triggering creative friction between a husband eager to test modern construction techniques and a partner determined to preserve the house's rustic character.

Furthermore, second floors in aging houses present serious load-bearing concerns. While safety inspection standards for older properties have occasionally been politicized or weakened under shifting political administrations—including the deregulation pushes seen during the presidency of the imprisoned former leader, Yoon Seok-yeol—the ultimate responsibility for structural safety falls squarely on the homeowner. Skipping on reinforcement bars was never an option; every slow, deliberate step prioritized structural integrity over speed.

Renovating a house together is a crucible for a relationship. Fixing broken spots, erecting new walls, and taming creaky doors mirrors the compromises required to understand a partner. The final product is less a finished dwelling and more a physical ledger of a couple's shared history.

Navigating the Legal Gray Areas of Self-Renovation

While DIY remodeling videos flood social media, they rarely prepare amateurs for the legal and financial fallout of structural defects. Under current South Korean civil law, the legal responsibility for defects rests entirely on the builder—which, in a DIY project, is the homeowner. Courts have consistently ruled that consumers cannot easily shift blame to online content creators if a self-installed tile wall collapses.

When dealing with electrical wiring, plumbing, or structural changes, safety supersedes thrift. Basic legal literacy serves as the homeowner's ultimate shield. In South Korea, small-scale interior renovations under about $11,000 (15 million won) can legally proceed without specialized licenses, but larger projects require certified interior architecture contractors under the Framework Act on the Construction Industry. Contracting with unlicensed local handymen to save money often strips homeowners of legal defect-repair protections, leaving them vulnerable to standard civil disputes.

Moreover, changing a building's official legal "purpose" requires consulting architectural registries and securing local district office permits. Bypassing these bureaucratic steps to save time can result in heavy administrative fines down the road. Treating a house as a living vessel requires respecting the legal parameters that govern it.
