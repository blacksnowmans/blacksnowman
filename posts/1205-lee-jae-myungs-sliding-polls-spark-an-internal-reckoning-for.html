Lee Jae-myung’s Sliding Polls Spark an Internal Reckoning for South Korea's Ruling Party

October 16, 2026

Inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, the mood within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) has turned palpably anxious. Just months after the dramatic political upheaval that saw former president Yoon Suk-yeol impeached, stripped of power, and sent to prison, the progressive administration led by President Lee Jae-myung was expected to command an unassailable mandate. Instead, the administration finds itself stumbling against an unexpected ceiling. Recent polling figures show President Lee’s approval rating sliding for two consecutive weeks, touching a precarious 35.2%.

For a ruling party preparing for critical legislative battles and parliamentary committee audits, a rating hovering in the mid-30s is more than an awkward headline. It is a structural warning sign. The decline exposes growing friction over presidential messaging, an escalating purge mentality within the grassroots base, and a dual-flank challenge from rival political forces. To understand why a 35.2% approval rating is causing such panic in Seoul, one must look at how the ruling party is attempting to enforce internal discipline while navigating the deep institutional scars left behind by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure.

The 55-Lawmaker Declaration: Drawing Battle Lines Behind Kim Yong

The clearest sign that the Democratic Party is circling the wagons came with the public release of a statement signed by 55 sitting lawmakers declaring their explicit backing for Kim Yong, the former deputy head of the Institute for Democracy and one of President Lee's closest political confidants. In South Korean legislative politics, lawmakers rarely append their names to public factional declarations unless an internal reckoning is underway. This was an unmistakable display of strength by the party’s pro-Lee core.

The roster spans heavyweight veterans and party elders—including Cho Jeong-sik, Kim Tae-nyeon, Park Jie-won, Seo Young-kyo, and floor leader Park Chan-dae—alongside influential mid-career lawmakers such as Kim Byung-joo, Han Jun-ho, and Kang Deuk-gu. Backing them up is an assertive vanguard of junior lawmakers like Kim Dong-ah, Lee Gun-tae, and Boo Seung-chan. Rather than concealing factional loyalties behind closed doors, these 55 politicians chose to openly display where they stand, creating an informal register of loyalty to the presidential line.

This coordinated declaration serves a dual purpose. For the party base, it signals that the core leadership remains unified behind Lee's closest circle. For wavering colleagues, it acts as a shot across the bow ahead of the upcoming candidate selection and nomination screening process. Tensions were already running high following unilateral maneuvers by lawmakers such as Chae Hyun-il, whose recent independent political conduct drew harsh internal criticism for disrupting party coordination and clashing with senior figures like Kim Min-seok. To grassroots loyalists, such deviations are seen as intolerable liabilities when the government is already losing ground in public opinion polls.

From Defense to Attack: Preparing for the Judicial Audits and Party Primaries

The party's response to slumping poll numbers is not a retreat to the center, but an aggressive push toward ideological combativeness. Within the Democratic Party, the consensus among active party members is that polite legislative decorum has failed. The priority for upcoming legislative battles—and the decisive metric for surviving the next round of candidate nominations—is raw political fighting capacity.

This aggressive posture will center squarely on the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee, traditionally the most contentious parliamentary body in Korean politics because it controls the flow of bills and oversees the judicial branch. The ruling party is preparing to deploy combative, legally trained lawmakers—such as Kim Dong-ah, Kim Nam-hee, Lee Gun-tae, Kim Hwang-gyu, and Park Gyun-taek—to spearhead the upcoming parliamentary audits. Their mandate is clear: dismantle the prosecutorial networks and systemic influence entrenched during the administration of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose background as a hardline prosecutor general defined his governance before his impeachment and imprisonment.

Simultaneously, the Democratic Party’s grassroots membership is flexing direct-democratic power. Activist party members are organizing explicit campaigns to de-select and defeat incumbent lawmakers who have wavered on party priorities or failed to champion government reforms. Within party channels, this aggressive vetting is framed not as factional vindictiveness, but as a necessary purification of the party ranks to ensure absolute legislative cohesion behind President Lee.

Communication Missteps and the Squeeze from Third Parties

Yet ideological zeal cannot fully mask the primary driver of the polling slump: self-inflicted wounds in public communication. The most damaging blow to President Lee’s standing came not from opposition attacks, but from the president’s own social media feed. After issuing a public apology to quell a previous controversy, Lee published a personal social media post just one week later that appeared to walk back the spirit of contrition. The misstep reignited public debate and left even loyal party supporters frustrated at having to defend an unforced error.

This unrefined communication strategy has contributed directly to an eroding political foundation:

  • Presidential Approval: Dipped to 35.2% following two consecutive weeks of losses, reflecting a critical drop in momentum during an early phase of governance when an administration typically commands public goodwill.
  • Rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party: The unapologetically progressive party founded by Cho Kuk has climbed to 6.3% in polling, siphoning away impatient progressive voters who feel the Democratic Party is moving too slowly on systemic reforms.
  • Pressure from the Far-Right: A reactionary political faction colloquially dubbed the "insurrection party" has captured 5.8% support, eating into the political landscape with radical counter-messaging.
  • Expanding Swing Vote: Independent, unaligned voters increased by 2 percentage points, demonstrating that moderate citizens are disconnecting from the political establishment amid persistent political warfare.

Surrounded on both sides, the Democratic Party faces a strategic dilemma. If it moderates its tone to reclaim the 2% that drifted into the unaligned camp, it risks losing its progressive core to the Rebuilding Korea Party. If it leans entirely into hardline rhetoric, it alienates the broader electorate required to maintain legislative governance.

The "Club" Philosophy: Can Combative Populism Govern?

Adding fuel to this debate is the recent recirculation of President Lee’s past remarks from his days as mayor of Seongnam. Opponents have frequently dredged up an old social media comment in which Lee wrote that when dealing with a violent threat, "a biting dog is mad, and a club is the medicine for a mad dog."

During his rise as a local executive, this brand of combative, plain-spoken populism cemented Lee’s reputation as an unyielding reformer willing to take on corrupt interests. Even today, hardcore party loyalists argue that such uncompromising force is precisely what is needed to thoroughly excise the abuses associated with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's time in office. In their eyes, conventional political niceties are inadequate for overhauling a compromised justice system.

For moderate voters, however, the re-emergence of that rhetoric raises uncomfortable questions about administrative temperament. Governing an entire nation requires the precision of a surgeon rather than the blunt impact of a club. When aggressive rhetoric is not matched by concrete economic relief, the public quickly tires of political combat. Transforming fighting words into competent governance remains the defining test of the current administration.

What to Watch Next

The Democratic Party now stands at a critical juncture where internal consolidation must translate into tangible legislative momentum. Over the coming weeks, several key benchmarks will indicate whether the administration can arrest its polling slide:

  • Performance in the Judiciary Audits: Watch how aggressively lawmakers like Kim Dong-ah and Lee Gun-tae press their case during parliamentary audits. Their ability to deliver substantive findings rather than mere political theater will determine whether the public views the hearings as genuine reform or partisan score-settling.
  • Presidential Communication Discipline: Observers should monitor whether the presidential office tightens review protocols over President Lee’s personal social media output to prevent further unforced contradictions of official stances.
  • The Primary Nomination Rules: Pay close attention to how the Democratic Party leadership handles grassroots primary challenges against moderate incumbents, which will reveal whether the party chooses complete ideological uniformity over broad-tent electoral appeal.
