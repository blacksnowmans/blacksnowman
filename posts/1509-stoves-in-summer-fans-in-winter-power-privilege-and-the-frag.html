Stoves in Summer, Fans in Winter: Power, Privilege, and the Fragile Credibility of Korean Justice

October 26, 2026

As autumn chills sweep across the Korean Peninsula, driver windows roll down along afternoon expressways, and residents pull long-sleeved hoodies out of storage, a traditional four-character idiom has resurfaced in South Korea's political discourse: Haro Dongseon (夏爐冬扇)—literally, "a stove in summer, a fan in winter."

The phrase, highlighted in the biography of the late progressive President Roh Moo-hyun written by former presidential spokesman Yoon Tae-young, describes objects that appear useless in their current season, yet quietly hold their value until the weather shifts. In South Korean politics, it has long served as a metaphor for surviving political exile, enduring dry spells, and waiting out hostile political climates. Back in 1997, after suffering bitter election defeats, a group of sidelined politicians opened a charcoal grill restaurant, suffocating in smoke while commentators mocked them as redundant "summer stoves." Yet those very individuals later rose to helm the nation.

Today, as South Korea navigates a complex political landscape under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, the philosophy of waiting out the storm carries renewed resonance. But for the South Korean public, patience is wearing thin—not because of political exile, but because of a deepening sense that the country's legal and institutional guardrails are being selectively applied to protect the powerful while punishing political rivals.

Selective Justice: The Unraveling Case of Han Dong-hoon’s Daughter

Few issues illustrate South Korea's crisis of legal equity more starkly than the handling of allegations surrounding Han Dong-hoon, an independent lawmaker and former high-profile prosecutor. During a recent National Assembly audit, Representative Yoon Kun-young of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea posed a direct question to Kim Ji-yong, head of the Serious Crime Investigation Agency (Jungsucheong, South Korea's specialized corruption investigative body): Is it common sense for a tenth-grade high school student to donate 50 laptops to a charitable organization?

Kim nodded in agreement, admitting plainly: "I do not think it is common sense."

That single nod exposed what critics describe as a blatant double standard in South Korean law enforcement. The allegations against Han’s daughter—which include ghostwritten academic papers by foreign contractors in Kenya, manipulated volunteer hours, copyright violations on e-books, and suspicious corporate laptop donations—were previously closed without indictment by the Seoul Metropolitan Police’s Anti-Corruption and Public Crime Investigation Team.

Data submitted to the National Assembly reveals that during its 20-month investigation, the police did not file a single search warrant against Han’s family. They conducted no face-to-face interrogations of key suspects, opting instead for a single written questionnaire for Han’s wife, attorney Jin Eun-jung. When police sought international judicial cooperation from U.S. authorities in late 2022 to verify foreign ghostwriting claims, the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office unilaterally rejected the request, claiming it was unclear whether foreign cooperation was necessary. The police never asked again.

This hands-off approach stands in glaring contrast to the aggressive prosecution of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk’s family during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. In Cho’s case, prosecutors launched dozens of raids across universities, high schools, and private residences, culminating in a nationwide audit of 1,033 minor-authored academic papers that saw admissions revoked for five students, including Cho's daughter, Cho Min. Today, with Cho leading the opposition Rebuilding Korea Party—which has seen its support steadily climb to 8.8% in recent automated polls—the perception of rubber-band justice continues to fuel public distrust.

Cracks in the Judiciary: Pressure Tactics and Judicial Inconsistency

The crisis of faith extends directly into the Supreme Court. Shocking allegations raised during National Assembly Judiciary Committee hearings point to internal coercion within the judicial administrative hierarchy.

According to Representative Kim Yong-min of the Democratic Party, Noh Kyung-pil, Minister of the Court Administration Office—the administrative arm of the Supreme Court—personally telephoned four recommended candidates for Supreme Court justice in mid-August, pressuring them to withdraw their candidacies to force the recommendation panel to dissolve.

Among those allegedly pressured was Judge Yoon Seong-sik, who leads the insurrection panel at the Seoul High Court. Prior to these phone calls, Judge Yoon’s bench had maintained a firm stance on accountability regarding historical abuses during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration, recommending strict 7-to-9-year prison terms for senior officials such as former Interior Minister Lee Sang-min and former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. Following the pressure controversy, however, the court issued a controversial acquittal for Han Duck-soo on perjury charges and slashed 16 years off the sentence of former Justice Minister Park Sung-jai.

Public outrage over such erratic judicial rulings is reflected in recent public opinion polls. In late October surveys, 63.6% of telephone-interview respondents supported issuing an additional arrest warrant for former First Lady Kim Keon-hee before her custody period expires. Moreover, 50.5% expressed sympathy for motions regarding Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over judicial bias. When citizens feel that judicial outcomes depend on political alignment rather than statutory law, the rule of law loses its democratic mandate.

Realignments on the Right: Oh Se-hoon, Lee Jun-seok, and the Conservative Shuffle

With the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) struggling to regain its footing—hovering between 36.7% and 42.6% in weekly tracking polls—key conservative figures are jockeying for leadership.

At the October 26 memorial service for former President Park Chung-hee at the National Cemetery in Seoul, Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon made a prominent appearance alongside PPP leader Jang Dong-hyeok. Mayor Oh publicly thanked Jang for urging prosecutors not to appeal after the Seoul High Court acquitted Oh in a recent trial, to which Jang replied that it was his duty as party leader.

Media outlets quickly seized on the exchange as the launchpad for Mayor Oh’s efforts to rally conservative lawmakers behind his presidential ambitions. Over the following two days, Mayor Oh hosted consecutive dinner meetings with key PPP lawmakers, including Kim Jae-seop, Kwon Young-jin, Eom Tae-young, and Cho Eun-hee. Positioned as a moderate conservative capable of bridging ideological divides, Oh has launched sharp critiques against the Lee Jae-myung administration's housing policies while attempting to solidify his stance as the premier conservative standard-bearer.

Concurrently, Lee Jun-seok, lawmaker and former head of the minor opposition Reform Party, continues to command significant media spotlight. Mainstream broadcast networks have heavily amplified speculation surrounding a potential alliance between Lee, Oh, and Han Dong-hoon. However, media critics note that key outlets frequently grant Lee prime-time platforms while glossing over serious unresolved controversies—including his alleged involvement in the Myung Tae-kyun campaign finance scandal, dubious nomination trades, and contentious local candidate selections. By allowing Lee to pivot to abstract political commentary without facing rigorous scrutiny, media coverage risks creating an artificially inflated political profile.

Capital, Media Privilege, and What to Watch Next

The erosion of institutional integrity is not confined to courtrooms and legislative chambers; it extends to local media, where corporate ownership increasingly compromises public trust.

A striking example occurred on August 1 on G1 Broadcasting, a Gangwon-based regional affiliate of commercial network SBS. During its primary evening news broadcast, G1 aired a glowing 3-minute-15-second feature story highlighting two teenagers who completed a demanding hiking trail along Seorak Mountain's Dinosaur Ridge. The story was framed as an inspiring feat of youthful perseverance. However, it soon emerged that the two featured teenagers were the biological grandsons of Jeon Jong-ryul, the main shareholder and CEO of G1 Broadcasting. Broadcast time and newsroom staff had been commandeered to produce a glorified family video.

Such incidents underscore how easily public airwaves can be co-opted when corporate interests operate without accountability. Whether in local newsrooms or federal investigative agencies, the privatization of public authority represents a growing threat to South Korean governance.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters the final months of the legislative year, several critical flashpoints will determine whether public confidence in national institutions can be restored:

  • The Serious Crime Investigation Agency’s Next Move: Watch whether Jungsucheong reopens investigations into the laptop donations and ghostwriting allegations surrounding Han Dong-hoon's daughter, testing whether law enforcement will apply equal standards to high-profile figures.
  • Supreme Court Administrative Audits: The Assembly’s investigation into alleged pressure tactics by Court Administration Office chief Noh Kyung-pil against Supreme Court candidates will be a crucial test for judicial independence.
  • Kim Keon-hee’s Legal Status: The Supreme Court's decision on the special prosecutor’s request for an extended arrest warrant for Kim Keon-hee will serve as a key indicator of judicial firmness toward former presidential circles.
  • Conservative Realignment Strategy: Monitor whether Mayor Oh Se-hoon’s dinner diplomacy succeeds in consolidating pro-Oh lawmakers within the People Power Party, and whether Lee Jun-seok’s Reform Party attempts a formal coalition ahead of upcoming electoral cycles.
