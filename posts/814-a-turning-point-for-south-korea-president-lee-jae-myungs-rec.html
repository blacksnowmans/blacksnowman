A Turning Point for South Korea: President Lee Jae-myung’s Recent Address

September 18, 2026

On September 18, 2026, the atmosphere inside the Yeongbingwan—the presidential state guesthouse—was heavy with expectation. For President Lee Jae-myung, the leader of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), this press conference was not merely a routine check-in on state affairs. It served as a moment of reckoning for his 472 days in office and a deliberate attempt to reset the tone of a political landscape currently defined by gridlock and hostility.

In a move that surprised many observers, President Lee addressed three lightning-rod issues head-on: he pledged not to seek a second term, ruled out the deployment of South Korean troops to the Strait of Hormuz, and solidified his stance on the long-debated reform of the national prosecution service. For international observers accustomed to the often-opaque nature of Korean internal politics, this performance was an exercise in direct, if risky, communication.

The Decision to Forgo Re-election

One of the most significant takeaways from the conference was the President’s explicit declaration that he has "no intention to seek a second term." By pre-emptively dismissing the possibility of a constitutional amendment for re-election, Lee effectively dismantled a narrative that had been gaining traction among his political opponents. This maneuver was a calculated attempt to focus his administration’s remaining energy on policy achievements and stabilizing the livelihoods of ordinary citizens.

The timing was not coincidental. Recent polling suggested that 63% of the public felt that if a constitutional amendment were to occur, the incumbent should renounce a future presidential bid. By aligning himself with this public sentiment, Lee is attempting to strip away accusations that he is "privatizing" power for his own gain. From a younger voter's perspective, this is a welcome gesture; people are far less interested in abstract constitutional debates than they are in tangible improvements to their daily economic lives. The challenge now for Lee is to prove that this promise is more than just rhetoric and that he can translate his rhetoric into concrete results that the public can actually feel.

Diplomacy and the Hormuz Question

Perhaps the most tense moment of the conference was when the President addressed the potential for military involvement in the Strait of Hormuz. Despite intense international pressure to participate in maritime security coalitions, Lee stated firmly that South Korea would not deploy its military or even its assets to the region, arguing that the nation cannot be involved in foreign wars.

This is a major signal of the administration's foreign policy philosophy: a rejection of the past inertia that saw South Korea align itself with military interventions in the name of the "national interest." While this stance is morally popular, it presents a massive diplomatic headache. The administration must now find a way to guarantee the safety of its commercial shipping lanes without the direct use of force. Moving forward, the DPK will need to develop sophisticated, non-military cooperation models to appease both domestic pacifists and international security partners.

Prosecution Reform and Internal Factionalism

For years, the reform of the powerful South Korean prosecution service—which holds both investigative and indictment powers—has been a cornerstone of the liberal agenda. President Lee reiterated his commitment to separating these two functions, framing the reform not as a way to avoid criticism, but as a necessary step to build a system of checks and balances. He also signaled a strategic shift: instead of getting bogged down in legal squabbles over past cases, his administration intends to focus on creating a sustainable, long-term legal framework.

However, the real danger to this reform lies within the Democratic Party itself. The President issued a rare, heartfelt plea to his own supporters, asking them to stop using derogatory labels and hateful language against political opponents or even internal dissenters. He rightly noted that when a party becomes consumed by internal purity tests, it loses the very momentum required for reform. The Democratic Party, as he put it, acts like a "car bumper"—it is meant to absorb shocks and protect the engine of the state, not to destroy the engine from within. For the party leadership, including representative Kim Min-seok, the task is to move beyond tribalism and refine the "fine print" of these reforms.

What to Watch Next

The President’s press conference was a clear attempt to stop a slide in approval ratings that had once dropped from 60% to 30%. Whether this represents a genuine turning point depends entirely on the next few months of execution:

  • Policy Delivery: The administration must move from "grand announcements" to "detailed implementation." The public is tired of slogans; they want to see the inflation of living costs cooled and the job market stabilized.
  • Diplomatic Balancing: We will see if the government can maintain its pacifist stance on the Strait of Hormuz while still navigating the intense pressure from its allies in Washington.
  • Party Discipline: Watch for the DPK's reaction. If the party continues to fragment into warring factions, the President’s agenda will likely stall in the National Assembly, regardless of how well-received his press conference was.

South Korea is currently navigating a period where old political orders are being challenged. For President Lee, the path forward is narrow: he must balance the demands of a fierce, polarized base with the need to govern for the entire nation. The next few months will reveal whether this press conference was the start of a new era of reform, or merely a temporary pause in a cycle of political turbulence.
