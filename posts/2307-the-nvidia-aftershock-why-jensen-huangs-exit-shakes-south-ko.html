The NVIDIA Aftershock: Why Jensen Huang’s Exit Shakes South Korea’s AI Ambitions

November 14, 2026

The lights in the office of the National AI Strategic Committee in Seoul stayed on late into the night this week. For South Korean policymakers and tech titans, the news coming out of Santa Clara was not just another corporate resignation; it was an existential threat to a national roadmap. When Jensen Huang, the co-founder and charismatic face of NVIDIA, abruptly stepped down, the shockwaves traveled instantly across the Pacific, wiping value off the KOSPI (South Korea’s benchmark stock index) and sending the Lee Jae-myung administration into a state of emergency. In the high-stakes world of artificial intelligence, where hardware is the new oil, the "Emperor" of chips has left his throne, and South Korea is left wondering if its supply of "black gold" is about to dry up.

The situation is particularly delicate for South Korea, a nation that has hitched its economic wagon to the AI revolution. From the massive fabrication plants of Samsung Electronics to the High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) lines at SK Hynix, the South Korean economy is the "kitchen" for NVIDIA’s high-end menu. Huang’s exit, amid a cloud of scandal and geopolitical intrigue, has exposed the terrifying fragility of a global supply chain that depends almost entirely on the leadership of a single individual. For a reader outside the peninsula, understanding this crisis requires looking past the stock tickers and into the deep-seated anxieties of a country trying to secure its place in a post-NVIDIA world.

A Leadership Void in the Heart of the AI Empire

The departure of Jensen Huang is not a standard executive transition. Under his tenure, NVIDIA became the most important company in the world, facilitating the generative AI boom that has dominated global markets since 2022. His sudden exit, fueled by allegations of personal misconduct and whispers of entanglement in U.S.-China intelligence operations, has left Silicon Valley and its global partners in a state of paralysis. In Seoul, the immediate reaction was visceral: NVIDIA shares plummeted 11%, and the KOSPI followed suit, closing down 1% as investors panicked over what a "leaderless" NVIDIA might mean for future contracts.

The internal atmosphere at NVIDIA is reportedly chaotic. Employees have been warned not to wear company-branded clothing in public, and a faction of the workforce is already circulating petitions for Huang’s return. Into this vacuum steps Colette Kress, the current Chief Financial Officer, who has taken the reins as interim CEO. However, market confidence remains low. Kress, whom Huang once famously described as looking like a "startled deer" during high-pressure moments, now faces the task of managing a company that was built entirely around Huang’s singular, aggressive vision.

The risk for South Korea is that an interim leadership will be inherently defensive. Massive Research and Development (R&D) projects and the bold, long-term supply agreements that Huang personally brokered may be put on ice as the company focuses on internal stabilization and legal defense. As competitors like OpenAI and Google begin circling NVIDIA’s top engineering talent, the "moat" that protected the company’s technological dominance is beginning to look increasingly shallow.

The 260,000 GPU Question and Government Intervention

In response to the crisis, the National AI Strategic Committee—the high-level body overseen by the Lee Jae-myung administration to spearhead Korea’s tech future—convened an emergency session. The primary goal was to assess the stability of the GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) supply chain. South Korea is currently in the middle of a massive national infrastructure build-out, requiring a staggering 260,000 NVIDIA GPUs to power new state-of-the-art data centers.

The committee received official confirmation from NVIDIA that the promised 260,000 units would still be delivered as scheduled. While this provided a momentary sigh of relief, the long-term outlook remains murky. The source of the anxiety isn't just the contract itself, but the geopolitical environment surrounding it. With U.S. export controls tightening and rumors of "spy wars" involving NVIDIA executives, the administrative hurdles for shipping high-end silicon to Asian hubs are growing taller. If the delivery of these 260,000 chips is delayed by even a few months, it could derail South Korea’s goal of becoming a "Top 3" AI global power by 2030.

The Lee Jae-myung government, which has championed a proactive industrial policy, now faces a difficult calculation. Relying on a single foreign provider for the "brains" of its national infrastructure is a strategic vulnerability. The current crisis has accelerated calls within the Blue House (the presidential office) and the Ministry of Science and ICT to fast-track the development of domestic AI semiconductors, reducing the "NVIDIA risk" that currently hangs over the nation’s digital sovereignty.

The Divergent Fates of Samsung and SK Hynix

While the government worries about infrastructure, South Korea’s "Big Two" semiconductor firms are running their own spreadsheets. For SK Hynix, the world’s leading producer of HBM—the specialized memory chips essential for AI processing—the NVIDIA crisis is a direct threat. SK Hynix has spent billions of dollars tailoring its production lines to NVIDIA’s specific requirements. If NVIDIA’s dominance wavers or if its product roadmap changes under new leadership, SK Hynix faces the prospect of a massive inventory glut and a collapse in margins.

On the other hand, Samsung Electronics sees a glimmer of opportunity. For years, Samsung has struggled to break SK Hynix’s near-monopoly on the NVIDIA supply chain. Now, as global tech giants look to diversify their suppliers to mitigate the risks associated with a destabilized NVIDIA, Samsung is positioning itself as the reliable alternative. The "NVIDIA Windfall" for Samsung could come in the form of new partnerships with AMD, Broadcom, and Google—companies that are currently forming a "United Front" to challenge NVIDIA’s hegemony.

However, this is not a zero-sum game where one Korean company wins while the other loses. Both firms are deeply integrated into the global "AI Value Chain." When NVIDIA’s stock crashes, it drags the entire sector down. The volatility has already hit domestic equipment manufacturers—the smaller "Sobu-jang" (Materials, Components, and Equipment) companies that sustain the semiconductor ecosystem. These firms, often valued at several hundred million dollars (hundreds of billions of KRW), saw their stock prices swing wildly, reflecting the uncertainty of the broader market.

Geopolitics, Spies, and the Malaysian Connection

The NVIDIA story took a turn into the world of espionage this week, further complicating the situation for Korean firms. Authorities in Malaysia and Singapore have launched investigations into allegations that NVIDIA chips were being diverted to China through "gray market" subsidiaries. Central to this investigation is a subsidiary of Megaspeed, where Alice Huang—reportedly linked to Jensen Huang—served as an executive. Rumors reported in Taiwanese media suggest that she may have ties to China’s Ministry of State Security, turning a corporate scandal into a major national security crisis for the United States.

This matters to South Korea because it acts as a bellwether for how the U.S. will regulate chip exports moving forward. If NVIDIA is seen as having "leaky" supply chains, the U.S. Department of Commerce is likely to impose even stricter oversight on all high-end chip shipments to Asia, including those bound for Seoul. South Korean companies often operate as a bridge between Western technology and global markets; if that bridge becomes a checkpoint in a cold war, the cost of doing business will skyrocket.

The "Spy Alert" in Silicon Valley has effectively ended the era of pure market logic in the semiconductor industry. Security costs are now part of the valuation. For Korean investors, the realization that a CEO’s personal life or a subsidiary’s shipping manifest in Southeast Asia can wipe out a year of gains in the KOSPI is a sobering reminder of how interconnected—and fragile—the tech world has become.

The Great Capital Migration: From Hardware to Software

As hardware manufacturers reel from the NVIDIA shock, the "smart money" is already looking for the next port in the storm. This has led to a frenzy surrounding the Initial Public Offering (IPO) of Anthropic, a leading AI safety and research company. Reports indicate that the IPO was oversubscribed by six times, with the company’s valuation soaring toward $1.75 trillion (approximately 1,874 trillion KRW). Even TSMC, the Taiwanese foundry giant, has reportedly invested up to $1 billion in Anthropic and OpenAI, signaling a shift in focus from the chips themselves to the models that run on them.

However, many analysts, including the likes of Michael Burry (the investor famous for "The Big Short"), are warning of a bubble. Burry has been vocal about the deteriorating profit structures of hardware giants following Huang’s exit, suggesting that the current rush into AI software might be a desperate attempt to find growth in a cooling market. In Korea, this shift is mirrored by a growing interest in "Physical AI" and software-driven robotics, as investors hedge against a potential "AI Winter" in the hardware sector.

The lesson for the global reader is that the AI trade is changing its rules. The "NVIDIA-only" strategy that dominated the last two years is dead. Capital is diversifying, and with that diversification comes extreme volatility. South Korea, as a manufacturing powerhouse, must now prove it can adapt to a software-centric world where the value is found in the algorithm, not just the silicon.

What to watch next

  • The NVIDIA Succession Plan: Watch for whether NVIDIA appoints an internal "technocrat" like Ian Buck (the creator of CUDA) or an external "heavyweight" to stabilize the board. A permanent CEO announcement will be the first step toward market recovery.
  • The 260,000 GPU Delivery: Keep a close eye on the actual shipping dates for the chips promised to the South Korean government. Any delay will be a signal that geopolitical or internal pressures are overriding existing contracts.
  • Samsung’s Quality Tests: Monitor whether Samsung Electronics can finally clear the final hurdles for its HBM3E chips to be used by NVIDIA’s competitors. This would be a major turning point for the company's valuation.
  • The Anthropic IPO Performance: As the first major AI "Unicorn" to hit the market after the NVIDIA crash, Anthropic’s performance will determine whether the AI investment cycle continues or if a broader market correction is imminent.
