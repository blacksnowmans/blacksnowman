Inside South Korea’s Rush to Put AI Into Machines—Before the Laws or Profits Catch Up

November 20, 2026

Next year, South Korea’s national police plan to send autonomous patrol robots onto public streets. Yet on November 19, police officials walked into the National Assembly—the country’s parliament—with an awkward admission: the machines are nearly ready, while the law is not. No statute currently defines who bears legal responsibility if a patrol robot collides with a pedestrian, wrongly detains a citizen, or records footage in violation of privacy rules.

On that exact same day, however, South Korea’s largest conglomerates, struggling robotics stocks, research universities, infrastructure engineers, and rural governors all converged on the same technological bet: physical AI. Unlike the generative AI models that summarize documents or generate images on a screen, physical AI embeds machine intelligence into hardware that moves, grips, inspects, and reacts inside messy real-world spaces.

For outside observers, it is easy to mistake the buzz for another Silicon Valley-style hype cycle. In South Korea, the urgency is far more structural. Confronting one of the fastest-aging populations in the developed world, severe labor shortages in shipyards and farms, and relentless manufacturing competition from China, Korea’s industrial establishment treats embodied AI less as a shiny novelty than as basic economic survival. A single day’s developments on November 19 offered a remarkably clear X-ray of how South Korea is trying to build that future—showing exactly where money and corporate power are flowing, and where profits and governance are falling behind.

LG’s Executive Shake-Up Turns Research Labs Into Profit Centers

When South Korea’s family-controlled conglomerates, known as chaebol, want to signal a strategic pivot, they do it through their year-end executive reshuffles. On November 19, LG Group—one of the country’s largest industrial groups, spanning electronics, batteries, and chemicals—announced its 2027 leadership lineup, promoting 132 executives while keeping nearly all of its major subsidiary CEOs in place.

The split decision carried a clear message from LG Chairman Koo Kwang-mo. Rather than shaking up top management and risking disruption in existing core businesses, LG concentrated promotions in four areas: AI data centers, robotics, research and development, and female leadership. The goal is to pull what LG calls "physical and agentic AI" out of corporate laboratories and turn it into paying industrial products. For employees inside LG, that shift carries real internal friction: career paths for traditional administrative managers are narrowing as budgets and authority tilt toward technical leaders who can commercialize factory and robotics automation. For consumers, it points toward a gradual shift away from standalone home appliances toward connected automation services.

Just as telling was what happened at LG Innotek, the group’s electronic components subsidiary, which promoted nine executives the same day—including Jo Ji-tae to executive vice president alongside a senior vice president. Rather than betting only on finished robots, LG Innotek paired physical AI specialists with leaders in high-density semiconductor substrates. That is a classic "picks and shovels" strategy. While finished robot makers struggle to prove they can turn a profit, autonomous machines still require advanced sensors and complex circuit substrates to process surroundings in real time—no matter which robotics brand ultimately wins the market. Smaller Korean suppliers still focused on generic components, meanwhile, face growing pressure to upgrade or lose business.

At the same time, LG AI Research, the group’s central AI lab, elevated specialists working on physical AI and EXAONE, LG’s proprietary foundation AI model, to executive rank. With global tech giants and startups competing fiercely for researchers who understand both software models and robotic control, handing out executive titles has become a defensive tool to stop top engineers from leaving. Putting lab leaders directly in executive seats also shortens the distance between foundational model design and factory deployment—though it widens the talent gap for smaller Korean startups and university labs that cannot match chaebol paychecks.

Red Ink at Home While Global Investors Chase Real-World Data

If LG’s promotions radiated confidence, South Korea’s stock market delivered a cold dose of arithmetic. An earnings review reported on November 19 showed that all five publicly listed Korean physical AI companies recorded operating losses. Even Clobot, a listed Korean robotics software and service provider that grew its sales, swung into the red. Customizing robot software for unpredictable job sites, maintaining hardware, and funding pilot deployments are simply costing more than early commercial contracts bring in.

Yet that red ink has not scared off entrants. On the very same day, 112 companies crowded into a pre-matching event for next year’s government-backed physical AI demonstration projects, hoping to pair up with industrial buyers. The paradox captures the central dilemma facing investors in Korean robotics stocks: companies cannot win future commercial orders without proving their machines in real factories and securing field data, even if early pilots deepen their losses. Well-capitalized players can absorb that burn rate to set industry standards, while cash-strapped startups risk bleeding out with every new project they take on.

Profitability at listed Korean robotics firms remains weighed down by heavy field-deployment costs, yet companies and private capital are accelerating their race to lock down real-world data and reference sites first.

Private capital flows announced on November 19 showed where global investors think the real bottleneck lies—and it is not flashy robot demo videos. In India, startup HumynLabs raised $47 million (63 billion won) in Series A funding to deploy robots on actual work sites and gather physical training data. Unlike chatbots trained by scraping text and images off the internet, robots learning to grip objects and keep their balance require messy, three-dimensional motion data collected from physical environments. Back in South Korea, startup SAGAK secured Series A funding from DRB Dongil, a mid-sized industrial parts and materials maker looking to accelerate automation on factory floors, while China’s heavy-equipment giant Sany Group used a Shanghai trade show to unveil an AI agent and physical AI platform built directly into construction machinery.

South Korean universities and mid-tier tech firms are scrambling to build a domestic talent and product pipeline to match. Also on November 19, Korean AI software firm Maum AI signed a three-way agreement with Doosan Robotics—one of Korea’s leading collaborative robot makers—and Hanyang University’s ERICA campus, a major industry-linked engineering hub south of Seoul, to pursue full-stack unmanned factory solutions rather than selling software and robotic arms separately. Meanwhile, UNIST (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology), located in South Korea’s shipbuilding heartland, announced eight new majors pairing shipbuilding and physical AI to train 2,000 engineers, and Wonkwang University partnered with local firm Miracle AI to train specialists.

Why a Rural Korean Province Wants $46 Million for Farm Robots

In South Korea, major technology booms almost always stay locked inside the capital region around Seoul. One of the more striking subplots on November 19 was a rural province trying to break that pattern using the national budget.

Jeonbuk Special Self-Governing Province—a largely agricultural region in southwestern Korea—is wagering almost its entire 2027 central-government funding push on physical AI. Governor Lee Won-taek met again with Planning Minister Park Hong-keun to request about $48 million (64.7 billion won) in additional 2027 national funding for key regional initiatives. Remarkably, about $46 million (61.7 billion won) of that request was dedicated to physical AI projects alone.

Why would a rural farming province ask the central government for tens of millions of dollars in robotics funding? Because South Korea’s agricultural and livestock sectors suffer from some of the country’s worst aging and labor shortages, yet standard factory automation fails outdoors on uneven ground and around live animals. On November 19, researchers at Jeonbuk National University held a policy forum proposing a physical AI livestock testbed center in rural Jangsu County. Nearby Gimje City pitched integrating local farming expertise with physical AI, while Wanju County outlined a regional plan linking physical AI and hydrogen energy to "sunlight income," a local solar power revenue-sharing model for residents. If Jeonbuk secures the funding, regional universities and agricultural machinery makers gain a subsidized proving ground that could create skilled maintenance and data jobs. If lawmakers trim the budget, local officials lack the tax base to build those centers on their own.

Patrol Robots in a Legal Void, Tunnel Bots on the Job

Nowhere is the gap between engineering speed and institutional readiness sharper than in South Korea’s public sector. When South Korea’s centralized National Police Agency told the National Assembly on November 19 that next year’s patrol robots lack a legal basis, it exposed a problem no corporate reshuffle can solve.

Deploying autonomous machines onto crowded sidewalks raises immediate questions over civil rights and legal liability. Without clear legislation, a malfunctioning patrol robot that injures a pedestrian or mistakenly flags an innocent passerby leaves police officers, municipal managers, and manufacturers in a legal gray zone. Public video capture and personal data protection rules are equally unsettled. Until parliament writes clear statutory boundaries, police commanders will remain reluctant to use the machines aggressively, and vendors will hesitate to sign large-scale supply contracts.

In hazardous infrastructure closed off to the public, by contrast, physical AI is already proving its value without waiting for new laws. Also on November 19, the Korea Authority of Land & Infrastructure Safety and the state-run Korea Railroad Research Institute announced they had conducted precision safety inspections inside tunnels using robots. Sending machines into dark, dusty, confined road and rail tunnels spares human inspectors from collapse and air-quality risks while collecting far more consistent structural data—gradually shifting human engineers from dangerous visual checks toward robot operation and data analysis.

Taken together, a single day of Korean AI news makes clear that physical AI has moved well past lab prototypes. Over the coming year, four concrete markers will determine whether this momentum produces durable industry or expensive dead ends:

  • Corporate execution: Whether LG’s newly promoted technical executives turn EXAONE software and semiconductor substrates into commercial robotics revenue in 2027.
  • Market survival: Whether South Korea’s five loss-making listed robotics firms—and the 112 contenders chasing next year’s pilot projects—can convert subsidized trials into operating profits.
  • Budget politics: Whether the National Assembly approves Jeonbuk’s $46 million physical AI funding request during 2027 national budget deliberations.
  • Street-level rules: Whether lawmakers pass liability and privacy laws for autonomous public-space robots before police patrol units roll out next year.
