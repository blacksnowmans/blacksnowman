A Constitutional Shield or a Political Sword? The Impeachment of South Korea’s Chief Justice

October 16, 2026

In the high-stakes theater of South Korean politics, the word "impeachment" usually targets a president. But today, the crosshairs of the Democratic Party (DP), the nation’s ruling center-left party, have shifted toward the very top of the legal hierarchy: Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. This is not merely a rhetorical flourish or a symbolic protest. For the DP leadership, it is being framed as an act of "constitutional self-defense" against what they describe as a "judicial coup"—a calculated attempt by the courts to use legal procedures to overturn the will of the voters and dismantle the current administration.

The tension has reached a boiling point as Kim Min-seok, a senior member of the DP’s Supreme Council, recently signaled that the party is moving beyond mere warnings. By mentioning the "first letter of impeachment," Kim has effectively put the judiciary on notice. The argument from the DP is stark: they claim the judiciary, under Cho’s leadership, is no longer a neutral arbiter but an active political combatant. The stakes are nothing less than the eligibility of President Lee Jae-myung to lead the country and the legitimacy of the democratic process itself.

The Mechanics of a "Judicial Coup"

To understand why the ruling party is considering such a drastic move, one must understand the specific grievances they hold against Chief Justice Cho. The DP alleges that the judiciary is engaging in a "modern-day judicial coup." This isn't a coup involving tanks and soldiers, but one involving "sentences and court assignments." The core of the accusation is that the court is manipulating the timing and assignment of sensitive political trials to achieve a specific outcome: the disqualification of President Lee Jae-myung from future elections or even the invalidation of his current mandate.

The DP leadership argues that the Chief Justice is using his administrative powers—specifically the power to assign judges and manage the court’s schedule—to create a "special task force" of sorts within the judiciary. This group, they claim, is dedicated to accelerating or delaying trials in a way that creates maximum political volatility. By weaponizing the written word of the law, the DP asserts that the judiciary is attempting to do what the opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), could not do at the ballot box.

The numbers behind this move are significant. Already, 112 lawmakers from the broader liberal coalition have signed onto a draft impeachment motion. In the South Korean National Assembly (the country's 300-seat unicameral legislature), an impeachment motion for a Chief Justice requires a majority vote to pass. Given the DP's current dominance in the Assembly, the passage of such a motion is not a question of "if" they have the votes, but "when" they choose to use them. Once passed, the Chief Justice’s powers are immediately suspended until the Constitutional Court makes a final ruling, effectively decapitating the judicial administration during a critical period.

Strategic Silence: The Case of Judge Lee Jin-kwan

A central pillar of the DP’s argument involves the curious case of Judge Lee Jin-kwan and a recusal request involving Judge Choi Sang-mok. To a foreign observer, these names might seem like minor bureaucratic details, but in the context of Korean judicial politics, they represent a front line in a war over judicial independence. The DP points to a specific incident where a request to recuse Judge Choi has been sitting at the Supreme Court for five months without a decision.

In standard practice, such requests are handled relatively quickly. However, by allowing this request to languish in "intentional silence," the Supreme Court has effectively paralyzed Judge Lee Jin-kwan’s court. Because the recusal issue remains unresolved at the highest level, the lower court’s proceedings are stuck in limbo. The DP views this as a deliberate "political calculation" rather than administrative backlog. They argue that the Supreme Court is intentionally tying the hands of judges who might not align with the Chief Justice’s perceived political leanings.

"What is so special about Choi Sang-mok that the Supreme Court has held onto this for five months?"

This question, frequently echoed by DP supporters, suggests that the "silence" of the Supreme Court is actually a loud message to the rest of the judiciary: "Do not step out of line." If the Supreme Court eventually accepts the recusal, it sets a precedent that the judicial leadership can effectively remove a judge from a case simply by letting a challenge sit on a desk until it becomes a de facto disqualification. This, the DP argues, undermines the conscience of individual judges and subordinates them to the political whims of the Chief Justice.

The 2027 "Nuclear Bomb" and the Electoral Clock

Perhaps the most explosive allegation is that Chief Justice Cho is preparing a "judicial nuclear bomb" to be detonated just before his retirement. Cho, born in 1957, is set to reach the mandatory retirement age for judges (70) on June 5, 2027. This date is not just a personal milestone; it sits right in the middle of the next presidential election cycle. The DP fears that Cho intends to use his final months in office to issue a definitive ruling on President Lee Jae-myung’s cases involving the Public Official Election Act.

The fear centers on a legal mechanism known as "remand with a finding of guilt" (pagi-hwansong). In the Korean legal system, if the Supreme Court overturns a lower court's decision and sends it back (remands it) with a specific instruction that the defendant is guilty, the lower court is legally bound by that finding. Under the Court Organization Act, lower courts must follow the Supreme Court's interpretation of the law in that specific case. There is almost no room for a lower court judge to reach a different conclusion once the Supreme Court has spoken.

If the Supreme Court, under a "Cho Hee-dae line" of justices, issues such a ruling, it could strip President Lee of his presidency or his right to run for re-election. The DP views this as a high-stakes design to achieve a "judicial assassination" of a political leader. By timing this for the eve of his retirement, Cho could theoretically leave a lasting political legacy that fundamentally alters the course of Korean democracy, all while shielded by the "independence" of the bench. The DP’s push for impeachment is, in their eyes, an attempt to defuse this bomb before the timer runs out.

Suspension of Power: The Constitutional Gamble

If the DP proceeds with the impeachment motion, the immediate effect is more important than the final verdict of the Constitutional Court. Under Article 65 of the Constitution, the moment the impeachment notice is delivered, the Chief Justice’s authority is suspended. This means Cho would immediately lose his power to appoint judges, influence case assignments, and nominate new Supreme Court justices.

During such a suspension, the Senior Associate Justice would take over as Acting Chief Justice. While some worry that a leadership vacuum would destabilize the courts, the DP argues that a "systemic" operation under an acting head is preferable to what they see as the "autocratic" and "partisan" leadership of Cho. In legal theory, an acting head is expected to maintain the "status quo" rather than embark on major policy shifts or controversial appointments. For the DP, "freezing" the judiciary in its current state is a win, as it halts the perceived "judicial coup" in its tracks.

This strategy mirrors past political battles in Korea. When Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (the former president who was impeached and is currently imprisoned) was in power, the use of administrative and prosecutorial power was a constant point of contention. The DP, having learned from the tumultuous era of Mr. Yoon's presidency, is now using the legislature's constitutional "check and balance" power to ensure that the judiciary does not become the last bastion of the previous administration’s influence.

The Silence of the Press and the Role of the Citizenry

One of the most striking aspects of this conflict is the divide in media coverage. While alternative media and online platforms are buzzing with detailed analyses of these "judicial coup" allegations, many of Korea's large, established conservative newspapers have remained relatively quiet or have dismissed the DP’s move as a "shielding" tactic designed only to protect President Lee from legal trouble. They often frame the DP's actions as an "attack on judicial independence."

However, the DP and its supporters argue that "judicial independence" is not a blank check for judges to act without accountability. They contend that when a Chief Justice is suspected of using his office for partisan ends, the only democratic recourse is impeachment. They warn that if the public remains indifferent, they risk becoming "cattle and pigs"—a common Korean metaphor for a citizenry that is easily manipulated and led by those in power. For the DP, the "uncomfortable truth" buried deep within the judiciary must be brought to light through the impeachment process.

The DP’s narrative is one of vigilance. They argue that the judiciary’s authority is not derived from the height of the judge’s bench, but from the trust of the people. If that trust is broken through "political sword-dancing" (kal-chum), then the constitutional remedy must be applied. The DP is banking on the idea that an informed and active citizenry will see through the "procedural" excuses of the court and recognize the underlying political struggle.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will be decisive for the Korean judiciary and the presidency of Lee Jae-myung. Here are the concrete milestones to watch:

  • Party Platform Adoption: Watch for when the DP officially adopts the impeachment of Cho Hee-dae as a formal party platform. This will signal that the "T-word" has moved from talk to action.
  • The 5-Month Delay: Keep an eye on the Supreme Court’s decision regarding the recusal of Judge Choi Sang-mok. If the court suddenly issues a ruling after months of silence, it may be a sign of the judiciary attempting to "de-escalate" to avoid impeachment.
  • Constitutional Court Appointments: As vacancies arise in the Constitutional Court, the battle over who nominates the replacements will become a proxy war for the Chief Justice’s own survival.
  • Public Sentiment: If the PPP and conservative media successfully frame this as "interference in the law," the DP may face a backlash. Conversely, if the public perceives the court as a "remnant of the Yoon Suk-yeol era," the DP will have the mandate to push forward.

The struggle over the Chief Justice is ultimately a struggle over who has the final say in a democracy: the people through their elected representatives, or the "guardians of the law" through their final sentences. As the DP prepares its next move, the very definition of "judicial independence" in South Korea is being rewritten in real-time.
