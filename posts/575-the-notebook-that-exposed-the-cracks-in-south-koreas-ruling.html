The Notebook that Exposed the Cracks in South Korea’s Ruling Party

October 02, 2026

There is a recurring, unsettling scene in South Korean politics: a high-ranking official is caught on camera, their private notebook exposed to the lenses of the press. While the leaking of these notes is often dismissed as a mere "happening," the contents usually paint a far more revealing picture than any official press release. They offer a raw, unvarnished look at the internal anxieties and power dynamics currently gripping the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country’s main liberal party.

When Kim Min-seok, a prominent leader within the DPK, was recently caught with his notebook open, it revealed a list of names and agendas that suggest the party’s core leadership is less focused on sweeping policy reform and more concerned with managing internal dissent and keeping tabs on online political influencers.

The Memo of Power: Influencers and Internal Surveillance

Last month, Kim’s notebook made headlines for a blunt note reading "Jin-sook/Han Dong-hoon OUT," referring to the DPK’s desire to neutralize the influence of prominent figures or rivals. The latest iteration of his notes, however, is even more telling. Scribbled alongside serious national policy items—like the relocation of the National Assembly’s administrative functions to Sejong City—were names of high-profile YouTubers and political commentators, such as Park Si-young and the head of the channel "Sisa Tapa."

To an outside observer, this might seem trivial. But in the context of Korean politics, where "influencer" channels can move thousands of votes and shape party sentiment, these names are heavy hitters. Kim is currently leading internal disciplinary proceedings involving the very people noted in his book. The fact that the ruling party’s leadership is treating these YouTubers as entities to be monitored, managed, or attacked suggests that the party is operating more like a private intelligence firm than a legislative body. It highlights how vulnerable large parties have become to the influence of "external speakers"—online personalities who wield immense power over the party's base.

This atmosphere of surveillance is further evidenced by a recent legislative proposal by DPK lawmaker Cho Gye-won. The bill seeks to strengthen laws against "dual party membership"—the practice of holding membership in two political parties simultaneously. While already illegal, it has long been used as a tactic to manipulate internal party primaries. Kim’s mention of "Shincheonji dual membership" (referring to a controversial religious group often accused of infiltrating political organizations) indicates a deep-seated paranoia within the party about being compromised from the outside.

The "Han Dong-hoon File" and the Obsession with Control

The "Han Dong-hoon file"—referencing the high-profile conservative leader and former Justice Minister—has evolved from a simple note into a symbol of a political culture that has abandoned policy competition in favor of perpetual information warfare. The ruling party seems to spend more time "filing" away dirt on rivals and monitoring internal critics than it does addressing the economic anxieties of the average citizen.

Even more revealing are the rumors that the notebook contained names of potential candidates for the Seoul Mayoral election. As the DPK’s approval ratings fluctuate, the party leadership’s gaze appears fixed not on the bread-and-butter issues of the public, but on internal power maps and the distribution of influence for upcoming local elections. When the party’s compass points toward YouTube channels and political hit-lists rather than public welfare, it suggests a leadership team that has lost its sense of direction.

The Tragedy of "Face-Saving" Politics in the Blue House

This obsession with control is not limited to the party; it has permeated the Blue House (the South Korean presidential office). Internal reports suggest that when questioned about controversial cabinet nominees, officials have dismissed calls for withdrawal with a singular, flawed logic: that replacing a nominee would damage the President’s "dignity" or make the administration look weak in front of its "hardcore" supporters.

The appointment process has morphed from an evaluation of character and competence into a high-stakes game of political ego. If the administration retreats, they fear it will look like they have bowed to pressure from their own party’s radical faction or their political enemies.

This is a fundamental miscalculation. Governance is not a zero-sum game of winning or losing face. By clinging to unqualified nominees to avoid the perception of defeat, the administration has created a recurring "personnel disaster." From former university presidents to high-level legal investigators, a string of appointees has been forced to resign under clouds of scandal, further eroding the administration’s moral authority. The Blue House has effectively turned its appointment power into a shield for its own stubbornness, rather than a tool to find the most capable public servants.

The Illusion of the "Core Supporter"

Perhaps most dangerous is the administration’s response to its falling approval ratings. Recent data shows a sharp decline in support for President Lee Jae-myung, even among the progressive base. Yet, those in the inner circle continue to diagnose the problem as a temporary annoyance caused by "noisy" critics or external economic factors, insisting that the "core" base remains unshakable.

This is a dangerous delusion. By dismissing any dissenting voice as not being a "true" supporter, the administration is effectively purging its own coalition. They are like a teacher who expels all the underperforming students and then claims the class average has improved. The classroom might look better on paper, but the actual student body—the voters—is shrinking, and the institution is failing.

What to Watch Next:

  • Policy vs. Purges: Will the DPK refocus on the legislative agenda, or will the "notebook politics" of monitoring YouTubers and internal rivals continue to dominate their output?
  • Personnel Reshuffles: Watch for whether the Blue House continues to defend controversial appointees at all costs, or if they adopt a more flexible approach to personnel to stem the bleeding of public support.
  • Voter Sentiment: Keep an eye on upcoming local election polling. If the gap between the "core" base and the broader public continues to widen, the administration’s strategy of excluding critics may lead to a significant political reckoning.

Ultimately, a government that surrounds itself with mirrors reflecting only what it wants to see is a government that has lost its connection to reality. As the notes in Kim Min-seok’s book suggest, the ruling party is currently trapped inside a fortress of its own making, oblivious to the fact that the walls are closing in.
