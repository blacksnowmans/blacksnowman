When Power Governs by Algorithm: Tragedies, Targets, and the Crisis Inside South Korea's Ruling Camp

October 18, 2026

When a firefighter loses his life inside a burning building, the unwritten compact of any constitutional democracy is straightforward: the state stops, honors the sacrifice, and sets to work identifying what went wrong. The grief is public, and the responsibility is institutional. Yet inside South Korea's ruling progressive establishment, that civic grief was recently hijacked for a very different purpose. Within hours of the tragic loss of a frontline firefighter, the incident was converted into factional ammunition, manipulated by party leaders to score points against internal rivals and demand public apologies from provincial leadership.

This episode is not an isolated rhetorical slip. It is the clearest symptom yet of a deeper institutional rot within the Democratic Party of Korea, South Korea's ruling liberal party. Ever since the dramatic fall of the previous conservative administration—which culminated in the impeachment, removal, and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—the ruling bloc promised a return to stable, predictable statecraft. Instead, Korean governance is increasingly held hostage by late-night social media broadsides from the very top of power, coordinated online intimidation against internal critics, and the quiet consolidation of an insular faction whose roots trace back to local politics in the commuter city of Seongnam.

The Weaponization of Public Tragedy in the Ruling Camp

The immediate catalyst for public dismay was the manner in which the party hierarchy handled the fallen firefighter. Rather than focusing on equipment standards, departmental command structures, or systemic firefighter welfare, political operatives transformed the death into a political battering ram. The party's internal messaging deliberately twisted the tragedy to corner Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae, demanding contrition and reframing a fatal workplace incident into a litmus test of factional obedience.

This tactic fits a well-established rhetorical formula favored by Democratic Party representative Kim Min-seok. The method is subtle on the surface but predatory in effect: begin with a high-minded expression of regret to establish procedural moral high ground, then attach a poisoned barb aimed squarely at an internal rival. In a widely discussed post from July 2022, Kim opened by lamenting a disruption surrounding candidate Jung Chung-rae, only to immediately pivot into demanding an apology from Governor Choo while conflating the matter with sweeping accusations of "verbal abuse, dual party registrations, ghost party members, and infiltration by the Shincheonji religious sect."

By stripping events of context and designing statements purely to demonize intra-party competitors, party leaders have turned grief into an instrument of administrative terror. The response from ordinary voters has been swift and unforgiving. Public opinion surveys recently recorded a sharp seven-percentage-point plunge in the Democratic Party's support rating—a steep decline that reflects growing exhaustion, even among base supporters, with the degradation of public discourse.

Digital Strongmanship: How Social Media Replaced Institutional Governance

At the center of this cultural shift sits the presidency itself. President Lee Jae-myung's fixation on social media has crossed from energetic public outreach into a structural risk for the Republic of Korea. During Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term in office, critics lamented an insular, tone-deaf governing style that ultimately paved the way for his impeachment and imprisonment. Yet the current administration presents an entirely new challenge to democratic norms: governance via the unfiltered, late-night impulses of a single executive's personal social media account.

South Korea's modern history offers stark warnings about authoritarian overreach. Under the military dictatorships of Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan, repression arrived via physical bayonets and the interrogation cells of the Korean Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA). Today's iteration is digital. By substituting institutional briefings and rigorous policy vetting with short, combative online posts, the president routinely points a finger at specific targets—a practice known domestically as jwa-pyo jjik-gi, or "coordinate targeting."

The mechanism was laid bare in the treatment of prominent economist Professor Choi Bae-geun. When Choi offered constructive critique regarding the administration's economic trajectory, he was met not with counterarguments, but with targeted online disapproval from the president, immediately followed by an orchestrated smear campaign claiming Choi was lashing out because he had been denied a coveted senior government post. Choi was forced into a humiliating public defense, stating plainly:

"What everyone is most curious about is whether I demanded a high-ranking position. I never did. I never asked for one. In fact, I never initiated contact with the president after his inauguration; it was the Presidential Office that contacted me first earlier this year."

Once the head of state identifies a target, loyalist online mobs—what Choi and other intellectuals have characterized as "Digital Red Guards"—descend upon the dissenter with coordinated digital harassment. Faced with this online gauntlet, elected lawmakers within the ruling Democratic Party have fallen into a terrified silence. While conservative opposition figures like Kwon Young-jin of the People Power Party and independent commentators like former lawmaker Lee Un-ju issue sharp critiques, the ruling party's own legislators remain paralyzed, terrified that a single presidential post will unleash a wave of harassment against their offices.

The Shadow of Seongnam and Backroom Networks

This reliance on digital enforcement is closely intertwined with who actually holds the levers of influence behind the scenes. During the annual parliamentary audits (gukjeong gamsa) in the National Assembly, lawmakers and domestic media have increasingly turned their scrutiny toward the so-called "Seongnam Line"—a close-knit circle of aides who have surrounded President Lee since his days as mayor of Seongnam, a satellite city south of Seoul.

Figures such as Kim Hyun-ji and Jeong Jin-sang, long operating away from the glare of official cabinet positions, have become lightning rods in legislative questioning. Observers have drawn attention to enduring organizational links connecting these aides to the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance (Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance), an influential progressive activist faction historically associated with the banned Unified Progressive Party and figures like former lawmaker Lee Seok-ki.

Professor Choi Bae-geun's own recollections offer a rare window into how these networks operated. After moving to Hanam in the late 1990s, Choi—a veteran of the prominent civic group People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy who had worked alongside the late Seoul Mayor Park Won-soon—helped establish the Hanam Democratic Alliance alongside local activists. When Lee Jae-myung's initial presidential campaign apparatus was forming, it was Dr. Kim Ji-young, an operative plugged into the Gyeonggi Dongbu networks, who brought Choi to a quiet Chinese restaurant in Hanam to meet Jeong Jin-sang.

During that meeting, Kim introduced Jeong as a figure who would play a role "equal to, or greater than, that of Yang Jung-chul to former President Moon Jae-in"—a reference to Moon's most trusted political architect. The meeting underscored that the Seongnam apparatus was not merely a team of municipal administrators, but a deeply ideological and insular cadre bound by years of shared struggles outside the mainstream party establishment.

The institutional cost of this arrangement has been devastating. The systematic, rules-based candidate nomination and party management framework built under former party chairman Lee Hae-chan has largely disintegrated. When informal inner circles eclipse official party-government consultative bodies, the transparent governance required of a constitutional republic gives way to opaque factional survival.

What to Watch Next: The Limits of Loyalty in South Korean Politics

South Korean progressivism drew its historic legitimacy from two pillars: procedural democracy and solidarity with the marginalized, principles championed by democratic icons like Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun. By converting a firefighter's sacrifice into an intra-party hit and using online crowds to terrorize internal critics, the current leadership is burning through that moral capital at an alarming rate.

To assess where this internal crisis heads next, domestic and international observers should keep a close eye on several concrete indicators over the coming weeks:

  • The 100-Day Leadership Milestone: Around November 24, Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok reaches his 100th day in office. This anniversary will serve as a crucial barometer for whether public backlash and falling poll numbers will force a pivot in messaging, or whether the leadership will double down on factional purges.
  • Parliamentary Audit Revelations: Continued testimony in the National Assembly regarding the unofficial influence of the Seongnam Line, particularly surrounding Jeong Jin-sang and Kim Hyun-ji, will test the administration's ability to maintain narrative control as the conservative opposition presses for deeper inquiries.
  • The Rebellion of Dues-Paying Members: Grassroots party members are actively debating whether to stage mass walkouts or hold their ground. Calls urging members not to resign their party memberships—insisting they stay to vote out entrenched factional operatives—will determine whether the party's rank-and-file can reclaim procedural democracy from within.

When state power abandons institutional restraint to vent personal grievance through 140-character digital decrees, the entire public apparatus begins to hollow out. Whether South Korea's governing party can correct course will depend on whether its lawmakers find the courage to break their silence before the public delivers its own verdict.
