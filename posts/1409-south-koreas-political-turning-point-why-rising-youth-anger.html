South Korea’s Political Turning Point: Why Rising Youth Anger and Third-Party Pressure Threaten President Lee Jae-myung

October 22, 2026

When President Lee Jae-myung took office following the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, he carried the hopes of a voter base desperate for stability, social fairness, and economic reform. Stepping into power after a turbulent era marked by executive overreach and broken promises to young citizens, the new administration promised a fresh social contract. Yet, barely into his term, President Lee finds his political honeymoon thoroughly shattered. The latest nationwide polling numbers indicate a sharp and structural crisis of public trust, driven not by routine political fatigue, but by deep disillusionment among young voters and growing fractures within the progressive coalition.

For international observers watching South Korea's high-stakes political arena, approval ratings in Seoul are rarely just quiet satisfaction meters—they are early warning systems. When an administration's approval falls into the low 30s while negative sentiment surges past 60 percent, legislative momentum grinds to a halt. In South Korea's presidential system, where executive power relies heavily on public mandate to push major economic and administrative reforms through a fractured National Assembly, such numbers signal a sudden freeze in governing power. To understand why this shift is happening now requires looking past the surface headlines and examining the converging pressures of real estate anxiety, controversial cabinet picks, and dynamic third-party movements on the left.

Inside the Numbers: A Deepening Crisis of Public Trust

The clearest snapshot of this shift comes from a survey conducted by Jang-gil Research between October 17 and 19, 2026. According to the poll, President Lee Jae-myung’s positive performance rating plummeted to 32.1 percent (22.6 percent reporting "doing very well" and 9.4 percent "doing somewhat well"). In stark contrast, negative evaluations surged to 66.1 percent—with an astonishing 56.7 percent saying the president is doing "very poorly." With only 1.9 percent answering "don't know," public sentiment has hardened quickly, leaving little middle ground for the administration to recover without decisive action.

What makes these figures especially dangerous for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) is the breakdown across demographics. The administration's steepest collapse has occurred among young South Koreans in their twenties and thirties—the very demographic whose fluid voting behavior routinely decides national elections. Among voters in their 20s, disapproval reached a staggering 77.2 percent, with approval languishing at just 29.2 percent. The numbers were even harsher among voters in their 30s, where approval dropped to a lowest-in-nation 21.3 percent, alongside 64.8 percent disapproval. Even among core middle-aged demographics in their 40s (40.4 percent positive to 58.0 percent negative) and 50s (33.9 percent positive to 65.5 percent negative), critical sentiment easily outpaces approval.

Geographically, the firewall protecting the administration has collapsed almost everywhere except the historic progressive stronghold of Honam (the Gwangju and Jeolla regions), where approval stands at 59.4 percent. Across the rest of the nation, the picture is grim: approval stands at 27.7 percent in Seoul, 25.2 percent in the southeastern Busan/Ulsan/Gyeongnam region, and a mere 19.2 percent in Daegu/Gyeongbuk. Most alarmingly for the ruling party, the crucial suburban battleground of Incheon and Gyeonggi Province registered only 31.0 percent support.

The drivers behind this widespread disapproval are concrete and economic. National polling analysis by Gallup Korea highlights three primary drivers of dissatisfaction: housing policy concerns (24 percent), controversial cabinet confirmation hearings (16 percent), and the broader cost-of-living crisis (10 percent). In particular, heated confirmation fights surrounding Justice Minister nominee Kim Seung-won and Gender Equality and Family Minister nominee Yong Hye-in have reopened sensitive debates over political privilege and institutional fairness—topics that resonate deeply with younger South Koreans struggling with stagnant wages and soaring living expenses.

The Changing Map: Progressive Split and Conservative Inroads

While executive approval has slid, party preference figures reveal a more complex and fluid political alignment. In overall party support, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (33.4 percent) and the main opposition conservative People Power Party (PPP, 31.2 percent) remain locked in a tight race within the margin of error. However, the most significant movement is occurring on the progressive flank with the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by prominent reformist Cho Kuk. His party saw its support climb 1.6 percentage points to reach 9.0 percent, followed by the Progressive Party at 2.2 percent and the Reform Party at 1.5 percent.

This rise highlights a deliberate strategy among left-leaning voters. Rather than defecting to the conservative opposition, many progressive supporters are maintaining their official DPK ties while strategically backing Cho Kuk’s party in opinion polls. By lending weight to a more uncompromising third-force party, progressive voters are sending a direct signal to President Lee's government to pursue aggressive prosecutorial and institutional reforms rather than slipping into establishment complacency. Analysts describe this as a tactical "two-track" maneuver—holding DPK membership while elevating the Rebuilding Korea Party to force the ruling leadership's hand.

On the opposite side of the spectrum, the main opposition People Power Party is making notable gains among young adults. In the 30s age bracket, the conservative PPP actually surged ahead of the DPK, leading 30.4 percent to 25.6 percent. This dynamic echoes the political realignment seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's era, when young voters abandoned conservative candidates after feeling betrayed by unfulfilled promises and economic stagnation. Now, as young adults face steep interest rates, high rent burdens, and an uphill battle for homeownership under the current government, their support is once again shifting away from the incumbent power.

Lame-Duck Signals and the Shadow War for Future Power

As an administration's approval rating hovers near the 30 percent mark, media outlets and political analysts inevitably turn their focus toward future power brokers. Recently, South Korean news organizations have begun commissioning polls specifically measuring public perception of political figures positioned as the direct counterweights to President Lee Jae-myung. When pollsters start explicitly testing counter-figures midway through a presidential mandate, it serves as an unmistakable marker of diminishing executive authority.

In recent surveys gauging who best serves as the primary opposition counterweight to the president, PPP leader Jang Dong-hyuk led the field at 17.8 percent, closely followed by former PPP leader Han Dong-hoon at 17.4 percent. On the progressive and independent sides, senior figures like lawmaker Choo Mi-ae (11.9 percent), political commentator Rhyu Si-min (10.0 percent), Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon (9.2 percent), DPK lawmaker Jung Chung-rae (7.0 percent), and Rebuilding Korea Party leader Cho Kuk (4.6 percent) all drew notable backing.

Within the conservative camp, the battle to succeed or challenge the current order is intensifying. A survey by Jowon C&I for Straight News found former PPP chief Han Dong-hoon leading prospective conservative presidential contenders with 18.7 percent support, closely trailed by Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon and PPP leader Jang Dong-hyuk. Meanwhile, digital sentiment analysis conducted by WinJi Korea Consulting showed that online mentions for PPP leader Jang Dong-hyuk spiked dramatically in late September—particularly following his aggressive rhetoric during meetings with military leadership and his calls for presidential accountability. However, while such hardline positioning rallies the conservative base, data shows it simultaneously triggers high negative sentiment among moderate voters, underlining the ongoing struggle within the opposition to build a broad coalition.

Political power abhors a vacuum. When a sitting president's approval sinks, attention naturally shifts away from the presidential office and toward rival figures offering an alternative vision. If the Lee administration fails to reverse its downward trajectory, this dynamic will only accelerate, leading to early maneuvering within both the ruling and opposition camps.

What to Watch Next: The Hard Path to Recovery

For President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party, rebounding from this political trough will require more than aggressive communications or blaming media coverage. Rebuilding momentum demands concrete policy shifts that address the everyday realities of South Korean voters. Observers should watch several key benchmarks in the coming months:

  • Personnel Resets and Cabinet Confirmation Decisions: The immediate test for the presidential office lies in how it handles stalled cabinet appointments, particularly for controversial nominees like Kim Seung-won (Justice) and Yong Hye-in (Gender Equality and Family). Withdrawing or adjusting these nominations could signal a willing pivot toward public sentiment on ethics and standards.
  • Targeted Real Estate and Livelihood Policies: Because housing anxieties drive nearly a quarter of all negative evaluations, the government's upcoming measures on mortgage lending rates, tenant protections, and housing supply will be critical to winning back voters in their 20s and 30s.
  • Managing the Progressive Coalition: The DPK leadership must navigate its complex relationship with Cho Kuk’s Rebuilding Korea Party. If the DPK tries to suppress its third-party ally, it risks further alienating core progressive voters who want faster reform; if it leans too far left, it risks permanently losing moderate swing voters in the capital region.
  • Opposition Strategy and Moderate Expansion: For the main opposition People Power Party, translating youth discontent into durable political support will require moving beyond hardline rhetoric against President Lee. Whether conservative leaders like Han Dong-hoon, Jang Dong-hyuk, and Oh Se-hoon can offer practical economic solutions will decide whether their recent gains among young voters are permanent or temporary.

In South Korea's vibrant and fast-moving democracy, public sentiment can shift as quickly as it forms. But as political history in Seoul repeatedly demonstrates, an administration that ignores the warning signs of economic distress and youth alienation risks watching its reform agenda slip away far sooner than expected.
