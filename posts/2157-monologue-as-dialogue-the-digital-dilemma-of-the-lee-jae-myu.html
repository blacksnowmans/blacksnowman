Monologue as Dialogue: The Digital Dilemma of the Lee Jae-myung Presidency

November 10, 2026

The studio lights are bright, the cameras are high-definition, and the atmosphere is carefully curated. On a Thursday night at 9:00 PM, South Koreans tuning in to YouTube saw a familiar face in an unfamiliar setting. President Lee Jae-myung, a leader who has built his political career on a reputation for "direct communication," was launching his new channel, "The President’s Y." Hosted by a former news anchor, the program was designed to bypass traditional media filters and explain the "Why" behind government policies directly to the people.

"If I hadn't communicated directly with the people, I probably wouldn't be in this position today," the President remarked during the broadcast, describing direct engagement as his "lifeline." But as the 30-minute pre-recorded session unfolded, a sense of unease began to settle among political analysts and the public alike. Instead of a breakthrough in transparency, the debut felt more like a polished monologue. For a president whose approval ratings have hovered in the high 30% range—with critics often citing "one-sided governance" as a primary grievance—the move into the YouTube studio raises a fundamental question: Is this an effort to listen, or simply a more sophisticated way to talk over the crowd?

The Trap of One-Way Digital Diplomacy

To understand why this YouTube channel is controversial, one must look at how President Lee Jae-myung has traditionally governed. Since his inauguration, the President has been a prolific user of X (formerly Twitter), often posting multiple times a day to defend his stance on judicial reform or economic policy. In his view, the traditional media is a distorted lens, and social media is the only way to reach the "real" public. However, this strategy has often backfired. Long-form posts intended to clarify complex issues like the prosecution of political rivals or housing market cooling measures frequently spark "interpretation wars," where supporters and detractors fixate on a single word or nuance, leaving the actual policy lost in the noise.

The launch of "The President’s Y" represents an institutionalization of this habit. While the Democratic Party (South Korea’s ruling liberal party) argues that the channel is necessary to fight misinformation, the People Power Party (the main conservative opposition) sees it as a waste of taxpayer money. They argue that the government already possesses the Korea TV (KTV)—a state-run broadcaster—and a massive press corps to disseminate information. Creating a "personal" studio for the President suggests a retreat into a digital echo chamber where tough, unscripted questions from journalists are replaced by pre-screened comments and friendly banter.

Communication is not defined by the volume of words produced by the sender, but by the quality of the response to the receiver’s concerns. Political science data from Gallup Korea consistently shows that the public’s frustration stems from a perceived lack of accountability on issues like the cost of living and the slow pace of promised reforms. Flooding the digital space with more "content" does not necessarily equate to more "connection." When a leader feels that the public "doesn't understand" his intent, the instinct is often to speak more. But in a democracy, the burden of understanding lies with the leader to hear what the public is saying, not the other way around.

Cults, Conspiracies, and the 0.4% Reality

While the President was adjusting his microphone in the YouTube studio, his own party was being torn apart by a controversy that sounds more like a political thriller than a policy debate. During the Democratic Party’s national convention in July, a narrative emerged that threatened to delegitimize the entire leadership contest. Kim Min-seok, who eventually won the chairmanship, leveled a shocking accusation: he claimed a "Triple Secret Alliance" consisting of anti-Lee factions, secessionists, and Shincheonji was attempting to hijack the party.

For context, Shincheonji is a controversial religious organization often described as a cult in South Korea. It gained international notoriety in 2020 for its role in a massive COVID-19 outbreak in the city of Daegu. In the hyper-polarized world of Korean politics, being linked to Shincheonji is a "scarlet letter" that can ruin a career. The accusation was aimed squarely at rival candidate Jung Cheong-rae, painting his supporters as religious infiltrators. The party’s digital bulletin boards exploded with vitriol, as members began accusing one another of being "spies" for the cult.

Months later, after a formal investigation by a party task force, the truth turned out to be far less dramatic. Initial rumors suggested that 30,000 to 50,000 Shincheonji members had mass-joined the party to swing the vote. However, the data revealed that only about 3,300 suspicious voters actually held voting rights for the convention. In a party with millions of members, this accounted for a mere 0.4% of the electorate. This tiny fraction was nowhere near enough to influence the outcome, let alone constitute a "secret alliance" capable of subverting party democracy. The "Shincheonji scare" was, in reality, a masterclass in black propaganda—a way to mobilize the base by creating an existential threat out of a statistical ghost.

The Farce of the 30-Minute Private Viewing

When the investigation results finally came in, the Democratic Party leadership faced a dilemma. Admitting that the Shincheonji threat was negligible would mean admitting that the Chairman had won his seat through a campaign of misinformation. Instead of a transparent release of the findings, the party staged what critics are calling a "bizarre comedy." They allowed lawmakers and regional chairpersons to view the investigation records in the Secretary-General’s office—but with draconian restrictions.

"It was a scene out of a spy movie. We were given exactly 30 minutes. No photos, no photocopies, and no handwritten notes. We had to memorize the numbers and leave."

This "private viewing" strategy backfired spectacularly. Because no one could take evidence out of the room, the lawmakers emerged with conflicting stories based on their own political biases. Supporters of the leadership claimed the records showed a "dangerous potential" that justified the initial warnings, while rivals argued the records proved the accusations were a total fabrication. Rather than settling the issue, the party created a vacuum where rumors and "second-hand testimonies" flourished. This culture of secrecy undermines the very foundation of party democracy. If a ruling party cannot trust its own members with data regarding its internal elections, how can it expect the broader public to trust its national policy data?

A Leadership Crisis Masked by Production Values

The disconnect between the President’s polished YouTube presence and the raw, ugly infighting within his party points to a deeper crisis of leadership. While the President sits in a studio talking about "philosophy," his party’s "Golden Time" for passing essential livelihood legislation and judicial reform is being squandered on internal purges and damage control. The Democratic Party is currently embroiled in debates over easing the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (a move that alienates its core progressive base) while simultaneously dealing with the legal fallout of the Shincheonji accusations.

Under South Korea’s Political Parties Act, spreading false information during a leadership election to get elected or to make a rival lose is a serious crime, punishable by up to five years in prison or a fine of 10 million won (about $7,500). Chairman Kim Min-seok recently offered a half-hearted apology, stating he was "sorry for the pain caused" during his "sincere effort to protect the party from religious intervention." But in politics, an apology with a "but" is rarely seen as an apology. By framing his misinformation as a "protection effort," he has doubled down on the narrative that his rivals were a threat, even if the numbers don't back it up.

This internal friction is the mirror image of the President’s YouTube dilemma. In both cases, there is an obsession with frame control—the idea that if you control the platform and the narrative, you can ignore the inconvenient facts on the ground. For the President, the "ground" is the cold reality of citizens struggling with debt and inflation. For the party, the "ground" is the procedural integrity of their own elections. In both instances, the leadership appears more comfortable in front of a controlled camera than in front of a critical, unscripted audience.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will determine whether "The President’s Y" becomes a meaningful tool for governance or a footnote in a failing communication strategy. The public should watch for several concrete developments:

  • The "Unscripted" Test: Will the YouTube channel eventually host a live, unedited Q&A session? If the program remains a pre-recorded, edited package, it will likely be dismissed as mere state propaganda, failing to move the needle on the President’s approval ratings.
  • Legal Challenges: Watch if the internal rivals within the Democratic Party decide to take the Shincheonji misinformation case to the courts. A formal investigation into Chairman Kim Min-seok’s claims could lead to a leadership vacuum if the "0.4% reality" is legally deemed as intentional false-fact propagation.
  • The "Doorstepping" Comparison: Critics are already comparing Lee’s studio sessions to the "doorstepping" (informal daily press briefings) attempted by Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during his presidency. While Mr. Yoon’s attempt ended in disaster due to lack of preparation and defensive rhetoric, it at least faced the press. If Lee continues to avoid the press corps in favor of his own channel, the "imperial presidency" narrative will likely strengthen.
  • Legislative Gridlock: As the Democratic Party deals with its internal "witch hunt" fallout, keep an eye on the budget season. If the party cannot consolidate its message, the main opposition People Power Party will find it easier to stall the government’s key initiatives, turning the President’s "Why" into a "Why bother?"

In the age of social media, the most powerful political tool is not a high-definition camera or a viral clip. It is the ability to stand in a room with people who disagree with you and answer their questions until the tension breaks. Until President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party leadership step out of the studio and into that room, their "communication" will remain a one-way street leading toward a political dead end.
