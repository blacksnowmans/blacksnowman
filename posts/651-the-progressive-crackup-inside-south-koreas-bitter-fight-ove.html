The Progressive Crackup: Inside South Korea's Bitter Fight Over Prosecution Reform

October 03, 2026

South Korea’s political ecosystem is currently gripped by a bitter, highly public family feud. Two of the progressive camp's most formidable heavyweights—former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae and veteran commentator and politician Rhyu Si-min—have turned their fire inward, launching blistering attacks against the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. For observers watching from the outside, the spectacle of longtime allies tearing into each other over personnel choices and stalled reforms might look like routine political infighting. In reality, it signals a deeper, more consequential anxiety: a growing fear among progressives that the very reforms they fought for are being hollowed out from within.

The Ghosts of Prosecutional Reform: Choo Mi-ae’s Regret

The immediate catalyst for the current storm is an appointment that has left South Korea's reformist camp deeply divided. When the presidential office submitted the nomination of Kim Ji-yong to head the newly established Serious Crime Investigation Agency—an organization designed to strip traditional prosecutional powers away from the dominant 검찰 (the prosecution service)—it triggered an immediate backlash from an unexpected source: Choo Mi-ae, currently governor of Gyeonggi Province.

Choo, who as justice minister in 2020 waged a legendary, bruising war against then-Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol, took to social media to air a stunning confession: her judgment in people was deeply flawed. She revealed that back in 2020, despite her stated desire to sideline traditional, hardline investigative prosecutors, she had elevated Kim Ji-yong to a senior role on the recommendation of her vice minister. That trust was ultimately betrayed, Choo argued, when Kim later joined a collective backlash by prosecutors resisting disciplinary action against corrupt officials.

By raising the alarm now, Choo is cutting through the administration's defensive talking points. Supporters of the nominee have pointed out that Kim once ordered reinvestigations into cases involving Yoon Suk Yeol’s family, suffering career setbacks under the subsequent conservative administration as a result. But Choo argues that labeling someone as "pro-Yoon" or "anti-Yoon" is a superficial framing that misses the core issue. At a time when thousands of frontline prosecutors and justice workers are looking for decisive leadership to anchor a brand-new investigative framework, appointing a compromise figure with a questionable reformist pedigree is a dangerous gamble.

Rhyu Si-min and the Metaphor of the Slaughterhouse

While Choo’s critique targeted personnel and institutional memory, Rhyu Si-min went straight for the ideological jugular of the Lee Jae-myung administration. Rhyu, an influential public intellectual and former minister, recently broke a long public silence to deliver a harsh verdict on the government's handling of prosecution reform, declaring flatly that the administration's current approach amounts to "deceiving the public."

Rhyu deployed a series of vivid metaphors that quickly dominated political talk shows and YouTube commentary. He likened President Lee’s handling of the prosecution issue to a man wearing shoes handed to him by the public—shoes that he clearly doesn't want to wear and walks in as if being dragged to a slaughterhouse. Rather than enacting a total structural rebuild of the country's investigative apparatus, Rhyu argued, the administration is offering cosmetic adjustments that ultimately leave loopholes for the entrenched prosecutional establishment to survive.

What makes Rhyu’s intervention particularly stinging is that it extends beyond policy to the internal culture of the ruling Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal party. Pointing to a series of recent controversies surrounding internal party nominations, appointments, and high-profile candidate selections, Rhyu leveled a devastating assessment: a party that tolerates such opaque systems has "gone bad." His critique underscores a quiet fear that the party is losing its democratic health, sliding into a top-down, vertically controlled power structure centered around the presidential office where internal dissent is treated as a threat rather than a corrective.

The Erosion of Institutional Scrutiny

The friction over high-level appointments highlights a broader systemic failure in South Korea's political culture: the steady hollowing out of legislative confirmation hearings. Originally designed as a vital democratic safeguard to vet the ethics and competence of high-ranking public officials, confirmation hearings at both the central and local levels have increasingly devolved into partisan rubber stamps.

When the ruling party holds a majority or controls local assemblies, opposition voices are routinely brushed aside, and dissenting opinions are treated as acts of political treason. Local government hearings in regions like Chungcheong have similarly fallen prey to political collusion, where candidates dogged by ethical controversies or clear lack of experience are routinely cleared as "qualified" simply because of political alignment. When confirmation processes become performative rituals rather than genuine tests of merit, power quickly rots from within.

The Lee administration's instinctual reaction to criticism—rumors of legal action or swift pushback against internal detractors—only deepens the democratic deficit. A government that tries to legally muzzle its own critics is signaling a profound insecurity, mistaking loyalty for competence.

What to Watch Next

As this public feud plays out, several key developments will determine whether South Korea's progressive camp can course-correct or whether it will fracture further:

    The Confirmation Fate of Kim Ji-yong: Watch whether the National Assembly pushes for a rigorous evaluation of the Serious Crime Investigation Agency nominee or if the ruling party forces the appointment through regardless of internal pushback. The Tone of Progressive Media: Observe whether mainstream pro-government commentators double down on painting critics like Choo and Rhyu as splitters, or if they begin to grapple with the underlying substance of their warnings. Grassroots Reactions: Pay attention to public polling and grassroots sentiment among liberal voters, who must decide whether they prioritize absolute party unity ahead of upcoming local elections or demand a return to genuine reformist principles.

Ultimately, the bitter arguments voiced by Choo Mi-ae and Rhyu Si-min are not born of a desire to see the progressive government fail, but of a terror that it is succeeding in the wrong ways. For an administration built on the promise of systemic change, ignoring these internal alarms risks turning the promise of reform into a cautionary tale.
