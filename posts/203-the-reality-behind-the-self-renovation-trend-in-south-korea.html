The Reality Behind the "Self-Renovation" Trend in South Korea

September 06, 2026

In an era of relentless inflation in South Korea, the dream of owning a home feels increasingly distant—and the prospect of renovating one can be just as daunting. For a typical 34-pyeong (approximately 1,200 square feet) apartment, getting a quote from a professional interior firm often leads homeowners to retreat in shock at the astronomical price tags. However, a viral trend has recently taken social media by storm: stories of homeowners completely transforming their dated apartments for as little as 13 million won (about $9,800).

At first glance, it sounds too good to be true. But a closer look reveals that this isn't just luck; it is a calculated effort to strip away the "market premium" of the interior design industry by having the homeowner take the helm as the project manager. But what does it really take to pull this off, and what are the hidden risks of taking the DIY route in a cutthroat construction market?

The Sweet Temptation of "Half-Price" Renovations

Hiring a "turnkey" interior firm is undoubtedly convenient—they handle the planning, the sourcing, and the headaches. But that convenience comes with a heavy price. Management fees, "reserve funds," and middleman margins often inflate the budget by over 30%. The strategy behind these viral low-cost renovations is simple: the homeowner acts as the general contractor. You hire individual specialists for carpentry, tiling, and film finishes yourself.

By cutting out the middleman, costs drop dramatically. However, there is a trap: these technicians are only responsible for their specific craft. They will not worry about the overall harmony of your home or the finer finishing details. If a space is left between a wall and a door frame, or if the transition between materials is sloppy, that is entirely your problem to solve. You are the site supervisor, and the technicians are merely your temporary employees.

The High-Stakes World of Project Management

Anyone who has tried this knows the site can turn into a battlefield. If the carpenter finishes the job and the film technician arrives only to declare, "The base work is poor, so I can't work on this," the entire project collapses into chaos. This is why experienced renovators warn that you must schedule "buffer days" between trades. One day of delay triggers a chain reaction that can spiral into a "cost bomb" of extra labor charges. If your scheduling fails, you may end up paying more than you would have with a turnkey firm.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • To save the 30–40% markup, you must act as your own site supervisor.
  • Strategic use of interior film can radically change the look of an older apartment.
  • Variables are inevitable; leaving gaps in the project schedule is not an option, but a necessity.

The Legal Quagmire: When DIY Goes Wrong

As the popularity of "self-renovations" grows, so do the disputes. Claims filed through service-matching platforms—similar to TaskRabbit or Thumbtack—have nearly tripled in South Korea over the past two years. When you bypass a professional firm, there is often no one to hold accountable when things go wrong.

If a piece of wallpaper peels, you might get lucky. But if the structural foundation of a partition wall is faulty, or if poor waterproofing leads to leaks in the unit below, the financial and legal liability falls entirely on the homeowner. In court, "I don't know who did it" is not a defense. If you haven't meticulously documented every step, taken photos of the raw materials, and kept the contact details of every worker, you will find yourself paying for repairs twice over—or worse, facing a lawsuit. Saving money upfront is not a bargain if it leads to years of litigation and stress.

The Hidden Costs of Regulation

Even if you are organized, you must contend with the "Apartment Management Regulations." Even if local laws allow construction between 8:00 AM and 6:00 PM, most apartment complexes enforce their own stricter rules—often limiting work to 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM, with a total ban on weekends. If a noise complaint shuts your site down, you still have to pay the daily wages of the workers waiting on-site. These are the "risk management" costs that many first-timers fail to factor into their spreadsheets.

Renovation as a Philosophy of Living

Beyond the spreadsheets, there is a design philosophy to consider. Many viral renovations favor a minimalist, all-white aesthetic. While it looks like a hotel in photos, white interiors are notoriously difficult to maintain. Scuffs, children’s drawings, and furniture dents show up instantly. A home should be designed for how you actually live, not just for how it looks on Instagram.

For many in their 30s, a home is both an asset and a sanctuary. Before you chase the "13-million-won miracle," ask yourself why you are doing this. If your goal is just to save money, be aware that you are trading your time, energy, and peace of mind for those savings. True professional skill deserves to be paid for. If you still choose to go the DIY route, treat it as a project of planning your life, not just a series of construction tasks. Document everything, expect the worst, and build a buffer for the inevitable surprises.

What to watch next:

  • Regulation changes: Keep an eye on local government guidelines regarding noise and waste disposal, as complexes are increasingly tightening rules to combat renovation-related friction.
  • Platform liability: Watch for new legislation aimed at regulating freelance worker platforms, which may eventually provide more protection for consumers—though this will likely come at the cost of higher platform fees.
