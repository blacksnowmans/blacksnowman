Tax Cuts, Stock Slumps, and Party Infighting: Inside South Korea's Ruling Party Crisis

November 11, 2026

For anyone watching South Korean politics, few pressure points are as raw as the intersection of property ownership and the stock market. In a country where an apartment is often the ultimate measure of middle-class security, and where retail investors—affectionately or ironically known as "ants"—make up a massive vocal block of the electorate, economic policy is high-stakes theater. On November 10, that theater played out in full public view during a plenary meeting of the Democratic Party, South Korea's ruling liberal party.

What was meant to be a routine strategy session quickly devolved into an open-air brawl over two of the most sensitive issues in Korean society: property taxes and capital market reform. As lawmakers from the capital region pushed to soften taxes on expensive homes to appease disgruntled urban voters, progressive watchdogs and left-wing factions cried foul, accusing the party of abandoning its core values for short-term electoral gains. Compounding the chaos, leadership scrambled to roll out emergency measures to stop a bleeding stock market, only to run headfirst into internal disagreements and opposition from the conservative opposition party, the People Power Party.

The Property Tax Revolt and the Ghost of "Tax Cuts for the Rich"

The immediate catalyst for the friction was a push by a faction of Democratic Party lawmakers representing the capital region. Fearing a severe backlash from Seoul voters—where skyrocketing real estate prices have long been a political graveyard for ruling parties—these lawmakers demanded a major pivot in property taxation. Specifically, they called for a blanket tax deduction threshold of 1.4 billion won (about $1 million) for single homeowners, even if they do not physically reside in the property.

The logic was purely electoral: with crucial local sentiments turning cold, politicians argued they could not afford to swim against the current. Whispers even circulated about extending tax relief to apartments valued at up to 2 billion won (about $1.4 million). But while the maneuver was framed as a pragmatic response to public mood, it triggered an immediate and ferocious internal backlash. Critics within the Democratic Party and across the broader progressive wing blasted the proposal as a betrayal of tax justice, labeling it a "tax cut for the rich" that would benefit a tiny sliver of wealthy property owners.

Civil society groups and minor progressive parties joined the chorus, demanding that the party drop the capital-pleasing proposals immediately. The fallout exposed deep ideological fractures within the ruling camp. While some party heavyweights attempted to downplay the confusion, the damage to the party’s policy consistency was already done. Meanwhile, the administration and the presidential office drew a sharp line, insisting on the preservation of policies that favor actual residents over absentee owners. With the government and the ruling party singing from different hymnals, the administration's policy credibility took a severe and visible hit.

Calming the Retail "Ants" and the Stalled Stock Market Push

While the property debate raged internally, the ruling party was fighting a parallel fire on the trading floor. Faced with a sharp and painful slide in the local stock market, Democratic Party leadership scrambled to soothe furious retail investors. Lawmakers emphasized an all-out push to stabilize the capital market, centering discussions on overcoming the "Korea discount"—the persistent undervaluation of Korean equities—and pushing through the newly minted "Anti-Stock Suppression Act" before the end of the regular parliamentary session.

Yet, the execution has proven messy. During the assembly meeting, proposals to overhaul mandatory public tender offer rules ran into stiff internal resistance. Cautious voices warned that rushing legislation without deep consultations with the conservative opposition would lead to legislative gridlock, forcing the party to table the measures without a concrete conclusion. What was meant to be a swift reassurance to anxious retail investors instead morphed into a glaring display of rushed policymaking and internal hesitation.

The political fallout quickly weaponized. The People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, slammed the ruling camp's legislative push as nothing more than fear-mongering and political theater designed to exploit public anxiety. The Democrats fired back, accusing the opposition of turning the tears of everyday retail investors into a partisan weapon. As both sides traded blows over who was to blame for the market slump, the concrete, structural reforms desperately needed by the market were once again pushed to the back burner.

Caught Between Electoral Survival and Policy Coherence

The chaos of November 10 highlights the fundamental dilemma facing the Democratic Party: how to balance the pragmatic flexibility required to win elections with the uncompromising principles of progressive tax justice. Moves designed to capture urban votes ended up alienating the party's traditional base, creating a political boomerang that hit leadership from both sides.

When policy pivots happen overnight in response to short-term polling fears, the public stops believing in the long-term vision. The frantic rush to pass market-stabilization bills without adequate internal consensus suggests a party reacting to crises rather than managing them. If the ruling camp hopes to recover its footing, it will need to stop treating economic policy as a series of panic-driven reactions and instead present a steady, unified roadmap.

What to Watch Next

  • Late November Policy Coordination: Keep a close eye on the official outcomes between the ruling party and the government regarding property tax adjustments. Any final compromise will signal whether the administration managed to rein in its rebellious lawmakers or surrendered to pressure from the capital region.
  • The Fate of Capital Market Legislation: Watch for whether the Democratic Party can actually forge a bipartisan consensus on the Anti-Stock Suppression Act and related investor-protection bills before the regular session ends, or if they will quietly stall out.
  • Public Sentiment Tracking: Monitor upcoming approval ratings and local polls in Seoul to see if these mixed signals successfully halted the slide in public trust, or if voters punished the ruling party for appearing disorganized.
