Why South Korea's Ruling Democratic Party Is Free-Falling in the Polls

September 07, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is flashing urgent warning lights for the country's progressives. According to a September 2026 poll by Realmeter, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has plunged to 37.4%. What makes this drop particularly painful for the administration is that the traditional "decoupling" effect—where a president's popularity diverges from that of their political party—has completely vanished. The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal party, is sinking right alongside the president. There was no honeymoon period; the public mood has turned ice cold almost overnight, leaving observers asking how the ruling party managed to alienate voters so quickly.

The Anatomy of a Poll Collapse: Dead Crosses and Alienated Youth

Recent polling numbers present a grim reality for the DPK. Realmeter's telephone interview surveys show the downward trajectory sliding to 47.1% with no floor in sight, while automated response system (ARS) surveys have the party stuck in the low 40s. More alarming than the numbers themselves is where those disillusioned voters are going: they are quietly drifting over to the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative opposition. This shift isn't happening because the conservatives have suddenly put forth brilliant policies; rather, it is purely a reflex vote driven by the ruling party’s repeated missteps. With the gap between the two major parties narrowing to within 10 percentage points, the progressive base is visibly fracturing.

History shows that past administrations—under both conservative and progressive leaders—suffered from factional infighting, but the current crisis goes deeper. It stems from a profound inability to read the room. Past data suggests that when a government hits a "dead cross" (where disapproval outpaces approval), the solution has never been a heavy-handed legislative drive. Instead, it requires genuine policy adjustments and message discipline. Right now, however, the DPK is consumed by internal power struggles while everyday citizens struggle under severe economic pain.

This disconnect is painfully clear among younger voters, particularly men in their 20s and 30s. When the ruling party rolls out youth policies, they are met with sheer cynicism. Young Koreans, acutely sensitive to the structural inequalities in modern society, are not looking for cosmetic gestures. They reject the patronizing photo-ops favored by establishment politicians—such as staging roundtables to "look approachable" or feigning bipartisanship by sitting across the aisle from political rivals. To a generation facing high living costs and shrinking opportunities, these staged displays read as hollow theater.

Untimely Constitutional Debates While the Economy Struggles

While inflation bites, housing prices remain volatile, and small businesses fight to survive, senior DPK figures have chosen to champion constitutional reform. Proposing to add historical pro-democracy movements—like the May 18 Gwangju Democratization Movement and the Bu-Ma Democratic Struggle—into the preamble of the South Korean Constitution carries symbolic weight, but the timing borders on tone-deaf. When citizens are walking a financial tightrope, pushing for structural power overhauls feels like an ivory-tower luxury.

Unsurprisingly, these initiatives instantly trigger public suspicion. Many voters immediately ask if the ruling camp is simply looking for ways to salvage its political leverage or carve out new power-sharing arrangements under a parliamentary system. Introducing structural reforms during an economic downturn is like pouring gasoline on a fire; it signals an administration that has lost its political survival instinct—what Koreans call nunchi, the intuitive awareness of the public mood.

Compounding this tone-deafness is political wordplay. Proposals for a "creative horizontal relationship" between the party and the presidential office leave voters scratching their heads. Adding convoluted modifiers to basic administrative concepts does nothing to hide policy failures. True political autonomy does not come from semantic acrobatics; it comes from the party having the courage to offer candid, critical feedback to the executive branch. Right now, the DPK is failing to do just that.

Police Reform and the Illusion of Public Safety

As the administration turns its attention from prosecution reform to policing, the debate has become bogged down by political framing. South Korea boasts one of the safest crime environments in the world, underpinned by dedicated law enforcement professionals. Yet political rhetoric frequently treats the police force as an institutional target ripe for overhaul rather than an essential public service.

Reforms are undoubtedly needed, but expanding police authority without matching it with adequate budgets, personnel, and robust oversight mechanisms creates a dangerous imbalance. When a government pushes to strengthen investigative powers without addressing the working realities on the ground, it risks overwhelming frontline officers and alienating the workforce. Real reform requires structural stability and institutional protection, not rushed, politically motivated expansions of state power.

The Upcoming Seoul Mayoral By-Election: A Final Wake-Up Call

The political stakes are coming to a head with the upcoming Seoul mayoral by-election, widely seen as a crucial barometer for the wider metropolitan electorate and a rehearsal for future legislative elections. Yet, rather than uniting behind pressing urban issues, the DPK remains tangled in internal factionalism. Centrist and independent voters care little about factional loyalties; they want competent city administration and a laser-like focus on livelihood issues.

If the ruling party continues to ignore the warning signs written across every major poll, the path ahead leads toward irreversible defeat. The drop in public confidence is not a media conspiracy or a passing storm; it is the cumulative result of broken lines of communication between the political elite and the public. Whether the DPK can recover its footing depends entirely on its willingness to drop the political spin, listen to harsh truths, and anchor its agenda back in the realities of everyday life.

What to Watch Next: Keep a close eye on upcoming secondary polling adjustments over the next month, public reaction to party leadership reshuffles, and whether local lawmakers begin publicly breaking ranks with the presidential office on economic policy.
