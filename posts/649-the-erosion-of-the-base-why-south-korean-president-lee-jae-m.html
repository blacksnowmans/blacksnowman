The Erosion of the Base: Why South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Support is Plummeting

October 03, 2026

For those watching South Korean politics closely, the current climate is marked by a pervasive sense of frustration. A common refrain, "Where has the core support base gone?" has become the defining sentiment of the season. To understand why the voters who were the architects of President Lee Jae-myung’s election victory have grown so disillusioned, one must look past simple polling fluctuations. What is happening is not just a statistical decline, but a loud, clear alarm bell signaling that the very foundation of the administration is cracking.

Recent polling from the Kyunghyang Shinmun—a major South Korean daily known for its liberal-leaning perspective—shows that the modest bump President Lee saw following a recent press conference was short-lived. His support is once again trending downward. While support among voters for the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the primary liberal party and Lee’s political base, once hovered in the high 90s, there is now genuine concern that it may soon drop below the 80% mark.

The most painful indicator, however, lies in the relationship with the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, firebrand reformist party led by Cho Kuk. Before a recent controversy involving candidate nominations in Pyeongtaek, the Rebuilding Korea Party’s support for President Lee was a staggering 94%, essentially functioning as a political alliance. Today, that number has plummeted to near 70%, with support among voters in their 20s barely clinging to 20%. Even within the DPK, internal surveys conducted prior to the party convention revealed that nearly half of the supporters of senior politician Jung Chung-rae—a key Lee ally—expressed opposition to the president. As prominent writer and public intellectual Rhyu Si-min pointedly remarked, the president has "deceived the public regarding judicial reform," failing to clearly articulate a path to separating investigative and prosecutorial powers. This is not just a drift of the political center; this is the decoupling of the "linchpin" that holds the administration together.

The Essence of the Decline: A Crisis of Core Loyalty

For the past year, the administration has pointed to a list of over 200 policy "achievements," ranging from employment statistics to regulatory adjustments, as evidence of success. However, in an era of soaring inflation and a struggling economy, a government approach that effectively says, "Look at our list of accomplishments," fails to resonate with a base that is feeling the heat of daily economic hardship. When a president elected with a mandate for deep reform appears hesitant, or worse, seeks to appease the establishment, the sense of betrayal among supporters is profound and cannot be quantified by charts alone.

The contradiction is most visible in the administration’s handling of judicial reform. President Lee has defended his personnel appointments by arguing that excluding former prosecutors from key investigative roles merely because of their background would be counterproductive. To the administration, this is a pragmatic approach. To the base, it is a fundamental misunderstanding of the mission. The promise of judicial reform was centered on the complete separation of investigation and prosecution to dismantle the perceived "prosecutorial state." By maintaining the status quo and compromising with the very institutions the base expected him to reform, the president has effectively removed the linchpin of his own reform agenda.

The Trap of "Addition by Subtraction"

The current DPK leadership appears caught in a tactical delusion: the belief that they can expand their base by "cleaning house." There is an ongoing move to sideline those associated with the former administrations of presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in—the so-called pro-Roh and pro-Moon factions—in favor of bringing in anti-Yoon (the previous conservative president) figures or political outsiders. While this might look like "broadening the coalition" on paper, it is stripping the party of its ideological soul.

Real political expansion occurs naturally when a party remains true to its identity and pushes forward with clear, bold reforms, attracting the moderate middle in the process. By contrast, the current strategy of alienating the base to accommodate new, often unclear political figures is a form of "subtraction politics." History offers a clear counter-example: the 2020 parliamentary elections, where the Democratic Party secured a massive 180-seat majority. That victory was fueled by the energy of the 2019 "candlelight vigils" in Seocho-dong, where citizens poured into the streets specifically to protect reform efforts from being undermined by the conservative establishment. When the party fought for reforms, the base stood behind it as a shield. Today, that connection is fraying because the administration has abandoned the very crusading spirit that built its power.

The High-Stakes Risk of the Rebuilding Korea Party

Perhaps the most immediate threat to the administration is its fraying relationship with the Rebuilding Korea Party. The DPK leadership has spoken of "merger" in vague terms while simultaneously acting in ways that alienate Cho Kuk’s party. This is a dangerous game of arrogance. Should the Rebuilding Korea Party decide to run independent candidates across the metropolitan regions in the upcoming general election, the result would be a split in the liberal vote, effectively handing victories to the conservative People Power Party (PPP).

The DPK’s internal factions seem more concerned with maintaining their control over nominations than with the broader electoral math. They appear to have forgotten that for the voters, the alliance between the DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party is not just a matter of convenience—it is a matter of survival. If the DPK fails to present a sincere roadmap for cooperation, they risk not the glory of 2020, but a crushing, systemic defeat.

What to Watch Next

The president’s frequent, emotionally charged social media outbursts, often targeting his own supporters, suggest a leader who is increasingly isolated and defensive. The difficulty in filling top-level positions, such as Chief of Staff, and lingering questions about the influence of specific regional cliques within the presidential office, have further deepened public distrust.

Moving forward, observe these two critical indicators:

  • Personnel Reshuffles: Will the president bring in voices that represent the core reformist agenda, or will he continue to rely on a insular circle that has failed to connect with the public?
  • The "Merger" Negotiations: Watch for concrete, public-facing agreements between the DPK and the Rebuilding Korea Party. If these talks remain stalled or hostile, the risk of a vote-split in the metropolitan areas becomes a near-certainty.

President Lee Jae-myung, who built his career as a highly competent, results-oriented "administrator," now finds himself in a position where administration is not enough. He must transition into a "politician"—a leader who can stitch together a divided camp, embrace dissenting allies, and navigate the complex, often messy, waters of genuine institutional reform. The core base has not yet closed the door entirely, but they have crossed their arms, waiting to see if the president has the capacity for deep self-reflection. If he continues to view his supporters as obstacles rather than partners, that door may close for good.
