The Tesla FSD Paradox: Why Thousands of Korean Drivers Paid for Tech They Can't Use

October 09, 2026

The scene is familiar to many modern commuters: driving home through the dark, a Tesla owner taps their dashboard screen, expecting the vehicle to handle the navigation. They have been waiting three years for this moment—ever since they paid an upfront premium of about $7,000 (9.04 million KRW) for Full Self-Driving (FSD) capability. Yet, the screen offers only a cold, static message: "Function Preparing."

This frustration, shared by tens of thousands of Korean Tesla owners, finally reached the halls of the National Assembly on October 8. During a parliamentary audit of the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT), the agency responsible for South Korea’s transportation policy, the reality behind Tesla’s "FSD" marketing was laid bare. It is a story of regulatory failure, blatant consumer discrimination, and a government seemingly paralyzed by its own lack of standards.

The Illusion of Innovation: 180,000 "Dead" Options

During the audit, Representative Jang Jong-tae of the Democratic Party of Korea (the ruling party, currently led by President Lee Jae-myung) projected a video showing a self-driving car skillfully navigating city streets and avoiding obstacles. He then called forward Seo Young-deuk, the CEO of Tesla Korea, for a direct interrogation.

The numbers presented were staggering. While Tesla has sold roughly 230,000 vehicles in Korea, only about 45,000 of them are actually capable of using FSD features, even when accounting for older hardware versions. According to data from the CarIsYou Data Research Institute, this means nearly 80% of Tesla owners in the country are driving cars that cannot utilize the very feature they paid for.

The situation is particularly dire for the Model Y, the most popular Tesla in Korea, accounting for 70% of the brand's local fleet. Out of 160,450 Model Ys on the road, a mere 9.6% can actually utilize FSD. For the other 145,000-plus owners, that $7,000 premium has essentially bought them a "picture on the wall"—a feature that is technically there but functionally non-existent. Frustrated by years of silence from Tesla Korea, many owners are now turning to collective legal action, demanding their rights as consumers.

Regulatory Arbitrage and the "Made-in-China" Barrier

The most infuriating aspect of this saga is that the ability to use FSD depends on where the car was manufactured. When pressed by Rep. Jang on why identical models have different FSD capabilities, CEO Seo admitted, "In Korea, regulations are applied differently depending on the country of origin."

Thanks to safety standards recognized under the Korea-U.S. Free Trade Agreement (FTA), vehicles manufactured in the United States can legally operate with certain FSD capabilities. However, vehicles imported from China—which now account for 99% of Tesla's imports to Korea—do not have the same regulatory clearance. Tesla has continued to collect high-priced FSD fees from customers buying Chinese-made models, despite having no clear roadmap for when (or if) those cars will ever receive authorization.

This regulatory gap has pushed some desperate owners into dangerous territory. Online communities have seen the rise of "illegal hacks," where owners attempt to install unauthorized hardware chips to force-enable North American FSD code. This is not just a violation of warranty; it is a crime under the Automobile Management Act, punishable by up to two years in prison or fines of up to $15,000 (20 million KRW). The government’s failure to provide a legal framework has effectively driven law-abiding citizens to become criminals.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport’s "Empty" Oversight

Perhaps the most damning revelation of the audit was the admission that the government has essentially been asleep at the wheel. When asked about safety records for FSD in Korea, Tesla Korea’s CEO claimed there had been no "significant" accidents despite over 100 million kilometers of driving. Rep. Jang immediately countered: "The car has traveled over 90,000 km [using FSD], and the Ministry has never conducted a single safety verification test. Did you refuse to cooperate?"

The answer, it seems, is that neither side bothered to try. Tesla Korea admitted they had never formally requested a test from the Ministry, and the Ministry, hiding behind the excuse of a lack of "international standards," has never initiated a safety audit. For years, tens of thousands of kilometers of self-driving data have been logged on Korean roads in a regulatory wild west.

While the rest of the world moves forward—with the U.S. commercializing driverless taxis and China testing Tesla’s FSD—South Korea is lagging behind. The Ministry claims it is preparing to adopt international safety standards (DCAS), but it currently lacks the specific technical criteria or a dedicated task force to evaluate FSD. This creates a terrifying prospect: if the government remains this sluggish, even South Korea’s own domestic manufacturers, like Hyundai, may find themselves unable to launch their own autonomous vehicles by 2028 because there is no regulatory "vessel" to contain them.

What to Watch Next

The parliamentary audit has forced the issue into the light, but the path forward remains obstructed by bureaucratic inertia. Here is what to monitor in the coming months:

  • Legislative Pressure: Look for the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport to finally be pressured into creating a specialized "regulatory sandbox" or domestic standard for FSD, rather than waiting indefinitely for international consensus.
  • Safety Mandates: Following the tragic fire incident in Anseong, where a trapped driver could not open the doors of a Tesla during a power failure, expect bipartisan pressure from lawmakers like Rep. Mo Gyeong-jong to mandate manual emergency release mechanisms for all electric vehicles in Korea.
  • Consumer Litigation: The class-action movement by Tesla owners is likely to gain momentum. If Tesla continues to collect fees for services it cannot legally provide in the Korean market, they may face significant court-ordered restitution.

The era of autonomous mobility is not coming; it is already here. But as this audit demonstrated, the government’s tendency to hide behind the excuse of "no existing standards" is an admission of incompetence. If the state continues to prioritize bureaucratic convenience over consumer safety and technical progress, Korea risks becoming a laggard in the global race, trapped in a maze of its own regulatory making.
