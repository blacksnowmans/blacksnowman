Inside South Korea's Presidential Office: Vacancies, Budgets, and the Gyeonggi-Seongnam Faction

October 28, 2026

Walk into South Korea's presidential headquarters 17 months into the current administration, and you will find an unusually quiet, half-empty office. While political observers in Seoul usually focus on high-profile parliamentary clashes, a quieter structural crisis has been unfolding inside the executive branch. Nearly 20 percent of the positions in the presidential secretariat remain vacant. Mid-level administrative officers—the cogs that keep the policy machine turning—are missing in more than a quarter of their posts. Yet, paradoxically, the presidential office's labor budget has ballooned by over 20 percent.

To outside observers, this looks like a severe bureaucratic recruitment failure. But inside Korean political circles, it is understood as something far more deliberate: a symptom of hyper-concentrated power, intense factional gatekeeping, and a widening crack within South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party, the liberal party currently holding the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung.

A Hollowed-Out Secretariat and the Resignation of Chief Kang Hoon-sik

The numbers coming out of the presidential secretariat paint a picture of an organization running on fumes. Out of 445 authorized positions, only 357 personnel are actually at work, with 145 officials having resigned since the administration took office. The strain is most acute among lower-to-mid-level working-level staff (Grades 2 through 5), where the vacancy rate sits at a staggering 27.5 percent. More than one in four desks meant to draft policies and coordinate ministries sit empty.

Ordinarily, senior presidential secretaries would step in to fill these ranks, recruit talent, and assert operational control. Instead, those senior officials find themselves largely powerless. According to ruling party insiders, even senior secretaries struggle to bring in candidates of their own choosing—a dysfunctional dynamic unprecedented in recent South Korean political history. Critical policy minds are being shut out, not because talent is scarce, but because access has been strictly cordoned off.

This boiling tension reached a head in September when Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik abruptly offered his resignation. Coming just days after the disastrous back-to-back collapses of cabinet nominees—and hot on the heels of a public apology from President Lee—Kang’s departure signaled the total breakdown of the administration's official personnel vetting line. Announced just an hour before the president embarked on an overseas trip to the US and Mexico, the resignation was a calculated political move to prevent a prolonged public brawl while the president was out of the country. With the chief of staff gone, the administration's core leadership triad has been effectively paralyzed.

The Rise of the 'Gyeonggi-Seongnam Line' and Kim Hyun-ji

Behind the personnel logjam and the paralysis of the official vetting apparatus lies a single, frequently cited name: Kim Hyun-ji, the Chief of the 1st Presidential Private Secretariat. Having transitioned from general affairs secretary to her current role, Kim is widely recognized as one of President Lee Jae-myung’s closest confidants, with a relationship stretching back nearly three decades.

Kim’s political roots date back to February 1998, when she joined a civic group in Seongnam—a satellite city south of Seoul—as a junior activist working alongside Lee, then a local human rights lawyer. Over the next twenty years, as Lee rose through the ranks from Seongnam mayor to Gyeonggi Province governor and finally to the presidency, Kim remained a core fixture of his inner circle. Today, within ruling party circles, she is widely viewed as the ultimate power broker inside the presidential office.

According to multiple testimonies from Democratic Party aides and former staffers, a tight-knit faction known as the "Gyeonggi-Seongnam Line"—centered around Kim—has completely seized control of presidential appointments. Even recommendations from sitting lawmakers frequently hit a brick wall if they lack the blessing of this inner circle. The official personnel recruitment system, nominally led by the senior secretary for personnel, has been effectively sidelined in favor of an informal gatekeeping network that prioritizes personal loyalty and historical ties over institutional competence.

The Budget Paradox: Empty Desks and Inflated Costs

This closed-door personnel management has created a bizarre, counterintuitive reality. While the secretariat suffers from a 20 percent vacancy rate and lower-level offices cry out for more hands, the presidential office's personnel budget for the upcoming year has surged by 21.6%, rising from about $32 million to nearly $40 million (448 billion won to 545 billion won). The overall presidential budget similarly expanded by over 20% to reach roughly $92 million (1.265 trillion won).

How does an administration spend significantly more money while filling fewer jobs? Critics argue it is the direct byproduct of an internal bottleneck. Because the inner circle screens applicants through an intensely narrow, loyalist filter, they fail to hire working-level staff, yet the administrative apparatus swells around them to sustain the faction's institutional footprint. What was meant to be a lean, reformist executive office has increasingly drawn accusations of functioning as a vehicle for resource hoarding and internal job preservation.

This imbalance turned the chief of staff into little more than a figurehead. Kang Hoon-sik reportedly attempted to reclaim some administrative authority from the Gyeonggi-Seongnam faction, only to be repeatedly rebuffed. When specific cabinet nominations were pushed through despite strong warnings from the official vetting line—only to crash during confirmation hearings—it laid bare the reality of a dual power structure: an official administration bound by law and accountability, and an informal core wielding actual control.

What to Watch Next

The consequences of this insular governance model extend far beyond internal staffing quarrels. Public trust in the administration's core economic pledges—particularly housing and real estate policies, a perennial make-or-break issue for South Korean voters—has eroded as a string of controversies involving officials and nominees has contradicted the government's anti-speculation rhetoric. With approval ratings hovering in the low 30s, the administration faces mounting pressure from both the conservative opposition (the People Power Party) and dissenting voices within its own liberal bloc.

To understand where South Korean politics is heading next, watch for these three concrete developments:

  • The Next Chief of Staff Appointment: See whether the replacement for Kang Hoon-sik is an independent political heavyweight capable of asserting institutional authority over personnel, or another loyalist from the inner circle. That choice will signal whether President Lee intends to double down on factional control or open up the administration.
  • Local Election Maneuvering: As upcoming local elections approach, monitor how candidate nominations are handled for key provincial and municipal posts. A continued monopoly by the Gyeonggi-Seongnam faction will likely trigger deeper fractures within the Democratic Party.
  • Real Estate and Vetting Policy Shifts: Look for any tangible institutional changes to how presidential and ministerial candidates are vetted. Unless the back-channel screening process is replaced by a transparent, merit-based system, similar scandals are all but guaranteed to recur.
