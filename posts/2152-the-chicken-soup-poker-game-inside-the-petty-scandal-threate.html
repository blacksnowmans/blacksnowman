The Chicken Soup Poker Game: Inside the Petty Scandal Threatening South Korea’s Judicial Reform

November 09, 2026

High in the wooded hills of Gogi-ri, a rustic valley in Yongin just south of Seoul, city dwellers routinely escape the capital to sit around low wooden tables and share steaming pots of baeksuk—whole chicken slow-boiled with medicinal roots and garlic. It is an unassuming setting, known far more for lazy weekend lunches and family outings than high-stakes political intrigue.

Yet according to an explosive front-page story in The Chosun Ilbo, South Korea’s most prominent conservative daily newspaper, this scenic valley was once the backdrop for illegal backroom gambling involving the nation’s prospective top law enforcement official. The newspaper reported claims that Lee Yong-cheol, President Lee Jae-myung’s nominee for Minister of Justice, participated in high-stakes poker games at a local chicken soup restaurant over a decade ago. The source of the claim is Yoo Dong-gyu, the embattled former planning director of the Seongnam Development Corporation and a central figure in South Korea’s long-running local development scandals.

For an international observer, the spectacle of a cabinet appointment hanging on an unverified poker game from the distant past might seem bizarre. In Seoul’s hyper-polarized political arena, however, this sudden revelation represents something far heavier: a calibrated opening salvo by conservative forces and entrenched judicial interests aiming to derail the incoming administration’s ambitious overhaul of the state prosecution service.

A 15-Year-Old Memory with Suspiciously Crisp Details

The controversy broke when The Chosun Ilbo published what it framed as an exclusive interview with Yoo Dong-gyu. Yoo alleged that he and Lee Yong-cheol had played poker together for cash on at least three distinct occasions during the early 2010s at the Gogi-ri chicken restaurant.

What caught public attention was the uncanny precision of Yoo’s recollection. He did not simply claim they played cards; he recited the game’s ledger as if reviewing yesterday’s financial statements. Yoo alleged that the accumulated pot reached around 2 million won (roughly $1,500), recalling that he initially lost 400,000 won (about $300), fell further behind by losing 800,000 won (about $600), and then staged a comeback to recoup his losses. He recalled that the game took place on a weekday, that broad daylight greeted them when they walked outside, and even claimed Lee had arrived accompanied by a driver.

Lee Yong-cheol issued an immediate, categorical denial. Calling the allegations outright malicious slander, the nominee maintained that he has never engaged in high-stakes gambling in his life. Lee’s camp posed a straightforward question to the public: Does it defy basic human experience that someone could accurately recall the exact amounts lost and recovered during an ordinary card game played over a decade ago?

Under ordinary circumstances, human memory blurs with time. Details fade into general impressions. Yet Yoo’s recollection emerged readymade with cinematic clarity and precise accounting figures at the exact moment Lee’s confirmation hearing approached. Yoo insisted his memory remained sharp because he rarely gambles, but critics see a rehearsed script designed to plant a fatal ethical stain on a reformist official.

The strategic choice of the number "three" is equally telling. In Korean legal and moral discourse, a single instance can be brushed aside as an ill-considered social misstep; three documented occurrences, however, establish a pattern of habitual behavior. By framing the allegation as a recurring habit with a specific dollar amount, the accuser provides conservative media and opposition lawmakers with pre-packaged ammunition for a public character assassination.

Who Is Lee Yong-cheol, and Why Does the Establishment Fear Him?

To understand why a decade-old game of cards warrants banner headlines, one must look at the nominee’s career and the factional fault lines running through South Korea’s legal establishment. Lee Yong-cheol is not merely a technocratic lawyer; he is a symbol of a decades-long struggle between progressive reformers and the country’s elite prosecutor corps.

Lee cut his teeth as a human rights lawyer affiliated with Minbyun (Lawyers for a Democratic Society), the progressive legal association that served as an incubator for South Korea’s democratization movement. During the administration of the late progressive President Roh Moo-hyun in the mid-2000s, Lee served inside the Blue House as a legal affairs secretary and civil affairs secretary. Roh’s presidency famously clashed with the state prosecution service over attempts to curtail its sweeping, monopolistic powers over investigations and indictments—a legacy that ended tragically when Roh took his own life amid a relentless prosecutorial probe after leaving office.

Lee’s ties to President Lee Jae-myung run equally deep:

  • He worked alongside the president at the progressive law firm Segil early in their careers.
  • He served on the transition committee when Lee Jae-myung was first elected mayor of Seongnam in 2010, laying the groundwork for municipal reforms.
  • He later directed the Seongnam Industry Promotion Agency and headed the national Defense Acquisition Program Administration (DAPA), proving his administrative bona fides.
  • Critically, Lee served as a defense attorney in the Daejang-dong development case, granting him an intimate, granular understanding of how prosecutors built their cases against progressive leaders.

For South Korea's conservative media establishment and the judicial elite, Lee’s nomination as Minister of Justice is an existential threat. The Lee Jae-myung administration took power with an explicit mandate to "normalize" the prosecution, overhaul internal judicial hierarchies, and review politically motivated indictments pursued during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned.

Having a seasoned insider who understands the machinery from both the Blue House and the defense table means the administration has chosen an architect capable of dismantling entrenched prosecutorial privileges. Unable to challenge Lee’s professional credentials or civil service record, critics have instead weaponized personal morality.

Sensationalism as Political Sabotage

The handling of the story by The Chosun Ilbo illustrates a recurring pattern in Seoul’s confirmation politics. Rather than cross-referencing Yoo’s allegations with corroborating evidence—such as interviewing restaurant staff, locating eyewitnesses, or substantiating the paper trail—the daily published the accuser's word as an unchallenged exclusive headline.

Political commentators quickly noted the disproportion between the office and the allegation. On popular public affairs programs, commentators openly mocked the coverage as "truly petty news" (jjachineun news), expressing disbelief that the primary test for the head of the nation's legal apparatus had been reduced to an unproven lunch-hour card game. As one commentator remarked:

"When the leading daily newspaper in the country leads its cabinet vetting coverage with a chicken soup restaurant poker rumor from fifteen years ago, it reveals more about the desperation of the press than the nominee’s fitness for office."

The credibility of the accuser makes the editorial choice even more suspect. Yoo Dong-gyu is currently entangled in numerous high-profile criminal trials and has repeatedly shifted his testimony over the past two years to align with prosecution-friendly narratives. Relying entirely on the unsworn word of a defendant facing heavy legal jeopardy without secondary confirmation abandons basic journalistic due diligence. The goal of such reporting is not fact-finding; it is setting an agenda of moral ambivalence so the public begins to associate the nominee’s name with "gambling" and "police suspicion."

The Post-Impeachment Backlash and the Conservative Opposition

This controversy does not exist in a vacuum; it is the opening skirmish in a broader institutional war. Following the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, South Korea’s political landscape fractured. The conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), found itself relegated to the minority, nursing bitter resentment over the fall of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s administration.

Lacking the legislative votes to block President Lee Jae-myung’s policy agenda outright, the People Power Party has leaned heavily on traditional confirmation-hearing warfare. Following the Chosun Ilbo report, PPP leadership immediately weaponized the gambling allegations, calling for Lee Yong-cheol to withdraw his nomination and threatening to boycott the confirmation hearings altogether.

The irony of this stance is lost on few observers. Having vigorously defended an administration whose leader now sits in a prison cell following a constitutional court trial, conservative lawmakers are now arguing that a dubious decade-old poker rumor represents an insurmountable ethical barrier to holding public office.

For the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, backing down is not an option. Yielding to an unverified story from a compromised witness would establish a ruinous precedent, signaling to conservative newsrooms that any progressive nominee can be eliminated simply by running an uncorroborated front-page leak. If the administration retreats on Lee Yong-cheol, its broader campaign to dismantle entrenched prosecutorial privileges and clear politically motivated charges from Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure could stall before it even begins.

What to Watch Next

As the National Assembly prepares for confirmation proceedings, the trajectory of this dispute will offer a clear forecast of the administration's broader battles over the state apparatus:

  • The Confirmation Hearing Showdown: Watch how the People Power Party handles the hearing. If the opposition refuses to question Lee on policy and instead obsesses over the Gogi-ri restaurant claims, it will demonstrate that their strategy relies solely on ethical delegitimization rather than substance.
  • The President’s Resolve: Under South Korean law, parliamentary approval is not strictly mandatory for cabinet-level ministers if the National Assembly fails to adopt a confirmation report within the statutory deadline. Watch whether President Lee Jae-myung moves forward with the official appointment regardless of opposition protests.
  • The Push for Judicial Overhaul: Once appointed, Lee Yong-cheol's immediate moves will reveal the scope of prosecutorial reform. Key indicators include high-level personnel rotations within the Supreme Prosecutors' Office and the initiation of formal procedures to review disputed past indictments.

The fight over a mythical 2 million won poker game is, in the end, not about cards at all. It is a battle over who controls the coercive levers of the South Korean state—and how far the old guard is willing to reach into the past to prevent the future from taking office.
