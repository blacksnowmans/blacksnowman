Why Hundreds of Thousands of South Koreans Are Walking the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Step off the train in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, the traditional starting point for the French route across northern Spain, and the scale of what lies ahead begins to press in. More than 800 kilometers of dirt tracks, stone paths, and grueling mountain passes stretch out toward the Atlantic coast. For anyone who has stood at that border town, the memory is rarely just about travel; it is about the physical toll, the quiet hours of introspection, and the strange, magnetic pull that draws hundreds of thousands of people to walk across a foreign country on foot.

Before global tourism ground to a temporary halt, Spanish government figures showed roughly 350,000 pilgrims completing the trek in a single year, including more than 8,000 from South Korea alone. What was once a medieval Catholic religious observance has morphed into a massive, secular spiritual retreat for modern people living hyper-accelerated lives. To understand why South Koreans, in particular, have fallen so deeply in love with the Way of St. James, we have to look past the picturesque scenery and examine the intense social pressures driving them toward the Spanish countryside.

The Legend of the First Martyr and the Starry Fields

The origins of the pilgrimage trace back to St. James the Great, one of Jesus's twelve apostles. According to tradition, James traveled to the Iberian Peninsula—which ancient Romans and Mediterranean peoples considered the literal "end of the earth"—to preach. Following his execution by beheading in Jerusalem in 44 AD, legend holds that his remains were miraculously carried across the sea in a stone boat and laid to rest in northwestern Spain. Centuries later, in the year 813, a hermit named Pelayo reported seeing a shower of guiding stars pointing to a specific burial mound. That discovery gave the destination its poetic name: Santiago de Compostela, meaning "St. James of the field of stars."

Dismissing the story as mere medieval folklore misses the sheer weight of history. By the 1100s, ordinary Europeans were risking their lives to walk across a continent plagued by banditry, disease, and political instability just to reach the shrine. This wasn't a casual vacation; it was an act of profound desperation and faith. For modern pilgrims, that historical gravity matters. As travel writers and philosophers often point out, a historic holy site offers a chance at personal transformation, whereas a manufactured theme park offers only amusement.

The Church maintains ancient traditions around the route, such as declaring a "Holy Year" (or *Jacobean Year*) whenever July 25—the feast day of St. James—falls on a Sunday. When the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted celebrations, the Vatican even extended the Holy Year through a second 12-month cycle, proving that even rigid religious institutions must bend before global crises. Yet for the secular majority of walkers, the theology is secondary. The route provides a framework to strip away the clutter of daily life, trading the crushing anxieties of modern existence for the simple, repetitive rhythm of left foot, right foot.

Why South Koreans Have Made the French Route a National Phenomenon

Among international visitors, South Korean pilgrims display a striking uniformity in their choices: roughly 99% of them opt for the Camino Francés, the classic French Route. While global pilgrims distribute themselves across various paths, South Koreans heavily concentrate on this main artery, which accounts for more than half of all overall traffic on the trail.

This concentration reveals something fascinating about contemporary South Korean society. The country's demographic profile on the trail spans two distinct groups: exhausted young people facing a grueling job market and intense social competition, and retirees from the generation that built the nation's economic miracle, searching for the meaning of their next chapter. Both groups face immense societal pressure to perform, succeed, and conform.

Stepping onto the French Route offers both isolation and community. The infrastructure along the path—villages with affordable pilgrim hostels (*albergues*), clear waymarking, and well-trodden paths—provides a safety net. For travelers coming from a high-stress society where individualism often feels isolating, the Camino offers an unexpected paradox. Although travelers set out alone, they quickly find themselves sharing meals, trading blisters, and swapping life stories with fellow walkers. Koreans traveling thousands of miles from home often find deep comfort in these communal bonds, walking solo by day while finding collective emotional support by evening.

From Tourism Statistics to the Aesthetics of Slow Living

Discussions around the Camino often touch on economic impact. Tourism remains a cornerstone of Spain's economy, and the pilgrimage generates billions of euros and millions of visitors precisely because it is an intentional, deeply branded cultural product. By contrast, critics in South Korea frequently point to their own tourism industry, which struggles with vast deficits in tourism spending despite waves of Hallyu (Korean Wave) pop-culture fans visiting Seoul. Domestic critics argue that while Korea excels at drawing short-term crowds, it often fails to build enduring, contemplative travel products that encourage visitors to slow down and spend deeply.

The French philosopher David Le Breton argued in his book *In Praise of Walking* that walking is an antidote to identity crises in the modern world. In an era dominated by high-speed transit and digital notifications, walking restores basic human sensory engagement. On the trail, the only metrics that matter are the texture of the dirt path, the smell of eucalyptus trees in the morning mist, and the sound of one's own breathing.

Statistical breakdowns of the trail show that while over 94% of travelers complete the journey on foot, others use bicycles, wheelchairs, or even travel with pack animals. The presence of disabled pilgrims completing the journey serves as a powerful reminder: the goal of the path is not speed or athletic conquest, but endurance on one's own terms.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea continues to grapple with record-low birth rates, fierce corporate competition, and a rapidly aging population, interest in restorative, slow-travel experiences is unlikely to fade. Several key developments and trends warrant close attention going forward:

  • Domestic Trail Imitations: Watch how provincial governments in Korea—such as Jeonbuk State—attempt to package local historical and religious trails for international and domestic tourists. Success will depend on whether they can move beyond superficial administrative projects to create genuine cultural narratives.
  • Outbound Travel Demographics: Keep an eye on travel agency data regarding senior and solo travelers booking long-form walking tours abroad, which indicate shifting retirement priorities among South Korea's baby boomers.
  • The Shift Toward Mindful Consumption: Observe how younger generations in Korea spend discretionary income, increasingly favoring experiences, mental wellness retreats, and physical challenges over traditional luxury goods.

The thousands of footsteps raising dust on Spanish dirt roads each morning are more than just vacation memories. They are physical manifestations of a collective desire to hit pause. Whether you ever lace up hiking boots for a month-long trek or simply decide to take a long, uninterrupted walk through your local neighborhood park this weekend, the core lesson of the trail remains the same: sometimes you have to leave everything behind just to find out where you stand.
