Why South Korea's Fatigue with Politics Goes Deeper Than Bad News

October 02, 2026

There is a distinct kind of exhaustion that settles in when following South Korean current affairs lately. It is not just anger at a specific policy or disbelief over a single scandal; it is a creeping sense of powerlessness. Every day, people go to work, pay their taxes, and try to build a slightly better society, only to open the news and find a reality that seems to be running backward. When bad actors appear to thrive while ordinary people bear the brunt of systemic fatigue, the natural reaction is a profound, heavy sigh.

This weariness was recently laid bare on "Ju Gija Live," a popular political commentary program hosted by journalist Joo Jin-woo. The discussions there captured a mood shared by many outside the immediate political bubble: a feeling that the foundational rules of justice, representation, and basic common sense are bending under the weight of political self-interest. To understand why this matters—and why foreign observers of South Korea's vibrant democracy should pay attention—we have to look past the daily headlines and examine the structural cracks forming in the country's judiciary, legislature, and political culture.

The Judiciary Under Fire: From High-Stakes Trials to Internal Purges

South Korea’s legal system has found itself at the center of the national storm, dealing with both the legacy of the chaotic December 2024 martial law attempt and the everyday perception that the courts are bending to political winds. The contrast between how the law treats major political figures and how it manages internal dissent within the judiciary has sparked intense public debate.

In late September 2026, the special counsel team investigating the martial law crisis demanded a 20-year prison sentence for Choo Kyung-ho, the former Deputy Prime Minister and finance minister who was serving as the floor leader of the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) at the time of the emergency decree. Prosecutors argue that Choo actively cooperated with former President Yoon Suk Yeol by intentionally obstructing fellow lawmakers from participating in the parliamentary vote to lift martial law, shifting the location of party meetings multiple times to strand legislators far from the National Assembly. Despite this, Choo dismissed the trial as a "political witch hunt." With a ruling slated for November, the outcome carries massive implications not just for Choo personally, but for the political stability of Daegu, where he currently serves as mayor.

Conversely, former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo recently secured an acquittal from the Seoul Central District Court on charges related to the Public Official Election Act. Han had visited the May 18 National Cemetery in Gwangju ahead of a potential presidential run, shouting, "I love you! I am a Honam person!" while distributing cash for meal ingredients—an act critics viewed as blatant vote-buying. The court ruled that Han did not legally qualify as a "person intending to become a candidate" at that exact moment, a technicality that left many observers bewildered given that his presidential ambitions were treated as an open secret nationwide.

This apparent double standard extends inside the courtrooms as well. Judge Huh Yong-gu, a senior presiding judge who previously made headlines for ordering a prosecutor to leave a courtroom during a high-profile case involving Seongnam FC, suddenly found his career on the line. The National Court Administration initially cited an "authoritarian and coercive attitude" as grounds to block his judicial reappointment review, but abruptly shifted its rationale to "substandard work performance" after public backlash. Critics, including journalist Joo Jin-woo, point to this shifting justification as a clear case of retaliatory personnel management aimed at "taming" independent judges who do not toe the institutional line under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae.

The Echo Chamber of Extremism and the War on Fact

Outside the courtrooms, the political arena has increasingly devolved into personal attacks, conspiracy theories, and historical revisionism. The degradation of political discourse has made compromise nearly impossible, transforming public debate into a series of performative grievances.

A prime example involves Myung Tae-gyun, a self-styled political broker whose entanglement with prominent conservative figures has kept investigators busy. In a recent appeal statement, Myung offered convoluted justifications for past remarks regarding Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, claiming that when he previously stated Oh "doesn't know well," he meant Oh lacked knowledge of the "specific details and strategies," rather than being completely unaware of unofficial opinion polls conducted on his behalf. Despite denials from Oh's camp, records show multiple poll briefings were delivered to the mayor's circle during the 2021 Seoul mayoral by-election campaign, highlighting a recurring pattern where political actors distance themselves from controversial operatives only when caught.

Meanwhile, figures like Kim Tae-gyu, the acting chairperson of the Korea Communications Commission, have faced heavy criticism for publicly downplaying the historical realities of authoritarian crackdowns and dismissing judicial precedents. Lawmakers from the ruling bloc have also engaged in aggressive rhetorical campaigns against cultural works, spreading baseless claims about the funding and foreign backing of the historical film Assassins, while hurling personal insults at actors. This brand of politics relies not on legislative achievement, but on generating outrage and feeding an insular base.

Why Representative Democracy Feels Broken

The cumulative effect of these spectacles is a deep political depression among the electorate. When citizens see that hard work and civic-mindedness do not automatically translate into a better society, cynicism takes root. The assumption that time naturally heals a nation's political ailments begins to wear thin when institutions appear actively resistant to reform.

Yet, critics argue that the root of the problem lies in the design of South Korea's representative democracy itself. The National Assembly, composed of 300 lawmakers, is frequently criticized for failing to reflect the actual average citizen, instead attracting hyper-partisan careerists driven by ambition and conflict rather than public service.

To break this cycle, some commentators and civic thinkers have floated radical structural overhauls—most notably, the introduction of a sortition system, or a citizens' assembly chosen by lottery. Under this provocative proposal, 300 ordinary citizens would be randomly selected from the general population to form a temporary legislature, serving as a true mirror of the public's actual voice. Proponents argue that subjecting these citizen-lawmakers to rigorous preparation and oversight, treating public service as a mandatory civic duty much like conscription, would shatter the insular ecosystem of professional political brokers.

What to Watch Next

For those observing South Korea from afar, the coming months will test the resilience of the country's democratic checks and balances. Several concrete developments will signal which direction the nation is heading:

  • The Choo Kyung-ho Verdict: The November sentencing for the former finance minister will be a critical litmus test for how the judiciary handles accountability for the martial law crisis.
  • Judicial Reappointments and Internal Pushback: Watch whether the National Court Administration proceeds with punitive measures against independent judges, which will indicate the degree of internal control over the courts.
  • Electoral and Campaign Investigations: Ongoing probes into political brokers like Myung Tae-gyun will reveal whether the legal system can effectively police the shadowy intersection of polling, money, and campaigns.
  • Legislative Dynamics: Monitor how the National Assembly handles institutional reforms concerning judicial administration and broadcaster oversight amid intensifying polarization.

Ultimately, the fatigue gripping South Korea's political landscape is real, but it is also a byproduct of a society that cares deeply about the health of its democracy. The remedy for systemic exhaustion is not passive resignation, but the rigorous recording of facts and a refusal to let cynicism normalize the erosion of public trust.

"When outrage and falsehoods dominate the political space, preserving common sense and institutional integrity becomes the ultimate civic responsibility."
