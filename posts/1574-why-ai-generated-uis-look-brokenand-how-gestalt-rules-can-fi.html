Why AI-Generated UIs Look Broken—and How Gestalt Rules Can Fix Them

October 28, 2026

You type a prompt into ChatGPT, Claude, or a specialized generative UI design agent: "Create a modern, clean mobile screen for a financial tracking application." Within ten seconds, a glossy app layout appears on your screen. It has all the right elements—numerical readouts, transaction rows, interactive buttons, and colorful icon badges. On paper, every feature you requested is present. Yet the moment you look at the interface, your eyes dart around frantically, unable to settle on a natural starting point. The screen feels cluttered, disjointed, and unexpectedly exhausting to read.

This frustrating experience has become a common hurdle for product managers, developers, and solo founders relying on generative AI to spin up digital interfaces. When faced with a chaotic screen, most users issue secondary prompts like "Make it look cleaner," "Make it prettier," or "Tidy up the layout." But generative models inevitably return another variation of the same problem: text blocks and buttons scattered without functional logic. The core issue is that AI generative tools treat UI elements as graphic tokens rather than cognitive structures. Without explicit rules grounded in human visual psychology, AI generates a shell of an interface that fails in practical product environments.

The Three-Second Rule and the Reality of Visual Perception

To understand why AI-generated screens feel wrong, one must examine how the human brain processes visual information. When a user opens a mobile app or landing page, they do not read the screen word-by-word like a novel. Instead, the brain scans the layout in giant visual chunks—profile blocks, primary action buttons, content lists—making an instinctual decision within three to five seconds about whether to stay or abandon the app. If the screen fails to present clear visual groupings during that tiny window, cognitive overload kicks in, conversion rates plunge, and users leave.

Over a century ago, German psychologists formalized the rules governing this automatic visual grouping, establishing Gestalt psychology. Psychologist Kurt Koffka famously summarized the field with the proposition that "the whole is other than the sum of its parts." The human brain, seeking to conserve energy, automatically compresses complex visual clutter into unified, meaningful shapes. When a person looks at the night sky, they do not count seven disparate dots of light; their brain connects the dots into the spoon-shaped constellation of the Big Dipper.

"The whole is other than the sum of its parts." — Kurt Koffka

In tech circles, there is a persistent myth that crafting clean interfaces requires an innate, unteachable "design sense." Yet veteran product designers and researchers note that visual balance is an engineered science built on foundational rules. When non-designers or prompt engineers embed quantifiable Gestalt rules—specifically Proximity, Similarity, and Figure-Ground—into their AI prompts and evaluation workflows, they can turn raw AI outputs into polished, commercial-grade interfaces.

Proximity and Similarity: Spatial Grammar for Design Agents

1. Proximity: Defining Relationships Through Margins

The principle of Proximity states that objects positioned physically close to one another are perceived as a single group sharing a common purpose. In digital UI, negative space (margins and padding) is not empty real estate; it is a functional tool that creates information hierarchy.

Consider the profile layout of the fashion platform Alta. The app displays numerical metrics like "551" directly above the label "Looks," separated from adjacent pairs like "499" and "Followers" by distinct horizontal padding. Without using artificial dividing lines or heavy background boxes, the variable spacing alone signals to the user's brain that each number and label form an isolated data pair. Similarly, healthcare app MyFitnessPal utilizes generous vertical margins between screen sections to instantly communicate that top summary metrics and lower log items belong to distinct functional categories.

Conversely, when AI tools fail at proximity, visual confusion reigns. On the sign-up screen of the app Superpower, label text was placed at an identical vertical distance between the top input field and the bottom input field. Because the spacing above and below the label was identical, the user's brain could not determine which input box the text belonged to. The rule for AI prompt guidelines is simple: elements within the same functional group must sit close together, while distinct groups must be separated by wider margins.

2. Similarity: Grouping Through Form and Color

The principle of Similarity dictates that human vision automatically links items that share similar shapes, colors, or visual weights, regardless of their distance from one another across the screen.

  • Color-coded semantics: In the expense-sharing app Splitwise, numerical amounts owed to the user are rendered consistently in vibrant green, while debts owed to others are rendered in orange. Users do not need to read individual line items; they scan the green visual clusters to instantly assess their balance. Microsoft Outlook applies similar color-coding logic across calendar categories to group disparate events into coherent schedules.
  • Shape-based filtering: Spotify's home layout uses distinct geometric shapes to partition screen functions. Circular avatars signify artist profiles, while rounded rectangular pills denote genre filters. This geometric discipline allows the brain to sort filters from recommendations in milliseconds.
  • Interactive weight: In the routine tracker Daily Routine, completed tasks adopt identical black container styles, immediately pulling completed actions into a single visual state across the page.

When similarity rules are broken, business metrics suffer. In the astrological subscription app Co-Star, the cancellation screen previously featured two side-by-side buttons: "Not Now" (to remain subscribed) and "Confirm" (to terminate subscription). Both buttons were styled with identical grey rectangular shapes, identical font weights, and identical dimensions. Because the UI failed to visually differentiate a destructive action from a neutral one, users frequently tapped the wrong button by mistake. To prevent cognitive fatigue and misclicks, primary conversion buttons must use prominent solid fills, while secondary or cancellation actions should be downgraded to subtle outline or text link styles.

This principle was similarly illustrated in early iterations of Blue Bottle Coffee's sign-up screen, where "Sign Up" and "Log In" were rendered as identical twin buttons. For a landing page where 90% of traffic consists of new visitors, presenting equal visual weight creates unnecessary decision friction. Elevating the "Sign Up" option into a bold primary button naturally draws the user's thumb toward the intended goal.

Figure and Ground: Isolating Attention in Dynamic Interfaces

The human perceptual system cannot process every visual stimulus on a screen with equal intensity. It instinctively elevates a clear focal point—the Figure—while pushing everything else into the background—the Ground. In digital products, managing this dynamic is essential for popups, modals, and dynamic media feeds.

When video platform Azar or note-taking app Fabric present critical modals or reporting menus, they cast a 70% to 80% dark overlay (dim layer) across the main interface. By plunging the active feed into darkness, the background visual noise disappears, transforming the white popup box into a high-contrast focal figure that demands singular focus. By contrast, student recruitment platform Handshake suffered usability issues on video screens when popups were triggered over bright background videos without sufficient dimming. The lack of luminance contrast caused the popup text to clash with the playing background video, forcing users to strain their eyes to navigate the menu.

The Challenge of Dynamic Images and National Standards

The break in Figure-Ground relationship is most common when text is placed directly over background photos. Retail platforms like GOAT have frequently struggled with hero banners where thin white typography is layered on top of multicolored product photos. Because photographs contain fluctuating highlight and shadow zones, the background image fights directly with the text for visual dominance, destroying legibility.

In actual product environments, marketing banner images are dynamic assets changed for seasonal campaigns—ranging from bright cherry blossoms in spring to dark motifs in winter. Expecting designers to manually swap font colors for every uploaded photo is unscalable. The technical solution is to enforce automated UI rules, such as inserting a semi-transparent dark gradient overlay between the photo layer and the text layer. This preserves the atmospheric mood of the photo while guaranteeing that the text remains the high-contrast figure.

These cognitive engineering principles extend beyond private commercial software. In South Korea, the government recently overhauled public administrative portals by publishing the Korea Design System (KRDS), a comprehensive digital service UI/UX guideline. Managed under South Korea's current administration, the KRDS standardizes public service interfaces across government agencies, ensuring that citizens—including elderly and visually impaired users—can access public administrative tools without layout confusion.

The KRDS explicitly codifies Gestalt principles into public software specifications: strictly enforcing consistent component padding (Proximity), distinguishing interactive buttons from static informational chips through standardized styling (Similarity), and mandating high-contrast dim overlays for critical popups and identity verifications (Figure-Ground). Whether in public infrastructure or consumer apps, reducing cognitive load is an industry-wide necessity.

What to Watch Next in AI-Driven UI Development

As AI design tools transition from simple image generation to automated code production, relying on vague aesthetic prompts is no longer viable. Teams that successfully harness AI for production-ready interfaces will move away from open-ended text inputs and toward structured visual frameworks.

  • Gestalt-driven prompt frameworks: Expect front-end development tools to incorporate explicit spatial constraints directly into prompt interfaces—forcing AI models to calculate margin ratios, color semantics, and surface layers before rendering UI code.
  • Automated cognitive linting: Design systems in tools like Figma will increasingly embed real-time visual perception checkers. These automated linting tools will analyze generated UIs for proximity violations, ambiguous button hierarchies, and insufficient figure-ground contrast before code is pushed to production.
  • Standardization across national and enterprise platforms: Following initiatives like South Korea's KRDS, more public institutions and global enterprises will publish strict visual engineering rulesets. These frameworks will serve as training baselines for domain-specific AI agents, ensuring that AI-generated screens comply with accessibility laws and cognitive standards out of the box.

The future of UI design does not belong to those who treat generative AI as a magic visual tool, but to those who supply AI with the precise cognitive rules human brains use to make sense of the world.
