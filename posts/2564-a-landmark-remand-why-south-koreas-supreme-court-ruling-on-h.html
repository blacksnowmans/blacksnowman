A Landmark Remand: Why South Korea’s Supreme Court Ruling on Han Duck-soo Complicates the Martial Law Trials

November 20, 2026

As midnight approached in Seoul on November 20, 2026, legal analysts and politicians were watching two clocks at once. One was the statutory deadline on the pre-trial detention of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, set to expire at the stroke of twelve. The other was the broader political and legal reckoning over South Korea’s December 3 martial law crisis, which led to the impeachment, removal from office, and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol, and ushered in the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party.

Just one day earlier, South Korea’s Supreme Court had issued an en banc ruling—meaning all participating justices sat together to hear the case—that stunned observers who believed the legal verdict on martial law was all but settled. Han, who had been sentenced to 23 years in prison in his first trial and 15 years on appeal for playing a key role in an insurrection, saw the Supreme Court’s nine-justice majority overturn part of his conviction and send the rest back to the Seoul High Court for retrial.

Initial media reports framed the ruling as a confirmation that Yoon Suk-yeol’s martial law amounted to insurrection. Yet during the November 20 broadcast of the current affairs YouTube program Hong Sa-hoon Show, former presidential secretary for civil affairs Lee Kwang-cheol, ruling Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Yong-min, and journalists Jang In-soo and Hong Sa-hoon argued that the ruling actually opened a major escape hatch for Han and his co-defendants. Inside the bench itself, Justice Kwon Young-joon’s majority opinion clashed sharply with a three-justice dissent led by Justices Oh Kyung-mi, Shin Sook-hee, and Kim Seong-su over how criminal intent and official responsibility should be judged.

Unconstitutional Is Not Insurrection: The Legal Mechanics of the Remand

To understand why the ruling caused such whiplash, it helps to separate constitutional violations from criminal insurrection under South Korean law. In his analysis on the Hong Sa-hoon Show, Lee Kwang-cheol noted that while the Supreme Court majority explicitly declared Yoon Suk-yeol’s December 3 martial law unconstitutional, illegal, and an infringement on citizens’ basic rights, it stopped short of defining the event as insurrection under the Criminal Act.

"There is not a single phrase saying it constitutes insurrection," Lee Kwang-cheol pointed out while reading the ruling text.

Lee explained that South Korea’s Constitutional Court—which handles presidential impeachments—and its criminal courts operate on entirely different legal tracks. The Constitutional Court decides whether an official violated the Constitution or statutes such as the Martial Law Act to warrant removal from office. Criminal courts, by contrast, must rigorously prove the statutory elements of insurrection: a riot paired with the specific intent to subvert the constitutional order. Confirming unconstitutionality, Lee argued, added nothing legally new to the criminal case.

The insurrection-related charges against Han rested on three main pillars:

  • Convening the Cabinet meeting immediately before martial law was declared;
  • Pressuring Cabinet members to sign official documents after the declaration;
  • Participating in discussions on blockading the National Assembly and cutting power and water to media outlets.

The Supreme Court majority overturned and remanded the first pillar—the pre-declaration Cabinet meeting—with instructions pointing toward acquittal. For the remaining two pillars, the justices wrote that there was ample room to recognize an intent to subvert the constitution, yet declined to finalize a guilty verdict themselves and sent the entire matter back to the Seoul High Court.

The catch, according to Lee Kwang-cheol, lies in South Korean appellate procedure. Non-binding remarks in a Supreme Court remand—such as stating that the post-declaration charges have room to be recognized, or affirming that martial law was unconstitutional—do not legally compel the lower court to convict. Because the Supreme Court instructed the Seoul High Court to re-examine whether Han is guilty of playing an important role in insurrection, the retrial essentially reopens the entire insurrection charge from scratch. If the Seoul High Court finds insufficient proof of insurrectionary intent on the remaining two counts, Han would be left facing only lesser charges of falsifying official documents, destroying official documents, and perjury—potentially slashing his 15-year sentence to roughly three years or even a suspended sentence.

Tightening the Bar on Intent and the Role of the Prime Minister

At the heart of the legal split is how South Korean criminal law treats a "purpose crime" (mokjeokbeom). Most crimes require basic intent, but purpose crimes demand an additional, specific objective—much like currency counterfeiting, which requires not just copying bills knowingly, but doing so with the purpose of putting them into circulation. Lawmakers created this threshold to prevent prosecutors from overextending state punishment.

In three landmark en banc insurrection-related rulings over past decades—the Kim Jae-gyu, Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo, and Lee Seok-ki cases—the Supreme Court had applied this standard relatively broadly, ruling that conditional awareness (dolus eventualis, or foreseeing a result and accepting the risk) sufficed to establish an intent to subvert the constitution. In Han’s case, however, Justice Kwon Young-joon and eight other justices raised the bar. While keeping conditional awareness as the baseline for knowledge, they ruled that prosecutors must also prove the defendant aimed to realize and was willing to accept the outcome—and, absent a confession, must back that up with compelling circumstantial evidence. Under that stricter test, closed-circuit television footage showing Han reviewing the martial law decree in a reception room was deemed insufficient proof.

Opinions on this shift divide sharply along legal and political lines. From a doctrinal standpoint, some observers view strict proof of intent as a defense of nulla poena sine lege—the principle that criminal punishment requires clear statutory grounds. Notably, during the Kim Jae-gyu trial under authoritarian rule, Supreme Court justices who dissented in favor of stricter intent requirements were hauled away and tortured by the Korean Central Intelligence Agency. Yet commentators on the Hong Sa-hoon Show questioned whom this rigor now serves. Unlike the U.S. Miranda ruling, which expanded procedural protections for vulnerable suspects, they argued that strict evidentiary hurdles in South Korea disproportionately shield high-ranking officials—citing the case of former Vice Justice Minister Kim Hak-ui, who was granted bail after a court accepted witness-contamination claims over a prosecutor’s pre-trial interview. Critics also warned that the majority’s observation—that the hurried Cabinet meeting mainly stemmed from Yoon Suk-yeol pushing martial law through unilaterally—effectively hands lower-court judges a ready-made rationale to excuse senior aides.

The "Guarantor Duty" Clash and the Three-Justice Dissent

The court also fractured over "omission offenses"—when a defendant is punished not for an active deed, but for failing to stop a crime they had a legal duty to prevent. Both the appellate court and the Supreme Court majority concluded that Han lacked the criminal-law status of a "guarantor" legally obligated to block the martial law declaration. They cited his lack of knowledge regarding troop deployment details, his September 2024 testimony to the National Assembly stating martial law was impossible, and the view that convening a Cabinet meeting was a legitimate step to follow constitutional procedure.

That reasoning drew fierce criticism from both the dissenting justices and the Hong Sa-hoon Show panel. Rep. Kim Yong-min argued that the prime minister—the second-highest figure in the executive branch, vice-chair of the Cabinet, and acting president in an emergency—cannot be judged like an ordinary bystander. He compared the majority’s view to watching a child drown and claiming immunity because one called emergency services from the riverbank instead of jumping in. Panelists also ridiculed the reliance on Han’s past statements against martial law, likening it to reducing a fraudster’s sentence because he used to say fraud was wrong.

Inside the Supreme Court, dissenting Justices Oh Kyung-mi, Shin Sook-hee, and Kim Seong-su directly attacked the majority’s decision to split Han’s actions into "before" and "after" the declaration.

"Saying there is ample room to recognize an intent to subvert the constitution in actions after the declaration, while denying that intent for actions before the declaration, is difficult to accept even as a matter of common sense," Justice Oh Kyung-mi wrote in her dissent.

Justice Oh added that the majority’s logic effectively granted impunity to officials’ pre-declaration conduct, emphasizing that it was citizens rushing in front of guns—not public officials—who stopped the December 3 martial law. Rep. Kim Yong-min echoed that critique on air, calling the sudden appearance of criminal purpose only after the declaration "like a comedy," and questioning why the Supreme Court passed the burden back to the lower court rather than issuing a final ruling itself.

The Midnight Detention Clock and Korea’s Bail Calculus

Beyond the retrial itself, the remand triggered an immediate dispute over whether Han Duck-soo could remain behind bars past midnight on November 20 under Article 92 of South Korea’s Criminal Procedure Act. For international readers, South Korea’s detention timeline operates on two separate clocks. During an investigation, police and prosecutors can generally hold a suspect for up to 30 days combined (10 days by police, 10 by prosecutors, and a single 10-day court-approved extension, with longer limits reserved for narrow National Security Act cases). Once prosecutors indict, that clock resets completely for the trial phase.

Under Article 92, a trial court gets an initial two-month detention window, which can be renewed twice in two-month increments for a maximum of six months per court level. Appeals courts and the Supreme Court are allowed an exceptional third extension—up to eight months—if defense filings or evidence requests require additional review. Under Article 66, the first day of detention counts as a full day, and the period expires at midnight on the final day even if it falls on a weekend or holiday, unless renewed beforehand.

The legal gray zone arises when the Supreme Court remands a case to the Seoul High Court. Legal practitioners are split on whether the remanded proceeding counts as a continuation of the original appellate instance—meaning that if the eight-month appellate limit has run out, continued detention is unlawful and Han must be freed—or as a fresh instance resetting the six-month clock. Rep. Kim Yong-min noted on the broadcast that even searching Supreme Court precedents back to 1964 yielded no exact match. In practice, he expected authorities would not release Han at midnight without a clear court ruling.

Even if Han remains detained for now, commentators said his path to release via bail looks increasingly wide. Under Articles 94 and 95 of the Criminal Procedure Act, courts must grant bail unless one of six exceptions applies, including offenses carrying prison terms exceeding 10 years, repeat offenses, or risks of flight or evidence tampering. Having already spent nearly a year in custody, if Han’s charges shrink on remand from insurrection to document falsification and perjury, both Lee Kwang-cheol and Rep. Kim Yong-min predicted he is highly likely to apply for and win conditional bail under Article 98. Should the retrial end in acquittal or a suspended sentence, his detention warrant would immediately become void under Article 331, with time already served credited toward any remaining sentence.

Shockwaves for Co-Defendants and What to Watch Next

The Supreme Court’s reasoning is already rippling across related martial law prosecutions. Journalist Jang In-soo noted on the Hong Sa-hoon Show that defense teams for co-defendants—including former Cabinet ministers Kim Yong-hyun and Lee Sang-min—now hold a powerful legal blueprint. Lawyers can argue that their clients did not seek to dismantle South Korea’s democratic order, merely followed presidential instructions passively in response to perceived legislative gridlock, and never confessed to insurrectionary intent. Kim Yong-hyun’s defense in particular can cite the Supreme Court’s dismissal of the reception-room CCTV footage as insufficient circumstantial proof.

Politically, the ruling offers fresh ammunition to the imprisoned Yoon Suk-yeol’s camp and the conservative People Power Party, now South Korea’s main opposition, who can argue that even the nation’s highest court did not finalize martial law as an insurrection. Combined with political fallout already swirling after the recent acquittal of Daegu Mayor Choo Kyung-ho, the remand puts renewed pressure on prosecutors and the ruling Democratic Party.

Going forward, three developments will determine whether this ruling reshapes the entire martial law reckoning or remains an outlier:

  • The Seoul High Court’s detention and bail decision: Whether judges treat the remand as a new appellate instance to keep Han detained, or grant him conditional bail under Article 95 as the insurrection charges weaken.
  • How the remand panel handles the post-declaration charges: Whether the Seoul High Court follows the Supreme Court’s non-binding cue to convict Han over the post-declaration Cabinet signatures and blockade discussions, or drops the insurrection charge altogether.
  • Lower-court adoption in accomplice trials: Whether trial judges handling Kim Yong-hyun, Lee Sang-min, and other former officials adopt Justice Kwon Young-joon’s heightened threshold for proving insurrectionary purpose.
