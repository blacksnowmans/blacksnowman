A $75 Billion Shield: Why South Korea Is Debating a Massive Stock Market Defense Fund

November 17, 2026

During a capital market policy roundtable with South Korean President Lee Jae-myung, Park Sei-ik, CEO of Chesley Advisory Services, delivered an unflinching assessment of South Korea’s financial vulnerability. Despite ranking among the world's leading economies, South Korea’s equity markets continue to operate like an un-fenced open field, left exposed to aggressive short-selling and speculative attacks by foreign institutions. To break this cycle, Park pitched a bold solution: establishing a 100 trillion won (approximately $75 billion) Stock Market Stabilization Fund as a national financial security shield.

For decades, South Korea’s domestic stock market—the KOSPI and KOSDAQ—has suffered extreme volatility driven by foreign capital flows and derivative expiration dates rather than corporate fundamentals. Sound domestic companies with strong balance sheets routinely watch their valuations plummet during global panics, allowing foreign capital to snap up domestic equity on the cheap. The proposal for a $75 billion cushion is designed not merely as a temporary market intervention, but as a permanent structural fix for the persistent "Korea Discount."

The Structural Vulnerability of the KOSPI: A Playground for Foreign Speculation

To understand why Korean market insiders are demanding a massive sovereign defense mechanism, one must look at how foreign derivatives trading has historically destabilized local equities. A notorious example occurred on November 11, 2010—often remembered in Seoul trading rooms as the "Pepero Day Option Shock."

Just two hours before the closing bell on an options expiration date, foreign speculative funds began heavily accumulating out-of-the-money put options that were theoretically destined to expire worthless at 0.01 won. As buying surged, option prices spiked to 0.05 won. During the final closing auction, foreign entities dumped trillions of won worth of spot shares onto the market simultaneously. The index crashed, allowing the foreign funds to pocket roughly 180 billion won ($135 million) in profits in a matter of hours. Conversely, a domestic asset manager handling client orders sustained a catastrophic 76 billion won ($57 million) loss and was forced into bankruptcy. Although financial regulators investigated and prosecuted the case, South Korea’s Supreme Court ultimately acquitted the foreign defendants in 2023, citing difficulty in proving direct causation. The ruling left domestic investors with a bitter lesson: foreign institutions could trigger artificial volatility and walk away without legal consequences.

In his 2008 book Currency Wars, Chinese economic writer Song Hongbing argued that national sovereignty rests on four pillars: territory, territorial waters, airspace, and finance. South Korea experienced the devastating impact of financial weakness firsthand during the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, when foreign creditors forced fire-sales of prime national assets—a process often described as "shearing the sheep."

Today, South Korea possesses immense domestic wealth. The country’s top five financial holdings companies command over 4,000 trillion won ($3 trillion) in combined assets. The National Pension Service (NPS) ranks among the world's top three pension funds with up to 2,000 trillion won ($1.5 trillion) under management, complemented by 500 trillion won ($375 billion) in private retirement pension accounts. Yet despite holding vast reserves, the country lacks an automated, coordinated execution mechanism to absorb aggressive foreign selling waves, leaving retail investors and national wealth exposed when panics strike.

The Mechanics of a $75 Billion Buffer: Capital Calls and Taiwan’s Playbook

Previous Korean government attempts to stabilize the stock market relied on funds sized at roughly 10 trillion won ($7.5 billion). In a combined market capitalized at nearly 5,000 trillion won ($3.75 trillion), a $7.5 billion pool is a drop in the bucket. Foreign institutional traders, capable of offloading more than $7.5 billion in equities over a two-day window, routinely ignored or exploited these smaller intervention attempts.

Park’s proposed 100 trillion won ($75 billion) fund operates on a fundamentally different scale. Designed under a "capital call" structure, participating institutions would not keep $75 billion sitting idle in cash. Instead, member institutions—including major commercial banks and asset managers—would manage their funds normally until the stock index breaches a predetermined crisis threshold. Once triggered, the fund would deploy an initial tranche of 10 trillion won ($7.5 billion) while broadcasting a clear commitment that 90 trillion won ($67.5 billion) remains in reserve to buy further declines.

Key Features of the Proposed $75B Stabilization Fund:

  • Deterrence Scale: Outforces foreign speculative sell-offs by establishing an overwhelming counter-bid capability.
  • Capital Call Structure: Money remains in short-term yield-bearing assets until market panics trigger automated execution.
  • Volatility Reduction: Caps extreme index drawdowns, helping elevate KOSPI Price-to-Earnings (P/E) multiples from sub-10x levels toward global averages of 15x.

This approach closely mirrors Taiwan’s highly successful National Stabilization Fund, administered by its Ministry of Finance. During periods of heightened global trade tension, Taiwan’s government fund stepped into the open market to aggressively purchase domestic tech flagships like TSMC and Foxconn when foreign investors dumped shares. By establishing a firm bid under key blue chips, Taiwan controlled market volatility, stabilized investor sentiment, and generated returns of up to 90% when markets normalized a year later.

Global tech leaders like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix possess world-class semiconductor technology, yet their share prices frequently suffer wild swings due to overall KOSPI volatility. Containing this volatility directly combats the "Korea Discount," assuring international long-term investors that South Korean asset valuations will not be erased overnight by short-term derivatives plays.

Mobilizing Pension Capital and Institutional Responsibility

Funding a $75 billion mechanism requires smart integration with existing domestic capital, particularly South Korea's growing retirement pension pool. Under Korean regulation, retirement pension plans must keep at least 30% of their holdings in principal-protected or low-risk assets. Major asset managers—such as Mirae Asset Global Investments (operators of TIGER ETFs) and Samsung Asset Management (operators of KODEX ETFs)—could launch specialized bond-hybrid stabilization funds. In normal market conditions, these funds would hold ultra-safe instruments like Money Market Funds (MMFs) and bank deposits, earning steady 3% annual interest. However, if an external shock causes the KOSPI index to plummet more than 30%, the fund’s cash holdings would automatically convert into high-grade Korean equities.

Once the market recovers by 26%—a rebound that historically occurs rapidly following steep panics—the fund would systematically liquidate its equity positions, locking in profits and returning to liquid cash. Compounding a 26% gain across 10 crisis-rebound cycles turns an initial capital base into a 10-fold return, providing safe, high-yielding growth for Korean retirees while supplying the state with an automatic, counter-cyclical market stabilizer.

Critics sometimes argue against state-backed or institutional market interventions, advocating for pure free-market price discovery. However, South Korea’s major financial institutions have earned record-breaking interest margins and managed hundreds of billions of dollars in exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Proponents argue these institutions share a civic responsibility to safeguard customer assets from predatory market manipulation during crises.

Historical precedents demonstrate the power of decisive domestic buying during global panics. Following the September 11 attacks in 2001, Kim Jung-tae, then-CEO of Housing Bank (now part of KB Financial Group), deployed 500 billion won ($375 million) directly into Korean equities over trade union objections. The KOSPI doubled from 460 to 940 within seven months, yielding massive returns for the bank. A similar 1 trillion won ($750 million) intervention during the 2003 SARS outbreak achieved comparable success within five months. These interventions align directly with classical economic principles dating back to Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776): temporary price dislocations in fundamentally sound assets inevitably self-correct.

Debunking the 'Semiconductor Winter' and What to Watch Next

A recurring driver of Korean market volatility is the outsized influence of foreign investment bank equity research. In September 2024, a widely circulated Morgan Stanley report titled "Winter Is Coming" triggered sharp sell-offs across South Korea's memory chip sector, echoing a near-identical warning the firm issued in November 2017.

Historical data shows that negative foreign investment reports often cause short-term market corrections lasting around 10 months. Paradoxically, the moment reported headwinds—such as margin squeezes in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)—actually materialize in corporate earnings, tech stocks frequently hit their cyclical floor and begin powerful rallies. Following the 2017 warnings, SK Hynix surged 55% and Samsung Electronics rose 44% over the course of 2019.

Memory semiconductors follow a well-defined three-year business cycle. Pullbacks of 40% to 60% from peak valuations have historically presented prime entry points for long-term investors focused on structural demand rather than transient headlines—a strategy exemplified by veteran Korean investors like actress Jeon Won-ju, who built substantial wealth by holding SK Hynix through multiple industry downturns.

What to Watch Next:

  • Legislative Trajectory: Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party of Korea and the Lee Jae-myung administration introduce capital market reforms incorporating capital-call stabilization funds into formal financial policy.
  • Financial Sector Buy-in: Monitor announcements from South Korea's top five financial holding companies regarding commitments to joint equity-stabilization pools.
  • Regulatory Enforcement: Track how the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) updates oversight on foreign derivatives trading and short-selling mechanics around options expiration dates.
