The Myth of the "Young-Geul": Why Korea’s Real Estate Market Isn't Collapsing

September 11, 2026

If you have been following Korean news lately, you have likely seen the headlines: interest rates are climbing, the Bank of Korea (the country’s central bank) has pushed its base rate to 3.00%, and the U.S. Federal Reserve’s rates are hovering around 3.75%. In this climate, getting a mortgage at 5.5% is considered a "good deal." For many, the immediate concern is the so-called "Young-Geul" crowd—a term that combines the Korean words for "soul" and "gathering," describing young people who borrowed every penny possible to buy homes before the market peaked.

Social media and YouTube comment sections are filled with anxiety. "Are these people going to go bankrupt?" "Is the housing market finally going to crash?" There is a pervasive narrative that an army of desperate, over-leveraged borrowers is waiting for the floor to drop out from under them. But as we look at the data and the reality of Korean banking, a very different picture emerges. The truth is that the "Young-Geul" generation—at least the one the media talks about—is largely a ghost of the past.

The Reality Behind the "Soul-Gathering" Panic

The image of the "Young-Geul" borrower dates back to the Moon Jae-in administration (2017–2022). During that period, aggressive government loan restrictions made it nearly impossible to buy a home through standard means. Potential buyers were forced to scrape together loans from parents, use risky secondary financial institutions, or engage in creative, often shaky, financing just to secure an apartment. The fear of being left behind in a skyrocketing market drove this extreme behavior.

However, that era ended abruptly in 2022. When the Bank of Korea’s base rate surged from 0.5% to 3.5% in just a few months, the leverage used by these speculators became unsustainable. Those who had over-extended were forced out of the market as interest payments quadrupled and "reverse jeonse" crises (where landlords cannot return rental deposits) hit the market. The people who survived that period are no longer the "Young-Geul" crowd; they are homeowners who weathered the storm and saw their assets recover as the market rebounded in 2024 and 2025.

Today, the notion that people are still "soul-gathering" to buy homes is a misunderstanding held by those who haven't stepped inside a bank recently. Anyone who attempts to get a mortgage today is met with a cold, hard reality check. Banks have a much better idea of your financial capacity than you do. If you want to buy an 800 million won (about $580,000) home, you will likely be told you need at least 600 million won (about $430,000) in cash. The days of easy, high-leverage borrowing are over.

The World’s Strictest Loan Regulations

South Korea currently enforces some of the most stringent lending regulations in the world. The government utilizes the "Stress DSR (Debt Service Ratio) Phase 3," a system that assumes interest rates will rise further when calculating your loan limit. Even if the current rate is 5.5%, the bank assesses your ability to pay as if the rate were 8.5%. Furthermore, borrowers are strictly capped so that principal and interest payments cannot exceed 40% of their annual income.

This is a dual-layered cage: the LTV (Loan-to-Value) ratio restricts how much you can borrow based on the home's price, and the DSR restricts it based on your personal income. Even high-income earners—even famous pop stars—often find that they can only borrow around 200 million won (about $145,000) regardless of the property's value. The state has essentially severed the easy link between credit and real estate. Consequently, the "reckless borrowing" that pundits fear is physically impossible under current regulatory filters.

The Paradox of Housing Costs and the "Jeonse" Problem

If someone does manage to secure a loan today, is it a disaster? Consider a homebuyer purchasing an 850 million won (about $615,000) apartment with a 600 million won (about $435,000) mortgage. With an interest rate of 5.5% and a 40-year term, the monthly payment is roughly 3.09 million won (about $2,200). While that sounds like a lot, the alternative in Seoul is the rental market, where monthly rents for similar properties often exceed 1.8 million won (about $1,300), plus a significant security deposit.

Paying interest on a home you own offers stability that the volatile rental market cannot. South Korea’s unique jeonse system (a massive lump-sum rental deposit) has historically kept housing costs artificially low, but that era is fading. As the government cracks down on non-resident homeowners, the supply of jeonse housing is drying up, forcing tenants into a skyrocketing monthly rent market. The irony is that the government's attempts to "protect" the market by restricting loans are actually fueling a rental crisis, making homeownership a necessary defensive strategy against rising housing costs.

What to Watch Next

The "bankruptcy tsunami" predicted by many never arrived because most Korean household debt is tied to mortgages on primary residences—assets people fight to keep at all costs. With the Korean economy seeing a boost from the massive semiconductor boom (led by giants like Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix) and trade surpluses hitting record highs, the fundamental economic base is stronger than many realize.

What to watch:

  • Rental Market Volatility: As jeonse supply continues to shrink due to strict residency requirements, watch for further spikes in monthly rent prices in core Seoul districts.
  • Regulatory Adjustments: Keep an eye on whether the government eases the "Stress DSR" if the economic pressure on younger families becomes a political liability.
  • Macroeconomic Data: Because the Korean economy is so heavily export-dependent, watch global trade relations and semiconductor demand; these are the true drivers of the country’s liquidity and asset pricing, far more than the localized fear of individual borrower defaults.

The lesson for the observer is clear: do not let the sensationalist rhetoric regarding "Young-Geul" borrowers cloud your analysis. We are not looking at a market on the brink of collapse, but rather a tightly controlled financial environment where the gap between those with assets and those without continues to widen. The focus should not be on hoping for a crash that isn't coming, but on how to navigate the current, constrained landscape of Korean real estate.
