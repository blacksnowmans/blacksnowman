South Korea's Shifting Political Tides: Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Plunging Approval Ratings Spell Trouble

October 02, 2026

Walk through the streets of Seoul or check any political bulletin board right now, and you can practically feel the electricity in the air. South Korean politics is experiencing a seismic shift, and the numbers coming out of recent polls are nothing short of jarring. President Lee Jae-myung—the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's primary liberal party—is facing a disapproval rating stubbornly entrenched above 60%. Meanwhile, sentiment favoring a harsh rebuke of the ruling administration sits at a staggering 52.8%. Politics is often described as a living, breathing creature, but the extreme polarization and bizarre decoupling of party loyalty from leadership approval we are seeing today is exceptional. Are these figures just temporary noise, or are they the opening drumbeat of a massive political reckoning?

The Hidden Math of Power: Approval Ratings and Parliament

The "Times Three" Rule

In South Korean political circles, a grim piece of folklore has persisted for years: the number of seats a ruling party can secure in a legislative election roughly equals the sitting president's approval rating multiplied by three. Of course, this formula is not a mathematical absolute; countless variables—such as regional dynamics, candidate quality, and local district maps—always come into play. Yet, when applied to historical data, the correlation is chillingly precise.

Look back at the 21st general election, when former President Moon Jae-in held a positive job evaluation of 53.7%, carrying the Democratic Party to an overwhelming 180-seat victory. Fast forward to the 22nd general election, when former President Yoon Suk Yeol languished with a 36.8% approval rating, leaving the conservative People Power Party to scrape together a miserable 108 seats. Why is this rule so feared? Because a legislative election in South Korea is never just a contest between parties; it functions as a brutal mid-term referendum on the incumbent administration. The moment a president's approval dips below 40%, securing a parliamentary majority becomes an uphill battle. With President Lee’s negative assessments now breaking past 60%, the political weight resting on the ruling camp ahead of the next elections is immense.

The Polling Divide: ARS Versus Phone Interviews

Interpreting South Korean polling data requires a keen eye for methodology. Automated Response System (ARS) polls often suffer from lower response rates, but they excel at capturing the unvarnished feelings of highly engaged political partisans. Phone interviews conducted by live operators, by contrast, yield higher response rates, but respondents frequently mask their true intentions out of caution. The gap between ARS and phone interview figures effectively measures the political fear and social pressure felt by average voters.

It is an open secret in political analysis that ARS methods tend to track closer to actual election outcomes. While phone interviews often skew higher for the Democratic Party due to progressive oversampling, ARS numbers tend to expose the harsher underlying reality. With President Lee's approval ratings remaining chronically weak, the steady erosion picked up by ARS surveys cannot be brushed off as statistical noise. It is the widening crack in a dam before it bursts.

The Cost of Ignoring Crises

Dismissing volatile poll numbers as mere "statistical errors" is a dangerous gamble. In the latter half of a presidential term, a plummeting approval rating is more than just a failed policy—it signifies a total loss of governing momentum. As veteran commentator and writer You Si-min has warned, the old rule of thumb that a 50% approval rating translates to roughly 150 seats shows just how limited the political license granted by the public truly is.

Electoral Defeats and the Transformation of Lee Jae-myung

The Fall of "Lee’s Picks"

The heavy losses suffered by key candidates backed closely by Lee's inner circle during local elections carry profound significance. The defeat of figures handpicked for strategic strongholds—including marquee mayoral and parliamentary races—was not merely a streak of bad luck. Why did the electorate reject them? Without confronting that question head-on, the current slide in approval is a tragedy foretold. While many loyal supporters express shock at these results, the party leadership often prefers to point fingers externally rather than engage in genuine self-reflection.

Local elections act as a crucible where regional grievances meet national politics. As You Si-min noted, the public had been sending cautious warning signals about the administration's trajectory for quite some time. Yet, power has a predictable habit of rewarding sycophants who whisper comforting falsehoods. Blaming commentators for pointing out these flaws is a massive overestimation of their influence; the public had already formed these doubts, and analysts were simply giving them a voice.

The Paradox of President Lee

A question frequently whispered in political circles today is: "Was President Lee always like this, or has he changed?" The fiery, reform-minded politician who campaigned for the office feels light-years away from the statesman currently projected through media channels. Is this simply the crushing weight of executive power, or the unmasking of his true self? While a definitive answer remains elusive, one thing is certain: this cognitive dissonance is breeding severe fatigue among his base.

As veteran broadcaster Kim Eo-jun has pointed out, if current trends hold, the genuine fear of handing over 180 seats to the opposition and sliding straight into a crippling lame-duck presidency is no longer abstract—it is a visceral panic. Cosmetic reshuffles, such as swapping out a chief of staff, will not cure the public's skepticism. From persistent controversies over personnel appointments to ongoing legal risks and defections among non-mainstream factions, approval ratings are the "mileage points" of public trust. When that mileage hits zero, blaming the media will not save the administration's future.

Statistics, Dogma, and the Loss of Rational Debate

Shattering the Progressive Oversampling Myth

The debate over "progressive oversampling" is a perennial fixture of Korean polling analysis. Unadjusted datasets that capture 30 to 40 more liberal-leaning respondents out of a 1,000-person sample naturally inflate approval metrics. Yet, the stark reality that President Lee’s approval has hovered around a dismal 45% even with these skewed samples serves as an ironic testament to a deeper truth: even the progressive base is beginning to look away. Treating these figures as meaningless statistical noise ignores the alarming reality that, even with a statistical tailwind, the administration can barely scrape together 45% support.

Parts of the ruling camp attempt to frame these numbers as biased attacks or hostile meddling, but the data refuses to lie. Recent results from major pollsters show negative evaluations outstripping positive ones by margins well outside the margin of error. When intra-party fractures and judicial controversies intertwine, simply emphasizing "better communication" will not coax the public back. Ordinary citizens see right through the political theater.

The Death of Policy-Centric Politics

In past eras, rational debates anchored in policy dominated South Korean elections. Today, politics has devolved into a rigid "good versus evil" narrative. Casting opponents as absolute villains to rally the faithful may yield short-term dividends, but it remains the single worst strategy for preventing the alienation of critical moderate voters. The dual goals championed by political experts—refreshing the administration's inner circle while consolidating the base—are fundamentally impossible without transparent governance and tangible policy achievements.

If high-profile resignations like Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-shik’s are treated as mere public relations exercises rather than genuine contrition, the descent into lame-duck status will only accelerate. Politics remains a human endeavor, and winning hearts starts with honesty. The current freefall in public approval asks a blunt question: Does this government possess the courage to acknowledge its mistakes and correct course? Without an answer, the future holds only a cold, unforgiving verdict.

What to Watch Next

  • Personnel and Cabinet Reshuffles: Monitor whether upcoming changes to presidential secretaries and cabinet members are substantive or merely cosmetic window-dressing designed to placate critics.
  • Intra-Party Fractures: Keep an eye on the non-mainstream factions within the Democratic Party; any formal breakaways will signal that the ruling bloc is splintering ahead of future legislative battles.
  • Judicial and Legal Developments: Track how ongoing legal controversies involving key figures impact public trust and accelerate or halt the administration's political erosion.
