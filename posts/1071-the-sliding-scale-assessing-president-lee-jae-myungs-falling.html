The Sliding Scale: Assessing President Lee Jae-myung’s Falling Approval Ratings

October 13, 2026

Walking through the halls of the National Assembly—the political heart of Seoul—the atmosphere has shifted from the steady hum of governance to a palpable, anxious silence. For weeks, the talk of the town hasn't been about major legislation or grand state visions, but about a series of numbers flashing on flickering monitors. As of the second week of October 2026, major polling firms like Realmeter and Gallup Korea have released data that paints a stark picture: President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has dipped into the low-to-mid 30s.

In a parliamentary democracy, a president’s popularity is the fuel for their policy engine. When that fuel runs low, the machine stalls. The opposition party, the People Power Party (South Korea’s main conservative party), has now edged ahead of the President’s Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party) within the margin of error. For a government that entered office with high hopes for systemic reform, these numbers are not just statistics—they are a flashing warning light.

A Warning Signal from the Public

The latest figures from Realmeter, showing a 35.2% approval rating versus a 61.4% disapproval rate, signal a deep disconnect between the Blue House and the electorate. Perhaps more concerning for the ruling Democratic Party is the shift in party identification. The current split—35.5% for the Democratic Party versus 41.3% for the People Power Party—marks a dramatic realignment from the momentum seen during the last general election.

Most alarming for the ruling party is the loss of its traditional core: the youth. Among voters aged 18 to 29, the People Power Party now leads with 48.8% support, compared to just 19.3% for the Democratic Party. While the President still maintains a stronghold among voters in their 40s and 50s, the erosion across other age brackets suggests a cumulative fatigue with the government’s policy direction. Analysts point to a "triple threat" of recurring failures: personnel appointments, national security management, and, most crucially, real estate policy. When the public feels their daily life is getting harder, they tend to stop looking at long-term promises and start judging the government on its immediate failures.

The Real Estate Crisis: When Policy Meets Reality

Nothing hits the South Korean electorate quite like the housing market. Recent polling shows a staggering 61% disapproval rate for the government's real estate policies, with only 19% approving. For a nation where housing is the primary vehicle for middle-class wealth and security, this is a crisis of confidence.

Despite the government’s frequent pledges to increase housing supply, the market remains volatile. The perception is that the government is indecisive, oscillating between over-regulation and half-hearted liberalization. As Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has noted, rising housing costs directly shrink disposable income, which in turn stifles domestic consumption. When a young person in Seoul looks at the distance between their rent and the cost of homeownership, they aren't looking at "policy frameworks"—they are looking at their own survival. Promises of "310,000 new units" ring hollow if they don't lead to actual move-ins and lower costs in the immediate future.

This failure is exacerbated by a perceived lack of communication. When a government ministry attempts to push through major changes to pension thresholds without prior public consensus, only to backtrack under pressure, it leaves the public feeling like the administration is governing from an ivory tower. Whether it’s labor reform or civil service appointments, the perception of "top-down" governance is creating a wall of distrust that is increasingly difficult to breach.

Diplomacy, Security, and the Question of Competence

The current administration is also facing heat over its approach to national security and diplomatic consistency. Critics have pointed to what they describe as a "double standard" in how the government handles international issues versus domestic security incidents involving South Korean troops. When the government adopts a hardline stance on foreign conflicts but remains seemingly tepid on domestic military accountability, it invites accusations of inconsistency.

Furthermore, the government’s reform agenda—including the restructuring of investigative agencies—has become a lightning rod for criticism. While many citizens initially supported the idea of dismantling the legacy of the previous administration, specifically the abuses of power during the era of the now-imprisoned Yun Seok-youl, the execution has been marred by internal party squabbles and questionable appointments.

The public no longer asks which party is "better." They are asking which party is competent and morally consistent. If the current government fails to prove that its reforms are about improving public life rather than shifting power, the label of "arbitrary rule" may stick.

What to Watch Next

The path forward for President Lee Jae-myung is narrow but clear. The administration must pivot from grand political gestures to the granular details of the "people’s livelihood."

  • Refined Policy Priorities: The government needs to stop promising future results and start delivering relief on housing and inflation.
  • Communication Over Proclamation: The habit of announcing policy via social media or press releases without prior grassroots consultation is a major liability. The government must engage in genuine, two-way dialogue.
  • Restoring Competence: The administration needs to prove it can appoint qualified people and manage the bureaucracy without the constant "noise" of internal scandals.

In the coming months, keep a close eye on the "undecided" demographic. Currently hovering around 32% in some polls, these voters are not necessarily aligned with the opposition; they are simply waiting to be convinced. If the government continues its current trajectory, these voters are likely to swing toward a "judgment" vote in future local elections. However, if the administration can demonstrate humility and a return to the "people-first" approach that defined its early days, the current polling slump could prove to be a wake-up call rather than a downward spiral. Politics, after all, is the art of winning the public’s heart—a heart that is currently guarded, waiting to see if the government is truly listening.
