Beyond the Copy-Paste Employee: How AI Agents Are Forcing a Return to the "Artisan Era" in South Korea

November 27, 2026

In the high-walled boardrooms of Seoul’s major corporations, an unusual scene is unfolding. Executive chairmen in their seventies and eighties, long accustomed to dictating strategy from behind mahogany desks, are leaning over keyboards, holding computer mice, and learning how to write prompts for AI agents. They are not doing this out of academic curiosity. They are doing it because the traditional corporate buffer—the army of middle managers who translated executive vision into slide decks and tracking spreadsheets—is rapidly evaporating.

For decades, South Korea’s economic growth ran on rigid, highly divided corporate hierarchies. But as autonomous AI software transitions from basic chatbots to agents capable of compiling data, drafting strategy documents, and verifying code, the rationale for administrative middle management has collapsed. This shift is at the center of the latest work by Song Gil-young, one of South Korea’s most prominent big-data experts and trend forecasters.

Song has systematically charted the structural unbundling of Korean society. In 2023, he analyzed the emergence of the "Nuclear Individual" (the break from traditional family- and company-dependent structures). In 2024, he pointed to the "Named Society" (where workers demand recognition by their personal names rather than corporate rank). By 2025, he identified the "Lightweight Civilization" (where bloated organizations strip away procedural bureaucracy). Looking ahead to 2027, Song introduces his newest concept: Sugo Inryu, or "Laboring Humanity"—a era where automated software eliminates superficial administrative work, leaving individuals with a clear choice: master authentic, deep craftsmanship or face professional irrelevance.

The 250-Year Fall of the Corporate Machine: Why Division of Labor Is Breaking Down

Since the Industrial Revolution 250 years ago, modern corporate capitalism built its efficiency on extreme division of labor. Workers were trained to act as specialized cogs in a larger system. In South Korea's rapid post-war industrial rise, this structure created millions of stable white-collar jobs. However, it also fostered a deep systemic vulnerability.

Song illustrates this flaw with a sharp metaphor: a corporate department head who spent thirty years at a major commercial bakery conglomerate managing flour procurement contracts, but retires unable to bake a single loaf of bread. In the old system, that manager was valuable because the corporation provided the surrounding apparatus—logistics, baking, packaging, finance, and marketing. Separated from the corporate machine, the individual is helpless. Song calls this modern archetype the "Copy-Paste Human"—someone who spends a career duplicating templates, routing memos, and transmitting orders downward.

In traditional Korean offices, duplicated administrative labor was widespread. A headquarters team of 20 people, a regional office team of 10, and a local branch team of five would all fill out identical report templates to send back up the chain. Standardized reporting turned distinct human beings into identical, standardized units.

AI agents have exposed the inefficiency of this arrangement. When software can aggregate regional branch metrics, draft a summary report, and highlight errors in seconds, human middlemen lose their function. As technology strips away this administrative overhead, it paradoxically revives a pre-industrial model: the Age of the Artisan. Once again, individuals are required to understand and execute entire processes from start to finish, using AI agents as an infinitely scalable digital staff.

The Death of Middle Management: When AI Takes Over Supervision

To understand why Korean workplaces are feeling this tension, one must look at traditional Korean corporate rank dynamics. For decades, white-collar careers followed a strict ladder from entry-level roles up to Kim Dae-ri (mid-level assistant manager) and eventually Bujang-nim (department head or general manager). A primary duty of the Bujang-nim was administrative supervision: checking up on Kim Dae-ri, verifying report figures, and monitoring deadlines.

Today, AI agents perform tracking, error-checking, and task verification faster and more accurately than human supervisors. As Song notes, execution-oriented employees are stepping into management functions. Armed with AI tools, a single junior or mid-level employee—the traditional Kim Dae-ri—can prompt digital assistants to run data analyses, generate visual assets, and draft initial proposals, delivering completed work directly to senior executives.

This leaves middle managers, whose core role was oversight, facing a sudden loss of purpose. This shift matches broader international labor trends:

  • Goldman Sachs reports that generative AI could directly affect roughly 300 million full-time jobs globally through task automation.
  • The International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimates that up to 60 percent of employment in advanced economies is exposed to AI integration.
  • PwC’s 2024 AI Jobs Barometer analyzed over 500 million job postings worldwide and found that productivity growth in roles requiring AI skills is 4.7 times higher than in non-AI roles.
  • The Korea Research Institute for Vocational Education and Training (a government-funded think tank) found in its workplace redesign studies that Korean companies achieving real productivity gains from AI are empowering frontline workers to directly generate output rather than adding management layers.

As corporate HR departments across South Korea respond to these shifts, corporate hiring strategies are changing. Rather than hiring waves of entry-level workers for routine reporting, organizations are identifying which tasks can be automated outright and blending the remaining responsibilities into streamlined roles for execution-capable professionals.

Three Rules for Survival: How to Become an "Original Human"

To navigate the transition away from corporate copy-pasting, Song outlines three practical execution rules designed to help professionals transition into "Original Humans":

1. Direct Execution ("직접 합니다")

Traditional corporate leadership prioritized delegating tasks and reviewing final results. In the AI era, effective leadership requires hands-on execution. Without understanding the operational mechanics of a task, a worker cannot write effective prompts or spot subtle flaws in AI outputs. Direct execution means operating software directly, refining prompts, and applying critical judgment to turn automated drafts into high-value work.

2. Public Publishing ("발행합니다")

Keeping research, skills, or ideas hidden inside personal desktop folders or internal company servers yields no long-term personal equity. Professionals must publish their work publicly—whether through writing, code repositories, video platforms, or books. Publishing creates verifiable proof of originality. It also exposes work to real market feedback, forcing the creator to refine their skills beyond internal corporate standards.

3. Putting Your Name on It ("이름을 겁니다")

Song references movie ending credits, where every contributor—from the director to the sound technician and driver—is explicitly named. Traditional corporations, by contrast, erased personal identity behind institutional titles like "Planning Team" or "Marketing Department." In South Korea, where formal corporate careers often end around age 60 while life expectancy extends past 100, relying entirely on a company brand is a risky long-term strategy. When a corporate logo is removed from a business card, a worker must hold a distinct craft tied directly to their personal name.

Solopreneurs, Taste Archiving, and the Return of the Oral Exam

This shift does not mean older professionals are excluded from the economy. Accessible AI tools allow workers in their 40s, 50s, and 60s to build single-person businesses without heavy capital investment or staff payrolls. Song points to solopreneurs who turn niche personal interests—such as vintage stationery collecting—into sustainable micro-businesses. By operating small physical studios open just three days a week and handling orders through reservation messaging systems, individuals can cultivate dedicated, nationwide client bases independently.

The primary obstacle to this approach is rarely technology; it is internal hesitation. Professionals often refrain from publishing independent work out of fear of public judgment. To this, Song offers a clear perspective: "I hope you are still embarrassed ten years from now." Feeling embarrassed by work created a decade ago is evidence of personal and professional growth. The key requirement when publishing is simply maintaining professional courtesy and adhering to social etiquette.

This demand for authentic, verifiable capability is already altering evaluation systems globally. In U.S. higher education and corporate hiring, traditional take-home essays and written assignments have been rendered unreliable by generative AI. Consequently, institutions are returning to a classic evaluation method: the one-on-one oral exam.

When an interviewer sits across from a candidate and asks them to explain the exact logic behind a specific paragraph or coding choice, applicants who relied on AI-generated copies are quickly exposed. Those who personally created, tested, and published their work demonstrate real mastery. Deep personal understanding has become the primary filter for talent.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea’s labor market adapts to advancing AI agent capabilities, several key developments will indicate how rapidly this structural transition takes place:

  • Corporate HR Restructuring: Watch whether major Korean firms systematically eliminate middle-management layers in favor of flat, project-focused execution teams centered on AI workflows.
  • Mid-Career Re-skilling Programs: Monitor whether corporate transition programs shift from traditional franchise business models toward training older professionals in personal branding, software prompting, and single-operator business management.
  • Growth of Micro-Brands: Track the expansion of independent, single-person content and commerce brands in South Korea that leverage automated software back-ends to operate without traditional corporate overhead.

The takeaway for international observers is clear: routine administrative functions within corporate structures are being automated. Future stability belongs to workers who leverage AI tools directly, publish their work publicly, and build personal expertise under their own names.
