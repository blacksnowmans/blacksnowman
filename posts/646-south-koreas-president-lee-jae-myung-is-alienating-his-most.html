Black Snowman

South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung Is Alienating His Most Loyal Supporters—And It's Threatening His Agenda

September 10, 2026

For weeks, followers of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung have watched his social media feeds with a growing sense of unease. At a time when the leader of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea should be projecting stability and focus as he runs the country, Lee has instead been reposting obscure online commentators. In one recent instance, he shared a post from an account demanding aggressive action against rival factions within the progressive camp. To the outside observer, it might look like quirky online engagement; to domestic political watchers, it looks like a self-inflicted wound that is deepening fractures within the governing coalition.

When even the president's most ardent base starts shaking their heads in disbelief, it is worth asking how a digital feedback loop of this kind took root—and how it is actively eating away at South Korea's progressive politics. President Lee, who commands a sprawling legislative majority in the National Assembly, finds himself facing an unexpected crisis: a steady bleeding of core voters who feel alienated not by the opposition, but by the administration's own internal squabbling and perceived compromises on reform.

How a Digital Echo Chamber Alienated the Core Base

The controversy stems largely from how the president interacts with his online supporters. Lee recently reposted a message from a long-time Democratic Party activist complaining about internal party rivals and demanding that party leadership crack down on dissenters. For a president holding the highest office in the land to amplify such partisan sniping sends a clear, and divisive, signal to his own camp. Rather than acting as a unifying figure, the presidency risks looking like a command center for factional warfare.

This approach has alarmed veteran politicians within the Democratic Party. As former lawmaker Chung Cheong-rae recently lamented on radio, chasing after moderate "rabbits" is pointless if the "domestic rabbits"—the core base that provides the party's foundational energy—end up fleeing the fold. That core constituency has historically sustained the liberal bloc's discipline. When the president himself begins irritating those loyalists, the anxiety shifts from internet gossip to a genuine political threat.

The numbers bear out the discomfort. While Lee’s overall approval ratings have hovered near 60% at various points, recent polling reveals a significant erosion among core supporters, with a notable block of voters actively withdrawing their backing. When a leader's most reliable backers begin to feel a sense of fatigue and betrayal, no amount of social media curation can patch over the widening cracks.

Cabinet Nominations and the Collapse of Political Defense Lines

Compounding these political missteps are growing controversies surrounding key government appointments. The nomination of Kim Seung-won for Minister of Justice has met with stiff public resistance, with negative evaluations climbing to nearly 47%. In the past, the Democratic Party possessed a well-oiled apparatus capable of defending ministerial nominees from opposition attacks. Today, those defense mechanisms appear to have evaporated, replaced by messy, public spats with political rivals.

When a cabinet nominee becomes a constant source of friction rather than a symbol of competence, it drains the administration's political capital. The public is left wondering whether these appointments serve the broader national interest or merely satisfy factional demands within the ruling party. As a result, the administration's moral high ground on issues like judicial reform—a cornerstone of the progressive platform—begins to erode.

A similar debate surrounds lawmakers holding concurrent cabinet posts, such as Rep. Yong Hye-in's nomination to lead the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family. While defending dual roles has historical precedent in South Korea, the lack of smooth political communication turns every administrative transition into an avoidable ideological battle. Appointments are, at their core, an exercise in persuasion. Without a clear narrative, even qualified candidates look like products of an insular political machine.

The Empty Promise of Reform Amid a 180-Seat Majority

At the heart of the disillusionment lies a deeper frustration with the pace and direction of reform. When South Korean voters handed the progressive bloc a commanding majority of roughly 180 seats in the National Assembly, they did not do so to watch politicians bicker over internal party dynamics or social media posts. They expected a fundamental overhaul of entrenched systems—most notably the sprawling powers of the prosecution service.

Yet, voters who demand a complete separation of investigative and indicting powers often find the administration hesitating at the crucial moment. To the core base, any sign of compromise with powerful state institutions looks less like pragmatism and more like a betrayal of reformist ideals. Influential liberal commentators have increasingly warned that if the government loses its reforming zeal, it risks handing future elections back to conservative opponents.

The danger for the administration is not just a dip in public opinion polls; it is the demobilization of the very people who carried it to power. True reform requires taking on entrenched interests, not getting bogged down in digital squabbles or defensive posturing.

What to Watch Next

As President Lee weighs his next moves, several key indicators will show whether the administration can right the ship:

  • Cabinet Confirmation Hearings: Watch how aggressively the ruling party manages upcoming ministerial confirmations and whether it can rebuild a disciplined defense strategy in the legislature.
  • Base Outreach vs. Digital Distractions: Pay attention to whether the president shifts away from reactive social media posting toward substantive policy speeches aimed at reassuring core and moderate voters alike.
  • Prosecutorial Reform Timelines: Look for concrete legislative steps on judicial reform that address the core base's demand for uncompromised institutional change.

South Korea's progressive movement has always relied on a delicate coalition of core loyalists and reform-minded citizens. Whether President Lee chooses to use his remaining political capital to heal those internal divisions or let them deepen will determine not only the success of his term, but the future of the liberal camp.
