How the Butterfly Stroke Was Born Out of Pure Stubbornness

October 07, 2026

Anyone who has ever dragged themselves to the local public pool to learn how to swim knows the specific, burning humiliation of the butterfly stroke. While freestyle offers speed, backstroke lets you breathe comfortably, and breaststroke feels like a dignified way to stay alive, the butterfly is a different beast entirely. It looks like a frantic, desperate collision between a drowning butterfly and a confused dolphin. It defies human biomechanics, drains your energy in seconds, and breaks the spirit of countless beginners.

If you try to explain its existence through the lens of evolution, it makes no sense. Charles Darwin taught us that only traits adapted for survival survive. Yet, the butterfly is wildly inefficient, exhausting, and completely unnecessary for basic human aquatic survival. To understand how such an agonizing monstrosity crawled out of the swimming pool and into the official Olympic program, we have to look past simple biology and examine how human beings stumble into progress: mostly by breaking the rules and refusing to accept the way things have always been done.

The Ancient Trap of the Breaststroke

To understand the absurdity of the butterfly, you first have to look at its parent: the breaststroke. In a traditional breaststroke, the swimmer pulls water with their hands gathered in front of the chest, sweeps them outward, and recovers them entirely underwater. The legs tuck toward the hips and push in a frog-like circular kick. Because the chest remains relatively parallel to the surface of the water, it earned the name "breaststroke."

This is arguably the oldest swimming form in human history. Neolithic cave paintings show figures executing breaststroke-like motions. Ancient Greeks and Romans used similar methods to cross rivers, and the world's first printed swimming manual, published in 15th-century Europe, focused heavily on the breaststroke as a method for preventing drownings. It was the ultimate instinctive, survival-based movement.

The problem arose when swimming shifted from a survival skill into a competitive sport. When the breaststroke was first added to the modern Olympic program in 1904, the rules were remarkably vague: push both hands forward simultaneously, keep your shoulders parallel to the surface, and touch the wall with both hands at the turn. Everything else was left up to custom.

For the next thirty years, swimmers adhered strictly to the unwritten law that everything had to happen underwater. Even as water resistance proved far more punishing than air resistance, athletes kept their arms submerged during the recovery phase simply because that was how it had always been done. The shackles of tradition bound their muscles tighter than any fatigue ever could.

The Brooklyn Teenager Who Flung His Arms Out of the Water

Major breakthroughs rarely happen in sterile laboratories; they usually begin with a rebellious teenager messing around. In 1933, at a beach in Brooklyn, a high school student named Henry Myers was practicing his breaststroke. During the recovery phase, Myers casually decided to fling both arms completely out of the water to bring them forward faster. It was a common-sense physical choice for anyone trying to bypass underwater drag—especially since Myers was primarily a freestyle swimmer, a stroke where arms naturally recover above the surface.

When he entered a local amateur race and tried out his bizarre arm-flinging technique, the results stunned everyone. He touched the wall three meters ahead of the local favorite in a fifty-yard stretch. Judges stared at the rulebook in sheer confusion. Eventually, they gave his time a pass by interpreting the rule requiring hands to be pushed "forward" as loosely as possible. They assumed this mutation would burn out too quickly to ever survive in elite 100-meter or 200-meter races.

They were almost right. At the 1936 Berlin Olympics, American breaststroke champion John Higgins decided to test a hybrid approach, using the above-water arm recovery to rocket out ahead of the pack. The crowd watched in awe at the commotion, but by the second lap, Higgins’ energy was completely depleted. He limped home in fourth place, behind Japanese and German competitors.

The global press had a field day. The New York Times mocked the stroke as a failed stunt, and British newspapers predicted the bizarre mutation would vanish from the sport entirely. But the hostile media coverage had an ironic side effect: Nazi Germany’s meticulous propaganda filming captured the event in stunning detail, broadcasting the "butterfly style" to athletes around the world. With twelve years until the next Olympics due to World War II, swimmers everywhere began quietly trying it out in secret.

Fixing the Lower Half: The Birth of the Dolphin Kick

While the upper body had broken free of the water, the lower body remained chained to the past. In the early 1930s, University of Iowa swimming coach David Armbruster noticed a glaring disconnect. Swimmers were throwing their arms through the air above water, but their legs were still executing the traditional, rigid frog kick. The upper body was trying to fly while the lower body was dragging an anchor.

Armbruster noticed his swimmer, Jack Sieg, experimenting with a tight, undulating leg motion that mimicked a fish tail. Armbruster introduced underwater photography to calculate the exact ratio between arm strokes and leg kicks. In 1935, he officially introduced the "Dolphin Butterfly Breaststroke"—a formula of one arm recovery paired with two rapid dolphin kicks.

This technical evolution also served as a physical salvation. Jiro Nagasawa, a top-tier medal hopeful in the traditional breaststroke, developed severe knee arthritis right before the Olympics. Sticking to the brutal angles of the traditional frog kick would have ended his career at age twenty. To spare his joints, Nagasawa locked his legs together and began undulating them up and down, just like a dolphin. Combined with the above-water arm recovery, this accidental adaptation unlocked a massive surge of explosive propulsion.

The Swimming Federation Surrenders to Evolution

Naturally, the swimming establishment fought back. The International Swimming Federation viewed the hybrid stroke as an abomination that threatened the historical sanctity of the breaststroke. Behind the European-dominated leadership's complaints lay a clear political motive: trying to check the surging dominance of American athletes who were rapidly modernizing the sport.

The federation enacted rigid rules forcing swimmers to keep their legs apart during the kick, trying every bureaucratic trick in the book to suppress Armbruster’s innovations. But athletes had already evolved. At the 1952 Helsinki Olympics, traditional breaststroke had effectively vanished. Every single finalist in the 200-meter event swam using some variation of the butterfly style, with Australia’s John Davies taking gold and shattering the world record.

Faced with reality, the federation finally surrendered. In 1953, they officially recognized the butterfly as a distinct, independent swimming stroke, drawing a permanent line between it and the breaststroke.

The story of the butterfly stroke offers a timeless lesson. No matter how aggressively a conservative establishment tries to defend outdated rules, human ingenuity and the relentless pursuit of efficiency will always carve a new path. Whether in sports, technology, or society, those who fear change inevitably lose to the innovators who dare to break the mold. The next time you watch someone flailing across the pool in a desperate battle with the butterfly stroke, remember that you are watching the triumph of human stubbornness over the limits of nature.
