The Illusion of the "Structural Majority": Why South Korea’s Lee Jae-myung Administration Is Stumbling

November 05, 2026

When President Lee Jae-myung assumed office following the unprecedented constitutional crisis that saw the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the incoming administration appeared to hold all the political cards. Riding a wave of civic mobilization against an attempted insurrection, Lee entered the presidential office with soaring approval ratings, further buoyed by an unexpected boom in the stock market. Yet, barely past the honeymoon phase, warning lights are flashing across the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. The administration finds itself ensnared in bitter internal friction, watching its once-impregnable lead in public opinion polls shrink to a statistical tie.

The root of this vulnerability does not lie in aggressive governance, but in a profound political and economic miscalculation. Rather than consolidating the coalition that carried him through political persecution, President Lee has embraced what his strategists call a "structural majority"—an effort to woo conservative and centrist voters by shelving fundamental reforms. By treating his 49.42% election victory as a mandate for ideological dilution rather than decisive socioeconomic restructuring, the administration risks alienating the core supporters who put it in power while failing to fix the structural economic rot that has plagued working-class Koreans for more than two decades.

The Fallacy of the 49.4% Mandate and the "Structural Majority"

President Lee has repeatedly defended his centrist tilt by arguing that he cannot simply govern as a factional champion. "If anyone accuses me of betrayal because I was elected on the back of one side's support, I never promised to be the president of only one side," Lee has argued, insisting that a modern democracy requires building a community for everyone. In internal party strategy sessions, his rationale is even blunter: if the Democratic Party relies solely on its own camp, it will inevitably remain a "structural minority." To secure long-term power, the argument goes, the government must dilute its partisan identity and move to the center-right to capture moderates and rational conservatives.

To many within the party’s grassroots base, this rationale sounds dangerously familiar. Critics inside the progressive camp view it as a modern resurrection of the infamous 1970s "sakura" politics, named after opposition leader Yu Jin-san, whose compromises with authoritarian elites were dressed up as pragmatism while demoralizing pro-democracy activists. Today, the administration is accused of adopting the same posture: dismissing vocal party activists and clear-cut reformists as "radicals," while framing concession to entrenched corporate and bureaucratic interests as "moderate expansion."

The people who stood in the freezing streets to defend Lee Jae-myung against aggressive political prosecution during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure were not cautious centrists calculating political odds. They were the committed progressive base. Discarding that base in the hope of winning over conservative swing voters is the political equivalent of dismantling the foundation of a house to add another story to the roof.

This strategy rests on a skewed reading of the election data. In the March 2022 presidential contest, Lee lost to Mr. Yoon by a razor-thin margin of 0.74 percentage points (47.83% to 48.56%). When he finally won the presidency in 2025—after Mr. Yoon was removed from office on April 3 following his declaration of emergency decree—Lee secured 49.42% of the vote. Despite running against a conservative establishment completely discredited by insurrection charges, his vote share rose by only 1.59 percentage points, falling short of a clean 50% majority. The combined vote of conservative candidates Kim Moon-soo and Lee Jun-seok reached 49.49%.

Shaken by this narrow margin, the presidential office concluded that progressive backing alone was insufficient to govern or win reelection. Yet in South Korea’s multiparty dynamic, a progressive candidate rarely crosses the 50% threshold. Even former President Moon Jae-in won the presidency in 2017 with just 41% of the vote amid the massive Candlelight Protests. By diagnosing his 49.42% share as a rejection of bold reform rather than a symptom of polarization, Lee has adopted what political scientists call a "lexicographic preference" strategy: prioritizing the avoidance of conservative backlash above all else, which fatally erodes his bargaining leverage and leaves his flank exposed to disillusionment.

The Illusion of the KOSPI Semiconductor Boom

The administration’s strategic drift was initially masked by pure economic luck. In the second quarter of 2025, a global explosion in demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure triggered a massive windfall for South Korea’s semiconductor exports. The benchmark KOSPI stock index surged, and President Lee’s approval ratings soared into the 60% to 70% range.

Flushed with market optimism, the presidential office began framing Lee as a "21st-century Park Chung-hee," invoking the technocratic, state-led growth model of South Korea's mid-century modernization drive. Policymakers operated under the assumption that rising equity prices would generate a broad "wealth effect," automatically convincing middle-class and moderate voters of the government's economic competence.

In reality, the rally had very little to do with domestic policy. It was an external liquidity wave driven by the global tech cycle. While stock market participants profited, the windfall failed to trickle down to ordinary households. Shielded by the temporary euphoria of the stock market, the administration postponed urgent legislative battles over judicial accountability, corporate governance, and economic redistribution. When external market tailwinds inevitably cooled, the administration was left without meaningful policy achievements, leaving everyday citizens exposed to high interest rates, persistent inflation, and stagnant household earnings.

Two Decades of Disillusionment: The Plight of the 94%

The fundamental reason why South Korea’s progressive camp struggles to build an enduring political majority has nothing to do with voter fatigue over "radical" reforms. Rather, it is the bitter realization among low- and middle-income families that successive Democratic Party administrations—from Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun to Moon Jae-in—failed to alter their economic trajectory in any meaningful way.

Economic indicators show a continuous four-decade deceleration in growth, paired with an even steeper collapse in domestic consumption:

  • 1982–1992: Average annual real GDP growth stood at 10.3%.
  • 1993–1997: Growth slowed to an average of 8.1%.
  • 1998–2007: Under the Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun administrations, growth dropped to 3.7%.
  • 2008–2016: Under conservative presidents Lee Myung-bak and Park Geun-hye, growth slipped further to 2.3%.
  • 2017–2025: Spanning the Moon Jae-in administration and the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, growth remained stalled at 2.3%.

Worse, real per capita disposable income growth has hovered around 2% since the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, while real wage growth has stalled below that mark. Corporate profits have accumulated, but household balance sheets have deteriorated. Comparing income brackets across recent administrations yields uncomfortable truths: the top 0.1% saw faster annual income growth under the progressive Moon administration than under the conservative Park administration, while lower-income deciles saw better or identical gains under the conservatives.

Data from the National Tax Service covering South Korea’s 26.36 million income earners in 2024 (roughly half the national population of 51 million) illustrates the staggering scale of this polarization. While the top 0.1% takes home roughly 90 million won (about $67,000) per month after taxes, the threshold to enter the top 7% drops to under 7 million won (about $5,200) per month. In other words, 94% of working South Koreans earn less than 7 million won a month.

At the center of the distribution, conditions are bleak. The median monthly income sits at just 2.18 million won (about $1,600). The bottom 40% earns less than 1.77 million won (about $1,300), falling below the statutory minimum wage in real purchasing terms. Meanwhile, South Korea's self-employed small-business owners have seen their real average monthly income collapse to 1.44 million won (about $1,070) in 2025, with roughly 80% earning less than the minimum wage. Telling this 94% that the administration must maintain a market-friendly, technocratic posture sounds less like pragmatic moderation and more like economic abandonment.

A Broken Tax System and the Price of Timidity

If the Lee administration wishes to build a genuine, sustainable majority, the battlefield is the tax code. On paper, South Korea appears to have a progressive fiscal structure, with a top marginal income tax rate of 49.5%—comparable to social-democratic systems like the Netherlands. In practice, the system collects remarkably little revenue and accomplishes virtually no redistribution.

South Korea’s personal income tax revenue as a share of GDP is roughly half that of the Netherlands. When measured by the reduction in income inequality achieved through fiscal transfers, the Dutch system improves distribution by 15 percentage points; South Korea manages just 7.2 percentage points, ranking near the bottom of the OECD alongside developing economies in Latin America.

The culprit is an opaque web of exemptions and deductions. Out of 1,131 trillion won (about $840 billion) in total declared income in 2024, approximately 410 trillion won (about $300 billion)—or nearly 40%—vanished through tax deductions. These write-offs deliver an aggregate tax benefit of over 100 trillion won (about $75 billion), disproportionately captured by the very wealthy. High earners in the top 0.1% receive an average of 113 million won (about $84,000) per person in annual deductions.

Closing these regressive deduction loopholes and introducing redistributive mechanisms—such as a nationwide land-holding tax—would create the fiscal room needed to raise South Korea's social transfer spending to OECD averages. That shift would provide an estimated 3.1 million won (about $2,300) per person annually, translating to roughly 12 million won (about $9,000) a year—or 1 million won a month—in direct purchasing power for a four-person working-class household. Delivering this tangible economic security is how a progressive government builds a durable majority, not by avoiding political fights with real estate conglomerates and chaebol lobbies.

What to Watch Next

The fallout from the administration's strategic ambiguity is already visible in public opinion polling. In a recent survey by EveryResearch, the Democratic Party's support stood at 40.6%, with the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, surging to 39.5%—a razor-thin margin of just 1.1 percentage points.

Three concrete indicators will reveal whether the Lee administration can arrest this decline or whether it will remain trapped in political paralysis:

  • Generational Reversals in the Polls: Watch demographic trends among voters in their 60s. Previously leaning Democratic at 48.4%, support in this cohort has tumbled to 38.5%, while the People Power Party has taken the lead at 44.1%. If this drift continues into older demographics and spreads to younger working-class voters, the ruling party's electoral coalition will fracture.
  • Regional Fall-out in Busan and South Gyeongsang (PK): The administration's wavering over flagship regional promises—particularly the controversial shelving of the state-run Korea Development Bank’s relocation to the southern port city of Busan—has caused support to stagnate in key battlegrounds outside the capital. Continued policy reversals will demonstrate whether "pragmatism" is merely cover for indecision.
  • Fiscal and Tax Reform Legislation: The critical test will be whether President Lee returns to the reformist platform that defined his rise: overhauling regressive income deductions, challenging speculative real estate wealth, and expanding targeted social transfers. If the administration continues to prioritize fiscal conservatism and corporate appeasement over structural redistribution, it risks cementing the very outcome it fears most: a demoralized base, an unimpressed center, and an early exit from power.
