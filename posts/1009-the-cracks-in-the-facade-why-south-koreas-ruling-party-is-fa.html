The Cracks in the Facade: Why South Korea’s Ruling Party is Facing a Political Crisis

October 12, 2026

There is a palpable sense of vertigo in Seoul’s political circles lately. When the current administration took power, it did so with a mandate for systemic reform and high hopes for a new era. Yet, as the latest public opinion polls roll in, the results are not just disappointing—they are alarming. For a government that rose to power on the promise of representing the people, the plummeting approval ratings are a stinging indictment of their first few years in office.

The most shocking metric is the collapse of support among the liberal base. In just one week, approval among this core group dropped by 14.0 percentage points, falling from 73.5% to 59.5%. For any political party, such a swift departure of their most loyal supporters is a flashing red light. But what is truly concerning is not just the numbers, but the sense of arrogance and disconnect from reality that seems to have settled over the halls of power.

The ‘Microphone Trap’: Kim Min-seok and the Cost of Excessive Noise

At the center of this turmoil is Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the current ruling party. Kim has recently signaled that he is considering a strategic pivot and a push for generational change, but the motivation is clearly defensive. His party is bleeding support, and the internal consensus is that the damage is largely self-inflicted.

The DPK has been plagued by a constant stream of aggressive messaging from its leader. Kim’s frequent social media outbursts and public targeting of party reformists—such as Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae and lawmaker Kim Yong-min—have turned the party’s internal dynamics into a circular firing squad. When a party leader uses their platform to berate their own allies, it demoralizes the base and confuses the electorate.

The irony is not lost on political observers. The party that campaigned on unity is now forced to admit that its leader has been talking too much. Reports suggest that the party leadership has had to issue "recommendations" to Kim to scale back his public statements. It is a classic case of political hubris: believing that every thought deserves to be broadcast, only to find that the noise has drowned out the party’s actual policy agenda. They have essentially engaged in a "media diet," but one has to wonder if simply turning off the microphone is enough to heal the resentment built up by months of petty internal squabbles.

The Golf Controversy and the President’s Social Media Habit

The crisis is not confined to the party leadership; it reaches all the way to the top. President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating has also dipped to 39.2%, while his party’s support has fallen to 39.9%. Meanwhile, the People Power Party, South Korea’s main conservative opposition, has seen a slight rebound to 35.4%, benefiting from the ruling bloc’s stumbles.

Adding fuel to the fire is a controversy involving President Lee’s choice of leisure activity during a national crisis. On the weekend that a commercial building in Daegu collapsed, injuring 38 people, it was revealed that President Lee had been at the Taereung Golf Course. While the administration claims the President was briefed and remained in control, the optics have been disastrous.

The opposition has pounced on this, labeling it "dereliction of duty." The situation is particularly awkward given the DPK’s own history; during the term of the disgraced former leader, now-imprisoned convict Yoon Seok-yeol (referred to as "Mr. Yoon" due to his removal from office), the DPK was relentless in criticizing his weekend golf trips. By engaging in the very behavior they once condemned as "emperor golf," the current administration has left itself wide open to charges of hypocrisy.

Furthermore, President Lee’s tendency to use his personal social media to engage in "keyboard warrior" style debates with critics is fueling concerns about his temperament. A President’s communication should be a bridge to the public, not an emotional outlet for settling scores. When the head of state is more focused on defending his record on Twitter or Facebook than on managing the country, the public loses the sense of stability they expect from their leader.

The Danger of Governing by Lecture

The core issue facing this administration is a shift from empathy to condescension. There is a fine line between "explaining" a policy and "lecturing" the public. Too often, President Lee and his associates respond to criticism with an attitude that suggests the public simply "misunderstood" their brilliant plans. This dismissive tone—the idea that the leader knows best and the voters just need to be educated—is a fast track to political irrelevance.

Democracy functions on the assumption that the leader serves the people, not the other way around. When a government begins to treat its citizens as students who need to be disciplined rather than masters who need to be satisfied, the relationship breaks down. You cannot bully a public into liking you; you can only earn their trust through humility and tangible results.

What to Watch Next:

  • The "Media Diet" Success: Will the DPK actually stay silent, or will the impulse to control the narrative lead to more outbursts?
  • Cabinet Reshuffle Rumors: There is growing pressure to overhaul the administration's foreign policy and security teams. If the President resists, expect the disapproval numbers to harden.
  • Policy Delivery: Can the administration pivot from fighting social media wars to addressing the economic pain points of the average Korean?

The next few months will be a test of character for President Lee. He must decide if he wants to be a leader who fights to be "right," or one who fights to lead effectively. Public sentiment is like the sea—it can carry a ship to its destination, but it can also capsize it in an instant. The current administration is dangerously close to the latter.
