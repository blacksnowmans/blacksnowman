Beyond the Screen: How South Korea Is Betting Big on Physical AI and Autonomous Robotics

November 05, 2026

Imagine a four-wheeled robot negotiating a steep, uneven slope, adjusting each wheel independently so its top platform remains perfectly level. It is not being steered by a human operator with a remote control; it is constantly reading the surface beneath it, adjusting its internal posture, and deciding how to navigate obstacles on its own. This is "Physical AI"—the transition of artificial intelligence from cloud-based text generators and chat interfaces into physical bodies capable of interacting directly with the real world.

While global attention has focused heavily on software tools like ChatGPT, South Korea’s industrial and political leaders are pivoting toward the physical hardware where AI meets real-world labor. From massive regional development projects in the coastal south to lightweight vision models built by video game giants, a distinct Korean blueprint for Physical AI is taking shape. This shift is not merely about making machines smarter; it represents a fundamental overhaul of local manufacturing, regional infrastructure, and public policy under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung.

From Server Racks to Steerable Wheels: The Rise of Physical AI

For the past several years, the central debate around artificial intelligence has focused on digital processing: parameters, token counts, and server rack capacities. Physical AI flips this focus by requiring algorithms to understand mechanics, spatial geometry, and gravity. Rather than processing text inside a data center, these systems ingest real-time spatial data to move physical arms, steer chassis, and perform manual tasks alongside humans.

A prime example of this movement is Hyundai Motor Group’s Mobile Eccentric Droid, known as MobED. MobED features an eccentric wheel system where four independent wheels adjust vertically to maintain a flat platform regardless of ground slope. Designed for applications ranging from autonomous logistics and facility security to mobility assistance for individuals with disabilities, MobED exemplifies how hardware design and intelligent software control must merge to overcome real-world physical limits.

Simultaneously, software developers are adapting their architectures to run on these physical frames. Gaming giant NCSOFT—traditionally known for developing massively multiplayer online games—recently unveiled "VACO Vision 3B" (배키 비전 3B), an ultra-lightweight spatial vision model built by its NC AI division. Achieving top-tier efficiency benchmarks, the model provides visual recognition capabilities for compact delivery robots and autonomous drones without requiring heavy, power-hungry computing hardware onboard. By repurposing rendering and spatial simulation techniques honed in game design, South Korean tech firms are providing the "eyes" and "brains" needed for low-power physical devices.

Regional Resurgence: Jeonbuk’s $950 Million Gambit in Saemangeum

The rise of Physical AI is also altering South Korea’s domestic economic planning. The local government of Jeonbuk Special Self-Governing Province, located in the country’s southwestern region, has launched a massive 1.3 trillion KRW (approximately $950 million) initiative aimed at transforming the province into South Korea's national hub for Physical AI and robotics.

Led by Governor Lee Won-taek, Jeonbuk's provincial government is anchoring this vision to Saemangeum—a massive coastal reclamation area historically known for large-scale infrastructure projects. The initiative features a proposed 500 billion KRW (about $370 million) Autonomous Articulated/Walking Robot (AAR) cluster spanning the neighboring cities of Iksan and Gunsan. Rather than attempting to compete directly with Seoul’s software startup scene, Jeonbuk is positioning itself as a real-world testing ground where heavy robotic machinery, agricultural automation, and autonomous transport can be certified and scaled.

Local academic institutions are aligning with this economic shift. Woosuk University, based in Jeonbuk, has launched specialized training programs combining Physical AI with hydrogen fuel cell technology to train engineers locally. This localized industrial strategy matches the overarching policy framework of the Lee Jae-myung administration, which emphasizes balanced regional growth (often framed around the concept of Eok-gang-bu-yak, or supporting weaker regions to balance concentrated power). For rural provinces facing youth out-migration, physical robotics offers a potential path toward industrial renewal without relying solely on traditional smoke-stack manufacturing.

Corporate Alliances, Synthetic Data, and Niche Robotics

Beyond regional hubs, South Korea's major telecommunications and software conglomerates are competing to build the underlying data infrastructure required for physical machines.

SK Telecom, the country’s top mobile carrier, together with its dedicated AI outfit SKAI, has set a major strategic milestone for 2027 to capture the physical automation market. A core element of their strategy involves generating high-fidelity "synthetic data." Because collecting physical sensor data across millions of real-world scenarios is prohibitively slow and expensive, SKAI is working alongside South Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT to generate synthetic datasets—simulated virtual environments where AI models can practice millions of physical tasks in seconds. This synthetic training ground is paired with strategic investments, including participation in global funding deals like Anthropic's IPO trajectory.

In parallel, specialized domestic robot makers are carving out high-value niches:

  • Clobot: The service robot specialist recently secured a 7.3 billion KRW (approx. $5.4 million) government research contract to develop intelligence architectures for humanoid robots, while recruiting top technical talent from Naver Labs to sharpen its spatial mapping capabilities.
  • Curexo: A specialized medical device company applying Physical AI to surgical robotics, improving real-time precision during complex procedures to reduce human error in operating rooms.
  • SLZ and Global Partners: Local spatial-AI startup SLZ is partnering with global automation firm Festo and Nvidia to build "digital twins"—virtual replicas of industrial plants that simulate physical machine interactions before deploying software to actual factory floors.

Robot World 2026 and the Societal Blueprint Ahead

The rapid momentum of the sector was on full display at Robot World 2026, South Korea’s flagship robotics industry expo. The event featured everything from humanoid units performing fluid movements to rugged autonomous drones designed for harsh environments. Yet beneath the technological showcase, the event underscored emerging social and policy challenges that South Korea must address.

Unlike the regulatory standstills seen during Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's term, the Lee Jae-myung administration has aggressively expanded regulatory sandboxes and direct state R&D investments to fast-track commercial deployment. However, this policy push brings significant questions regarding labor market disruption and public data rights:

  • Labor Market Transitions: As Physical AI moves into logistics, medical services, and basic labor, policymakers face growing pressure to create retraining frameworks for workers whose roles are automated.
  • Public Infrastructure vs. Corporate Dominance: Current policy debates center on ensuring that government-funded synthetic datasets and public testing grounds remain accessible to startups and SMEs, rather than being monopolized by top chaebols (conglomerates).
  • Data Dividends: Discussions are underway regarding how the wealth generated by automated public infrastructure might be recirculated into public safety nets or digital dividend systems.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves to cement its position in the global physical robotics supply chain, several key indicators will reveal whether these plans succeed:

  • Legislative Appropriations for Saemangeum: Watch for upcoming National Assembly budget hearings regarding the 1.3 trillion KRW ($950 million) allocation for Jeonbuk's AAR cluster and testing grounds.
  • Commercial Deployment of Lightweight Models: Track whether vision models like NCSOFT's VACO Vision 3B achieve commercial adoption among independent domestic logistics and drone startups over the next 12 months.
  • Synthetic Data Governance: Monitor joint guidelines from the Ministry of Science and ICT and SK Telecom regarding data privacy, public domain access, and synthetic dataset standards.
