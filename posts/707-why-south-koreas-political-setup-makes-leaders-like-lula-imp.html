Why South Korea's Political Setup Makes Leaders Like Lula Impossible for the Next Decade

October 05, 2026

Picture a teenager working on a factory floor in Gyeonggi Province, hands calloused from long hours, before a sudden industrial accident on a heavy press machine leaves him permanently disabled. For most, that would mark the end of any conventional future. Yet for Lee Jae-myung—now the leader of the Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal opposition—that grueling start was just the beginning of an improbable climb. Born the son of slash-and-burn farmers in a remote mountain village in Andong, Lee taught himself through high school equivalency exams, earned a law degree, became a local mayor, and eventually rose to become a powerhouse presidential contender.

This kind of trajectory reads like high drama, forged in the crucible of South Korea's rapid, painful modernization. But it is precisely this raw background that sits at the center of an increasingly bitter debate in Seoul's political circles. Some critics argue that because of the deep polarization surrounding figures like Lee, South Korea will not see another leader of his kind—someone akin to Brazil's Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva—for at least the next ten years. Comparing a South Korean opposition leader to Brazil's legendary working-class president lays bare the hidden fractures, class anxieties, and fierce tribalism shaping modern South Korean politics.

From Mountain Shanties to Factory Floors: The Roots of the Underdog Narrative

To understand the fierce devotion Lee inspires, and the equally intense alarm he triggers, you have to look at his beginnings. Born into stark, grinding poverty in North Gyeongsang Province, Lee experienced hunger as a daily reality. Sent to work as a young boy in the industrial complexes of Seongnam, he endured the brutal realities of South Korea's unregulated manufacturing boom.

The accident that crushed his arm in a press machine—leaving him with a lifelong disability that exempted him from military service—remains both a personal scar and a political badge of honor. Pulling himself up to pass the bar exam and graduate from Chung-Ang University's law department is an undeniable human victory. For an outsider who never walked the elite corridors of Seoul National University or the prestigious judiciary tracks to smash his way into the center of national politics was a seismic shock to the country's establishment.

To his supporters, this background makes him far more than a typical politician; he is a living proxy for their own unfulfilled struggles. The country's political arena has long been dominated by silver-spoon elites who speak a different language and inhabit a different reality. Lee's supporters see his blunt, unvarnished style as proof that he truly understands the bitterness and humiliation of life at the bottom.

Thousands of miles away, the life of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva follows a strikingly similar arc. Born into crushing poverty in rural Pernambuco, northeastern Brazil, Lula worked as a shoeshine boy and street vendor before finding work as a metalworker near São Paulo. He emerged as a defiant labor leader, organizing massive strikes against Brazil's military dictatorship.

When Lula co-founded the Workers' Party and eventually won the presidency in 2002—becoming Brazil's first working-class leader—the world watched Latin America shift. Programs like Bolsa Família lifted millions out of absolute poverty, turning Lula into a folk hero with approval ratings soaring above 80 percent by the end of his second term. Yet that glory was followed by a devastating fall: corruption charges, imprisonment, and a seemingly finished political career, only for the Supreme Court to annul the convictions and pave the way for his stunning 2022 comeback.

The Parallel Lives of Outliers and the Establishment's Fear

The most powerful thread connecting Lee and Lula is the archetype of the self-made outsider. In political systems traditionally monopolized by polished, pedigree-driven elites, these figures are anomalies. Their rough edges, unfiltered speech, and deep connection to the working class are read by their bases as authentic political instinct, entirely untamed by institutional norms.

Yet that same outsider status invites fierce, unyielding resistance. To the political establishment, figures like Lee and Lula are erratic wild cards who threaten established rules and unspoken compacts. Lula's imprisonment and Lee's ongoing legal battles are viewed by their followers as systemic retaliation by entrenched power structures terrified of losing control.

This underdog narrative functions as a double-edged sword. For supporters, it creates an unbreakable bond of shared suffering. For detractors, it serves as the breeding ground for dangerous populism and social division. Their life stories function as living proof of the deep class fractures running through both South Korean and Brazilian society.

Naturally, this dynamic breeds hyper-polarized politics. Lee's political base operates almost like a tight-knit faith community, viewing him as a savior sent to strike back at an indifferent establishment. His signature direct communication style offers catharsis to a public exhausted by economic stagnation and widening inequality.

Conversely, opponents look at the same phenomenon with outright alarm. Critics argue that Lee's legal risks, combined with a fiercely loyal fandom that marginalizes internal dissent, erode procedural democracy and deepen tribal warfare. Politics ceases to be an art of compromise and turns into total war: you are either entirely with us, or you are an existential enemy.

🔥 Lula Returned, But Why Can't South Korea Embrace Its Own?

The observation that "leaders like Lula won't emerge in South Korea for another decade because of Lee Jae-myung" captures the secret wish of the conservative establishment. Lula survived imprisonment to reclaim the presidency by sheer force of popular will. But in South Korea's rigid political topography, could an outsider with such a disruptive personal history survive and rise again after Lee?

South Korea's invisible walls of privilege are far more tightly locked and skillfully defended than Brazil's. While the establishment may desperately wish for Lee's permanent exit, his trajectory serves as an uncomfortable reminder of why a raw, battle-scarred leader from the working class remains so persistently demanded by a large segment of the public.

The Cost of Endless Political Warfare

The constant cycle of investigations, court hearings, and public polarization surrounding Lee has dragged South Korea's political system into a state of chronic exhaustion. The National Assembly often functions less as a house of legislation and more as an extension of a courtroom where rival camps trade blows.

As this zero-sum game drags on, public cynicism deepens. The pervasive sentiment that "they're all the same anyway" suffocates the seeds of genuine reform. Lula's dramatic political resurrection in Brazil was ultimately supported by a society resilient and flexible enough to allow for second acts. South Korea's current political climate, by contrast, feels trapped in a permanent mud fight.

Whether South Korea manages to move past the towering figure of Lee Jae-myung or remains permanently defined by it, the future of the nation's political evolution looks fraught. When political survival becomes dependent on weaponized judicial processes and partisan retaliation, future generations of innovative, establishment-defying leaders will find the path completely blocked. The arena becomes an exclusive country club for elite politicians nurtured inside the safe greenhouses of the major parties.

In Brazil, Lula was able to expand social safety nets and challenge old hierarchies because a broader public consensus eventually backed him. South Korea, however, remains dominated by an interlocking web of elite institutions—spanning the legal profession, major corporate conglomerates, mainstream media, and political parties—that fiercely protect their turf.

The immense hostility directed at a former child factory worker rising to lead a major political party reveals just how deeply the establishment fears losing its monopoly. The relentless pushback against Lee is not merely about one man; it functions as a standing warning to anyone from the bottom who dares to aim too high.

What to Watch Next

  • The Legal Timeline: Keep an eye on the progression of Lee's ongoing court rulings, which remain the single most volatile variable in South Korea's electoral calendar.
  • Intra-Party Dynamics: Watch how the Democratic Party handles internal dissent and whether alternative moderate voices can break through the dominant factional loyalty.
  • Policy Shifts on Inequality: Observe whether either major party introduces substantive economic reforms aimed at the structural gig-economy and working-class instability that fueled Lee's rise in the first place.
