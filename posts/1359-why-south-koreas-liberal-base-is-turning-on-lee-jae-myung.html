Why South Korea's Liberal Base Is Turning on Lee Jae-myung

October 20, 2026

When public approval ratings begin to slide in South Korean politics, the standard playbook in the presidential office rarely changes. Aides schedule an intimate press conference, promise softer communication, and blame either relentless attacks from the opposition or general public weariness with contentious policy drives. Inside the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea's liberal head of state, that same reflexive script has been deployed in recent weeks. But behind the closed doors of liberal strategy sessions and across grassroots political forums, a far more alarming diagnosis is taking hold: the administration is hemorrhaging support not because it moved too fast, but because it hesitated.

Public opinion tracking, including surveys conducted by pollster Park Si-young and other research institutions, reveals that the bleeding is not concentrated among swing voters or conservatives who backed the main opposition, the conservative People Power Party. Instead, the sharpest decline is coming from the very core of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea's loyal base. These are voters who took to the streets and mobilized to bring Lee into office with an explicit, non-negotiable mandate: dismantle the unchecked investigative apparatus of the prosecution once and for all. Seeing that pledge stall under the guise of political prudence has sparked a wave of disillusionment that carefully curated media briefings cannot soothe.

The Warning Signs Behind Falling Approval Ratings

To an outside observer, a president's sagging numbers during a heavy reform cycle might seem routine. Western commentary often treats institutional reform as a secondary issue behind macroeconomic headwinds and foreign affairs. In South Korea, however, power agency reform—specifically the overhaul of the state prosecution service—is the gravitational center of modern domestic politics.

For decades, South Korean state prosecutors held a globally unprecedented monopoly over both direct criminal investigations and indictments. The abuses stemming from that concentrated authority culminated in the tumultuous tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, a former prosecutor general who rose to the presidency, wielded prosecutorial power ruthlessly against political opponents, and was ultimately impeached, removed from office, and imprisoned. For the citizens who lived through that crisis, Yoon's downfall was not merely a political transition; it was supposed to mark the definitive end of the "prosecutorial republic."

When President Lee took office backed by a comfortable working majority held by the Democratic Party of Korea in the National Assembly, expectations were unambiguous. The new administration was expected to enact swift, structural, and irreversible legislation stripping prosecutors of direct investigative functions. Yet as weeks turned into months, the clear statutory deadlines outlined in campaign blueprints gave way to talk of pacing, caution, and consensus-building. When poll numbers dipped, the presidential office reacted with conventional political cosmetics—attempting to dilute criticism of an "uncommunicative" leadership style through casual roundtables with reporters. To a base that demanded institutional demolition, these PR maneuvers felt like a trivial answer to a historic crisis.

The Myth of "Reform Fatigue" and the Moderate Trap

Whenever a progressive administration in Seoul hits an impasse on structural reform, conservative media outlets and cautious party insiders inevitably raise the specter of "speeding." The argument goes that the public is simply exhausted by years of legal clashes, judicial appointments, and parliamentary brawls, and that pushing harder alienates moderate voters.

This diagnosis, however, fundamentally misreads what is happening inside the electorate. Reform hardliners and progressive commentators, including outspoken figures such as former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook, have fiercely contested the idea that the base is suffering from fatigue caused by overreach. As Choe bluntly observed in recent public remarks, supporters of prosecutorial reform did not lose personal privileges or material perks; what they are losing is their fundamental trust that justice will ever be served. Their anger is not born of exhaustion from battle, but of utter exasperation at watching an administration armed with a legislative majority pause in deference to entrenched bureaucratic resistance.

In truth, South Korean prosecutorial reform has never traveled at excessive speed. Over consecutive administrations, it has moved in fits and starts, frequently water downed into awkward, half-baked compromises whenever senior prosecutors pushed back. Career prosecutors have spent decades mastering institutional survival, utilizing targeted leaks, internal networks, and sympathetic media outlets to sow public doubt. Treating their institutional resistance as a legitimate call for "speed regulation" traps the governing party in a self-inflicted paralysis. When an administration with full legislative power blinks before an entrenched elite, moderate voters see weakness, while loyalists see betrayal.

Ghosts of the Past: Why the Prosecution Reform Debate Is Existential

To understand the depth of emotion driving this revolt within the liberal camp, one must understand the collective trauma embedded in the Democratic Party's modern history. This is not an academic debate over administrative flowcharts; for South Korean progressives, it is a matter of life and death.

Two decades ago, the late progressive President Roh Moo-hyun attempted an open, democratic dialogue with frontline prosecutors, hoping to win institutional independence through goodwill and mutual respect. That strategy ended in tragedy: after leaving office, Roh was hounded by relentless prosecutorial leaks and investigations, ultimately leading to his death in 2009. Years later, during the administration of Moon Jae-in, ambitious prosecutorial reform bills were once again compromised and delayed out of political caution. That half-finished overhaul created the exact institutional vacuum and political backlash that catapulted Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol to power.

The lesson South Korea's progressive civil society drew from these successive failures was unforgiving: the prosecution service will never reform itself, and any government that offers it an olive branch will eventually be consumed by it. With Yoon now stripped of office and behind bars, supporters view the current political window as a once-in-a-generation opening. To see the Lee administration hesitate now—worrying about bad press or short-term polling dips—strikes many core voters as repeating the exact strategic errors that paved the way for past disasters.

The Legislative Calculus: What to Watch Next

With cosmetic communication drives falling flat, the Lee Jae-myung administration faces a stark choice. Trying to straddle the fence will likely continue to depress approval numbers without winning over conservative skeptics. To halt the slide, the governing party will have to re-anchor itself in concrete legislative action rather than high-minded rhetoric.

Over the coming months, observers of South Korean politics should watch several critical flashpoints to see whether the administration decides to break the impasse:

  • The revision of the Prosecutors' Office Act and Criminal Procedure Code: The primary test of government resolve will be whether the Democratic Party uses its parliamentary dominance to introduce and pass clear revisions abolishing the prosecution's remaining direct investigative powers.
  • The establishment of a pure Indictment Office: Reform advocates demand that the current prosecution agency be formally reorganized into an office responsible strictly for maintaining public indictments and trials, transferring investigative authority entirely to independent police and investigative agencies. Watch whether draft legislation reflecting this separation makes it to the legislative floor.
  • Presidential messaging shifts: Listen closely to how President Lee frames institutional reform in upcoming addresses. If the rhetoric pivots from vague discussions of "national unity" back to the resolute language of "completing unfinished power agency overhauls," it will signal that the administration has recognized the real source of its political bleed.

In South Korean political history, governments rarely survive by retreating from the very mandates that brought them to power. The sovereign public that handed the Democratic Party its mandate does not possess infinite patience. If the Lee administration continues to flinch in the face of bureaucratic friction, its approval ratings may find a new, even lower floor. But if it presses forward to finish the institutional restructuring it promised, the core supporters who built this administration will return to defend it.
