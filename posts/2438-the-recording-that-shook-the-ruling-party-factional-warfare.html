The Recording That Shook the Ruling Party: Factional Warfare in the Lee Jae-myung Era

November 17, 2026

Imagine a quiet room in Namyangju, a city just east of Seoul. It is mid-October, and about 90 minutes are spent in what was supposed to be a candid, private dialogue between a high-ranking politician and local party members. There are no cameras, no official stenographers—just the raw, often messy exchange of political strategy and grievances. For a month, that room remained silent. Then, on November 12, a 45-minute recording of that meeting was leaked to the press, and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) was plunged into a civil war.

The protagonist of this recording is Choi Min-hee, a member of the DPK’s Supreme Council—the party's highest decision-making body. In the leaked audio, she appears to question the political judgment of the President, makes controversial claims about religious influence within the party leadership, and criticizes cabinet ministers as "yes-men." What followed was a whirlwind of ethical investigations, high-level boycotts, and cryptic social media warnings from President Lee Jae-myung himself. For those watching from outside South Korea, this is more than a simple leak; it is a window into the fragile unity of the party currently governing the country and the ruthless internal discipline used to maintain it.

A Private Conversation Becomes a Public Crisis

The controversy began when Sisa Journal, a prominent news magazine, published what they claimed was a "bombshell" recording of Choi Min-hee. The setting was a closed-door meeting on October 16 with party members in Namyangju. While the original meeting lasted an hour and a half, the leaked audio covered only about half that time. This discrepancy has become the primary shield for Choi and her allies, who argue that the recording was "stitched together" or "maliciously edited" to remove the broader context of her remarks.

Choi Min-hee, along with fellow DPK lawmaker Kim Young-hwan and other supporters, maintains that the meeting's true purpose was to discuss how to support President Lee Jae-myung during a difficult political stretch. However, the fragments that reached the public told a different story. They depicted a leader speaking with a level of skepticism—and perhaps a lack of caution—that is rarely tolerated in the highly disciplined environment of the DPK. Within hours of the report, the Blue House (the South Korean presidential office) expressed "strong regret," and Kim Min-seok, the DPK’s Party Leader, took the unprecedented step of ordering an immediate internal probe by the party’s ethical investigation team.

This rapid escalation turned a "slip of the tongue" into a systemic crisis. In the Korean political landscape, the DPK under President Lee Jae-myung has cultivated an image of ironclad unity, especially following the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. To have a member of the Supreme Council caught on tape questioning the party’s direction—and doing so in a way that suggests internal fractures—threatens the very foundation of the current administration’s authority.

Lapses in Memory or Lapses in Loyalty?

The most explosive parts of the recording involve Choi’s comments on the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. According to the transcript, Choi suggested that the President had initially been against the impeachment, preferring a negotiated resignation. She was quoted as saying, "Even for the President, his political judgment isn’t always right," and suggested that "winning over the moderates" was the reason for avoiding a hardline impeachment stance.

These remarks are radioactive because they contradict the official history of the DPK’s rise to power. President Lee Jae-myung’s political identity is inextricably linked to his consistent and successful push for the impeachment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. On November 13, Choi held a press conference to clarify, claiming she had simply confused the names of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol and Han Duck-soo (the former Prime Minister). She argued that her comments about "hand-holding" and "negotiated exits" referred to the Prime Minister’s situation, not the President's historic move against Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

However, the recording contained other, perhaps more damaging, assertions. Choi mentioned receiving tips that influential and controversial religious groups—specifically the Unification Church and Shincheonji—had actively supported Party Leader Kim Min-seok during internal elections. In South Korea, ties to these groups are a political third rail; Shincheonji, in particular, has been a lightning rod for public anger since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. By suggesting that the current Party Leader owed his position to such groups, Choi wasn't just making a mistake; she was questioning the legitimacy of the party’s leadership.

Furthermore, Choi’s critique extended to the President’s cabinet. She reportedly labeled Song Mi-ryung, the Minister of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs, and Chief of Staff In Kwang-yeon as "yes-men." She suggested that the President’s decision to promote vice-ministers to cabinet positions was a sign of a "do-it-all-myself" mentality. While such critiques are common in democratic debate, hearing them from within the President’s own inner circle has provided significant ammunition to the opposition People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party).

The Speed of Discipline and the Boycott in Response

The reaction from the party leadership was swift and severe. Within three days of the leak, Party Leader Kim Min-seok moved the case from a preliminary investigation to the DPK Ethics Tribunal, the body responsible for formal disciplinary actions like suspension or expulsion. This speed is unusual in Korean politics, where parties often take weeks to "verify facts" before acting against their own.

This "fast-track" discipline triggered an immediate backlash from the "Pro-Chung-rae" faction—a group of high-ranking party members allied with Jung Cheong-rae, another influential Supreme Council member. On November 13, in a dramatic show of defiance, several Supreme Council members boycotted the official leadership meeting. Instead, they held a joint press conference on the sidelines, accusing Kim Min-seok of "unfair framing." They argued that while Kim was quick to investigate a colleague for a private conversation, he had ignored the very allegations of religious influence mentioned in that conversation. "The one who should be investigated," they claimed, "is Kim Min-seok himself."

The rift has now spread to the broader party apparatus. The "Gyeonggi Innovation Gathering" and the "National Innovation Gathering"—powerful groups of grassroots activists and pro-reform members—along with a coalition of first-term lawmakers, have released statements demanding the "highest level of discipline" for Choi. They have even called for the resignation of other Supreme Council members who supported her, such as Lee Seong-yoon and Han Min-soo. If these members were to resign, it could trigger a collapse of the current leadership structure, potentially forcing the party into an emergency committee or a premature leadership election.

The President Breaks His Silence: "Arrows from Behind"

As the internal temperature reached a boiling point, President Lee Jae-myung took to social media. On November 14, he posted a message on X (formerly Twitter) praising Lawmaker Chun Joon-ho, his former Chief of Staff. The message was cryptic but pointed:

"As is always the case, the hardest thing to endure is the arrow flying from behind. Let us wisely overcome whatever obstacles lie ahead."

The following day, the President was even more direct, writing that it is "unacceptable for a public official who speaks of reform to use the name of reform to hinder it." While he named no names, the timing was unmistakable. To the party faithful, "the arrow from behind" was a clear reference to Choi Min-hee’s leaked criticisms. The President’s intervention effectively signaled to the party that this was no longer a matter of private opinion, but a matter of loyalty to the administration’s core mission.

This internal friction is not entirely new to the DPK. Critics within the party have pointed out that similar "rebellions" occurred during Moon Jae-in’s tenure as party leader, where members would boycott meetings to signal dissent. However, the current stakes are arguably higher. With the opposition People Power Party watching closely, any sign of a "civil war" within the DPK threatens the administration’s ability to pass its legislative agenda and maintain its public approval ratings, which Choi herself noted had been under pressure.

What to Watch Next

The fate of Choi Min-hee now rests with the DPK Ethics Tribunal. While the political pressure for a harsh sentence is immense, the tribunal is technically an independent body. If they opt for a lighter punishment—or if they find that the "selective editing" of the recording genuinely misrepresented her intent—it could lead to a secondary explosion of anger from the pro-reform factions and the first-term lawmakers who have staked their reputations on her removal.

Keep a close eye on the following developments:

  • The "Emergency Committee" Trigger: If more Supreme Council members resign or are forced out, watch for a shift toward a "Non-Emergency Measure Committee" (비상대책위원회). This would signal a total breakdown of the current party leadership under Kim Min-seok.
  • Cabinet Reshuffling: Following Choi’s "yes-men" comments, the President may feel pressured to prove his independence from his advisors or, conversely, may double down on his current staff to show strength.
  • The "Religious Influence" Investigation: While the focus is currently on Choi, the allegations regarding the Unification Church and Shincheonji's involvement in party elections have been voiced. Whether the party chooses to investigate these claims or bury them will tell us a lot about the internal power dynamics between the Kim Min-seok and Jung Cheong-rae factions.

In the end, this crisis is a reminder that in the high-stakes world of Korean politics, privacy is a luxury and loyalty is the only currency that truly matters. A 45-minute recording has managed to do what the opposition could not: force the ruling party to turn its gaze—and its weapons—inward.
