The Crisis of South Korea’s Judiciary: Impeachment, Politics, and the Search for Neutrality

September 23, 2026

South Korea’s political landscape is currently caught in a feedback loop where the “politicization of the judiciary” and the “judicialization of politics” have become inseparable. The recent momentum within the National Assembly to push for the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae is not merely a tactical political strike; it is a fundamental challenge to the integrity of the South Korean legal system and its constitutional guardrails. As the lines between judicial independence and administrative oversight blur, the country is left asking a difficult question: Where does the sanctuary of the judiciary end, and where does legitimate democratic accountability begin?

The Impeachment Controversy: Judicial Justice or Political Theater?

The controversy centers on allegations that the administrative machinery of the Supreme Court was weaponized to expedite specific, politically sensitive trials. Critics of Chief Justice Cho point to the handling of the election law case involving Lee Jae-myung, leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country’s main liberal opposition). According to these claims, the case was fast-tracked from receipt to the Grand Bench—the full panel of Supreme Court justices—in a mere nine days. Furthermore, allegations suggest that a specific group of judicial researchers reviewed the case in a pre-assignment stage, a move that critics argue undermines the standard, randomized allocation of cases.

In South Korea, where the memory of the “Yang Sung-tae Judicial Scandal”—a massive influence-peddling crisis during the conservative Park Geun-hye administration—is still fresh, these allegations carry significant weight. During that era, the court was accused of monitoring judges and trading rulings for political favors. For many, the current impeachment push feels like a painful sequel. However, the ruling People Power Party (the main conservative party) dismisses the impeachment effort as a vengeful political maneuver by a liberal bloc unhappy with the outcome of recent court rulings.

The core of the issue is procedural legitimacy. While a judge cannot be impeached simply because a verdict is unpopular, the abuse of judicial administrative power—such as manipulating case assignments or timelines—is a legitimate constitutional concern. The challenge for the National Assembly is to prove that the judiciary acted as a political tool rather than an independent arbiter, without themselves becoming the very thing they seek to purge: a political force that uses the constitution as a weapon.

The Bitter Costs of Political Polarization

The current climate has led to a degradation of political discourse. Former Unification Minister Jeong Se-hyun, a veteran observer of North-South relations, has recently highlighted the loss of “political dignity” in the National Assembly. When parties turn their full arsenal against the judiciary, they risk eroding the public’s trust in the basic institutions that stabilize a democracy.

The internal atmosphere within the courts is equally strained. Chief Justice Cho has issued rare public denials of these allegations, yet reports suggest widespread unease among rank-and-file judges who feel the judiciary is becoming a pawn in a larger power struggle. The lesson from the Yang Sung-tae era was that a judiciary that cannot self-regulate will eventually be dismantled by external prosecutors, a process that leaves the entire legal system scarred. If the current impeachment discussions are to have any merit, they must move past partisan shouting and focus on a transparent investigation into whether the court’s administrative, not judicial, functions were compromised.

Diplomatic Sovereignty: Beyond the Washington-Beijing Binary

The obsession with domestic political warfare often comes at the cost of addressing South Korea’s precarious position in international affairs. Former Minister Jeong Se-hyun argues that the country’s diplomatic strategy is currently suffering from a lack of sovereignty. For years, there has been a divide between the “independent” faction and the “pro-US” faction in South Korean security circles. Jeong suggests that the current diplomatic team is too often overly deferential to the United States, failing to leverage the country’s position effectively.

As the potential for a second Trump presidency looms, the need for a pragmatic strategy has never been greater. Instead of blindly following Washington’s lead, South Korea should be prepared to use its own chips—such as proposing the exchange of liaison offices or negotiating nuclear freeze agreements in return for phased sanctions relief. Jeong emphasizes that the goal of an alliance should be mutual interest, not unilateral obedience. Furthermore, he points to a strategic nuance that is often overlooked: North Korea is increasingly wary of China’s territorial and political ambitions. A sophisticated South Korean diplomacy could exploit these frictions to secure regional stability rather than remaining tethered to a rigid, Cold War-era binary of US-China competition.

Central to this humanitarian vision is the resumption of separated family reunions. Once a regular fixture of inter-Korean diplomacy—occurring 16 times between the 2000 summit and the end of the Roh Moo-hyun presidency—these reunions are more than just a social service. They are the bedrock of trust. Prioritizing these humanitarian ties, regardless of the political temperature, remains the most viable path to keeping the possibility of peace alive.

What to Watch Next

As we move forward, the focus should remain on three critical areas:

  • Judicial Reform: Watch for the formation of an independent oversight body or a formal inquiry into the Supreme Court’s case management procedures. The legitimacy of the impeachment motion depends entirely on the evidence of procedural abuse, not the political fallout of the court's rulings.
  • Diplomatic Realignment: Observe whether the foreign policy and security teams begin to pivot toward a more “transactional” strategy ahead of potential shifts in US leadership. The test will be whether the government can publicly signal its own interests rather than merely reacting to the agendas of superpowers.
  • Political Integrity: Monitor the internal dynamics of both the Democratic Party and the People Power Party. If the obsession with the impeachment of the Chief Justice continues to overshadow legislative responsibilities, public fatigue will likely reach a breaking point, potentially forcing a shift in leadership strategies as the next election cycle approaches.

True justice is not found when the scales are tipped by the weight of political power, but when the institution remains balanced enough to hold both the state and the opposition to the same standard. Whether the current path leads to a necessary cleansing of the judiciary or a dangerous expansion of political interference will be defined by the transparency of the process in the coming months.
