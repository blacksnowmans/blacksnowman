Cult Accusations and Political Wordplay: Inside the Smear Scandal Rocking Korea’s Ruling Party

November 06, 2026

For an 80-year-old political veteran who has spent four decades organizing grass-roots support for South Korea’s liberal movement, few things could be more humiliating than being branded a secret cultist overnight. Yet that is precisely what happened during the recent national convention of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s ruling party. In the heat of an internal leadership race, long-time party members suddenly found themselves accused of belonging to Shincheonji—a secretive, widely reviled religious fringe group—and of conspiring against party leadership.

The man at the center of the storm is Lawmaker Kim Min-seok, a prominent senior figure within the DPK. What began as a series of unverified hints dropped on the campaign trail has spiraled into a bitter struggle over political ethics, factional warfare, and party governance. When forced to address the fallout, Kim’s televised response fell far short of a clear retraction: "To our party members, I feel both sorry and grateful," he remarked, adding that he felt "a deep sense of discomfort." To the grass-roots loyalists whose reputations were tarnished, this soft-pedaled rhetoric was less an apology than a calculated attempt to avoid accountability while leaving internal wounds wide open.

The Anatomy of a Non-Apology and Political Mudslinging

To understand why this dispute has provoked such intense anger among party regulars, one must look closely at how political apologies function in South Korea’s high-stakes legislative environment. A genuine public apology typically requires three elements: a clear admission of factual error, a direct request for forgiveness from the injured parties, and concrete measures to prevent a recurrence. Kim Min-seok’s statements contained none of these.

Instead of addressing the specific damage caused by his allegations, Kim retreated behind broad, evasive phrasing. By declaring himself simultaneously "sorry and grateful," he attempted a rhetorical trick: offering nominal regret while acting as if forgiveness had already been granted. He further attempted to reframe the scandal as an abstract debate over internal party reform and institutional improvement, deliberately shifting focus away from the unfounded attacks he launched against his own colleagues.

This tactic is a classic example of political deflection. In the days leading up to a key general meeting of DPK lawmakers, Kim preempted an official internal report by abruptly announcing that there was "no clear evidence" to support the rumors—without actually taking personal responsibility for spreading them in the first place. Floor Leader Park Chan-dae had been prepared to deliver a formal briefing on the matter, but Kim’s sudden media appearances effectively neutralized the momentum for a rigorous internal review. By presenting himself as a self-sacrificing figure who made a hard choice for the greater good of the party, Kim sought to shield his own leadership standing while abandoning the victims of his rhetoric.

Collateral Damage: From Suncheon Rumors to Grassroots Victims

The real-world consequences of these allegations were felt far beyond parliamentary meeting rooms. During the party convention, supporters of candidate Jung Chung-rae and local party organizers across several regions were systematically targeted in online forums and private messaging groups. They were labeled not just as political rivals, but as covert members of Shincheonji and anti-factional saboteurs opposing the main party line.

The damage was particularly severe in the southern city of Suncheon, represented by DPK Lawmaker Kwon Hyang-yeop. In the weeks preceding Kim’s partial backtrack, a false rumor circulated relentlessly across local chat networks claiming that 6,000 members of the Shincheonji cult had registered en masse as voting DPK members in Suncheon. Local party operations were virtually paralyzed as volunteers and long-time staffers were subjected to intense online harassment, accused of taking orders from an apocalyptic sect.

The human cost of such smear tactics is profound. For dedicated party elders who built their lives around liberal political activism, being lumped in with an undercover religious cult represents an existential character assassination. To launch such attacks without verification, and then walk away with a nonchalant gesture once the election cycle ends, reveals a cynical disregard for the very people who form the backbone of the party organization.

Old Habits of the Activist Generation

For veteran political observers in Seoul, Kim Min-seok’s maneuvers carry a strong sense of deja vu. Critics within and outside the party have drawn direct parallels between this scandal and tactics used decades ago during South Korea’s pro-democracy movement in the 1980s. During that era, competing student activist factions—most notably the National Liberation (NL) and People’s Democracy (PD) camps—frequently engaged in vicious whisper campaigns, accusing rival student leaders of covertly colluding with police informants or state security agents. Once an election for the student council concluded, the winning side would routinely brush the allegations under the rug, claiming that unity was required for the broader struggle.

Similar patterns reemerged during the 2002 presidential election, when high-stakes campaign mergers led to intense behind-the-scenes maneuvering. Kim’s critics argue that his political methodology has remained fundamentally unchanged: weaponize extreme, damaging accusations during critical contests, secure political leverage, and then frame the aftermath as a noble sacrifice for party unity.

This historical pattern highlights a persistent double standard within Korean political culture. The Democratic Party has routinely demanded rigorous ethical standards and legal accountability from its political adversaries—most notably during the impeachment and subsequent imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration was repeatedly criticized by liberals for spreading disinformation and abusing state power. Yet when senior figures within the ruling party engage in unverified character assassination against their own comrades, party elites frequently push for quiet internal compromise rather than formal disciplinary action. This inconsistency undermines the party’s moral authority at a time when it controls both the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung and a parliamentary majority.

Cult Infiltration Facts Versus Political Mudslinging

The controversy is further complicated by the fact that religious infiltration into political parties is a genuine institutional concern in South Korea, not merely a conspiracy theory. Investigations by joint law enforcement task forces and internal party audits have confirmed that religious organizations, including Shincheonji, have previously attempted mass registration drives to influence candidate primaries.

During past primary cycles, the main conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP) faced documented instances of organized cult enrollment, leading to the prosecution of Shincheonji leader Lee Man-hee on charges related to illegal organizational interference involving over 56,000 members. Internal DPK audits similarly estimated that between 30,000 and 50,000 members out of the party’s 1.55 million registered voters (roughly 1.9% to 3.2%) may have loose ties to fringe religious groups.

However, official investigations by joint police-prosecution task forces found absolutely no evidence of an organized "three-way secret alliance" between DPK leadership candidates like Rep. Jung Chung-rae and Shincheonji leadership. The institutional challenge of managing party membership rolls was deliberately transformed into a political weapon to target internal opponents.

Key Takeaway: Preventing fringe religious groups from infiltrating political parties is a legitimate administrative task that requires transparent system fixes. Transforming that systemic issue into an unverified smear against political rivals destroys internal democracy and victimizes innocent party workers.

Rather than addressing this distinction, senior party figures—including policy leaders like Lawmaker Han Jeoung-ae and Lawmaker Kim Young-bae—sought to close ranks during general legislative meetings, arguing that lawmakers should focus on religious reform proposals rather than dwelling on internal disputes. This top-down attempt to enforce harmony ignored the clear distinction between perpetrators and victims, signaling to the grass roots that political expediency takes precedence over truth and internal justice.

What to Watch Next

The Shincheonji smear controversy is far from settled, and its resolution will serve as a crucial test for party governance under President Lee Jae-myung's administration. As the DPK seeks to maintain political momentum, several key indicators will reveal whether the party can repair its internal cohesion and uphold its commitment to member-driven democracy:

  • Ethics Committee Proceedings: Watch whether formal complaints submitted against Lawmaker Kim Min-seok are referred to the DPK Ethics Review Council, or if party leadership quietly shelves requests for disciplinary action.
  • Formal Restitution and Reinstatement: Track whether party leadership issues an official, documented exoneration for the grass-roots organizers and regional chapters in Suncheon who were falsely accused of cult ties.
  • Reform of Party Primary Rules: Monitor upcoming proposals from the DPK’s party management task force regarding digital voter verification and screening mechanisms designed to prevent real covert group enrollments without triggering witch hunts.
  • Grassroots Member Movement: Observe whether aggrieved party members form organized coalitions to challenge elite-driven candidate selection processes in upcoming local elections, signaling a deeper rift between the party hierarchy and its base.
