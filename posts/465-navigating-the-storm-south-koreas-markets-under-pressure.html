Navigating the Storm: South Korea’s Markets Under Pressure

September 30, 2026

As September draws to a close, the mood in the South Korean stock market feels precarious, to put it mildly. Driven by the U.S. Federal Reserve’s interest rate hikes, foreign investors have dumped approximately $2.2 billion (3 trillion won) worth of shares onto the market. It is a massive "selling bomb" that no single buyer could hope to absorb.

The sentiment on the trading floor is palpable. Traders are lamenting the lack of remaining trading days before the holidays—less than ten sessions—and the sheer weight of the sell-off. Yet, remarkably, the KOSPI (Korea Composite Stock Price Index) managed to hold its ground at the 6,870 level, closing with a marginal decline of just 0.27%. While declining stocks outnumbered gainers three-to-one and trading volume hit a yearly low, the fact that the market didn’t collapse entirely is a small but necessary sigh of relief.

Macroeconomic Headwinds and the "Sell Korea" Fear

We cannot dismiss this downturn as a simple, fleeting correction. The macro environment is punishing emerging markets: U.S. 10-year Treasury yields are firmly above 5%, and international oil prices are hovering north of $105 per barrel. These factors exert constant pressure on the valuation of Korean equities.

The exodus of over $8 billion (11 trillion won) by foreign investors in recent sessions is deeply rooted in the fear of global monetary tightening. However, unlike in past crises, the market is finding a floor. Large semiconductor companies have been buying back their own shares, and institutional investors have stepped in to provide defensive support, neutralizing some of the impact of the indiscriminate "Sell Korea" trend. While the era of high U.S. interest rates won't resolve overnight, now is the time to coldly differentiate between the "real winners" that will survive this storm and the "rotten limbs" that need to be pruned.

Semiconductor Equipment: The Engine in the AI Race

Even amid this gloomy macroeconomic backdrop, the KOSDAQ—South Korea’s index for growth-oriented and tech firms—and semiconductor equipment stocks are showing surprising vitality. Companies like HPSP, EO Technics, Wonik IPS, and PSK are holding their own. Their strength is clear: as AI infrastructure demand explodes, expanding semiconductor facilities has shifted from a strategic choice to a matter of survival.

Chinese memory firm CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies) has announced an ambitious, eye-watering investment of $18–$19 billion to double its capacity. Simultaneously, Korea’s own memory giants are pouring trillions of won into R&D and facilities, fueled by massive cash reserves from legacy DRAM sales and the surging popularity of HBM (High Bandwidth Memory). Because the market is confident that demand will stay robust through 2028, the earnings visibility for these equipment manufacturers is brighter than ever.

Cracking the "Korea Discount": Beyond Superficial Reform

For the Korean market to take a leap forward, it must excise its archaic institutional infrastructure. Leaders from the Korea Exchange have recently emphasized the need to "replace the rusted pipes" in the system. Foreign investors have long been deterred by outdated settlement practices and inefficient collateral processes. While building a system to eradicate illegal "naked" short-selling is essential, there is significant concern regarding the proposed shift to "T+1" settlement (settling trades one day after the transaction). If pushed too quickly, the existing back-office systems could buckle under the strain, leading to systemic settlement failures. Reform must be methodical, not a race toward a superficial finish line.

Furthermore, the "top-down" approach of government-led valuation programs and corporate law revisions has clear limits. Real change requires a shift in corporate management mindsets and active "shareholder activism." Currently, the market suffers from a predatory structure where majority shareholders often siphon off premiums when selling control of a company, completely leaving minority shareholders behind. Without legal enforcement of pro-rata distribution—ensuring that all shareholders, regardless of size, are treated equally during ownership transitions—these "value-up" programs will remain nothing more than empty slogans.

The Future: AI Dilemmas and Platform Wars

The recent entry of Toss (a major South Korean financial "super-app" operated by Viva Republica) into the commerce sector via a partnership with Oasis, a fresh-food delivery firm, signals a shake-up in the e-commerce landscape. By leveraging its 30 million users, Toss is aiming to challenge the dominance of Coupang. It is a classic "lock-in" strategy: integrating financial services with daily household shopping to keep users within the Toss ecosystem.

Meanwhile, the AI industry faces a different kind of reality check. Companies like Anthropic are moving toward IPOs, revealing a stark paradox: while revenues are soaring twelve-fold, operating losses are ballooning concurrently. It is a growing pain shared by Big Tech and generative AI firms alike—the realization that explosive demand and technological superiority do not automatically equal immediate profitability. Beyond the glittering IPO spotlights, the market will eventually demand a cold, hard verification of whether these firms can generate sustainable profits after the infrastructure spending binge ends.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on how the government handles the integration of the KOSDAQ index into national pension fund benchmarks. If these massive funds—which currently overlook the KOSDAQ—begin to shift their investment mandates, it could provide the liquidity necessary for the market to move beyond being a retail-driven speculative playground and toward becoming a hub for long-term growth. Until then, investors should focus on companies with rock-solid fundamentals rather than the volatile headlines of the day.
