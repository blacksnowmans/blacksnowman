Police Raids, 110 Witnesses, and a Factional Civil War: The Crackdown Inside South Korea's Ruling Camp

October 22, 2026

Late on the night of Wednesday, October 21, two full-sized police riot buses pulled up outside the Seoul offices of Sisa Tapa, a prominent progressive YouTube news channel. Inside the studio, staff were preparing for the next morning’s live broadcast. Instead of broadcasting, they watched officers fan out through the building with search-and-seizure warrants, seizing digital records and equipment before proceeding directly to the private residence of the channel’s head, Lee Jong-won.

The raid sent immediate shockwaves through South Korea’s political sphere. Search warrants against domestic media figures are notoriously sensitive, but what made this raid particularly jarring was where it originated: under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, carried out not against conservative opposition figures, but against a voice within the progressive ecosystem. What on the surface appears to be a hyper-technical election law investigation is increasingly viewed by insiders as the opening salvo in a fierce, bitter internal civil war within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea.

A Live Broadcast and a Disproportionate Show of Force

The formal criminal charges leveled against Lee Jong-won center on events that took place during livestreams on August 14 and 15. Police allege that Lee guided his viewers on how to respond if they received telephone surveys for internal Democratic Party primary polls, suggesting that respondents misstate their age and residential region. Investigators argue this constituted criminal obstruction of business against both polling agencies and the Democratic Party's election management committee.

Under South Korean jurisprudence, however, turning on-air commentary into a criminal charge of business obstruction faces a remarkably steep legal hurdle. Lower courts handling past primary disputes have consistently ruled that proving obstruction requires demonstrating clear, malicious intent or coordinated organizational conspiracy to skew results. Casual advice, spontaneous live banter, or expressions of opinion during a streaming broadcast rarely cross the legal threshold into serious criminal interference.

More glaring is the question of police procedure. The broadcast in question was streamed publicly; the unedited video resides permanently on YouTube servers and digital archives. The evidence required to assess whether a crime occurred was already in the hands of the public and law enforcement. Why, then, dispatch riot buses and execute heavy-handed search warrants on both an office and a private residence?

The method is uncomfortably reminiscent of the heavy-handed raids conducted during Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure against investigative outlet Newstapa over defamation claims. During Mr. Yoon’s administration—before his impeachment, removal from office, and subsequent imprisonment—prosecutors routinely raided newsrooms and homes under the guise of investigating published reporting, using narrow warrants as pretexts to seize phones and laptops in search of unrelated lateral leads. To see the same playbook deployed under a progressive government has triggered sharp disillusionment among party loyalists.

The True Target: Moon Jae-in and the January Trial

To understand why a YouTube streamer's poll commentary warranted an armada of police officers, one must look at the escalating offensive against the party's "pro-Moon" faction—the loyalists of former President Moon Jae-in. The raid on Sisa Tapa did not happen in a vacuum; it coincided with an increasingly aggressive push by state investigators against the former president himself.

On January 11, former President Moon is scheduled to stand before a citizen participatory trial on charges of bribery. The core of the prosecution's case strains conventional legal logic: it centers on approximately 200 million won (roughly $150,000) paid in salary and housing allowances to Moon’s former son-in-law, a man surnamed Seo, by Thai Eastar Jet, a budget carrier affiliate owned by former lawmaker Lee Sang-jik. Prosecutors claim that because this salary relieved Moon of the burden of supporting his adult daughter and her husband, the employment income effectively constituted a direct personal bribe to the sitting president.

The political nature of the trial became undeniable when public prosecutors submitted a request to summon an astonishing 110 witnesses. The presiding court expressed open bewilderment at the unprecedented figure, rejecting the vast majority and capping the witness list at 18. Demanding 110 witnesses for a citizen jury trial is not a standard evidentiary strategy; it is an exercise in political theater, designed to subject the former president to a grueling, drawn-out public humiliation.

At the same time, special prosecutor proposals aimed at scrutinizing fabricated indictments and past prosecutorial overreach remain stalled. Critics argue that a thorough, independent review of manipulated charges would inevitably expose the political machinery that targeted the Moon administration in its final years—a machinery that elements of the current ruling camp appear content to repurpose for their own factional advantage.

YouTube Agitators and the Return of the Student Left

The ground game for this intra-party purge is not being fought in traditional committee rooms, but on the algorithmic battlefield of partisan YouTube. Channels branding themselves as "New Lee"—such as Spiks TV and Kim Yong-min TV—have spent recent months reviving old political scandals from the Moon era, including the Sillajen stock manipulation affair and the Burning Sun nightclub scandal. These controversies, once weaponized by conservative opponents to paralyze Moon’s Blue House, are now being dusted off by hardline pro-Lee commentators to permanently destroy the moral authority of the party’s traditional mainstream.

Behind this aggressive rhetoric lies an older, deeper current in South Korean radical politics: the revival of the "National Liberation" (NL) faction, historically associated with groups like the Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance. In South Korea's student movement history, the progressive camp split between the class-struggle-oriented "People's Democracy" (PD) faction and the nationalist, anti-imperialist NL faction. The NL tradition, while electorally marginalized for decades due to dogmatic stances on North Korea and hostile organizational methods, has long excelled at factional infiltration and relentless internal warfare.

The footprint of this alignment emerged clearly in a recent public opinion survey conducted by STI for the minor, hard-left Progressive Party. The poll showed presidential approval sitting at 37.0% against a 59.8% disapproval rating, while 23.9% of voters who supported the administration earlier this year had defected. Intriguingly, the Progressive Party used this data to argue that the drop did not stem from core progressive disillusionment, parroting the exact talking points pushed by "New Lee" online influencers. Instead of addressing broader public fatigue, these groups have sought to steer the administration toward fringe ideological pet projects—such as demanding the unilateral reclamation of wartime operational control (OPCON) from Washington.

Veteran figures of the traditional progressive movement, including author and former minister Rhyu Si-min, have voiced alarm at this drift, warning that incompetent and factionally vindictive aides are steering the ruling apparatus into self-destructive dead ends.

Cracks at the Blue House: The Pressure on Hong Ik-pyo

The institutional friction generated by this ideological shift is now threatening key personnel inside the presidential office. At the center of the storm is Senior Presidential Secretary for Political Affairs Hong Ik-pyo.

Hong is widely regarded as an institutionalist who embodies what party veterans call the "Democratic Party DNA"—a tradition forged in the pro-democracy movements of the 1980s that prioritizes procedural democracy, diverse factional coalition-building, and resistance to authoritarian police tactics. Because Hong retains credibility across both pro-Moon and pro-Lee wings of the party, he represents a formidable internal obstacle to those advocating a scorched-earth campaign against internal rivals.

Persistent rumors of Hong's imminent replacement or forced resignation have alarmed party moderates. If Hong is pushed out, it will be taken as confirmation that the administration intends to unleash the newly structured Serious Crime Investigation Office (SCIO) without restraint, turning state investigative powers inward to complete the purge of internal critics.

Meanwhile, the political cost of this relentless infighting is mounting. Since Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok took office, Democratic Party support has tumbled by 11.7 percentage points. Backbench lawmakers speak privately of an oppressive atmosphere where candid dissent invites immediate warnings, internal audits, and coordinated online harassment from militant fan clubs. Rather than governing with a decisive mandate, the ruling coalition is fracturing under the weight of its own internal rivalries.

What to Watch Next

The raid on Sisa Tapa signals that South Korea's ruling camp has crossed a threshold from uneasy coexistence into open internal purge. In the coming weeks, several concrete milestones will reveal how deep this rupture will go:

  • The January 11 Moon Jae-in Trial: The opening of Moon’s citizen participatory trial will determine whether the pro-Moon faction rallies its remaining parliamentary base or fractures permanently under legal and public pressure.
  • The Fate of Hong Ik-pyo: Any personnel reshuffle involving the Senior Secretary for Political Affairs will signal whether the Blue House intends to moderate its course or give hardline factionalists full control over state coordination and investigative policy.
  • The Role of the Serious Crime Investigation Office: Watch closely for whether new inquiries initiated by the agency target conservative opposition figures or continue to focus inward on figures connected to the previous progressive administration.
  • Parliamentary Discipline and Dissent: Pay attention to whether moderate lawmakers begin openly challenging party leadership decisions, a development that could threaten the ruling party's legislative discipline heading into upcoming electoral cycles.
