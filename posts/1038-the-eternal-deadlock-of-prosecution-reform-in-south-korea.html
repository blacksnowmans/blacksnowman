The Eternal Deadlock of Prosecution Reform in South Korea

September 23, 2026

Picture sitting in a living room in Seoul on any given evening, watching the news ticker crawl across the screen. Another raid by investigators, another fiery press conference from a major political party, another high-profile public figure summoned for questioning. For decades, this has been the steady pulse of South Korean politics. Beneath the daily theater of accusations and counter-accusations lies an institution that holds an extraordinary, almost unchecked monopoly over justice: the prosecution service.

In the world of Korean political commentary, reforming this massive agency is the debate that never sleeps and never resolves. To some, it is the ultimate democratic imperative, a long-overdue clipping of unchecked wings. To others, it is nothing more than a political shield used by corrupt politicians to dodge accountability. When prominent progressive figures like author and public intellectual Rhyu Si-min weigh in on the issue, their frustration is rooted in a simple reality: they have watched firsthand how the unchecked power of prosecutors can warp the fundamental fairness of South Korean society.

The Structural Trap: Why Prosecution Reform Keeps Failing

The Dangerous Monopoly of Investigation and Indictment

The reason prosecution reform in South Korea has remained stuck in neutral for decades is not merely a matter of sloppy legislation. At the heart of the problem is a structural peculiarity: the prosecution holds both the power to investigate and the power to indict. This dual authority has fostered a powerful bureaucratic cartel, tightly bound to the shifting tides of the country's political geography.

For years, progressive politicians have argued that this imbalance destroys institutional accountability. When a single agency decides both whether to investigate a crime and whether to take it to trial, it breeds a culture of impunity. Critics call the inevitable result "selective justice"—the uncanny habit of high-level investigations appearing and disappearing depending on who holds executive power.

Yet the conversation almost always devolves into an emotional tug-of-war. The core of the issue should be about dispersing power, not catching specific rivals. Instead, every time reform is floated, conservative politicians warn of a breakdown in crime-fighting capabilities, while progressives point to the weaponization of the office. Caught in the middle, everyday citizens struggle to find anything resembling a reliable, impartial justice system.

When Reformers Fall in Love with Power

Past attempts at reform, such as the creation of the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) or legislative adjustments to divide investigative jurisdictions, were hailed as historic breakthroughs. In practice, however, they have largely fallen short of public expectations. Turf wars between police and prosecutors only added to public confusion, while mechanisms designed to check elite power often ended up neutralized.

This reveals a bitter irony: even the political forces pushing for reform quickly grow accustomed to the sweet taste of that very same power. Rhyu Si-min and other veteran critics have long warned against this trap. Changing laws on paper is useless if the people operating the machinery lack the genuine will to restrain themselves. Today’s politicians frequently use the banner of "reform" as a tactical smoke screen—either to defend their own allies from legal exposure or to deploy state machinery against political enemies.

The Opposition's Legal Risks and the Politics of Prosecution

The Lee Jae-myung Case as a Litmus Test

No current debate highlights this dysfunction more clearly than the ongoing investigations surrounding Lee Jae-myung, leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal opposition party). For the prosecution, these are straightforward corruption probes. For the opposition, they are nakedly political weaponization.

What matters most is not just the guilt or innocence of a single politician, but whether the methods used by investigators are fair. Frequent raids, relentless summons, and leaks to the press evoke memories of South Korea's authoritarian past. When the public watches the news, they are rarely asked to weigh evidence; instead, they are forced to calculate which side holds more raw power. This corrosive dynamic drains public trust in the judiciary down to its foundations.

A society cannot build a lasting consensus when the law is viewed as flexible depending on the target. While a politician’s legal troubles should never be granted blind immunity, neither can the abuse of state investigative powers be excused under the banner of law enforcement.

The Conservative Defense of the Status Quo

On the other side of the aisle, the People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) points to these same investigations as proof of a functioning rule of law. Yet their rhetoric carries a dangerous contradiction: any criticism of the prosecution's methods is frequently branded as an attack on constitutional order itself.

This dynamic proves why reform is so urgently needed. When an investigative agency is perceived as sharing an invisible lifeline with the ruling establishment, the judicial system loses its core purpose and becomes a partisan instrument. True democracy requires that the citizens and politicians alike retain the right to criticize state organs without being accused of subversion.

The Intellectual Dilemma and Public Fatigue

Figures like Rhyu Si-min offer sharp, unsparing critiques of how reform efforts repeatedly self-destruct. When public intellectuals use harsh language to describe the failures of reformist politicians, it stems from deep disappointment over missed opportunities. They dissect how political engineering co-opts reform, turning lofty ideals into empty slogans.

The tragedy is how these critiques are consumed by the public. Too often, citizens use an intellectual's commentary merely as ammunition for their own pre-existing tribal beliefs—proof that "my side is right." But the underlying message of figures like Rhyu is precisely the opposite: the absolute necessity of judicial independence that transcends partisan loyalties.

If society keeps waiting for politicians to voluntarily surrender their favorite weapon, the wait will be eternal. True and lasting pressure has always bubbled up from the bottom, not trickled down from the top. The millions who filled Seoul's squares during past civic uprisings understand this deeply: leave everything to the political elite, and they will inevitably compromise for their own survival.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea looks toward future elections and legislative cycles, several concrete markers will reveal whether any genuine shift is occurring:

  • Legislative moves on structural separation: Watch whether future assemblies make any serious, non-partisan attempts to completely separate investigative powers from indictment authorities, rather than just shifting jurisdiction between police and prosecutors.
  • External oversight mechanisms: Look for proposals to introduce real civilian or judicial oversight over the prosecution's authority to drop cases without trial.
  • Partisan rhetoric vs. institutional proposals: Pay close attention to how political candidates address the judiciary. A candidate who treats judicial independence as a constitutional safeguard rather than a political football is the one actually offering a systemic fix.
  • Civic monitoring initiatives: Observe whether civil society groups manage to maintain pressure on human rights abuses during investigations, regardless of which party is in power.

Politics can pause, but the march toward real justice cannot afford to stand still. Until South Korea builds a legal architecture where no ruling power can use the badge and the indictment as a political club, the debate over prosecution reform will remain the unfinished business of the nation's democracy.
