A Coalition at the Crossroads: Why Rhyu Si-min’s Warning Struck a Nerve in Lee Jae-myung’s Blue House

October 29, 2026

When South Korean President Lee Jae-myung took to social media recently to announce that he was looking into a viral grievance posted on FM Korea—a fiercely anti-establishment, conservative-leaning online community dominated by young men—the political ripple effects were instantaneous. The post in question criticized municipal subsidies that prioritized multicultural mothers for access to public postpartum care centers over native Korean mothers. By signaling that he was prepared to examine the policy and correct potential unfairness regardless of partisan origin, Lee delivered a direct challenge to the binary logic of contemporary Korean politics: supporters and opponents alike are sovereign citizens, he insisted, and wrongs should be righted no matter who points them out.

Yet rather than being applauded as an act of unifying statesmanship, the gesture sparked an immediate and furious rebellion among his own party’s core base. Hardline loyalists within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea accused the president of pandering to the enemy and breaking ranks. Into this brewing tempest stepped Rhyu Si-min, one of South Korea’s most prominent progressive public intellectuals, best-selling authors, and a former cabinet minister. Rhyu delivered a scathing, unvarnished critique of both the administration’s dropping approval ratings and the Democratic Party’s allergic reaction to internal dissent. The ensuing public feud has laid bare the central crisis confronting South Korea’s progressive government: caught between the demands of an uncompromising partisan base and the cold reality of declining public trust, the administration risks losing the very coalition that put it in power.

Reaching Across the Aisle—and the Backlash from the Base

To understand why a debate over municipal postpartum care centers could ignite such fury, one must understand how deeply entrenched camp tribalism has become in South Korea. The political landscape is fiercely polarized between the ruling center-left Democratic Party of Korea and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. In this hyper-partisan environment, political identity often dictates everything from which YouTube channels citizens watch to which online communities they frequent.

President Lee’s willingness to publicly address an issue surfaced on FM Korea was an explicit attempt to puncture that partisan wall. In his message, Lee laid out a simple premise: a government does not belong to the faction that elected it, but to the entire nation. Public office and public budgets are financed by every taxpayer, and clinging to partisan bias while wielding state power is an unsustainable path for any government.

Yet for a vocal segment of his core supporters, political survival is viewed strictly as a zero-sum war. To them, acknowledging a point raised on a conservative message board looked like a betrayal. Within hours, party message boards and progressive commentary channels filled with accusations that the president was succumbing to conservative framing. This reflexive hostility illustrates the trap facing South Korea's governing party: a political movement originally formed around democratic values and progressive reform has increasingly turned its own camp into an inviolable sanctuary, where any willingness to self-correct is tarred as treachery.

The Ghost of Yoon Suk-yeol and the Fragility of Coalitions

In analyzing the danger of this tribal mindset, Rhyu Si-min offered a chilling parallel that strikes directly at the nerves of South Korean lawmakers: the political collapse of Yoon Suk-yeol. Yoon, who was impeached, stripped of power, and is now imprisoned, saw his administration disintegrate largely because he dismantled the very coalition that elected him.

As Rhyu pointed out, Yoon did not build his political power base from scratch; he inherited a broad conservative alliance constructed through years of groundwork. Yet once in office, convinced of his own political invincibility, Yoon began purging the architects of his victory—driving out young conservative party leader Lee Jun-seok and marginalizing moderate coalition partner Ahn Cheol-soo. By cannibalizing his own camp and alienating the moderate voters who provided his winning margin, Yoon eroded the bedrock of trust upon which any presidency must stand. When public support cratered, he had no reservoir of goodwill left to draw upon, culminating in his dramatic downfall.

The lesson for the Democratic Party today, Rhyu argued, is both urgent and uncomfortable. Power is inherently rented. Governments win office by assembling a coalition of core believers and swing voters; they lose office when arrogance leads them to assume they can govern solely for their loudest partisans. If the Democratic Party begins purging its own internal critics and shutting out moderate sentiment, it risks walking the same self-destructive path of factional fragmentation that doomed its predecessor.

A Three-Stage Framework: Why Presidential Polls Really Drop

When presidential approval ratings begin to slide, the instinct of 여의도 (Yeouido—South Korea’s equivalent of Capitol Hill) is almost always to blame public relations. Politicians rush to television studios to argue that a complex tax policy was misunderstood, or that the public simply needs to be educated on the nuances of real estate regulation. Rhyu Si-min dismissed this approach entirely, putting forward a three-stage diagnostic framework for understanding why leaders lose public backing:

  • Stage 1: Trust in the leader as a person. Does the public fundamentally believe that the president possesses the character, sincerity, and basic fairness to govern in their interest?
  • Stage 2: Fidelity to the agreed-upon direction. Is the president staying true to the foundational vision and core promises that persuaded voters to grant them power in the first place?
  • Stage 3: Policy efficacy. Are specific legislative measures, economic interventions, and administrative directives working as intended?

According to Rhyu, politicians obsess over Stage 3 because it allows them to deflect blame onto policy wonks or adversarial media. But voters do not turn against an administration merely because the price of groceries rose or a specific bill stalled in the National Assembly. They turn away when Stage 1 and Stage 2 begin to buckle—when they begin to harbor fundamental doubts about who the leader is serving and whether the government has lost its moral and strategic compass.

This dynamic also sheds light on the lingering grievances surrounding President Lee’s electoral victory. While loyalists frequently complain that Lee was unfairly targeted and "demonized" by state prosecutors and the conservative press during the campaign, Rhyu's analysis suggests that blaming external enemies is an excuse for political complacency. Lee won a tightly contested election without securing an outright majority of the vote. The subsequent surge of the Rebuilding Korea Party—a progressive splinter party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk that drew enthusiastic support from disaffected reform voters—was a direct warning shot. It showed that many progressive and independent voters felt the mainstream Democratic Party had grown complacent, entitled, and disconnected from genuine reform.

An Echo Chamber That Demands Cheerleaders, Not Critics

The response to Rhyu’s analysis from within the Democratic Party leadership only reinforced his central argument. Rather than engaging with the substance of his warning, high-profile party figures—including lawmakers Park Hong-keun, Park Jie-won, and Jung Jin-wook—launched fierce personal counterattacks. Rhyu was publicly branded an "insurrectionist voice" and dismissed with insults, with some calling him a "shaman rather than a writer."

When you offer praise, they call you an indispensable ally; the moment you offer bitter medicine, they brand you a traitor shaking the foundations of the party.

This aggressive silencing of constructive criticism marks a troubling departure from the historical traditions of South Korea’s progressive movement. The towering figures of the Democratic Party’s past, former Presidents Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun, achieved historical stature precisely because they cultivated vigorous internal debate and contested ideas openly. Today, the party often resembles an echo chamber where competition revolves around displays of unwavering personal loyalty to the presidential office.

Silencing the crowd does not help the players on the field score goals. If anything, trying to muzzle criticism only deepens the cynicism of non-partisan and moderate voters who want to see tangible governance rather than ideological discipline. This dilemma is underscored by the current economic climate. While the Lee administration has mobilized the Korea Fair Trade Commission to levy aggressive investigations and massive fines against corporate conglomerates and tech platform giants, ordinary Koreans have yet to feel significant relief in their everyday cost of living. Heavy regulatory battles often end up mired in long, expensive legal disputes with top corporate law firms, producing political theater but little immediate economic respite for working families.

What to Watch Next

The controversy ignited by Rhyu Si-min is not merely an insider squabble among progressive pundits; it is a preview of the structural pressures that will define President Lee Jae-myung’s tenure. How the Blue House and the ruling party handle this friction will determine whether the administration can maintain governing momentum or slowly retreat into an isolated partisan fortress. In the months ahead, several concrete dynamics will reveal which path the government is taking:

  • The fate of bipartisan outreach: Watch whether President Lee follows through on his commitment to address non-partisan grievances—such as reviewing the municipal postnatal care subsidies surfaced by FM Korea—or quietly retreats in the face of pushback from his core base.
  • Party-presidential dynamics: Observe whether the Democratic Party leadership continues to attack internal critics and reformist factions, or opens formal channels to incorporate critical feedback from broader progressive voices, including supporters of the Rebuilding Korea Party.
  • Economic deliverables versus regulatory spectacle: Track whether the administration pivots from high-profile Fair Trade Commission crackdowns on major conglomerates toward pragmatic, measurable measures that lower household expenses and stabilize the domestic economy.

South Korea’s democratic history has repeatedly demonstrated that political power is precarious. A government that surrounds itself only with cheers while treating every warning as sabotage invariably accelerates its own isolation. For President Lee, the true challenge of the coming years will not be silencing his critics, but proving he has the fortitude to listen to them.
