The Hidden War: Why the U.S. is Engineering a Weaker Dollar

September 11, 2026

For those of us in our 30s, the exchange rate has long ceased to be a trivial number checked only before a vacation. It has become a high-stakes indicator that dictates the real-world value of our paychecks, the cost of our mortgage interest, and the health of our stock portfolios. When the South Korean Won (KRW) against the U.S. Dollar (USD) fluctuates near the 1,500 level—a threshold that feels particularly volatile—we aren't just looking at market noise; we are looking at a fundamental shift in the global order.

Recent analysis by Professor Kim Kwang-seok, a prominent economist often cited in Korean media for his insights on market trends, suggests that the current "complex equation" of the exchange rate is governed by a deliberate U.S. strategy. The prevailing narrative in Seoul’s financial circles is that Washington has begun an active campaign to suppress the value of the dollar. But why would the world’s leading superpower intentionally sabotage its own currency? The answer lies in the intersection of Trump-era political strategies and a massive "reverse flow of capital" driven by the artificial intelligence boom.

The Origins of the Modern Currency War

To understand what is happening, we must look past the surface-level trade rhetoric. When reports surfaced—often linked to analysts like Steven Myron—suggesting that "the tariff war is evolving into a currency war," many dismissed it as standard protectionism. However, the reality is far more calculated.

The United States has suffered from chronic trade deficits for decades, a structural issue stemming from the erosion of its domestic manufacturing base. This trade deficit translates directly into a fiscal deficit. To fund the government, the U.S. issues massive amounts of Treasury bonds, driving interest rates up. This high-rate environment makes it difficult for domestic companies to issue corporate bonds, effectively choking off investment. When the U.S. pressured China with tariffs in 2018, Beijing countered by devaluing the Yuan, rendering the tariffs ineffective. Washington learned a crucial lesson: tariffs alone are not enough; the dollar itself must be managed.

Herein lies the American dilemma: as the issuer of the world’s reserve currency, the U.S. wants a strong dollar, but that same strength kills domestic manufacturing. By artificially suppressing the dollar, the U.S. aims to regain export competitiveness and lure production back to American soil. This is the essence of 21st-century currency warfare.

The "Second Plaza Accord" and the Targeting of the Yen

How does a superpower lower its currency value? It forces other nations to strengthen theirs. The primary target in this strategy is the Japanese Yen. The coordinated interventions in the U.S.-Japan currency markets seen throughout this year are not merely attempts at "market stabilization." They represent a modern-day "Plaza Accord"—the 1985 agreement where major powers agreed to depreciate the dollar against the yen and the mark.

The U.S. Dollar Index is heavily weighted by the Euro (57.6%), the Yen (13.6%), and the British Pound (11.9%). While the dollar maintains dominance in this basket, rising values in these major currencies force the index down. By pressuring Japan to allow the yen to appreciate, the U.S. effectively lowers the dollar’s relative value. For Tokyo, trapped under the U.S. security umbrella, rejecting this "global financial restructuring" is nearly impossible. The U.S. is essentially trading the interests of its allies to secure its own industrial revival and maintain its hegemonic grip on the global financial system.

The Tariff-Currency Trap: Tariffs are rarely just about the price of goods. When the U.S. threatens a 20% tariff, the objective is often to force the target nation to appreciate its currency by a corresponding amount. Nations that cannot afford to lose access to the massive U.S. consumer market are structurally forced to either revalue their currency or buy more U.S. Treasuries to appease Washington.

Reading the Signs: Capital Flows and Corporate Behavior

Exchange rates are not random; they follow predictable leading indicators. Professor Kim points to the growth rate of M2—the total money supply—as a primary signal. When a country floods the market with cash, the currency’s value naturally dilutes. As the U.S. approaches the 2026 midterm elections, it is expected to aggressively expand the money supply to stimulate the economy. As this liquidity hits the market, the dollar’s value is likely to weaken, potentially stabilizing the Won-Dollar exchange rate back toward the 1,300 level.

In South Korea, we have seen an anomaly: despite record-breaking semiconductor exports, the Won has remained weak. This was primarily because Korean corporations were holding onto their massive dollar earnings rather than converting them back into Won. However, this is changing. As the market senses that the dollar may be peaking, companies are aggressively converting their holdings to lock in profits, a trend that is now acting as a downward force on the exchange rate.

The AI Boom and the Path Forward

The U.S. economy is currently operating on borrowed time. GDP-driving capital expenditure (CAPEX) in data centers and AI software is being sustained despite negative cash flows, largely to ensure that the economic data heading into the late October midterm election cycle looks robust. Once those elections conclude, the artificial engine of government-backed liquidity may sputter.

However, South Korea is in a unique position. While many feared that geopolitical risks would cripple the Korean economy, it is currently projected to grow by roughly 3.3%. This is due to the "AI-led reverse flow of capital." Semiconductors and AI-related exports are currently so vital to the global supply chain that they are effectively insulating the country from external shocks. The technological necessity of Korean chips is overriding geopolitical volatility.

What to watch next:

  • The U.S. M2 Supply: Monitor how aggressively the U.S. expands its money supply as the midterm elections near. A sharp increase is a clear signal of impending dollar weakness.
  • Corporate Conversion Rates: Keep an eye on how quickly Korean tech giants move from holding dollar reserves to converting them into local currency. This is a real-time indicator of the market’s internal sentiment.
  • CAPEX Stability: If major U.S. tech firms continue their massive AI infrastructure spending through October, the dollar will likely remain under pressure, benefiting emerging market currencies like the Won.

For the individual investor, the lesson is clear: do not panic based on headlines alone. The "dollar suppression" we are witnessing is a calculated political move. By understanding the context—why the U.S. is pressuring its allies and why tech spending is being forced at all costs—we can move from being passive victims of global volatility to informed observers of the next great financial shift.
