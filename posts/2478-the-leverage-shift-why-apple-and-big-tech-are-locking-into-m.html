The Leverage Shift: Why Apple and Big Tech Are Locking into Multi-Year Chip Deals with Samsung and SK Hynix

November 18, 2026

For decades, the global memory chip business lived and died by the brutal whipsaw of the commodity cycle. Tech giants like Apple, Google, and Dell treated dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) as an interchangeable utility. When supply outpaced demand, they squeezed South Korean suppliers on price; when the market tightened, they held out until new fabrication plants came online to flood the channel. Long-term volume guarantees were rare, and pricing commitments stretching beyond a few quarters were virtually unheard of.

That dynamic has broken. Faced with memory prices that have surged nearly 270% over the past cycle and component costs threatening the fundamental economics of consumer devices, the world’s most powerful device makers are capitulating. Apple is currently in late-stage negotiations with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix—the twin titans of South Korea’s semiconductor industry—to secure four- to five-year Long-Term Supply Agreements (LTAs) for DRAM. In an industry where memory now accounts for as much as 40% of the total bill of materials for flagship smartphones, the buyer’s market has officially vanished.

When the 'Super Buyer' Flinches: The Rise of Long-Term Supply Contracts

In South Korean business parlance, the dominant client in any commercial relationship is traditionally dubbed the super gap—the party holding all the cards, setting terms, and demanding compliance. Apple has occupied that throne for nearly two decades. Its sheer purchasing volume and meticulous supply chain control meant component suppliers routinely bent to its will just to remain in Cupertino’s favor.

Yet the ongoing explosion in artificial intelligence hardware demand has upended that hierarchy. Memory fabs are converting vast swaths of high-grade wafer capacity to High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) and enterprise-grade DDR5 to feed hyperscale data centers, effectively starving the conventional mobile and PC sectors. As spot prices skyrocketed, Apple realized that relying on rolling, short-term purchasing rounds left its consumer hardware roadmap exposed to catastrophic cost volatility.

By seeking commitments spanning up to five years, Apple is prioritizing supply certainty and downside price protection over tactical bargaining. But this maneuver carries profound consequences for the wider tech industry:

  • A reinforced price floor: By tying up substantial portions of Samsung's and SK Hynix's production capacity in long-term contracts, the amount of unallocated, liquid memory floating on the open spot market will shrink dramatically. This functions as an institutional backstop against future price collapses.
  • Margin starvation for second-tier hardware makers: Unlike Apple, mid-sized smartphone and consumer electronics makers lack the capital or balance sheet muscle to sign four-year volume commitments. They will be forced to absorb volatile spot prices or accept allocation cuts, widening the competitive gulf between top-tier OEMs and the rest of the market.
  • The validation of supplier dominance: Negotiating multi-year contracts during a high-price environment confirms that Big Tech expects structural memory constraints to persist well into the late 2020s.

Meanwhile, the third major member of the global memory oligopoly, Idaho-based Micron Technology, has encountered operational friction that further tilts leverage toward the South Korean majors. Alongside a 36% drawdown in its share price from recent highs and heavy institutional options hedging, Micron recently suffered a fatal industrial accident involving an overturned excavator at a fab demolition site in Taichung, Taiwan. The resulting scrutiny from Taiwanese labor regulators, combined with rumors of packaging bottlenecks around Nvidia's upcoming architectures, has raised investor concerns over execution risks just as Samsung and SK Hynix ramp their output.

The $245 Billion War Chest: Korea’s Aggressive Capital Deployment

South Korea’s chipmakers are not treating this cycle as a temporary windfall. Instead, they are weaponizing record earnings to construct an insurmountable capital barrier against competitors. Combined capital expenditures and research-and-development spending from Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix surged by 71.6% year-on-year, crossing 140 trillion won (approximately $105 billion).

Backing this historic expansion is a war chest of liquid assets and cash deposits exceeding 329 trillion won (roughly $247 billion). In an industry where a single next-generation cleanroom can cost $15 billion before installing a single tool, balance sheet scale is the ultimate survival mechanism. Yet, how each company directs this capital reflects two very different strategic postures.

SK Hynix, riding high on its early lead in supplying HBM to Nvidia, has claimed an astonishing milestone: its DRAM and HBM production allocations are effectively sold out through 2029. To secure this momentum, SK Hynix has formed four dedicated investment entities in the United States, expanding partnerships with AI infrastructure providers like Penguin Solutions and betting heavily on next-generation optical interconnects. However, this blistering growth comes with significant customer concentration. While its US-derived revenue has surpassed 147 trillion won (about $110 billion), roughly 35 trillion won ($26 billion) stems from Nvidia alone. In a geopolitical environment marked by escalating US-China trade curbs and sudden swings in enterprise infrastructure budgets, that dependence represents both an engine and an anchor.

Samsung Electronics, on the other hand, is executing a painful but necessary restructuring of its contract-chipmaking (foundry) division. Rather than continuing to fight for low-margin business in legacy 8-inch wafer processing, Samsung is winding down those older lines to reassign engineers, fab space, and capital toward 12-inch wafers and advanced 2-nanometer turnkey manufacturing. By bundling its proprietary 2nm node with its upcoming HBM4E memory in an all-in-one packaging package, Samsung secured a major AI chip order from Meta. The transition has created domestic friction: South Korean design firms (fabless companies) that relied heavily on Samsung’s 8-inch lines now face sudden cost pressures to migrate their designs to 12-inch wafers, pleading for government intervention. Furthermore, Samsung's marquee foundry build in Taylor, Texas, has posted operational losses through the first three quarters as pre-production ramp expenses mount.

Beyond HBM: Next-Gen Architectures and Toolmaker Independence

Even as current-generation HBM drives historic margins, the industry is already approaching a physics wall. Stacking twelve or sixteen DRAM dies vertically on a silicon interposer generates severe thermal dissipation problems and data-routing bottlenecks that simple architectural iterations cannot resolve. Consequently, the battle lines are expanding beyond standard HBM.

Alternative computing paradigms are already surfacing. South Korean design house SemiFive recently showcased next-generation AI chip architectures utilizing 3D-IC vertical stacking that dispense with HBM entirely. Concurrently, researchers from Georgia Tech alongside Nvidia engineers have published work on "HBF" (High-Bandwidth Flash)—an architecture that stacks specialized NAND flash memory rather than DRAM to slash read latencies for large-scale model inference at a fraction of the power and cost.

While the chipmakers redesign their memory topologies, a critical domestic transformation is taking place within South Korea's equipment and materials ecosystem (known locally as so-bu-jang). Historically dependent on imports from Japan, Europe, and the United States, South Korea’s memory dominance has long rested on fragile equipment supply lines. That dynamic is beginning to shift:

  • Hanmi Semiconductor continues to post resilient profitability on the back of its critical dual-TC bonding equipment, an indispensable tool for packaging high-stack HBM dies.
  • Justem secured global recognition for its proprietary "JFS" second-generation humidity control solution, a system vital for suppressing wafer defects during ultra-fine line processing.
  • YEST was tapped by Samsung Electronics to deliver evaluation units of its High-Pressure Hydrogen Annealing (HPA) systems for 1d (the fourth generation of 10-nanometer class) DRAM production lines before year-end.

These equipment suppliers represent South Korea's secondary line of defense: if foreign export controls or supply shocks hit primary tooling, domestic hardware players must be capable of sustaining fab operations.

The Downstream Squeeze: From Budget Phones to Market Divergence

While the upper echelon of the semiconductor industry enjoys record pricing power, the real economy is beginning to feel the strain. The rapid escalation of memory procurement costs is colliding with stagnant consumer purchasing power.

In response to soaring DRAM and NAND input costs, Samsung's own mobile division is preparing to raise retail prices on its mass-market Galaxy A series smartphones by up to 19%. For years, mid-tier and budget smartphones served as volume drivers, absorbing legacy memory inventory. Now, with memory comprising nearly half of the component cost, manufacturers can no longer cross-subsidize cheaper models without destroying operating margins. Across the B2B tech sector—from municipal public security equipment to entry-level office computers—vendors report that price-hike negotiations with end clients are falling apart, threatening a sharp drop in terminal unit sales.

This dynamic introduces a dangerous feedback loop. If soaring memory prices choke off final demand for consumer electronics, memory makers could eventually face an inventory buildup in standard commoditized chips, even as high-end AI silicon remains undersupplied.

That tension has spilled into equity markets. Short-term sentiment has turned volatile, driven by caution ahead of Nvidia's operational earnings and downward price adjustments for generic NAND flash. South Korean institutional funds, such as the widely watched ACE Semiconductor ETF run by Korea Investment Trust Management, have actively reshuffled holdings, trimming positions in high-flying SK Hynix to lift their allocation in Samsung Electronics back to 20%. Yet global smart money sees structural resilience: US private equity firm Dragoneer recently acquired approximately 2.4 trillion won ($1.8 billion) worth of SK Hynix stock, betting that long-term contract lock-ins will smooth out cyclical downturns.

What to Watch Next

The transition from a speculative spot market to a contract-driven industry marks a structural shift in how computing hardware is funded and manufactured. Over the coming quarters, several milestones will signal whether this new paradigm will endure:

  • Finalization of Apple's LTA Terms: Confirmation of the finalized terms between Apple and the Korean majors—specifically the pricing formulas and indexation mechanisms—will set the baseline benchmark for how Google, Amazon, and Microsoft negotiate their client-device memory contracts through 2029.
  • Samsung Foundry’s Taylor Fab Progress: Watch for yield stability metrics and official high-volume manufacturing timelines out of Samsung's Texas fab. If Samsung can successfully scale 2nm turnkey chips for Meta without further drag on its operating margins, it will prove that its integrated device-plus-foundry model can challenge TSMC's packaging stranglehold.
  • The Health of Consumer Electronics: Monitor sell-through rates for mid-tier devices like the Galaxy A series following retail price adjustments. If consumer pushback triggers order cancellations, it will test whether memory suppliers can maintain pricing discipline purely on the back of hyperscale enterprise demand.
