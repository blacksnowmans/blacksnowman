Inside South Korea’s Moving Scam Crisis: How Big Brands and False Ads Exploit Homeowners

October 14, 2026

Moving day in South Korea usually begins with high hopes and a familiar ritual: opening a smartphone app to compare moving companies. Consumers type in their details, scroll past glossy brand logos, and sign a contract expecting the polished, professional service of a major corporation. The names feel trustworthy, and the promises sound unbeatable.

Yet when the moving truck finally pulls up on the actual day, those corporate logos are nowhere to be found. Instead, a crew of unfamiliar local subcontractors shows up, tossing boxes around and stuffing furniture haphazardly into the truck. Homeowners hand over hundreds or even thousands of dollars, often with no real idea of who is actually handling their belongings. Behind the glossy apps and household brand names lies an unregulated, fragmented network of subcontractors—leaving millions of movers wondering how a routine life transition became such a gamble.

The Illusion of the Best Price: Inside the FTC Crackdown

South Korea’s moving market relies heavily on information asymmetry, a vulnerability that two prominent moving service brands, Chakhan Eisa and Logen Eisa, recently exploited until antitrust regulators stepped in. The Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC), South Korea’s primary antitrust agency, slapped the two companies with a combined fine of 219 million won (about $160,000) alongside corrective orders for deceptive and exaggerated advertising.

Both brands flooded digital platforms with unverified claims such as "national lowest prices," "minimum discounts on all moving types," and "the most affordable moving service available." They even inflated their cumulative consultation and quote inquiry figures on search ads and official websites to create a false sense of overwhelming popularity.

Logen Eisa used a similar playbook, marketing itself as South Korea’s premier moving brand by using accolades like "Korea Customer Satisfaction No. 1" while deliberately concealing the obscure, niche organizations that actually issued the awards. Consumers were easily led to believe the title reflected objective industry-wide dominance. Worse still, Logen Eisa disguised simple customer quote inquiries as the total number of clients who had actually booked their services—conflating casual price-checking with actual paying customers.

These deceptive practices violated Article 3, Paragraph 1 of the Fair Labeling and Advertising Act, which requires clear, verifiable proof for claims of being "number one" or the "lowest price." For years, however, major corporations have calculated that the profits generated by misleading advertisements far outweigh the government fines.

Direct Antitrust Intervention and Soaring Consumer Complaints

The KFTC’s recent move marks a notable shift. It is the first major wave of direct, large-scale fines targeting the corporate headquarters of moving companies rather than smaller, individual franchise locations. In the past, government intervention was largely limited to establishing standard consumer dispute guidelines, leaving most grievances to be handled through individual mediation by the Korea Consumer Agency.

While the corporate offices insulated themselves from direct scrutiny, consumer complaints skyrocketed. According to data from the Korea Consumer Agency, formal grievance filings regarding moving services surged by about 59% over a two-year period, jumping from 603 cases in 2023 to 961 cases. Nearly half of these complaints involved outright breach of contract, followed closely by property damage and inadequate after-sales service.

Notably, Logen Eisa—the target of the recent KFTC penalties—ranked number one in total consumer complaints, with Chakhan Eisa trailing closely behind at the top of the grievance list. The statistics exposed a stark reality: behind the polished corporate facades, consumer protections in the moving industry were rapidly eroding.

The Sweet Temptation of Deceptive Advertising
Companies continue to run exaggerated ads because the revenue generated by deceiving consumers dwarfs the regulatory penalties. Millions of dollars pour in from clients misled by fake "No. 1" titles, rendering multi-thousand-dollar fines little more than a cheap cost of doing business. Slap-on-the-wrist punishments fail to deter corporate moral hazard. Only the full confiscation of illegal profits and the permanent market expulsion of repeat offenders will adequately protect consumers.

The Subcontractor Shell Game: Famous Brands, Unknown Workers

The deceptive practices of Chakhan Eisa and Logen Eisa are symptoms of a much deeper structural flaw. Consider Paran Eisa, another major brand that heavily features celebrity endorsements in its marketing. Customers routinely report being hit with unexpected on-site fee hikes, furniture damage, lost items, and unexplained delays. On moving day, the trucks that arrive often display Logen Eisa logos instead, and the workers rush through the job using casual methods that contradict company manuals.

This reveals the fundamental deception at the heart of the industry: consumers sign contracts expecting a high-end, corporate level of service backed by national brand equity. In reality, the labor is almost always outsourced to underfunded local subcontractors or temporary labor pools. The corporate headquarters pockets the marketing and sales commissions, transferring all practical labor liability down to ambiguous lower-tier entities.

Another household name, Yeonggu Clean, has similarly populated the upper tiers of consumer complaint lists over the past five years. Unfamiliar partner agencies—often never mentioned during the initial contract phase—frequently show up on moving day to deliver subpar service. While utilizing partner networks is not inherently illegal, concealing this arrangement during the sales process directly infringes upon the consumer’s right to make an informed choice.

A 2024 investigation by the Korea Consumer Agency into major moving platforms exposed widespread legal non-compliance. Under South Korean law, digital platforms are legally classified as mail-order intermediaries and must clearly state on their initial landing pages that they are not transaction parties. Major players—including KGB Moving, YES2424, Moving Lab, Tongin Express, Logen Eisa, Miso, Yeonggu Clean, and Jimsa—all omitted this mandatory disclosure from their main screens, with several hiding the terms away in obscure subpages.

Furthermore, the investigation found that during the quote-request process, not a single platform provided the exact detailed address of the moving provider, and only two—Yeonggu Clean and Paran Eisa—properly supplied business registration numbers. Others failed to provide basic corporate names or phone numbers, effectively acting as "ghost companies" impossible to track down when disputes arise.

The root of this dysfunction lies in the industry's extremely low barrier to entry. By late 2023, South Korea had 3,475 licensed freight transport brokerage businesses, of which roughly 67% were small-scale operations with a capital or asset valuation of 100 million won (about $72,000) or less. Combined with an overly complex web of direct, franchise, platform, and brokerage models, this structure ensures that when accidents happen, headquarters and local branches endlessly point fingers at each other.

Trapped on Moving Day: The On-Site Fee Trap

Moving day is high-stakes. Elevator reservations are locked in, building management schedules are strict, and previous leases must be terminated. Knowing this, moving crews frequently exploit the homeowner's lack of alternatives. Once the packing starts, workers often invent sudden excuses—claiming the volume of belongings exceeded the estimate or that ladder truck conditions are unsuitable—and demand hundreds of dollars in extra cash on the spot.

Because the customer cannot simply halt the move and unpack the truck, they have little choice but to pay. This bait-and-switch routine, where companies offer low estimates over the phone or via photos only to pressure the homeowner for cash on moving day, is an entrenched industry problem.

When victims try to complain to corporate headquarters, they are typically told to "resolve the issue directly with the on-site work crew." The crew, in turn, shrugs and claims they were just following orders from above. No one takes responsibility, leaving the consumer's wallet as the sole target.

Practical Checklist for a Safe Move

  • Insist on an in-person visit for estimates rather than relying on phone calls or photos.
  • Explicitly detail everything in the contract, including crew size, truck tonnage, ladder truck requirements, and extra fee conditions.
  • Cross-check business registration numbers to verify that the contracting headquarters matches the actual work crew.
  • Verify licenses through the official South Korean freight transport brokerage database (hugi.or.kr or similar authorized portals).
  • Document everything by photographing appliances and furniture before and after the move, and establish clear written damage compensation terms in advance.

What to Watch Next

As the KFTC moves to tighten enforcement under the Fair Labeling and Advertising Act—extending the lookback period for repeat violations from three years to five and increasing base penalty rates—the pressure on deceptive companies is mounting. However, lawmakers and consumer advocates argue that until South Korea introduces much higher penalty caps and a "three-strikes-and-you're-out" permanent market expulsion rule for habitual offenders, corporate giants will continue treating fines as a minor business expense.

What to monitor in the coming months:

  • Additional KFTC Sanctions: Watch whether antitrust regulators expand their investigations beyond Chakhan Eisa and Logen Eisa to penalize other major platforms and franchised moving networks for deceptive advertising.
  • Legislative Moves in the National Assembly: Look for proposed amendments to significantly raise the legal ceiling on fines for misleading trade practices, moving them closer to international standards seen in the US and Europe.
  • Platform Compliance Audits: Check if the Korea Consumer Agency follows up on its platform transparency surveys to enforce strict initial-screen disclosures for online moving brokerages.

Until structural reforms reshape South Korea's moving sector, consumers must rely on meticulous documentation, in-person estimates, and strict contract terms to protect themselves from an industry where corporate brands hide behind unaccountable subcontractors.
