Seoul’s 90-Week Housing Surge: Record Prices Meet an 80,000-Listing Deadlock

November 01, 2026

Picture sitting in a Seoul café, scrolling through the morning news in late October. On one screen, a headline flashes an unsettling milestone: Seoul apartment prices have climbed for 90 consecutive weeks, hitting an all-time high for annual growth since record-keeping began. On another, real estate boards show a bizarre counterpart: nearly 80,000 unsold listings are piling up across the capital like snowdrifts in an alley. Homes are supposedly more valuable than ever, yet nobody is buying. Transactions have practically flatlined, caught between government credit squeezes and stubborn homeowner asking prices.

For anyone standing outside Korea looking in, this paradox might look like ordinary post-pandemic market friction. But inside the country, it represents a high-stakes standoff involving lingering economic bubbles, heavy-handed government interventions, and the quiet desperation of families locked out of the housing ladder. Understanding how Seoul reached this point requires looking past the daily press releases and examining a property market where government policy and basic human psychology are locked in a permanent tug-of-war.

The 90-Week Ascent and the Politics of Blame

To understand the current tension, one has to look backward. The market momentum driving these numbers did not appear overnight. The persistent belief that Seoul real estate is an unassailable fortress traces back through years of speculative fever—including the years under former conservative leader Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration's credit-backed borrowing incentives left a legacy of hyper-inflated valuations. Now, under the progressive administration of President Lee Jae-myung, policymakers are scrambling to cool down a market that refuses to decelerate.

Unsurprisingly, the political fallout has been fierce. The People Power Party, South Korea's main conservative party, has seized on the 90-week streak to attack the Lee administration, accusing the government of crushing the dreams of working-class families through restrictive policies. Yet critics point out a glaring contradiction: the conservatives conveniently ignore that the structural imbalances fueling today's bubbles were heavily seeded during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure, when buyers were encouraged to leverage up and dive headfirst into debt.

For ordinary, non-homeowning citizens, these political crossfires offer little comfort. A 90-week uninterrupted climb means nearly two years of watching the horizon of homeownership recede. The constant drumbeat of rising prices breeds a deep-seated fear of missing out—the sense that if you do not buy right now, you will never catch up. Yet this momentum is increasingly decoupled from real economic growth, driven instead by a desperate flight of liquidity into a perceived safe haven.

When 80,000 Listings Meet a Frozen Market

The most revealing symptom of today’s market is not the rising price index, but the 80,000 unsold properties stacking up across Seoul. This is what real estate watchers call a "transaction cliff." Homeowners, convinced that their apartments should command peak prices, refuse to lower their asking prices. Meanwhile, buyers are frozen in place, hemmed in by stricter lending limits implemented by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport and terrified of buying at the absolute top of the market.

This paralysis is no longer confined to affluent southern districts like Gangnam. Even in traditionally working-class northern neighborhoods—areas historically known as "Todo-gang" (Nowon, Dobong, and Gangbuk)—properties are piling up with double-digit increases in inventory. Real estate agents report that even when owners list properties, nobody comes to look. Young buyers who stretched their finances to the absolute limit are trying to sell, but potential buyers cannot cross the stringent credit thresholds set by regulators.

When listings accumulate without sales, it usually serves as a flashing red light for the wider economy. Real estate transactions do not just affect buyers and sellers; they drive moving companies, interior renovation businesses, and local service industries. If this liquidity freeze persists, the standoff between stubborn sellers and cash-strapped buyers will eventually break, raising the specter of a sharp, painful price correction.

The Push for Supply: Ministry Plans Versus Seoul City Ambitions

Faced with mounting pressure, the central government is betting everything on supply. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport has pledged to accelerate housing construction, aiming to break ground on 31,000 units within Seoul before the end of the year under its August housing measures. The goal is to flood the psychology of the market with a clear message: help is on the way, and scarcity is ending.

Yet supply takes time, and a promise of thousands of apartments years down the road does little for a family struggling with rents today. To speed things up, the government has leaned into public-led development models—such as a 1,700-unit project in areas like Bulgwang-dong where private redevelopment is too sluggish to get off the ground. By stepping in directly to iron out disputes, public developers aim to secure housing without leaving vulnerable residents behind.

At the same time, local authorities are pushing their own aggressive agendas. The Seoul Metropolitan Government has called on the central government to revise laws to allow a 1.2-fold increase in floor area ratios for private redevelopment projects. While higher floor ratios boost project profitability for developers, critics warn of potential overdevelopment and a lack of necessary public infrastructure. Coordinating between the central government's focus on stability and the city's push for deregulation remains a delicate balancing act; any policy whiplash only hands speculators a roadmap to exploit.

The Splitting Core: Gangnam's Resilience and the Fading Reconstruction Dream

Even as the broader market stumbles, wealth continues to concentrate in familiar places. The once-untouchable market for older apartment complexes slated for reconstruction is showing deep cracks. High interest rates and soaring construction costs mean that the supplemental fees required to fund reconstruction projects have skyrocketed, crushing the appeal of what Koreans call *mom-tech*—enduring harsh living conditions in a decrepit building while waiting for a jackpot payout.

As a result, speculative enthusiasm for aging complexes has cooled significantly, with prices dropping by hundreds of millions of won in several areas. The era when any old apartment could guarantee instant wealth is fading.

Yet this localized correction has only deepened Seoul's internal divide. Even as residents in first-generation new towns like Bundang look at their own delayed reconstruction prospects and eye an exit, their capital flows almost exclusively toward one destination: Gangnam. Investors are abandoning fringe redevelopment projects in favor of absolute certainty in the city's most prestigious districts. This "flight to quality" widens the chasm within the capital itself, while leaving provincial real estate markets to wither under the weight of regional population decline and neglect.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves past the close of October, the path forward for the property market depends on a few critical developments:

  • The Breakthrough of the Inventory Standoff: Monitor whether the 80,000 piled-up listings begin to clear through voluntary price drops by desperate sellers, or if the transaction freeze hardens into a prolonged liquidity drought.
  • Execution of Public Supply Targets: Watch for tangible progress on the Ministry of Land's pledge to break ground on 31,000 housing units in Seoul this year, particularly whether public-led projects can overcome local friction.
  • The Central-Local Policy Coordination: Pay attention to whether the central government and the Seoul Metropolitan Government can reconcile their approaches regarding floor area ratios and regulatory easing without sparking new speculative waves.
  • Regional Inequality Metrics: Observe if policymakers begin to address the severe polarization between overheated Seoul sub-markets and collapsing provincial real estate ecosystems, where tighter regulations intended for the capital often inflict collateral damage.
