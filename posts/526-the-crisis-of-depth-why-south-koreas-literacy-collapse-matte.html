The Crisis of Depth: Why South Korea’s Literacy Collapse Matters

September 10, 2026

Your finger hovers over the smartphone screen, endlessly swiping. You find yourself adrift in a sea of short-form videos, interrupted only by a ping from KakaoTalk—South Korea’s dominant messaging app—before falling back into the bottomless pit of an Instagram feed. By the time you look up, the day has evaporated. We are consuming content at an unprecedented rate, yet there is a gnawing, paradoxical emptiness settling into our minds.

In the age of generative AI, many argue that the human brain no longer needs to think deeply. After all, why struggle to write a report or draft a project plan when a few lines of a prompt can generate a "perfect" result in seconds? Yet, this ignores a fundamental truth: AI itself relies on vast amounts of "raw data" written by humans to function. If even artificial intelligence requires high-level text to learn and produce results, what does that mean for the human brain? Ignoring the "input" of reading is not just a sign of nostalgia; it is a warning for our cognitive survival.

The Erosion of Literacy in a Digital Age

Across South Korea, there is growing alarm over a noticeable decline in "literacy"—not just the ability to read words, but the ability to comprehend complex context. Headlines frequently feature bewildered stories about students who confuse "geumil" (a formal term for "today") with "Friday," or who struggle to grasp basic concepts like the bidirectional nature of a "round trip."

The numbers support this anxiety. According to a 2024 survey by the Korean Federation of Teachers' Associations, the country's largest teachers' union, 91.8% of educators believe student literacy has declined compared to the past. Among adults, the situation is even more sobering. The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism’s 2023 National Reading Survey found that six out of ten South Korean adults did not read a single book in the past year. The average annual reading rate among adults has plummeted to just 3.9 books, and the overall reading rate has been cut in half since the survey began in 1994. Short-form media and dopamine-driven digital platforms are effectively eating away at our cognitive circuits.

The disparity is also deeply economic. The reading rate among households with a monthly income of over 5 million won (about $3,700) is 54.7%, while it drops to 9.8% for those earning under 2 million won (about $1,500). When asked why they don't read, most cite lack of time due to work or studies, or simply preferring smartphones and games. Our brains, soaked in the instant dopamine of digital media, are losing the stamina required for complex logic.

Reading as an Active, Evolving Dialogue

People often pick up a pen thinking, "I’ve read one book, so I can write one." This is a fantasy. As the saying goes, "You don't write a book because you've read one; you need to read a hundred to write one." Writing is the output; reading is the essential input. Without the massive, filtered intake of deep reading, there is no substance to output.

Books are not static collections of information; they are living organisms. Consider the classic adult fable Hope for the Flowers by Trina Paulus. A ten-year-old reads it and sees a simple story about caterpillars climbing a pillar. A person in their fifties who has just been forced into early retirement reads the same book and sees a crushing reflection of their own life spent climbing a corporate ladder that leads to nothing. The book hasn't changed; the life trajectory of the reader has. That is the power of deep reading: the text matures along with the reader.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • When the "input" of reading vanishes, human expressive and critical thinking abilities inevitably atrophy.
  • Six out of ten South Korean adults read zero books annually, favoring shallow, fragmented information consumption.
  • Books are living organisms; their meaning shifts and deepens as the reader grows.

Practical Steps to Reclaim Your Focus

To improve your literacy and analytical skills, you must move beyond the habit of skimming. Here are three strategies to sharpen your cognitive depth:

  • Abandon the Myth of Perfection: You don’t need to understand every word to benefit from a book. Focus on catching the "big picture" of a chapter. Summarize the author's intent in your own words before moving on. Think of it as a lifeline that keeps you from getting lost in a complex text.
  • The Power of Repetition: If you want to build true reading stamina, read one difficult book multiple times rather than ten easy ones superficially. This is like digging for water—digging one six-meter hole is far more effective than digging two three-meter holes. Each pass through the book reveals layers you missed before.
  • The "In My Pocket" Method: When you encounter a sentence that hits home, do not just scan it. Manually type it out or write it down. This act of transcription acts as a catalyst, etching the idea into your memory just as a photograph preserves a travel memory.

Choosing the Right Lens for Every Text

Different books require different approaches. When reading a novel, research the author’s background and era; knowing the context makes the dialogue and characters come alive. With essays, don't feel pressured to read every word; focus on finding one or two insights that you can "put in your pocket" and apply to your life. For academic texts, look for the "signature menu"—the core keyword that the author repeats throughout the book. Once you identify the backbone of their argument, you have mastered the essence of the work.

The state of our reading habits is a mirror. If we fill our minds with the fragmented, shallow stimuli of short-form videos, our ability to think will remain shallow. But if we commit to the slow, deep breath of reading, we invite the universes of others into our own. As we look ahead, the goal is not to become "well-read" for the sake of credentials, but to reclaim the capacity for reflection that modern technology has tried to dismantle. Tonight, pick up that dusty book on your nightstand—not to finish it, but to start a conversation with someone else's life.
