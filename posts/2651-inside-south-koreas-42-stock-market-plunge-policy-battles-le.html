Inside South Korea's 42% Stock Market Plunge: Policy Battles, Leveraged Bets, and a Widening Gap With Taiwan

November 23, 2026

For much of the past few years, South Korea and Taiwan appeared to be riding the same wave. Both economies depend heavily on semiconductors, with chipmakers accounting for more than 20% of market weight in each country, and both stood to gain from the global artificial intelligence boom. Yet by mid-2026, investors watching Seoul's benchmark KOSPI index were looking at a very different screen than their counterparts in Taipei. After peaking near 3,600 in June 2026, South Korea's main stock index tumbled more than 1,200 points—a drop of roughly 42%—while Taiwan's market held within an 8.5% pullback from its own highs.

While many market participants point to U.S. interest rates and global recession fears to explain Seoul's sell-off, a pointed Korean market commentary—drawing heavily on analysis from economist Choi Bae-geun—argues that domestic policy decisions and regulatory blind spots played a decisive role. According to that critique, rather than cushioning panic in a market heavily exposed to foreign capital, President Lee Jae-myung's administration took steps—and avoided others—that amplified volatility and accelerated capital flight.

A Tech Rally Divided: Seoul Drops While Taipei Holds

To understand why the divergence has rattled South Korean households, it helps to know how deeply equities now matter in the country. South Korea counts roughly 14 million retail stock investors, meaning swings in the KOSPI—the Korea Composite Stock Price Index, the country's primary equity benchmark—reverberate far beyond brokerage desks into everyday consumer confidence.

Comparing cumulative returns since late 2022, when the release of ChatGPT kicked off the global AI investment cycle, highlights how sharply the trajectories split. According to figures cited in the Korean commentary:

  • Taiwan's benchmark index climbed 191% over that period and stayed close to its peak, experiencing only an 8.5% adjustment.
  • The U.S. Nasdaq gained 119% over the same stretch.
  • South Korea's KOSPI rose 113% at its height, reaching the 3,600 level in June 2026, before dropping roughly 42% and slipping below the Nasdaq's cumulative return.

The commentary attributes much of this reversal to a 31.2% exit by foreign investors, who hold more than 35% of South Korea's total market capitalization. Foreign ownership across the country's five largest companies by market value fell by more than 15%. While government officials have emphasized that underlying corporate fundamentals remain sound, critics argue that pointing to global macro headwinds overlooks why a semiconductor-heavy neighbor like Taiwan absorbed similar global shocks without suffering a comparable collapse.

Market Mechanics: Leveraged ETFs, Short Selling, and Missing Buffers

Beyond macroeconomic sentiment, structural rules governing how shares trade in Seoul differ sharply from those in Taipei. South Korea permits individual stocks to rise or fall by up to 30% in a single trading session, three times wider than Taiwan's strict 10% daily price limit. During downturns, critics in the source argue, that wider band speeds up panic selling by triggering margin calls and forced liquidations—where brokerages automatically sell off investors' leveraged holdings to cover losses.

Short selling—a practice in which investors borrow shares to sell them and profit from price declines—also looms large in Seoul, with daily short-selling trading value averaging over 1 trillion won (about $720 million). The Korean commentary contends that without stronger safeguards, foreign and institutional traders can press downward momentum more aggressively in Seoul than in more tightly buffered markets.

The contrast in crisis management became especially visible during external shocks. When fighting involving Iran rattled global markets in early 2024, stock volatility in South Korea jumped to around 17%, nearly four times Taiwan's 4.5%. The commentary credits Taipei's stability to its National Financial Stabilization Fund: Taiwanese authorities deployed 500 billion Taiwan dollars (about $15.5 billion), purchasing equities and carefully pacing later sales so supply would not overwhelm demand. Taiwan used that stabilization mechanism ten times in a single year, whereas South Korea kept its own stock market stabilization fund—used only three times since 2000, during a household debt crunch, the 2008 financial crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic—on the sidelines.

Even more contentious was a new financial product introduced in Seoul on May 27. Just before a sharp drop in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, South Korean regulators allowed single-stock leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs), designed to deliver twice the daily return—or loss—of an individual company's shares. Critics argue this move poured fuel on the fire: as share prices fell, leveraged funds had to execute mechanical selling to rebalance their daily exposure, compounding downward pressure. During this period, volatility measured by standard deviation exceeded 38 in South Korea compared with 11 in Taiwan, and individual Korean investors lost an estimated 3 trillion won (about $2.2 billion) in these products.

"This is not a natural phenomenon. It is the result of the government's incompetence," the Korean commentary states, arguing that regulators approved high-risk products without reading market conditions while later distancing themselves from responsibility.

Tax Proposals, Fiscal Deficits, and the Politics of Rhetoric

The market turmoil is also tightly intertwined with domestic politics roughly 18 months into the presidency of Lee Jae-myung, leader of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea. South Korea's broader economy was already navigating fragile domestic demand following a late-2024 martial law crisis that had chilled consumer activity. Against that backdrop, the commentary takes aim at both the administration's fiscal direction and its public messaging.

On the fiscal side, the Lee administration has faced mounting scrutiny over how it uses public funds. Even as a semiconductor boom brought in 45 trillion won (about $32 billion) in tax revenue, the country accumulated a 32 trillion won (about $23 billion) fiscal deficit. Rather than pursuing structural reforms to dismantle entrenched economic privileges, the commentary argues, the government leaned on short-term measures such as "livelihood recovery consumption coupons" that added to public debt without raising real incomes for low-income households.

"Reform is not a matter of generating new income and handing it out as charity," economist Choi Bae-geun said in remarks quoted by the commentary, comparing the approach to past patterns under previous leaders including Lee Myung-bak, Park Geun-hye, and Mr. Yoon Suk Yeol, who was removed from office via impeachment and is currently imprisoned.

At the same time, talk of a "windfall tax" (a special levy aimed at recapturing excess profits in targeted industries such as banking and energy) alarmed global investors. According to the source, each time the proposal resurfaced, affected sectors swung sharply, reinforcing a perception among foreign funds that South Korea carries unpredictable regulatory risk. The commentary notes the irony that falling share prices ended up hurting working-class and retail investors—groups central to the Democratic Party's own political base.

Communication missteps deepened frustration. At a two-hour capital markets roundtable, President Lee remarked that "when a mountain is high, the valley is deep," characterizing the sell-off as a natural correction after earlier gains. Critics viewed the remark as detached, arguing that it brushed aside policy missteps and eroded trust among retail investors. While roughly 80% of the 20 market experts at the meeting reportedly flagged the absence of concrete measures—such as a 50 trillion won (about $36 billion) stabilization fund or tighter short-selling rules—officials debated whether state intervention was appropriate and reiterated that fundamentals remained intact. The KOSPI fell for five straight sessions afterward. The commentary also dismissed the government's "KOSPI 5000" special committee as political target-setting that encouraged rushed policies like leveraged ETFs rather than structural market fixes.

Asset Deflation and What to Watch Next

The stakes now extend well beyond equity portfolios. In South Korea, falling stock prices are colliding with a cooling property market, creating what economists call a "reverse wealth effect"—when shrinking asset values prompt households to cut back on everyday spending. In the second quarter, average household consumption expenditure fell 1.2% from the prior quarter to 3.677 million won (about $2,650). Meanwhile, roughly 24 trillion won (about $17 billion) that analysts had hoped might rotate out of real estate into equities failed to lift stocks; instead, both markets slid together, with Seoul apartment transaction volume and total sales value dropping by more than 40% between July and September.

For global investors and observers following South Korea's markets and politics, several concrete indicators will signal whether the slide stabilizes or deepens in the coming months:

  • Stabilization fund and ETF policy reversals: Watch whether financial authorities move beyond rhetoric to deploy the 50 trillion won (about $36 billion) market stabilization fund, curb single-stock 2x leveraged ETFs, or revisit short-selling rules and the ±30% daily trading band.
  • The fate of windfall tax proposals: Any formal retreat from punitive taxes on financial and energy firms—or, conversely, legislative steps to advance them—will heavily influence whether foreign capital returns to Seoul's largest stocks.
  • Third-quarter consumption and housing data: With household spending already contracting and Seoul apartment transactions down sharply, upcoming quarterly consumption figures will show how severely equity losses are feeding into broader domestic deflation.
  • Foreign ownership trends in chipmakers: Because semiconductors drive both the KOSPI and tax revenues, tracking whether overseas investors halt their exit from South Korea's top five companies will be a key test of whether market confidence is recovering.
