The Salary Secrecy Trap: Why South Korea’s Hiring Culture is Failing Both Companies and Workers

October 14, 2026

Every job seeker in South Korea is intimately familiar with the frustration: you find a promising role, you check the requirements, and then you hit a wall. Instead of a salary, you are met with vague phrases like "subject to company regulations" or "to be determined after interview." This practice of hiding the most critical piece of information—how much the job actually pays—is a chronic ailment in the South Korean labor market.

It is more than just a communication barrier. It is a fundamental inefficiency that treats a job seeker's time and energy as disposable. When over 60% of job postings refuse to disclose salary information, it serves as a sobering scorecard for just how far our labor market has drifted from transparency. The time has come to dismantle the outdated "blind hiring" culture and move toward a system where companies and candidates can engage as equals.

The Vicious Cycle of Salary Secrecy

From the perspective of a job seeker, a job posting is essentially a contract proposal. Yet, the reality of the Korean market is bizarre. Companies will demand every detail of a candidate’s life—past projects, career history, certifications, and sometimes even family background—while hiding the price of the labor they are seeking behind the vague curtain of "negotiable after interview." This is not merely an oversight; it is a structural imbalance where companies use information asymmetry to define applicants as the "weaker party" and exert power over them.

It is no longer rare for a candidate to go through multiple rounds of interviews, only to be offered a salary near the minimum wage upon reaching the final stage. This leads to deep feelings of betrayal and fosters a pervasive distrust in the entire recruitment process. Companies suffer, too. By failing to state a salary range, they attract a massive pool of unqualified candidates, forcing hiring managers to waste hours reviewing documents and conducting interviews with people who would never accept the actual offer. Ultimately, hiding salary is a strategic failure that multiplies costs for both sides.

HR managers often argue that "salary is internal confidential information" or that "it only needs to be revealed to the successful candidate." However, data shows that only about 4% of companies disclose starting salaries. This isn't about protecting secrets; it is about institutional inertia—doing things the way they have always been done. In an era where corporate transparency is synonymous with competitiveness, a fear of salary disclosure is a quiet admission that a company lacks the confidence to attract talent on its own merits.

Global Standards vs. Domestic Stagnation

South Korea’s current "Act on Fair Hiring Procedures" prohibits false advertising, but it stops short of mandating the disclosure of salary information. Companies have exploited this legal blind spot for years. Meanwhile, the global standard is shifting rapidly. The European Union’s "Pay Transparency Directive" and similar legislation appearing in various U.S. states have turned salary disclosure from an optional gesture into a matter of survival.

Under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, discussions on revising the hiring law have gained momentum. The logic is simple: salary disclosure is the bare minimum requirement to guarantee a young job seeker’s "right to know." We should be benchmarking countries like Japan, where employment laws force companies to specify wages and working conditions, with penalties—including public shaming—for those who fail to comply. We need a fundamental shift in perception: the price of labor is not a secret; it is basic market information.

Critics often claim that mandatory disclosure infringes upon a company's autonomy. However, countless studies have proven that transparency increases organizational trust and reduces turnover. If a company truly values talent, presenting the information candidates want most is the first step toward fair hiring.

The Value of Pay Transparency

According to a PwC report, companies with high pay transparency show a significantly stronger correlation between performance and rewards. For job seekers, it means focusing on roles that match their market value; for companies, it means cutting unnecessary recruitment costs. Salary disclosure is the most efficient investment in reducing the "trust cost" of hiring.

Strategic Disclosure: Pricing and Quality

The dilemma of whether to disclose salary essentially boils down to what kind of talent a company wants to attract. Disclosing a salary upfront is a "price-first, quality-second" policy. By stating a specific salary, you attract talent that fits that budget. In a market where 20-year veterans and fresh graduates often apply for the same roles, a clear salary range acts as a powerful filter.

The benefit is a significant drop in early turnover. When candidates know the salary, they self-select. This prevents the "I expected more" disappointment that leads to employees quitting shortly after joining. Conversely, the "quality-first, price-second" policy—hiding the salary until the end—leads to frequent, painful negotiations that often collapse, wasting the HR manager's time and energy.

Many managers fear that disclosing new-hire salaries will cause friction with existing employees who may feel they are being treated unfairly. The solution is "banding"—disclosing a range, such as $21,000 to $25,000 (28 to 33 million won), rather than a fixed number. This leaves room for negotiation while giving candidates a clear idea of their market worth. If existing employees ask why a new hire earns more, management should be prepared to explain the "procedural fairness" behind it. If the salary is based on specific, verifiable skills and experience, transparency actually builds trust rather than destroying it.

Designing a Compensation Culture

Not every company should use the same disclosure method. For companies with seniority-based systems—such as manufacturing plants or retail—where collective performance matters more than individual output, disclosing clear starting salaries is best for organizational stability. It prevents internal competition and sets clear expectations.

For modern, skill-based industries—like tech, fashion, or creative services—salary ranges by job group are essential. It is the hallmark of a true HR expert to be able to publicly define why a developer’s starting salary differs from a salesperson’s, and to link those differences to project outcomes and market value. When a company establishes clear salary tables, it sends a message that the organization is managed by merit and logic, not by the whim of a manager.

Transparency is the foundation of fairness. As we look toward the future, the integration of public labor data with job information to create industry-wide wage standards is an inevitable evolution. The era of hiding behind "company regulations" is ending. The companies that win the war for talent will be those that view transparency not as a burden, but as a core pillar of their brand. Transparency is a promise to the candidate, and for the company, it is the most honest way to prove its value.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on the National Assembly, where debates regarding the mandatory disclosure of salary ranges are currently stalled. The push by the Lee Jae-myung administration to modernize labor laws will likely face pushback from conservative groups, but as the youth unemployment crisis persists, the pressure to reform the "black box" hiring system will only grow. Companies that choose to disclose salaries voluntarily now will gain a significant head start in employer branding.
