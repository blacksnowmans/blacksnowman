Slow Art in a Fast City: What Seoul’s Autumn Exhibitions and Books Reveal About Endurance and Class

November 24, 2026

Walk into an art exhibition in Seoul on a weekend afternoon and the first sound you are likely to hear is not hushed conversation, but the soft click of smartphone shutters. In South Korea’s hyper-connected capital, cultural outings are often shaped by injeung-syat—literally "proof shots" snapped to verify on social media that you visited the latest gallery opening or bought the newest trending book. Yet a set of exhibitions and publications drawing attention across South Korea this autumn quietly pushes back against that rush.

From a hillside gallery in Seoul’s upscale Hannam-dong neighborhood to a massive glass retail complex in the financial district of Yeouido, and onto the country’s fall reading lists, four very different works are asking audiences to slow down. Two are solo shows by artists defined by stubborn repetition: 91-year-old Korean sculptor Kim Yun-shin and 20th-century Italian still-life master Giorgio Morandi. The other two are books that probe emotional and social endurance: Anne Carson’s genre-bending work on betrayal, The Beauty of the Husband, and South Korean sociologist Yeon Hye-won’s decade-long study of vocational high school students.

Taken together, they highlight a growing tension in South Korea’s cultural life. Beneath a surface obsessed with novelty and quick consumption, there is an appetite for art and writing forged over decades of isolation, physical strain, and overlooked labor.

A 91-Year-Old Sculptor With a Chainsaw Returns Home

In Hannam-dong—a Seoul enclave known for embassies, luxury boutiques, and international contemporary art spaces—Lehmann Maupin gallery is hosting New Continuity, a solo exhibition by Kim Yun-shin that is open to the public free of charge. Born in 1935, Kim is 91 years old, yet her reputation in South Korea carries an unlikely nickname: "the grandmother with a chainsaw."

Rather than relying solely on delicate chisels, Kim attacks heavy timber with a roaring chainsaw, slicing directly into the wood with a physical intensity that rivals artists a third of her age. For foreign visitors unfamiliar with modern Korean art history, her biography explains why this exhibition feels overdue. In her 40s, Kim left South Korea and settled in Argentina after becoming captivated by the trees and timber there. South American art circles recognized her talent long before her homeland did—so much so that Argentina is home to a dedicated Kim Yun-shin Museum. Only now is South Korea catching up and properly reappraising her career.

The core theme of Kim’s work is continuity: stepping into a relationship with raw wood, uncovering new forms hidden inside the grain, and situating those forms within the passage of time. Walking through the gallery, the sculptures create contrasting rhythms. Some pieces sit with heavy, grounded stillness; others slice through the air in dynamic curves. Those chainsaw lines trace where decades of solitude in a foreign country collided with artistic exhilaration. Through her long life in South America’s natural landscape, Kim distilled a philosophy centered on the interplay between what changes and what refuses to move—a reminder, as South Korean critics note, that sculpture is what remains after an artist’s own life has been carved away.

Inside a Shopping Mall, Quiet Bottles Push Back Against Noise

If Kim Yun-shin’s show commands awe through raw physical energy, an exhibition across town in Yeouido achieves the opposite effect through radical stillness. Inside The Hyundai Seoul—a flagship department store known for drawing huge crowds of young urbanites—the sixth-floor cultural space ALT.1 is presenting an exhibition of 20th-century Italian painter Giorgio Morandi.

In South Korea, major department stores double as prime cultural venues, placing fine art just steps away from luxury retail and crowded food courts. That setting makes Morandi’s work feel especially subversive. He spent his career painting humble, everyday objects—mostly bottles and bowls—without flashy color or dramatic narrative. A casual viewer might dismiss his output as endless repetition, yet Morandi used that narrow focus to conduct relentless experiments in light, surface planes, and spatial arrangement.

What sets this Seoul exhibition apart is its decision to display the actual physical objects Morandi used alongside his finished paintings. Standing in front of the vitrines and canvases, visitors can compare the real bottles—imagining their weight, surface texture, and gathering dust—with the hushed compositions on the wall. As the exhibition’s critical commentary puts it:

Morandi repeatedly painted the same still lifes while changing their arrangement and angle to study the composition of color and planes.

In a city saturated with high-speed digital imagery, seeing the real props next to the paintings turns a passive gallery stroll into a lesson in attention. Morandi’s quiet rooms ask viewers to pause and reconsider how much depth can be found simply by looking closely at the same ordinary thing day after day.

From Private Grief to the Class Divide in Korean High Schools

A similar demand for patience defines two books gaining notice on South Korean reading lists this season—one examining private heartbreak, the other dissecting a structural wound in Korean society.

The first is The Beauty of the Husband by Anne Carson, the acclaimed poet, essayist, translator, and classicist whose personal resilience—she took up boxing at age 76 and continues writing while living with Parkinson’s disease—mirrors the rigor of her prose. Described as a "fictional essay," the book resists neat categorization. On the surface, it deals with a devastating subject: a husband’s infidelity and endless lies. Yet rather than offering a straightforward chronicle of betrayal, Carson captures the paradoxical persistence of deep, complicated love alongside the pain. Her writing reads like a tightly wound psychological drama, showing how a mind confronts unbearable truth by forging order through sentences.

Where Carson looks inward at personal agony, South Korean sociologist Yeon Hye-won turns outward to expose a fault line that foreign observers often miss when looking at South Korea’s economic success. Her book, Boys of the Technical High School (Gong-eop-go Sonyeondeul), is the product of 10 years of close-range interviews with 30 students enrolled in industrial and technical high schools.

To understand why Yeon’s reporting hits a nerve in South Korea, it helps to know how rigidly tracked the country’s school system is. While elite academic high schools funnel students toward top universities and white-collar careers, vocational technical schools—known locally as gong-eop-go—are frequently looked down upon in a society obsessed with academic credentials. Yeon’s decade of fieldwork strips away stereotypes and documents the discrimination and inequality built into those teenagers’ daily lives.

Crucially, her analysis shows how the school system actively shapes these teenagers into "male laborers," pushing them to sacrifice or harden parts of their identity to fit narrow social expectations. The clash between what society demands of young workers and the reality they endure on the ground forces uncomfortable questions about how South Korea values manual labor. Yeon’s goal is not to put young people’s hardship on display for sympathy, but to bring voices long ignored into the written record and spark a serious public conversation.

Beyond the Photo Op: What to Watch Next in Seoul’s Cultural Scene

The common thread linking a 91-year-old chainsaw sculptor, an Italian still-life painter, a classicist dissecting a broken marriage, and a sociologist tracking working-class teenagers is an unwillingness to take shortcuts. Yet South Korean cultural observers point out a persistent irony: even works built on 10 years of fieldwork or 90 years of lived experience risk being flattened into fleeting social-media trends. Approaching a sociological record of labor inequality or a lifetime of sculpture merely for a quick photo op misses what makes them worthwhile.

As Seoul’s autumn cultural season unfolds, three developments are worth watching closely:

  • A broader reckoning with diaspora artists: Kim Yun-shin’s belated recognition at home raises the question of whether major Korean galleries and museums will dig deeper into the archives of older Korean artists who spent their prime years working abroad in regions like Latin America.
  • Public debate over vocational education: Whether Yeon Hye-won’s 10-year portrait of technical high school students remains confined to literary circles, or crosses over into policy discussions about youth labor and South Korea’s educational hierarchy.
  • Commercial spaces betting on stillness: Inside retail hubs like The Hyundai Seoul’s ALT.1, curators face constant pressure to deliver crowd-pleasing spectacles; how audiences respond to quiet, contemplative shows like Morandi’s will signal whether slow art can hold its own in Korea’s fastest-moving commercial spaces.

Art and serious writing are rarely meant to serve as background scenery. This season in Seoul, the works leaving the strongest mark are those that ask readers and gallerygoers to put their phones away, accept a bit of discomfort or silence, and meet the creator’s patience with some of their own.
