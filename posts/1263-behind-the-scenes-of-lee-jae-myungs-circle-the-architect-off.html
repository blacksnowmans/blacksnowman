Behind the Scenes of Lee Jae-myung’s Circle: The Architect Offer, Ideological Myths, and the Anatomy of Power

October 18, 2026

In South Korean presidential politics, the courtship of prominent academics usually follows an unspoken, cynical script. Campaigns seek out recognizable scholars to lend credibility to hasty manifestos, and in return, these professors—often derided in Seoul as "polifessors"—are quietly promised cabinet portfolios, ambassadorships, or lucrative directorships at state-owned enterprises. When candidate rosters are drawn up, public debate fixates on factional arithmetic rather than ideological architecture.

Yet the internal formation of the inner circle surrounding President Lee Jae-myung, candidate of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea, followed a fundamentally different trajectory. A vivid window into this process has emerged through the testimony surrounding economics professor Choi Bae-geun, who was approached not merely to rubber-stamp economic policy, but to assume the historical mantle of "Sambong Jeong Do-jeon"—the 14th-century scholar-statesman who drafted the institutional and philosophical blueprints for the founding of the Joseon Dynasty. The behind-the-scenes account of this vetting process sheds light on the real operational philosophy of President Lee's team, clears away persistent ideological smears, and reveals how key power brokers operated behind closed doors.

Dismantling the Ghost of the "Eastern Alliance"

For years, conservative opponents, led by the main conservative People Power Party and right-leaning media outlets, sought to tether Lee Jae-myung to a shadowy network known as the Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance (Gyeonggi Dongbu Yeonhap). Rooted in satellite cities southeast of Seoul such as Seongnam, Hanam, and Yongin, this collective of radical leftist activists and former student organizers had long been treated by political opponents as an ideological bogeyman, alleged to have infiltrated Lee's campaign machinery since his early days as mayor of Seongnam.

To anyone outside the insular world of South Korean factional politics, the smear sounded damning. But examining the actual grassroots history reveals a far more nuanced reality. The network’s origin was closely tied to authentic civic reform movements, including figures hailing from the southwestern city of Mokpo who were historically aligned with the "Donggyo-dong faction"—the veteran democratic loyalists of former President Kim Dae-jung. One such local activist gained national prominence after filing South Korea’s very first taxpayer lawsuit to halt the waste of public funds, later establishing community institutions such as the Dandelion School.

This civic drive took a dramatic turn when activists petitioned the Board of Audit and Inspection to investigate entrenched local corruption surrounding an apartment redevelopment project in Hanam, a suburban town of barely 130,000 residents at the time. The audit uncovered roughly 20 billion won (about $15 million) in illicit developer graft, sending shockwaves through the local establishment. This success sparked an experiment in everyday grassroots democracy, culminating in ordinary homemakers running as independent candidates and winning seats on the municipal council.

While members of the Gyeonggi Eastern Alliance had indeed operated within that same local ecosystem and urged civic leaders to run under radical party banners, Lee Jae-myung maintained a clear, deliberate distance. Internal vetting within Lee's team demonstrated that the future president refused to allow his administration to be hijacked by any single factional dogma. Instead, he treated local activist networks with a detached pragmatism, separating effective civic oversight from dogmatic political entanglements.

The Gatekeeper: A Year of Ideological Vetting with Jeong Jin-sang

Entering Lee Jae-myung’s campaign orbit required passing through a singular checkpoint: Jeong Jin-sang. Within Korean political corridors, Jeong was known as Lee’s alter ego—a confidant whose influence rivaled, or even exceeded, that of Yang Jeong-cheol, the legendary behind-the-scenes strategist who helped engineer former President Moon Jae-in’s rise to power.

Jeong had shared the political trenches with Lee since their municipal days in Seongnam. Meeting him was not a courtesy call; it was a grueling intellectual trial. In stark contrast to the opaque, privatized influence peddling that would later characterize the disastrous governance seen during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ultimately impeached, removed from office, and sent to prison—Jeong acted as a fiercely loyal, policy-first gatekeeper. His mission was to determine whether a thinker’s ideas could genuinely fuse with Lee’s pragmatic governance model.

"If you take state power, I will explain the tasks you must accomplish from the ground up, beginning with an educational revolution."

Rather than a swift, transactional alliance, what followed was a grueling, year-long dialogue between Professor Choi, Jeong, and Lee himself. The conversations bypassed the usual political trivialities and focused on deep structural crises: South Korea’s suffocating socio-economic inequality and the hyper-competitive academic rat race. Choi argued consistently that any macroeconomic reform would be built on sand unless the country dismantled the rigid, test-driven educational hierarchy that crippled household finances and distorted labor markets. The lengthy vetting proved that Lee’s team was willing to spend months testing intellectual compatibility rather than simply hunting for decorative campaign surrogates.

The "Sambong" Blueprint and the Refusal of State Privileges

When the campaign formally invited Choi into the fold, the role they offered was deliberately framed around a monumental historical precedent: Sambong Jeong Do-jeon. In Korean history, Jeong Do-jeon was the mastermind who not only helped King Taejo overthrow the corrupt Goryeo Dynasty, but also established the legal codes, civil examination systems, and urban layout of the new capital in Seoul. Lee’s inner circle was asking Choi to design the comprehensive state blueprint for an incoming administration, pledging to back him just as royal ministers supported the grand architect of old.

To road-test these structural concepts, the campaign proposed executing pilot projects through the Gyeonggi provincial government, which Lee was then governing. They offered immediate, tangible administrative resources, including a direct offer: if Choi recommended a capable protégé from his research seminar, the provincial government would immediately appoint that individual as a Grade 3 civil servant.

In South Korea’s notoriously rigid bureaucratic hierarchy, a Grade 3 post (equivalent to a deputy director-general) is a coveted senior executive rank that career bureaucrats spend decades striving to attain. It was an extraordinary concession designed to give Choi operational leverage. Yet, Choi turned the offer down immediately.

His reasoning was unequivocal: appointing a personal student to a high-ranking bureaucratic role, no matter how pure the policy intention, would reek of cronyism. It would hand political ammunition to opponents and impose an immediate ethical liability on Lee himself. This refusal to trade policy counsel for personal patronage provided Lee's reform platform with an unassailable moral baseline.

The "Blank Check" Offer from Policy Chief Lee Han-joo

The campaign’s determination to recruit top-tier visionaries was underscored further by a visit from Professor Lee Han-joo. Long regarded as Lee Jae-myung’s chief policy mentor and the leader of his policy planning machinery, Lee Han-joo approached Choi with an offer that amounted to an open checkbook:

"The governor told me that he will create whatever position you desire. If that position does not currently exist, he instructed me to create a new agency or post just to bring you on board."

In the cutthroat world of Yeouido—Seoul’s legislative district—such an offer is virtually unheard of. Scholars regularly compromise their principles just to secure a ministry seat or a brief vice-ministerial tenure. Choi’s response, however, cut directly against the grain of typical political ambition: "The position is not what matters. I am already doing my work. The goal is not to invent a title for me to help Governor Lee; let us reflect on what the nation actually requires."

This dynamic demonstrated that the core policy platform of the current Lee Jae-myung administration was forged not through the backroom barter of cabinet posts, but through a deliberate, high-stakes intellectual pushback between ambitious leaders and uncompromised thinkers.

What to Watch Next

Understanding these campaign-era negotiations provides critical context for evaluating the current political landscape under the Lee Jae-myung administration. As the ruling Democratic Party of Korea advances its legislative agenda against the opposition People Power Party, several key dynamics will reveal whether this foundational philosophy continues to hold:

  • Educational Structural Reform: Watch whether the administration moves forward with substantive, radical reforms to college admissions and public education financing, reflecting the core diagnosis that economic relief cannot succeed without addressing the educational rat race.
  • Senior Personnel Appointments: Observe whether President Lee maintains his historical aversion to creating vanity posts for campaign supporters, preserving the merit-based, task-oriented standard that governed his early vetting process.
  • Pragmatism vs. Factional Pressure: As the opposition attempts to revive outdated ideological labels against the administration, watch how firmly the presidential office adheres to non-dogmatic, results-oriented governance over factional loyalty.

Power can be either an instrument of patronage or a tool of systemic transformation. The quiet deliberations that preceded President Lee Jae-myung's victory remind us that the durability of an administration’s reform drive depends entirely on whether its foundations were drawn by self-serving courtiers or by architects willing to turn away the spoils of office.
