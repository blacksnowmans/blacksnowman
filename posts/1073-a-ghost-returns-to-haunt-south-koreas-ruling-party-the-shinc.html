A Ghost Returns to Haunt South Korea’s Ruling Party: The Shincheonji Infiltration Scandal

October 13, 2026

For years, invoking the name "Shincheonji" in South Korean politics was the ultimate weapon of partisan destruction. A secretive fringe religious sect whose clandestine operations became notorious during the COVID-19 pandemic, Shincheonji was repeatedly wielded by liberal politicians as a cudgel against their conservative rivals, alleging the right wing had traded policy influence for mobilized cult votes. Now, that same weapon has detonated inside the house of the accuser.

South Korea’s political sphere was thrown into turmoil following an explosive scoop by the business daily Maeil Business Newspaper. According to internal reports, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea—the party of President Lee Jae-myung—discovered evidence that between 30,000 and 50,000 members of the Shincheonji Church of Jesus had systematically enrolled as party members during a party convention vetting process. What makes the revelation particularly lethal is not just the staggering headcount, but the disclosure that an internal task force documented this infiltration and reported it straight to the very top echelon of party leadership.

The 50,000-Member Dossier and the Leaks at the Top

The controversy centers on an internal investigation spearheaded by Representative Park Sung-won, who chaired a special task force examining party membership registration. According to the reported findings, the task force's conclusions were briefed directly upon the instructions of Party Leader Kim Min-seok. The investigation allegedly confirmed that tens of thousands of sect adherents had flooded into the party's ranks, an organized voting bloc large enough to swing primary elections and reshape local leadership contests.

In South Korean party politics, active party members who pay monthly dues hold immense sway. They vote directly on parliamentary candidate nominations and internal leadership posts. A disciplined bloc of 30,000 to 50,000 disciplined voters represents an existential disruption to internal party democracy.

Yet the immediate crisis for the Democratic Party leadership is twofold: the reality of the infiltration itself, and the suspicious circumstances surrounding the leak. To reliably cross-reference a political party's member roll against a roster of Shincheonji adherents is virtually impossible through public channels. Such church rosters are intensely guarded, previously obtained only by state prosecutors and police during historical raids. The fact that party brass possessed such data—and that the volatile findings were subsequently leaked to the press rather than resolved transparently—has fueled intense speculation about internecine score-settling among rival party factions.

The Irony of an Accuser Trapped in Its Own Rhetoric

To grasp why this revelation has triggered such widespread disbelief, one must understand the political trauma surrounding Shincheonji over the past five years. During the conservative administration of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was ultimately impeached, removed from office, and is currently imprisoned—the Democratic Party relentlessly hammered the conservative People Power Party (now the main opposition) over alleged ties to the sect. At its peak, that pressure led to search warrants on conservative party member registries and even public demands for party dissolution.

Having established the doctrine that sect membership within a major political party constitutes an unforgivable breach of democratic integrity, the ruling party now finds itself trapped in a dilemma of its own design. Opposition conservatives have been handed an open goal: if a conservative party infiltrated by religious adherents was deemed corrupt and dangerous, what does that make a ruling party harboring as many as 50,000 of them?

This dynamic has provoked profound resentment among grassroots Democratic Party members. During bitter internal primary battles, reformist and independent factions were often smeared by rival party insiders as covert "anti-Lee Shincheonji" agents. Rank-and-file loyalists who bore the brunt of those smears now watch the upper leadership scramble to conceal a genuine, systemic infiltration on their own watch.

Comfortable Incumbency and the Decay of Reform

The scandal cuts deeper than factional squabbling; it exposes an ideological drift within the ruling bloc. Following the turbulent drama of Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment and the Democratic Party's return to power under President Lee Jae-myung, public expectations for deep structural reform were high. The party drew its authority from massive civic mobilization and pledges of radical transparency.

Yet critics argue that once ensconced in the presidential office and parliamentary dominance, the ruling camp fell victim to the traditional inertia of the South Korean political establishment. Inside the party, veteran politicians—particularly those tied to the aging "586 generation" of student activists from the 1980s—have faced sharp criticism for settling into cozy compromises under the banner of "centrist rationality."

"We succumbed to the market; we became the system." That self-indictment, once whispered by progressive critics, is becoming a mainstream grievance among supporters who feel the party has traded its reforming zeal for the perks of power.

When uncomfortable truths emerge, the instinct within the party apparatus and friendly partisan commentators has increasingly been to circle the wagons, dismiss investigative reports as media conspiracies, and attack critics for "firing upon their own troops." Rather than acting as neutral watchdogs, large swathes of the media landscape have traded rigorous investigative reporting for partisan stenography, further eroding public trust in democratic institutions.

What to Watch Next

As the Democratic Party attempts to contain the fallout, several critical developments will signal whether this scandal leads to a genuine cleanup or deepens the party's fractures:

  • The Accountability of the Leadership: Watch whether Party Leader Kim Min-seok and Representative Park Sung-won formally address the reported task force findings. If the leadership attempts to quietly bury the report without explaining how tens of thousands of members joined unchecked, demands for senior resignations could split the party hierarchy.
  • The October 25 Runoff Vote: The party faces a high-stakes internal runoff election on October 25. The Shincheonji revelation has already altered the internal campaign landscape, forcing candidates—including influential figures like Representative Jung Chung-rae—to take clear stances on factional alliances and candidate verification processes.
  • Conservative Counteroffensives: The main opposition People Power Party will undoubtedly demand formal parliamentary inquiries and prosecutorial scrutiny into the Democratic Party's membership rolls, mirroring the exact investigations liberals demanded of conservatives in past election cycles.

Power in a democracy is borrowed, never owned. If South Korea's ruling progressives believe their moral credentials from the impeachment era grant them immunity from the very standards they imposed on their enemies, the coming elections will deliver a harsh correction.
