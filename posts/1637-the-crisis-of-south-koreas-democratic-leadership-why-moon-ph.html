The Crisis of South Korea’s Democratic Leadership: Why Moon-Phase Approvals and Falling Ratings Spell Trouble for Lee Jae-myung

October 28, 2026

To watch the shifting political metrics in South Korea right now is to feel a quiet, heavy dread. Recent polling data reveals that the approval rating for South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung has dipped below the critical 40% threshold, while disapproval has surged to roughly 60%. For a ruling administration, these are not mere statistical anomalies or the routine noise of shifting public opinion. They are the blaring alarms of a serious counter-current in popular sentiment—one chillingly reminiscent of the early, structural fixations that ultimately led to the downfall and impeachment of former leader Park Geun-hye, and more recently, the downfall of his conservative successor.

When a chief executive loses the foundational momentum and trust of the public, the shockwaves extend far beyond the fate of a single administration. The direction of governance wobbles, administrative resources grind to a halt, and every legislative, budgetary, and personnel tool loses its force. As the classical maxim goes: the ruler is the boat, and the people are the water—the water can easily capsize the boat if it turns rough. Understanding the perilous reality facing the Lee Jae-myung government requires looking past the surface numbers to examine the deeper fractures within the ruling camp.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The sliding approval ratings for South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung have settled into a dangerous 60% disapproval vs. 35-40% approval deadlock.
  • Public alienation stems from three critical pillars: human empathy and trust, ideological promises, and tangible economic policies.
  • Leadership vacuums within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea are accelerating a tribal fracture, driving voters toward minor reformist parties like the Rebuilding Korea Party.

The Chilling Reality of Sinking Approval Numbers

The Sharp Cross-Pollination of ARS and CATI Polling Data

A granular look at the polling data reveals that the trajectory of public sentiment completely inverted in the latter half of 2026. Looking back at monthly averages from late 2025 through late 2026, the overwhelming momentum of the early administration has vanished. During the spring of 2026, automated response system (ARS) polls recorded positive evaluations reaching an impressive 67% and 65%, with telephone interviews (CATI) hovering in the low-to-mid 70s. Yet this high-altitude flight lasted less than a year before facing a steep cliff.

By July 2026, negative evaluations began an aggressive upward march in ARS data, crossing the threshold in August to reverse the gap at 43.6%. In subsequent surveys, positive evaluations stalled at a stagnant 41.5%, while negative sentiment climbed dramatically to 57.7%. This is not a minor statistical wobble; it is definitive proof that the axis of public evaluation has tilted firmly toward rejection.

The CATI data has proven equally unforgiving. By September 2026, negative evaluations pushed past positive ones well outside the margin of error, cementing a state of reversal for over a month. Realmeter surveys paint an even starker picture, with positive marks crashing to 35% and negative evaluations hitting a shocking 62%. Gallup Korea charts a similar collapse past the 50% disapproval mark. Regardless of the pollster or methodology, a profound loss of confidence is sweeping the nation.

📊 By the Numbers

Comprehensive major poll indicators show a locked structure of roughly 60% negative versus 35–40% positive evaluations.

Both ARS and CATI methodologies confirm a severe "golden cross" inversion starting around August and September 2026, where disapproval decisively eclipses approval.

The 60-35 Trap and the Echoes of Past Impeachments

The current 60-35 dynamic—60% negative to 35% positive—is far from arbitrary. Its true terror lies in how closely it mirrors the early trajectory of the fallen administration of Yoon Seok-youl, who is currently imprisoned following his impeachment. After taking office in May 2022, Yoon initially enjoyed a honeymoon phase backed by local election victories. Yet, within 100 days, his public approval collapsed into the 35% bracket. From that moment until his tragic end amid martial law turmoil, that exact 60-35 adversarial split remained rigidly fixed.

When support settles into a 60-35 lock, the political fallout is severe. During Yoon's tenure, his party suffered a crushing 17-point defeat in the 2023 Gangseo district by-election, followed by a total rout in the 2024 general election. Weekly tracker data shows the Lee Jae-myung administration trapped in that exact same danger zone. Entering the 100-day mark with these numbers matches the grim scorecard that preceded historical collapse.

In South Korean politics, approval ratings are the ultimate leverage. They force shifts in policy, personnel, and legislative tone. If the Blue House focuses solely on minor 2-3% statistical bumps while burning millions on endless polling, it is willfully blinding itself. The signal sent by the public since mid-2026 is unmistakable: a majority has begun issuing a vote of no confidence.

The Fatal Gap Between Job Approval and Personal Favorability

Analyzing job performance metrics requires looking at a companion metric: personal favorability. Drawing from historical experience within the Roh Moo-hyun administration, a vital pattern emerges. Even during fierce political attacks and low job performance scores, Roh enjoyed a personal favorability rating that consistently ran at least 15 points higher than his administrative marks.

Because a deep, underlying trust existed in his sincerity and life path, citizens maintained an emotional fallback position even when they disagreed with specific policies. The sentiment of "I like the man, but his policies are frustrating" provided an emotional reserve that could always bounce back during a crisis.

Yet, what is President Lee Jae-myung's reality today? While comprehensive personal favorability metrics are rarely published, if favorability has cratered alongside his 35% job approval, the administration faces an existential crisis. When personal trust vanishes alongside administrative approval, the emotional stepping stones required to turn public sentiment around simply evaporate. An administration depleted of its favorability capital will inevitably shatter at the first stiff wind.

The Three Pillars of Governance and the Restless Public

Policy Tools, Economic Backlash, and Reform Limits

Evaluating governance in a presidential system requires returning to basic human axioms: people react intensely to personal incentives, pursuing gain while avoiding pain; individuals possess internal yardsticks of right and wrong and will resist what they deem unjust even at a loss; and people naturally seek agency over their own lives. These three axioms form the harsh grading scale of public administration.

Governance operates across three dimensions: policy execution, ideological alignment, and personal trust in the leader. Policy execution directly impacts individual livelihoods and definitions of fairness. The Lee administration's pushes—such as commercial law reforms to eliminate the "Korea Discount" on stock valuations or youth budget allocations—are designed as state instruments. However, real estate and tax adjustments inevitably generate friction.

Even well-intentioned policies will trigger an immediate backlash if they fail to improve daily life or impose unfair burdens on specific groups. The moment the public concludes, "This hurts me" or "This is unjust," policy instruments stop acting as administrative fuel and turn into liabilities. The administration must coldly assess whether its ongoing reforms are hopelessly disconnected from practical public needs.

🔥 A Point to Ponder

When a ruling camp prioritizes lofty rhetoric over the practical economic realities and sense of justice held by everyday citizens, policy stops winning hearts and starts isolating the regime.

An administration that abandons its promised trajectory while failing to improve material living standards will inevitably be abandoned.

Ideological Drift and the Alienation of Core Supporters

The second dimension involves the ideological trajectory of the regime. While the conservative People Power Party emphasizes free-market competition, economic growth, and order, South Korea’s progressive core—anchored by the Democratic Party—has long championed balanced growth, welfare, social fairness, human rights, and equality. The voters who shared this vision for decades formed the "core support base" that powered Lee Jae-myung into office.

Yet the root cause of the current slide lies in a profound ideological disruption. Core supporters look at recent personnel appointments, budget allocations, and legislative maneuvers and ask: "Is this really the path we promised?" and "Why are we drifting away from our foundational commitments to equality and fairness?" This ideological drift fundamentally shakes tribal loyalty.

While winning over hardened opponents from the other side is notoriously difficult, losing internal allies who shared decades of values happens with terrifying speed. When an administration stumbles away from its promises, its core base turns away with chilling swiftness. A regime stripped of ideological identity is a ship without a rudder.

The Erosion of Personal Trust and the Permanence of Discontent

The third dimension is the most critical and lethal: personal trust in the natural person holding the presidency. South Korea operates under a powerful presidential system, meaning that administrative culture and political tone inevitably reflect the temperament of the chief executive. Observers vividly recall how the rough language and erratic style of the Yoon Seok-youl era drew constant criticism that the entire state apparatus had been dragged down to his personal level.

A similar dynamic is unfolding around President Lee Jae-myung. Early missteps in communication, political tone, and an arrogant handling of national tasks by the ruling circle have bred deep public distrust. Once a population decides that leadership is untrustworthy or inflexible, no amount of sophisticated policy packaging can break through the wall of public cynicism.

When personal trust collapses, trying to rescue approval ratings through policy alone becomes nearly impossible. The notion that "he is unlikable, but gets things done" only functions in the narrowest of contexts. Once public alienation hardens, every policy effort is dismissed as hollow spin. This widespread distrust of executive attitude is the most toxic headwind facing the administration.

The Splintering of the Progressive Tribe and Party Crisis

Plunging Party Ratings and Fractures Among Progressives

The president’s sinking approval has triggered an immediate collapse in support for the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s primary progressive party. ARS data shows DPK support tumbling from a robust 47% down to 39.7%, shattering the 40% floor. Conversely, the minor, reform-oriented Rebuilding Korea Party saw its backing rise from 5.7% to 8.8%. CATI surveys mirror this movement, showing the DPK sliding from 44.8% to 41.9% while the Rebuilding Korea Party climbed from 3.5% to 6.2%.

Even more shocking is the internal fracture within the progressive base. ARS data indicates that DPK support among self-identified progressives plummeted from 74.4% to 60.6%—a stunning 13.8 percentage point drop. Over the same period, progressive support for the Rebuilding Korea Party more than doubled from 10.5% to 22.4%. Middle-of-the-road moderate voters similarly abandoned the DPK in droves, falling from 49% to 41.9%.

These figures spell out an undeniable truth: nearly a third of the traditional DPK base has grown deeply alienated and turned away from current governance. Ignoring this exodus with the complacent assumption that "they will eventually come back" is hollowing out the foundation of the progressive camp.

💭 Personal Thoughts

An administration cannot succeed without restoring its tribal base. Instead of branding internal defectors as "traitors," the current DPK leadership must reflect on its own arrogance and ask why once-loyal voters are scrambling for lifeboats.

The Leadership Deficit of DPK Chief Min-seok Kim

Amid this tribal splintering, the leadership of DPK Chairman Min-seok Kim has yielded disastrous poll numbers. Survey evaluations of Kim’s job performance show positive ratings slipping from 33% down to 31% in ARS surveys, while negative marks climbed from 60% to 63.4%. Even within the progressive base, disapproval of Kim crossed the majority line, rising from 45.9% to 52.5%.

Kim took office boasting that he would raise approval ratings by 10 percentage points within 100 days. Instead, he has demonstrated a stunning capacity to alienate his own allies. A skilled political leader taking the helm should immediately reach out to rival factions, offering contrition and unity to bridge internal divides.

Instead, Kim’s leadership substituted genuine outreach with rigid orthodoxy, crushing internal dissent while pandering upward to power. This mirrors the exact dysfunction seen during the conservative People Power Party’s previous leadership struggles under Kim Gi-hyeon, whose subservience to the presidential office led his party to historic defeats. A leadership devoid of inclusive vision is accelerating the fragmentation of the progressive camp.

The Arrogance of Power and Forgotten Historical Lessons

Political science recognizes the concept of voter mobility—the shifting of allegiances away from a ruling party due to policy failures or political incompetence. A well-established political maxim dictates that a ruling party’s legislative seat count roughly converges on the president’s approval rating multiplied by three. If approval stays trapped at 35%, future general election seats could easily plummet toward the 100-seat danger line.

When voter mobility is high, even a minor policy misstep causes the entire regime to wobble, stripping away political productivity. A healthy core support base should act as a shock absorber during tough times. Yet, intoxicated by power, the current ruling circle has fallen straight into the trap of seeking more control, faster and longer.

Restoring public trust requires humility before historical lessons. A ruling party surrounded only by cheerleaders too terrified to speak truth to power cannot weather rough political seas. Without abandoning institutional arrogance, the administration faces a bitter harvest of judicial risks, public skepticism, and drifting national projects.

The Rise of Lifeboats and the Progressive Dilemma

The Rebuilding Korea Party as a Political Lifeboat

The surge in negative evaluations for President Lee and the concurrent bounce in support for the Rebuilding Korea Party are two sides of the same coin. Initially launched as an aggressive "icebreaker" vessel dedicated to prosecutorial reform and regime confrontation, the Rebuilding Korea Party has shifted character. It is now functioning as a "lifeboat" for disillusioned progressive voters fleeing the DPK.

As the massive vessel of the Democratic Party appears to take on water amid policy drifts and leadership incompetence, progressive voters are jumping ship. For those exhausted by the Lee administration's trajectory, minor reformist alternatives offer a temporary political sanctuary.

Yet a lifeboat is only an emergency craft for surviving a sinking ship; it can never replace a massive fleet capable of navigating the open ocean. Its organizational and numerical limits prevent it from steering national governance. The tragic reality of voters leaping from a floundering flagship into lifeboats encapsulates the current crisis of the South Korean progressive movement.

Contradictions and Mixed Motives Surrounding Party Mergers

Beneath the rise of minor reformist parties lies a deep-seated internal contradiction regarding potential mergers with the DPK. Long-standing supporters of minor reform parties often view integration favorably, believing that progressive consolidation is essential for political stability.

Conversely, the brand-new arrivals who fled to these minor parties out of sheer frustration with Lee Jae-myung and the DPK leadership view integration with utter hostility. Their sentiment is simple: "We risked our lives jumping off a sinking ship to reach this lifeboat; why would you tie this lifeboat back to the wreck?"

This identity crisis continues to generate severe friction within the progressive ecosystem. Without addressing the root causes of voter alienation, administrative officials risk turning every political maneuver into a desperate exercise in rearranging deck chairs on a sinking vessel.

What to Watch Next

As South Korean politics heads into the next legislative cycle, several concrete indicators will determine whether the Lee administration can pull out of its tailspin or face terminal decline:

  • Upcoming Polling Trajectories: Watch whether approval ratings manage to break back above the critical 40% floor in major independent polls (such as Gallup Korea and Realmeter), or if the 60% disapproval ceiling solidifies permanently.
  • Intra-Party Dissent and Leadership Reshuffles: Monitor upcoming internal DPK meetings for open challenges against Chairman Min-seok Kim. A refusal to alter party leadership dynamics will signal whether the ruling camp intends to double down on its current insular strategy.
  • Legislative Compromises on Economic Bills: Keep a close eye on how the administration handles controversial economic legislation, particularly corporate law revisions and youth welfare budgets. Any willingness to negotiate modifications with the conservative opposition will show whether the Blue House understands the limits of executive overreach.
  • Third-Party Realignments: Track whether minor factions like the Rebuilding Korea Party choose to formally merge with the DPK or chart an independent course ahead of local elections, which will measure the true extent of tribal fracturing on the left.
