The 36.5% Warning: Why South Korea’s President is Losing His Core Base

September 30, 2026

The latest figures from News Tomato, a prominent South Korean polling agency, paint a grim picture for the administration: the President’s approval rating has slumped to 36.5%, while the disapproval rating has climbed to 60.3%. To the casual observer, this might look like a typical weekly fluctuation in the volatile world of Korean politics. But for those watching the long-term trends, these numbers are not a mere ripple—they are the sound of a structural foundation cracking.

What makes this particular decline catastrophic for the ruling administration is not just the total percentage, but who is walking away. The 40-to-50-year-old demographic—historically the bedrock of political stability and engagement in Korea—has turned its back, with disapproval rates in this cohort hitting 57% and 60.4% respectively. Even in the Honam region (the southwestern provinces), which is the traditional power base for the opposition Democratic Party of Korea (the liberal-leaning main opposition party), the President’s support is faltering, squeezed by a 51.2% disapproval rate.

The Erosion of the Political Heartland

In South Korean politics, the "4050" generation is often viewed as the country’s civic backbone. When this group shifts, it signals a deep-seated exhaustion with the current trajectory of state affairs. The data shows that the President’s approval among 40-somethings is just 41.4%, and among 50-somethings, it is a mere 37.3%.

Perhaps most alarming for the administration is the reaction of the moderate middle. Roughly 6 out of 10 citizens have expressed dissatisfaction with the President’s recent press conference. When disapproval spills over into non-partisan demographics, it suggests that the administration has lost the "benefit of the doubt" that typically insulates a leader during their term. The cold reality is that this decline is not the result of external attacks or political sabotage; it is the result of an administration that has failed to meet the benchmarks it set for itself, ignoring the mounting economic pressures on the average household.

The Paradox of an Idle Opposition

The irony of the current political landscape is that the opposition, the Democratic Party, is not leading because it is offering a bold new vision. In recent party favorability polls, the Democratic Party sits at 36.9%, just behind the ruling People Power Party (South Korea's main conservative party) at 38.2%. The opposition has been plagued by its own scandals and lack of clear alternatives, yet the government’s incompetence is so stark that it acts as a "reflexive gain" for the opposition. Voters are not flocking to the Democrats because they are inspired; they are gravitating there because the ruling party has made governing look like an impossible task.

The "Rhee Si-young" Controversy and the Mirror of Public Opinion

A recent flashpoint in the national discourse involves Rhee Si-young, a prominent writer and former political strategist known for his candid, often sharp critiques of the current administration. When Rhee famously described the President’s policy direction as a "path to certain failure," he was met with fierce backlash from the President’s core supporters—the "Pro-Lee" faction—who labeled him a "suicide bomber" of the political movement.

The establishment saw his words as a betrayal, but the public saw them differently. In a striking disconnect from political elites, 58% of the public characterized Rhee’s comments as "sound criticism," while only 30.9% viewed it as "groundless undermining of the President." Even in the Gyeongsang provinces, a stronghold for the conservative People Power Party, 66.7% of respondents agreed that Rhee’s critique was valid and necessary.

This suggests that while the political class is busy bickering over factional loyalty, the average voter is looking for someone—anyone—to voice the frustrations they feel in their daily lives. The fact that the public is more receptive to a critic than to the administration’s own messaging is a damning indictment of the government’s refusal to engage in self-reflection.

The Cost of Closing Ears

The current state of Korean politics is defined by an inability to absorb internal dissent. There was a time, during the Roh Moo-hyun administration, when intense, heated debate over major issues like the Korea-U.S. Free Trade Agreement was treated as a vital part of democracy. Today, the ruling party and the administration treat any deviation from the official line as an act of treason or division.

This "echo chamber" politics has real-world consequences. With youth unemployment stubbornly high and Seoul real estate prices continuing to strain household budgets, the political focus remains stuck on "us vs. them" narratives. When an administration stops listening to its own critics, it eventually stops hearing the warnings of the people.

What to Watch Next

  • Local Election Maneuvering: With the next local elections on the horizon, watch to see if the ruling party attempts a "personnel refresh" to distance itself from the President, or if they double down on current policies.
  • Policy Pivots: If the approval rating slides into the sub-30% range, look for sudden, desperate shifts in policy, particularly regarding housing or labor, as the government attempts to stem the bleeding.
  • Opposition Unity: Monitor whether the Democratic Party can successfully translate these "reflexive" support gains into actual policy momentum, or if their internal divisions prevent them from capitalizing on the government's weakness.

The history of Korean politics has shown time and again that when a power structure stops listening to the public, the public eventually finds a way to force a change. For this administration, the 36.5% mark is not just a statistic—it is the final warning before the conversation shifts from "critique" to "judgment."
