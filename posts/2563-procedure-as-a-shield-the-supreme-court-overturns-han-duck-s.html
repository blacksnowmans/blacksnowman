Procedure as a Shield: The Supreme Court Overturns Han Duck-soo’s Insurrection Sentence

November 20, 2026

Just before half past nine on the night of December 3, 2024, Han Duck-soo, then serving as South Korea’s prime minister, walked into the presidential office. In his hands were documents detailing a decree that would suspend regular constitutional governance. Outside, the machinery of an unconstitutional martial law order was already beginning to turn. Yet over the next forty-nine minutes, the country’s second-highest executive official did not mount a collective cabinet resistance or demand an immediate halt. Instead, he made phone calls to assemble cabinet ministers to fulfill a legal quorum.

That forty-nine-minute window became the central battleground of modern South Korean constitutional law. On November 19, 2026, the Supreme Court of Korea delivered an en banc decision that upended the legal aftermath of that night. In a 9-to-3 majority ruling, the court threw out a 15-year prison sentence previously handed to Han for his role in the insurrection, remanding the case to the Seoul High Court. The majority concluded that Han’s actions prior to the formal declaration of emergency martial law—specifically advising that a State Council meeting be convened and working to gather the required quorum—constituted a legitimate execution of official duty under the Constitution and the Martial Law Act, and thus should be treated as acquitted of insurrection charges.

The ruling reversed what had been a steady trajectory toward severe punishment: Han had received a 23-year sentence in the first trial and 15 years in the appellate court. While supporters of the decision frame it as a defense of strict statutory interpretation, critics and legal scholars view it as a dangerous precedent that divorces legal procedure from substantive justice.

A 9-to-3 Reversal: How the Supreme Court Split the Timeline

To understand the court’s decision, one must look at how the bench dissected the chronology of December 3, 2024. Under Article 89 of the South Korean Constitution, the declaration of emergency martial law requires formal deliberation by the State Council, the country’s top executive cabinet body. The Supreme Court majority anchored its logic on this exact requirement. Because the law mandates cabinet deliberation, Han’s recommendation to hold the meeting and his efforts to gather ministers were interpreted as attempts to follow statutory procedure, not an act of rebellion.

The majority drew an uncompromising line at 10:18 PM—the exact moment emergency martial law was formally declared. Under this framework, actions taken after the declaration could warrant finding an intent to subvert constitutional order, but actions taken before that moment were deemed lawful bureaucratic conduct. In essence, the majority concluded that seeking to satisfy constitutional requirements cannot be treated as a criminal act of insurrection, invoking the principle of in dubio pro reo—that ambiguous criminal intent must be interpreted in favor of the defendant.

This reasoning has ignited fierce pushback. Constitutional experts argue that by treating the preparation of an unconstitutional decree as ordinary civil service due diligence, the highest court has provided future authoritarian maneuvers with a procedural blueprint. If an executive official diligently checks procedural boxes while enabling an unconstitutional power grab, that official can now claim immunity under the mantle of administrative routine.

"Quorums and Signatures as Weapons": Justice Oh Kyung-mi’s Dissent

The counterweight to the majority ruling came from Justice Oh Kyung-mi, who penned a searing minority opinion characterizing the majority’s argument as a "logic that grants immunity."

Justice Oh focused on the specific nature of a self-coup—an assault on constitutional democracy orchestrated not by rebel soldiers from the outside, but by an incumbent head of state. In such scenarios, anti-constitutional acts almost invariably masquerade behind the outward forms of official executive administration. Procedural compliance, she argued, is not proof of innocence; it is the very mechanism by which an illegitimate takeover seeks public legitimacy.

"The procedure of a State Council meeting is a dangerous weapon that perpetuates insurrection," Justice Oh wrote, emphasizing that "not only guns and knives, but quorums and signatures are also weapons of insurrection."

Justice Oh attacked what she termed the majority’s "scissors jurisprudence"—the artificial severance of the timeline at 10:18 PM. She pointed out the absurdity of assuming an official was acting as an upright civil servant at 10:17 PM, only to spontaneously transform into an accomplice to insurrection two minutes later. By viewing the collection of signatures and the gathering of quorums in total isolation from the unlawful martial law order they were meant to authorize, Justice Oh argued that the majority ignored the substantive contribution Han made to the plot.

She also addressed the majority’s reliance on the presumption of innocence. The doctrine of in dubio pro reo exists to ensure strict evidentiary scrutiny, she noted, not to sanitize the administrative machinery of a coup into legitimate official duty.

Forty-Nine Minutes: The Timeline of December 3, 2024

The debate between the majority and minority rests upon the minute-by-minute timeline inside the presidential office during the final hours of December 3, 2024:

  • 9:29 PM: Han entered the presidential office and spoke with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, raising the necessity of meeting a State Council quorum before any martial law decree could be issued.
  • 9:37 PM: Han called a cabinet member who was on the road, urging them to hurry to the office to meet the headcount.
  • 9:37 PM – 10:16 PM: Over more than forty minutes, having already seen the draft proclamation, Han raised no public objection, did not convene arriving ministers to organize resistance, and focused entirely on the procedural logistics of securing a quorum.
  • 10:16 PM: Mr. Yoon finished delivering his unilateral remarks to the assembled officials.
  • 10:18 PM: The emergency martial law decree was officially announced.

The Supreme Court majority placed significant weight on an earlier Constitutional Court decision from March 24, 2025, which had dismissed the National Assembly’s impeachment motion against Han. However, Justice Oh pointed out a critical gap: when the Constitutional Court issued that dismissal, investigators had not yet obtained internal CCTV footage from the presidential office. That footage, analyzed in subsequent criminal proceedings, captured Han's active coordination to complete the quorum, presenting a very different picture from the passive bystander narrative accepted in the earlier impeachment trial.

The divergence is especially stark given the broader political context. On April 4, 2025, the Constitutional Court unanimously dismissed Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol from the presidency, declaring his decree a flagrant violation of the basic democratic order. Mr. Yoon was subsequently arrested and remains imprisoned. For many observers, it is contradictory that the head of state sits in prison for an unconstitutional coup, while the prime minister who actively furnished the legal formalities for that coup has had his criminal culpability wiped clean by the top court.

Precedent and Double Standards: From 2014 to the Remand Bench

The Supreme Court’s leniency toward Han stands in sharp contrast to South Korea’s prior landmark insurrection cases. In the 2014 case involving former lawmaker Lee Seok-ki (Supreme Court Decision 2014Do10978), the judiciary maintained an exceptionally strict threshold for crimes against the state. In that ruling, the court held that instigating the overthrow of the constitutional order through violence exceeded political freedom, convicting the defendant of inciting insurrection based on ideological meetings and radical rhetoric, even in the absence of an executed physical operation.

On December 3, 2024, by contrast, the martial law decree was actually issued. Heavily armed military forces descended on Yeouido and stormed the National Assembly building. The disruption of normal constitutional governance was not theoretical rhetoric; it was underway. What stopped the full execution of the coup was not administrative restraint from the cabinet, but unarmed citizens who gathered in freezing conditions to block soldiers with their bodies, aided by field-level soldiers who showed restraint. As Justice Oh’s dissent noted, the court itself exists today because citizens placed themselves between military rifles and the legislature.

By applying a forgiving standard of administrative procedure to an actualized executive coup while historically applying draconian standards to political speech, the Supreme Court has opened itself to charges of a double standard that shields high-ranking state bureaucrats.

What to Watch Next at the Seoul High Court

The immediate consequence of the Supreme Court's ruling is that Han Duck-soo’s case returns to the Seoul High Court for a retrial. Observers must watch two critical legal and structural dynamics as the remand unfolds:

  • The Scope of Remand at the High Court: The Seoul High Court is bound by the Supreme Court’s legal determinations on the pre-10:18 PM timeline. The appellate judges will have to decide whether any surviving post-declaration conduct by Han carries independent criminal liability, or whether the entire insurrection charge will result in an effective acquittal or a drastically reduced sentence.
  • Administrative Precedent for Bureaucrats: The decision leaves behind a contentious legal framework for South Korea's civil service. With the ruling establishing that formal procedural acts—such as assembling quorums and signing documents for an unlawful decree—can be defended as legitimate official duties, legal scholars and lawmakers are debating whether legislative reforms are necessary to define administrative complicity during constitutional crises.

The remand battle at the Seoul High Court will demonstrate how South Korea’s judicial system resolves the tension between procedural formalism and constitutional defense. Whether the lower court strictly confines itself to the Supreme Court's narrow legal boundaries or incorporates the severe warnings laid out in the dissenting opinion will shape the legal accountability of executive officials for years to come.
