When Police Raid the Studio: South Korea’s Ruling Party Fractures Over YouTube Commentators

October 20, 2026

In a brightly lit studio in Seoul, cameras were rolling as political commentators dissected party strategy, electoral odds, and internal rivalries. In South Korea, where political YouTube channels routinely draw hundreds of thousands of live viewers, such broadcasts are not merely sideshows—they are where the heart of political mobilization beats. But what began as familiar election banter during the ruling Democratic Party of Korea’s internal convention recently turned into a scene out of a legal drama. Police officers swept into media studios, executing search warrants and seizing hard drives, mobile phones, and broadcast archives.

The raids sent shockwaves through South Korea’s political establishment. The official trigger? Allegations that popular progressive commentators instructed viewers on how to manipulate opinion polls during the Democratic Party’s internal leadership race. Yet, far from being seen as a routine criminal probe, the aggressive search warrants have sparked a fierce debate over state overreach, freedom of expression, and an escalating civil war inside the ruling party itself.

From Livestream Rhetoric to Police Search Warrants

To understand why a police raid on a digital media office has caused such an uproar, one must understand the unique weight of "new media" in South Korean politics. Outlets like Park Si-young TV, operated by political strategist Park Si-young, are not fringe operations. They function as critical public plazas where progressive voters, party members, and lawmakers interact outside the traditional mainstream press.

The current law enforcement action centers on accusations that commentators on these channels violated election laws or engaged in business interference by allegedly guiding supporters on how to respond falsely to opinion polls during internal party primaries. During competitive primaries, commentators often passionately urge supporters to mobilize, answer phone surveys promptly, or ensure their demographic voices are represented.

However, law enforcement authorities elevated these on-air comments into grounds for formal criminal investigations, executing search-and-seizure warrants against Park Si-young Co., Ltd. and associated figures. Critics argue that using physical police force to penalize political rhetoric voiced on a broadcast sets a dangerous precedent for South Korea’s public square.

Former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook expressed deep frustration over the heavy-handed law enforcement response, lamenting, "I really don’t know why they need to make news out of things like this." For Choi and other progressive observers, the spectacle of flashing press cameras and police raids over casual broadcast remarks appears less like a search for truth and more like a theatrical display designed to chill political commentary.

An Internal Civil War: When the Ruling Party Sues Itself

Perhaps the most troubling aspect of this drama is where the legal complaints originated. This was not a weapon forged by the conservative opposition, the People Power Party. Instead, the criminal complaints were filed by factions within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea following its intense internal leadership convention.

Under South Korea's current political landscape—where President Lee Jae-myung leads the government following the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol—the Democratic Party holds substantial political authority. Yet, the aftermath of the party's internal election has left bitter wounds. Rather than reconciling after the votes were tallied, rival factions turned to police and prosecutors to settle political scores.

Lee Ji-eun, head of the Democratic Party’s local chapter in Seoul’s Mapo District, voiced heartbreak over the unfolding conflict, calling the situation deeply regrettable. She questioned the logic of a scenario where figures within the winning camp filed criminal complaints against commentators and colleagues who had supported rival candidates during the primary.

In South Korea, political disputes are increasingly transformed into criminal matters—a phenomenon local analysts refer to as the "judicialization of politics." Instead of using internal party arbitration or public debate to handle primary friction, political actors routinely fire off criminal complaints. When law enforcement agencies enthusiastically act on these filings, internal party democracy is effectively handed over to police investigators.

Memos, Factions, and the Ghost of a 'YouTube Blacklist'

As details of the investigation emerge, political insiders point to a more sinister underlying tension: the controversy surrounding the so-called "Kim Min-seok notebook."

Kim Min-seok, a prominent senior lawmaker and key leadership figure within the Democratic Party, reportedly maintained detailed notes and memos regarding party figures and media strategy around the time of the convention. Suspicions have exploded among progressive commentators that these internal notes are being used as a reference to marginalize and target specific media voices who refused to line up behind particular party elites.

Lee Hye-yeon, a senior consultant at Park Si-young TV, publicly highlighted these concerns, pointing out that the timing and targets of the police raids suspiciously align with rumors swirling around Kim Min-seok’s memos. "There is a very strange odor coming from this notebook of party leadership," Lee noted, questioning whether law enforcement was acting as an enforcer for party insiders seeking to discipline independent media.

This suspicion taps into a deep historical nerve in South Korea: the fear of state-sponsored political "blacklists." Progressive commentators recall with bitterness the systematic blacklisting of journalists, artists, and media figures during past conservative administrations, including during Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure before his removal from office. The idea that a progressive ruling party might now be overseeing its own digital "YouTube blacklist"—using police investigations to intimidate friendly but non-compliant commentators—has horrified party regulars and free-speech advocates alike.

What to Watch Next

The police raids on progressive YouTube channels mark a crucial test for South Korean political culture. When political speech and internal party primary enthusiasm are subjected to search warrants and criminal charges, the boundaries of democratic participation begin to shrink rapidly.

For international observers monitoring South Korean politics, several concrete developments will signal where this issue is headed next:

  • Democratic Party Leadership Intervention: Watch whether President Lee Jae-myung and senior party leaders step in to mediate the internal complaints, or whether they allow factional score-settling to continue through the criminal justice system.
  • The Scope of the Police Investigation: Pay attention to whether the police expand their search warrants to other progressive media channels, or if prosecutors ultimately decline to indict commentators over broadcast remarks.
  • Legislative Reform on Primary Rhetoric: Observe whether the National Assembly considers legal clarifications to prevent routine political commentary and voter mobilization during party primaries from being treated as criminal offenses.

Ultimately, a healthy democracy relies on robust, sometimes boisterous political debate. Settling internal primary grudges through police raids on broadcast studios risks damaging the very open public square that built the ruling party’s political foundation in the first place.
