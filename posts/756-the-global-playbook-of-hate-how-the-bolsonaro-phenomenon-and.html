The Global Playbook of Hate: How the Bolsonaro Phenomenon and MAGA Reached South Korea

October 06, 2026

A strange kind of political déjà vu has settled over South Korea. Across the globe, intense political upheavals once thought confined to distant shores—most notably in Brazil—are now echoing through the daily lives of ordinary South Koreans. The rise of right-wing populism, the aggressive politicization of religion, and the spread of unverified conspiracy theories are no longer isolated national events. Instead, they are part of a coordinated, transnational script.

To understand how this happened, we have to look past local headlines and examine the invisible threads connecting American evangelical movements, Brazilian right-wing politics, and the ultra-conservative and religious fringes of South Korea. What looks like spontaneous domestic anger is often the result of an imported playbook designed to erode democratic norms from the inside out.

From the Tropics to Seoul: The Transnational Evangelical Network

When former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro rose to power, political analysts recognized it as more than just a standard conservative victory. It was the culmination of a decades-long project that fused Brazil’s traditionally Catholic demographic with hardline American-style Protestant evangelicalism. This was not merely a spiritual shift; it was the creation of a disciplined political bloc.

Bolsonaro’s political ascent relied heavily on ideological alignment with the MAGA (Make America Great Again) movement in the United States. By weaponizing religious authority, these networks framed political opponents not simply as rivals with differing views, but as moral evils that needed to be vanquished. South Korea is currently experiencing a striking parallel. Here, specific religious factions have attempted to monopolize political agendas, leveraging pulpits to amplify social divisions using models already field-tested in Latin America.

Key figures illustrate just how standardized this global strategy has become. In Brazil, televangelist and political strategist Silas Malafaia—featured prominently in the Netflix documentary A Tropical Apocalypse—played a pivotal role in rallying the far-right base behind Bolsonaro. Malafaia is the archetype of the "political pastor," using religious prestige to subvert democratic institutions and normalize attacks on the state.

In South Korea, this role has frequently been mirrored by figures like Pastor Jun Kwang-hoon, who has similarly attempted to bend national politics to religious will through mass rallies and direct confrontation with state authority. While the storming of the Brazilian Congress in Brasilia may feel like a world away, the foundational mechanics behind it—the deep-seated narratives of election fraud and virulent anti-intellectualism—remain alive and well in South Korean public discourse. These tactics, largely popularized by strategists like Steve Borer and the wider American alt-right ecosystem, function as a universal manual for populist agitation.

The Domestic Footprint: Foreign Ideology Meets Local Grievances

This global alignment is not merely conceptual; it has sought direct physical and organizational grounding in South Korea. Events like "Build Up Korea" have served as direct conduits for American far-right ideologies to project influence into the country. These gatherings have actively sought to forge ties between domestic Christian-right organizations, rising anti-China sentiments, and xenophobic narratives.

Under the benign-sounding banner of defending "free democracy," these imported forums often cultivate exclusionary hatred disguised as patriotism. It is a sobering reality that segments of South Korea’s evangelical community have eagerly embraced these external alliances, inviting foreign political engineering into domestic affairs in pursuit of narrow partisan gains.

This ideological export has also trickled down into the streets. In bustling, high-density districts of Seoul like Myeongdong and Hongdae, young activists associated with these radical networks have been documented shouting hostile slogans at foreign visitors and tourists. These street-level actions are not random outbursts of youthful rebellion; they function as the front-line enforcement of an imported nationalist-populist identity. By manufacturing a permanent state of grievance, these groups transform generalized societal anxiety into a predictable, highly energetic political fuel.

"When religion is used as a shield for political ambition, and the expulsion of the 'other' is rebranded as supreme patriotism, democracy ceases to be a system of governance and becomes a target for destruction."

The Threat to Democratic Institutions and the Weaponization of Doubt

The ultimate goal of this transnational network is not policy reform, but the systematic erosion of faith in democratic systems. Across multiple continents, the playbook is identical: before, during, and after elections, the legitimacy of the vote is relentlessly called into question. Truth is rendered secondary to tribal mobilization.

In Brazil, this culminated in direct assaults on state buildings. In South Korea, the persistent undercurrents of election denialism—often nurtured through local conferences and amplified by digital echo chambers—seek to achieve the same corrosive effect. Organizations inspired by global umbrella movements, such as CPAC (Conservative Political Action Conference), continue to probe for vulnerabilities within South Korean society, testing how far institutional trust can be stretched.

Yet, South Korea has thus far managed to avoid the absolute systemic collapse witnessed in some peer democracies. Despite sharp political polarization and recurring strains on public trust, the broader public has repeatedly leaned toward institutional stability. However, complacency is dangerous. The forces attempting to leverage religious fervor and cultural anxiety to regain political dominance have not disappeared; they are simply waiting for the next crisis.

What to Watch Next

As these cross-border ideological currents continue to flow, observers should monitor several concrete indicators to gauge the health of South Korean democracy:

  • Financial and Organizational Transparency: Watch for increased scrutiny and legislative proposals regarding foreign funding of domestic political-religious events and fringe think tanks.
  • Legal Boundaries on Hate Speech: Pay close attention to how law enforcement and municipal governments handle public harassment and xenophobic demonstrations in high-traffic urban areas like Myeongdong and Hongdae.
  • Institutional Resilience: Observe how independent judicial and electoral bodies handle ongoing disinformation campaigns concerning election integrity and state administration.
  • Mainstream Religious Backlash: Track whether moderate and mainline religious denominations in South Korea begin to forcefully distance themselves from politicized extremism.

Democracy is never permanently secured; it requires constant vigilance against the weaponization of manufactured rage. By recognizing that domestic radicalism is often just a local franchise of a global network, citizens and policymakers can better isolate and neutralize the threat before it takes deeper root.
