Why the Kospi Triggered Circuit Breakers: Anthropic, Inflation, and South Korea's Market Fear

October 15, 2026

Sitting in front of a trading terminal during a sudden market crash brings back a distinct chill—the kind that hasn't been felt widely since the 2008 global financial crisis. Recently, South Korea's major stock indexes, the Kospi and Kosdaq, hit simultaneous sell-side circuit breakers. Order books emptied out, selling pressure overwhelmed bids, and trading floors in Seoul resembled war zones. This was not a routine technical correction; the speed of the decline has been breathtaking, wiping away the optimism of retail investors who have long propped up local markets.

To understand what happened on this "Black Thursday," we need to look beyond Seoul. South Korea’s export-driven economy is deeply sensitive to global liquidity shifts. The current panic is driven by a dangerous combination of U.S. inflation data, soaring borrowing costs, and growing anxiety that the artificial intelligence boom—the last great engine of global growth—might be running on borrowed time.

Anthropic's $2 Trillion IPO and the Global Liquidity Drain

Rumors that Anthropic, the prominent artificial intelligence research company and a leading rival to OpenAI with backing from Google and Amazon, would unveil its third-quarter financials to institutional investors ahead of a massive public offering have sent tremors through global markets. The target valuation being floated is an astronomical $2 trillion. To put that in perspective, that valuation would eclipse even Meta, which sits around $1.9 trillion.

While tech bulls view this as a historic milestone, institutional investors see a giant liquidity black hole. Preparing for an IPO of this magnitude requires enormous pools of capital, pulling liquidity out of other global assets. For Asian markets like South Korea, this capital drain has intensified foreign selling pressure, leaving local exchanges starved of the foreign inflows they rely on to stay afloat.

Beneath the eye-popping valuation lies intense debate over accounting practices and revenue recognition. Unlike OpenAI, which uses a net revenue basis, Anthropic's model includes sales through cloud partners in its gross revenue totals. Furthermore, AI infrastructure operates on high operating leverage: companies rent massive computing clusters upfront. If token sales slow or token prices drop—and AI token prices have already fallen more than 50% from their May highs—profitability evaporates. For Anthropic to justify a $2 trillion valuation, it must triple its current revenue by 2028, a tall order if the broader market begins pricing in an AI bubble.

The U.S. Inflation Shock and the Death of Rate-Cut Hopes

The illusion that central banks would ride to the rescue with aggressive interest rate cuts was shattered by the latest U.S. Consumer Price Index (CPI) report. Core inflation pressures—particularly in services and housing—refused to budge, pushing the monthly CPI increase to its highest rate since January 2025. Energy prices may fluctuate, but sticky housing costs mean inflation is proving difficult to eradicate.

The market's reaction was swift and unforgiving. Hopes for immediate monetary easing evaporated overnight, with the implied probability of an upcoming Federal Reserve rate hike jumping sharply. As benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury yields pushed past 5.3%, it became clear that high borrowing costs are here to stay. Investors who spent months convincing themselves that a soft landing was guaranteed are now confronting the reality of expensive capital.

🔥 The End of Wishful Thinking
The current panic is not simply about high numbers on a spreadsheet. It stems from the chilling realization that even the AI sector—the market's last line of defense—is vulnerable to the gravity of high interest rates.

The CoreWeave Crunch and the AI Subprime Analogy

The stress in tech is nowhere more evident than in the financing models of AI infrastructure providers like CoreWeave, which build massive GPU data centers using borrowed money and rent them out. The business model relies on cheap debt and high profit margins. But with interest rates spiking into the 15% to 17% range, that math breaks down completely.

Bloomberg recently compared certain AI corporate debt instruments to "subprime tranches," noting that bond prices have slipped below 80 cents on the dollar. This indicates that debt markets no longer fully trust these firms' ability to service their interest payments. When the foundational layer of the AI ecosystem faces a credit crunch, the shockwaves inevitably reach hardware suppliers, chipmakers, and global tech supply chains.

As debt risks mount, capital is staging a massive migration back into safe-haven government bonds. Why chase speculative tech yields when risk-free sovereign debt pays over 5%? This flight to safety has created a vicious cycle, pulling capital away from equities and deepening the correction across Asian markets.

Structural Vulnerabilities in South Korea's Market

For South Korea, these global headwinds expose acute structural weaknesses. The local equity market remains heavily dependent on two corporate giants: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, South Korea's dominant memory chip manufacturers. When foreign investors buy these two stocks, the Kospi rises; when they sell, the market has no alternative engine to cushion the fall.

While memory chips are foundational to AI hardware, foreign institutional investors have begun signaling that the current semiconductor cycle may be peaking. Unfortunately, the domestic market lacks the depth to absorb heavy foreign outflows. Local retail investors, disillusioned by tumbling valuations, are increasingly parking their money in real estate or steering clear of stocks altogether.

Yet it is worth remembering that South Korean corporate fundamentals remain relatively robust. Export data is solid, and companies like Samsung are steadily improving their shareholder return policies through dividends. Selling fundamentally sound companies purely due to macro-driven liquidity crunches is rarely a winning strategy over the long haul.

💭 Personal Reflections on the Crash
In markets like this, the greatest danger is reacting with half-baked conclusions. Fleeing in a panic or stubbornly refusing to adjust are equally flawed approaches.

What remains true is that as long as real businesses generate real cash flow, asset prices eventually reflect fundamental value. Today's sell-off serves as a harsh stress test, proving whether our market has matured beyond a speculative frenzy.

Circuit breakers and plunging indexes are deeply uncomfortable to witness, but once the fear subsides, the true value of surviving companies will emerge with greater clarity.

What to Watch Next

As markets attempt to find a floor, investors should monitor a few concrete indicators in the weeks ahead:

  • U.S. Treasury Yields and Fed Signals: Watch whether the 10-year yield stabilizes below 5.3% or continues to climb, as this dictates the discount rate applied to all global tech valuations.
  • Anthropic IPO Filings: Pay close attention to institutional demand and final valuation targets during Anthropic's upcoming roadshows, which will gauge overall investor appetite for mega-cap AI offerings.
  • Foreign Net Buying in Seoul: Track whether foreign capital returns to Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, or if outflows continue to pressure the broader Kospi index.
  • AI Corporate Debt Spreads: Monitor yields and secondary market pricing on infrastructure debt issued by companies like CoreWeave to see if credit stress remains contained or spreads wider.

Markets freeze, but economic reality does not. Navigating this volatility requires patience, cold logic, and a close eye on the macro debt cycle.
