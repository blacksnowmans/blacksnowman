The Theater of the Absurd: How South Korean Politics Detached Itself from Reality

October 05, 2026

Step away from the fast-paced headlines of Seoul's financial districts and digital hubs, and tune into the everyday commentary of South Korean politicians. At times, listening to the nation’s political class feels less like analyzing a modern democracy and more like falling backward through a time warp. While voters grapple with slowing economic growth, household debt, and the relentless squeeze of inflation, the country's political elite are busy staging psychological reenactments of mid-century military conflicts and chanting bizarre onomatopoeic phrases on social media. For a foreign observer trying to understand the pulse of Asia’s fourth-largest economy, the disconnect between official political discourse and ground-level reality is nothing short of staggering.

Fighting Ghost Wars on the Nakdong River

The surreal tone of contemporary South Korean politics was recently encapsulated by remarks from senior figures within the progressive camp, notably Kim Min-seok, a prominent representative of the Democratic Party of Korea (the country's main liberal opposition party). Eschewing the conventional wisdom that South Korean elections are won or lost in the fiercely contested battlegrounds of the Seoul metropolitan area—the literal and economic heartland surrounding the Han River—Kim declared that the true core of the upcoming general election would be the Nakdong River.

For a Korean audience, invoking the Nakdong River is an immediate and heavy-handed historical callback. It recalls the Nakdong River Perimeter, the desperate, blood-soaked defensive line in 1950 where UN and South Korean forces made their final stand against advancing North Korean troops during the Korean War. To frame a modern legislative election around this geographic obstacle is not just strategically bizarre; it is deeply anachronistic. Instead of addressing the anxieties of urban renters, young job seekers, or small business owners, politicians are retreating into the comfortable, combative bunkers of Cold War-era imagery.

This performative militarism treats governance not as an exercise in public policy, but as an existential war game. By casting political opponents as existential threats requiring military-style containment, leaders attempt to bypass the messy work of economic stewardship. If you can convince your base that the nation is permanently on the brink of collapse, you never have to explain your plan for housing prices or youth unemployment. It is an inward-looking strategy designed entirely for tribal mobilization, completely divorced from the practical needs of ordinary citizens.

Polling Data and the Reality Distortion Field

While politicians play generals on television, cold statistics offer a sobering counter-narrative. Recent regional approval ratings paint a grim picture for the ruling establishment, revealing a chasm between the political class's self-perception and public sentiment. In the capital region surrounding Seoul, the approval rating has slumped to 28.2%. In the traditional conservative stronghold of Daegu and North Gyeongsang Province, it sits at 30.4%, while the Busan, Ulsan, and South Gyeongsang region (known collectively as PK) registers 35.5%.

These numbers are not statistical noise; they are a flashing red warning light. Yet, the political machinery responds not with introspection, but with spin. Rather than asking why the electorate is deserting them, leaders retreat further into their ideological echo chambers, interpreting disastrous metrics through an increasingly distorted lens. When data defies their preferred narrative, they simply dismiss the data.

This insular bubble has created a bizarre parallel universe. While families across the country struggle with the high cost of living, political spokespersons busy themselves with finding silver linings in sinking polls, blaming external economic headwinds or shadowy political conspiracies. But public trust, once eroded by this kind of willful blindness, is extraordinarily difficult to claw back. The widening gap between the ruling elite's optimism and the public's pessimism points toward a volatile political future.

The Bizarre Rhetoric of 'Dom-Dom-Dom'

Perhaps the most baffling symptom of this detachment is the linguistic degradation found on politicians' official social media channels. In a recent display that left commentators questioning the mental state of the nation's leadership, online messaging devolved into avant-garde nonsense. In attempts to rally support for local port developments, figures traded substantive policy proposals for inexplicable chants of "dom-dom-dom," mimicking the sound of drums to cheer on Busan's North Port.

To read an official political statement interspersed with rhythmic drum noises is to witness the total collapse of serious political communication. Where once there were debates on maritime logistics, regional economic revitalization, or trade infrastructure, there are now awkward attempts at internet meme culture that fall flat on arrival. It is an embarrassing spectacle—politicians attempting to appear youthful and relatable by adopting internet slang, only to sound completely detached from reality.

When elected officials reduce serious governance to a bizarre talent show characterized by onomatopoeia, voters are left to wonder whether anyone is actually minding the store. It signals an extreme trivialization of public office, where substance is entirely sacrificed for attention-grabbing noise.

What the Online Communities Are Saying

If you want to find the true temperature of South Korean society, you do not look at parliamentary transcripts; you look at the nation's massive network of online forums and mother-focused community boards (known locally as *mom cafes*). Here, the prevailing mood toward both the presidential office under the People Power Party (the conservative ruling party) and the broader political establishment is not anger—it is profound alienation.

The sentiment online is remarkably uniform: the political class has abandoned the public. While citizens worry about soaring grocery prices and job insecurity, both the ruling party and the opposition are perceived as locked in a petty, self-serving power struggle. The dominant emotion is no longer active political engagement, but a weary, hardened apathy. People have stopped expecting competence, which means they have stopped being disappointed—a dangerous threshold for any democracy.

This disconnect is rooted in a fundamental arrogance of power. Politicians assume that voters can be managed through emotional manipulation, dramatic historical framing, and cutesy social media gimmicks. They treat the electorate as an audience to be entertained rather than citizens to be served.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves toward its next major electoral cycles, several key indicators will determine whether the political class snaps out of its collective delusion or accelerates its descent into irrelevance.

  • Metropolitan Polling Trends: Watch closely to see if approval ratings in Seoul and the surrounding Gyeonggi province rebound or if the sub-30% erosion hardens into a permanent shift.
  • Substance vs. Slogan Shifts: Monitor whether the major parties abandon archaic framing devices—like the Nakdong River rhetoric—in favor of concrete economic platforms addressing household debt and inflation.
  • Voter Turnout and Demographics: Pay attention to engagement levels among younger voters on online platforms, whose growing apathy could translate into unpredictable protest voting.

For now, South Korean politics remains trapped in a theater of its own making—one where politicians march across ghost battlefields to the sound of imaginary drums, entirely oblivious to the rising tide around them.
