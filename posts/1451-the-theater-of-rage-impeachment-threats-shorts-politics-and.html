The Theater of Rage: Impeachment Threats, 'Shorts Politics,' and the Strain on South Korean Democracy

October 25, 2026

Inside the committee rooms of South Korea’s National Assembly in late October, the usual hum of policy scrutiny has been replaced by high-decibel fury. Lawmakers stand up from their leather chairs, gesticulating wildly, hurling sharp barbs, and staging walkouts—not necessarily to persuade their colleagues across the aisle, but to capture the precise ten-second burst of righteous indignation that will play well on a mobile screen. Outside the chambers, the political temperature is hotter still: ruling party figures have floated the once-unthinkable prospect of impeaching the nation’s top judge, while the conservative opposition claims the constitutional separation of powers is being dismantled in broad daylight.

South Korea is currently navigating a fraught political climate under President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea. With former leader Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol having been removed from office by impeachment and currently serving time in prison, the political landscape remains scarred by deep institutional trauma. Yet instead of ushering in an era of stable governance, the aftermath has unleashed an intense contest between the governing Democratic Party and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party. October 24 served as a stark encapsulation of this new reality: an escalating confrontation over the judiciary, an annual legislative audit hijacked by social media algorithms, and a bitter fight over the historical soul of Korean conservatism.

A Chief Justice Under Fire: Partisan Warfare Over Judicial Independence

The most acute flashpoint in Seoul is the prospect of impeaching Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. In South Korea, the Chief Justice heads the highest court of ordinary jurisdiction and wields significant institutional authority over judicial administration. Following a contentious appellate court ruling that touched sensitive political nerves, figures within the ruling Democratic Party openly raised the necessity of pursuing an impeachment motion against Cho, arguing that the judiciary has failed to uphold legal justice and has lost touch with popular common sense.

The response from the conservative opposition was immediate and scathing. Spokespersons for the People Power Party denounced the move as a naked attempt to intimidate the judiciary, warning that hauling judges before the legislature whenever a ruling displeases the majority strikes at the very heart of the separation of powers. The National Assembly promptly devolved into an icy standoff.

Yet this confrontation runs far deeper than a procedural squabble over one bench decision. In recent years, courtroom dockets in Seoul have increasingly become the arena where political survivals and party destinies are decided. When a single appellate verdict can alter the trajectory of a sitting government or threaten the viability of an opposition party, legal rulings inevitably face hyper-partisan scrutiny. Both sides have contributed to this dynamic over the years, routinely leveraging legal investigations and judicial decisions as weapons in partisan warfare.

The dilemma now confronting Korean society is that judicial independence was never designed to be an absolute shield against public scrutiny, nor was it intended to be a target for legislative retribution. When citizens come to believe that trials are dragged out or decided based on political calculations, the moral authority of the judiciary crumbles. Conversely, when the ruling party wields impeachment as an instrument to discipline the bench, the bedrock principle of equality under the law is eroded, leaving ordinary citizens with little faith in the institutions meant to protect them.

The Rise of 'Shorts Politics': How YouTube Algorithms Hollowed Out Parliament

While the constitutional fight simmered over the Supreme Court, lawmakers inside the parliamentary complex were busy conducting the annual parliamentary audit of state administration—known locally as the gukjeong gam-sa. Historically modeled as an exhaustive check on executive agencies, state-run corporations, and public budgets, this weeks-long audit is intended to uncover bureaucratic mismanagement and propose concrete legislative remedies.

Instead, this year's proceedings have been widely criticized for degenerating into what observers call "Shorts politics" (syocheu jeongchi)—a race to generate viral, vertical-video snippets tailored for YouTube Shorts and TikTok feeds. Rather than presenting thick binders of policy analysis or drilling down into state spending, legislators increasingly craft performative outbursts, provocative insults, and rehearsed shouting matches designed to catch the eye of platform algorithms.

The incentives behind this shift are plain: a sixty-second clip of a politician berating a witness generates millions of views, energizes partisan supporters, and drives immediate political donations. Complex, structural problems—such as soaring living costs, youth unemployment, housing insecurity, and fraying social safety nets—do not fit neatly into an algorithm that rewards instantaneous dopamine hits. As a result, critical policy investigations are quietly sidelined.

The long-term cost to democratic debate is immense. Algorithm-driven media environments thrive on conflict and villainization; to perform well on short-form video, opponents must be framed not as partners in negotiation, but as enemies to be eradicated. This constant production of outrage has lowered the barrier between fringe internet subcultures and mainstream political discourse, creating an echo chamber where extreme rhetoric is legitimized by lawmakers seeking higher engagement figures.

Identity Crisis on the Right: Historical Legacies and Far-Right Drift

This atmosphere of polarized confrontation has also exacerbated an identity crisis within the conservative opposition. Also on October 24, Kim Min-seok, a senior supreme council member of the ruling Democratic Party, launched a scathing attack on the People Power Party regarding the status of Lee Jin-sook, the chairperson of the Korea Communications Commission (KCC)—South Korea’s primary media and broadcasting regulator.

Lee, who has been the subject of an impeachment vote in the National Assembly, has faced fierce criticism from liberals over accusations of attempting to muzzle public broadcasters and expressing controversial historical viewpoints. Kim challenged the conservative leadership directly, declaring that the People Power Party must either cast out Lee or remove the portrait of former President Kim Young-sam from its party headquarters.

The invocation of Kim Young-sam (commonly known by his initials, YS) strikes at a raw historical nerve. In the 1990s, Kim Young-sam led South Korea's first civilian government in decades, famously purging the clandestine military clique known as Hanahoe and dismantling the remnants of the country's military dictatorships. For modern conservatives, claiming YS's mantle represents an effort to maintain credibility as an institutional, reform-minded conservative movement that respects democratic norms.

However, the Democratic Party's critique points out an uncomfortable reality: during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the conservative establishment leaned heavily into the orbit of fringe internet commentators, conspiracy theorists claiming rigged elections, and hardline street movements. By continuing to shelter figures accused of revisionist stances, critics argue that the People Power Party has strayed far from the democratic reforms championed by Kim Young-sam. For rational conservatives, shedding this reliance on extreme elements remains the central, unresolved challenge needed to win back mainstream public trust.

A Global Echo: The Lessons from France’s Streets

These domestic convulsions are not unfolding in a vacuum. Beyond South Korea's borders, advanced democracies are wrestling with similar fissures where economic pressure meets political polarization. On that same day in late October, France was shaken by widespread unrest as tens of thousands defied government protest bans to march against the rising cost of living and the surging influence of the far-right.

French authorities deployed an extraordinary force of 70,000 police officers across the country and took the drastic step of shuttering the world-renowned Louvre Museum in Paris. The protests laid bare a severe crisis of legitimacy: while citizens struggled under the weight of surging food and energy prices, the government's insistence on austerity and administrative bans failed to contain the public outcry.

Crucially, the French demonstrations fused economic grievances with a clear anti-far-right agenda. French citizens took to the streets acutely aware that when mainstream political institutions fail to address day-to-day economic despair, reactionary populism fills the vacuum by directing anger at scapegoats. The barricades in Paris offer a sobering parallel for Seoul: economic anxiety, if left unanswered by parliamentary leaders obsessing over viral theatrics, creates fertile ground for deeper societal fractures.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea moves through the final months of the legislative calendar, the clash between the executive, legislative, and judicial branches will shape the country's institutional health for years to come. Key developments to monitor include:

  • Formal Action on the Judiciary: Watch whether the Democratic Party formalizes an impeachment bill against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae or moderates its stance amid warnings about judicial autonomy. Any legislative move against the Chief Justice would trigger an unprecedented constitutional showdown in the Constitutional Court.
  • The Fate of the Communications Regulator: The ongoing legal and political battles surrounding KCC Chair Lee Jin-sook will serve as a bellwether for how South Korea's media environment is regulated, and whether the conservative People Power Party shifts toward the center or doubles down on its controversial personnel.
  • Audit Fallouts and Legislative Realities: With the annual audits wrapping up, the test will be whether parliament pivots from performative short-form confrontations to passing urgent livelihood legislation addressing high inflation, household debt, and sluggish domestic consumption.

When politics prioritizes virality over governance, ordinary citizens are invariably the ones left bearing the cost. Restoring institutional trust in South Korea will require lawmakers to step away from the smartphone cameras and return to the unglamorous, difficult work of constitutional compromise.
