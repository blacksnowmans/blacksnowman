Preparing for Seoul’s Vacancy: Inside the Democratic Party’s High-Stakes Gamble

October 24, 2026

Inside the conference room of the National Assembly on the morning of October 23, the mood among senior leaders of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea was unusually combative. Microphones were live, cameras broadcast the proceedings nationwide, and Party Leader Kim Min-seok wasted no time in delivering a political shockwave. Rather than waiting for the Seoul High Court to hand down its verdict, Kim openly signaled that Oh Se-hoon, the conservative mayor of Seoul, was on the verge of losing his post—and that the ruling party was already preparing for a snap by-election to replace him.

For observers outside South Korea, the mayoralty of Seoul is not merely a municipal office. Leading the capital city of nearly ten million people carries a stature rivaling top ministerial portfolios and often serves as a primary launching pad for the presidency. By preemptively declaring the mayor’s legal doom, the Democratic Party was not simply commenting on an ongoing trial; it was firing the opening salvo in a struggle to recapture the nation's political center of gravity.

The morning meeting of the Democratic Party's Supreme Council, its chief executive body, quickly became a microcosm of the political battles now defining the administration of President Lee Jae-myung. From courtroom maneuvering and battles over law enforcement oversight to an unexpected internal flare-up over a controversial religious sect, the session laid bare the aggressive posture—and the delicate internal tensions—of South Korea’s governing party.

Anticipating a Mayoral Vacancy: The Legal Squeeze on Oh Se-hoon

At the heart of Kim Min-seok’s opening remarks was the pending appellate decision concerning Mayor Oh Se-hoon, a senior figure in the conservative People Power Party, South Korea's main opposition party. Oh has been battling charges that carry a mandatory forfeiture of office upon a guilty verdict. Having already faced a damaging ruling in his first trial, Oh has pinned his political survival on overturning that judgment in the appellate court.

The ruling party’s leadership, however, sees no legal path forward for the conservative mayor. Kim stated bluntly that the second-instance court was almost certain to uphold the sentence stripping Oh of his seat, arguing that the defense had introduced no compelling new evidence to challenge the initial conviction. "We are mentally preparing for an early Seoul mayoral election," Kim announced, adding that the party has already begun vetting potential candidates.

The People Power Party reacted with immediate outrage. Conservative lawmakers accused the ruling party leader of blatant judicial interference, arguing that pronouncing a public official guilty before the court speaks is an attempt to intimidate judges and manufacture public consensus. To the opposition, declaring a snap election before the ruling is handed down crosses an unacceptable institutional line.

The Democratic Party leadership dismissed the backlash. Their stance is that a realistic reading of the trial records leaves little doubt about the outcome, making it irresponsible for the governing party not to prepare for an administrative vacuum in the capital. Having suffered bitter losses in past municipal contests, the Democrats view the potential by-election as an unmissable opportunity to consolidate their grip on the Seoul metropolitan region and reinforce the legislative agenda of President Lee Jae-myung.

Clashing Over Power Agencies: Who Controls the Investigators?

The friction between the two major parties extended far beyond City Hall. Later in the morning, debate erupted over the restructuring of the state investigative architecture—a cornerstone of President Lee's institutional reform agenda.

The flashpoint was a procedural issue with deep constitutional implications: the decision to have the head of the newly established Serious Crimes Investigation Agency attend meetings of the State Council, South Korea's cabinet. Lawmakers from the People Power Party, including Representative Chung Jung-sik, seized on the arrangement to level an extraordinary accusation. Chung claimed that compelling an agency chief to attend cabinet meetings compromised the body's investigative independence and constituted grounds for the impeachment of President Lee Jae-myung, alleging it was a veiled attempt by the president to meddle in ongoing judicial matters.

Democratic Party Floor Leader Han Byung-do took to the podium to dismiss the impeachment threat as pure "comedy." Han argued that bringing investigative chiefs into the cabinet room actually advances institutional integrity rather than undermining it.

"Bringing an agency head before a cabinet session that is broadcast live to the public is transparent democratic oversight," the floor leadership argued, contrasting the move with the covert backroom maneuvers seen during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol.

Under the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later removed from office by impeachment and is currently serving a prison sentence, the presidential office faced relentless criticism from progressives for weaponizing prosecutorial networks behind closed doors. Democratic leaders, joined by Representative Yang Bu-nam on radio broadcasts, stressed that the historical absence of the Prosecutor General from cabinet meetings never guaranteed neutrality; instead, it merely shielded political directives from public view. By placing investigative leaders in full view of the public during televised executive sessions, the Lee administration claims it is substituting backchannel pressure with lawful, transparent administrative accountability.

A Live-Mic Collision Over the Shincheonji Infiltration Probe

While the leadership presented a united front against the conservative opposition, the broadcast captured an unexpected crack within their own ranks. As the cameras rolled, a sudden argument broke out between two Supreme Council members, exposing simmering internal disputes over the party's handling of the Shincheonji Church of Jesus—a fringe religious sect that has repeatedly found itself at the center of South Korean political scandals.

Supreme Council Member Lee Sung-yoon confronted Party Leader Kim Min-seok directly, demanding that the party release the findings of its internal investigation into allegations that Shincheonji members had systematically registered as party members to covertly influence internal selections. Lee argued that transparently disclosing the objective findings to the rank-and-file membership was the only way to clear away lingering suspicions and put internal factional whispers to rest.

The demand sparked an instant, visceral pushback from Supreme Council Member Park Sun-won, who oversaw the internal probe. Visible anger flashed across the table as Park cut in:

"I am the person in charge of this investigation," Park fired back over an open microphone. "Fine, let us go straight to the National Assembly press briefing room and lay it all bare."

The unexpected clash brought the room to a standstill. Kim Min-seok and Han Byung-do stepped in immediately to defuse the confrontation, visibly concerned about airing internal factional divisions on live television. The meeting was quickly transitioned behind closed doors, and a party spokesperson later announced that the leadership would revisit the question of whether to publish the report after private deliberation. For a governing party trying to project stability, the raw exchange served as an awkward reminder of internal distrust that has yet to be fully smoothed over.

Industrial Ambitions, Budgets, and the Labor Dilemma

Despite the political crosscurrents, the governing party spent the remainder of the session attempting to steer the public narrative toward governance and economic stewardship. Kim Min-seok attended the inaugural ceremony of the party's Special Committee on Support for K-Defense, chaired by Representative Kim Byung-joo, pledging extensive legislative and budgetary support to position South Korea's defense manufacturing sector as a central pillar of both national security and economic growth.

In tandem with industrial backing, Kim reiterated plans to reactivate the Special Inspector system—an independent oversight mechanism designed to investigate misconduct among senior officials and presidential relatives. The post had been left dormant during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, a lapse the Democratic Party has long criticized as a failure of institutional checks and balances.

On social and safety policy, the party moved to secure dedicated funding for victims of past domestic tragedies. The party’s special task force on the Itaewon crowd crush, led by Representative Nam In-soon, announced a resolution to allocate 13.2 billion won (approximately $9.6 million) in next year’s national budget to fund the independent investigative commission probing the October 2022 disaster. Complementary legislative moves included proposals by Representative Heo Young to strengthen fire-resistance standards for commercial warehouses and Representative Kim Kyo-heung's push to establish a public medical graduate school at Incheon National University.

Yet the party’s legislative drive faces obstacles from traditional allies. Labor rights advocates and the country’s two largest umbrella union confederations have raised fierce objections to the proposed Mega-Regional Strategic Industry Special Act, arguing that deregulation within special economic zones threatens hard-won labor protections. Balancing the macro-level demand for high-tech competitiveness against progressive commitments to workers' rights remains one of the sharpest policy dilemmas facing the ruling majority.

What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will determine whether the Democratic Party's aggressive posture pays off or triggers an untimely backlash:

  • The Appellate Ruling on Mayor Oh Se-hoon: The immediate test is the Seoul High Court’s ruling. If the court upholds a sentence that invalidates Oh’s office, the capital will be thrown into a fierce snap election campaign, reshaping the political landscape ahead of nationwide local races. If Oh survives, Kim Min-seok’s preemptive rhetoric will provide the opposition with formidable political ammunition.
  • The Shincheonji Disclosure Decision: The party leadership must decide how to handle the findings of its internal infiltration probe. Suppressing the report risks alienating grassroots members who demand transparency, while releasing it could ignite an intra-party feud over factional affiliations.
  • The Budget Showdown: As the national budget moves to the center of parliamentary debate, watch whether the Democratic Party can successfully pass its targeted spending—such as the 13.2 billion won allocated for the Itaewon disaster investigation—without provoking a protracted legislative freeze with the People Power Party.
  • Labor Negotiations on Industrial Special Zones: Watch for potential revisions to the Mega-Regional Strategic Industry Special Act as union federations escalate protests, testing the Lee administration's ability to maintain solidarity with its core progressive voting base.
