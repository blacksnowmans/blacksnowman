The Silent Telegram: Inside President Lee Jae-myung’s Rift with His Reformist Base

November 02, 2026

Inside the presidential administration in Seoul, a private Telegram message was dispatched to a long-time ally. The sender was President Lee Jae-myung, leader of the ruling liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). The recipient was Choi Bae-geun, an emeritus professor of economics at Konkuk University who had spent years helping build the theoretical foundation of Lee’s campaign platform. President Lee’s message carried a tone of defensive confusion: “What exactly about me has changed?”

Prof. Choi did not type a response. As he later revealed, he found the question so thoroughly disconnected from the administration's policy trajectory that he felt replying was pointless. “I was so flabbergasted that I didn’t even reply,” Choi recalled. That single unanswered text message has since exposed a deep ideological rift within South Korea's governing power center—a sharp fracture between an administration pivoting toward the political center and the reform-minded base that helped bring it to power.

A Telegram Exchange Unravels a Political Coalition

The rift between President Lee and Prof. Choi is not a minor personal disagreement between two old campaign comrades. Choi was a central figure during Lee’s 2021 presidential primary run, providing economic weight to Lee’s signature policy vision. His public disclosure of their private exchange sent shockwaves through South Korea’s progressive camp, laying bare a fundamental conflict over policy direction and political governance.

To understand why President Lee asked why he had changed, one must look at the electoral trauma that reshaped his political strategy. In the March 2022 presidential election, Lee narrowly lost to conservative candidate Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, capturing 47.83% of the vote. Following the political chaos of Mr. Yoon’s brief declaration of martial law, subsequent impeachment, and imprisonment, Lee won the presidency on his second attempt. Yet even in that victory, Lee garnered 49.42% of the vote—falling short of an absolute majority.

A simple calculation showed the administration how fragile its victory was: had rival conservative candidates unified their tickets, the outcome could easily have flipped. This lack of a clear majority created deep anxiety within the presidential office. Administration strategists concluded that relying solely on the Democratic Party’s traditional base was insufficient for stable governance or future electoral victories. This realization gave rise to the administration's "structural majority theory"—a deliberate, rightward policy pivot aimed at courting moderate and conservative voters.

However, critics view this strategy as a misreading of South Korea's electoral history. In 2017, following the impeachment of Park Geun-hye, President Moon Jae-in secured the presidency with 41.08% of the vote. The core issue facing the DPK was not merely a raw percentage calculation, but two decades of accumulated frustration among working-class voters who felt liberal administrations consistently failed to address systemic economic inequality, skyrocketing housing costs, and rising household debt.

Whisper Campaigns and the Rise of Digital Cyber-Attacks

The tension escalated publicly on October 9, when President Lee posted a sharply worded message on his social media account. Without naming specific individuals, the president criticized internal detractors, writing that “the real problem lies with those who demand major positions despite lacking the capability, and then attack from within under the banner of radical reform when things don’t go their way.”

The post sparked immediate controversy. Representative Kim Yong-min, a prominent DPK lawmaker known for pushing aggressive reform agendas, publicly rejected the insinuation on Facebook, asserting he had never sought government posts. Almost simultaneously, anonymous political intelligence sheets (known locally as jirashi) began circulating across online communities and messaging apps on October 10 and 11. These rumors claimed that Prof. Choi had demanded the governorship of the Bank of Korea—South Korea's central bank—earlier in the year and turned against the administration after being rejected.

When pressed for clarity, the presidential office took a passive stance, stating it could not confirm the facts. By issuing an unnamed public rebuke while remaining silent on spreading rumors, the presidential office effectively fed its critics to hardline online supporters.

On October 18, Prof. Choi broke his silence on his YouTube channel to defend his reputation. Denying that he had ever sought high office, Choi clarified the events surrounding the central bank post:

“Earlier this year, the president’s team asked me for recommendations regarding the Bank of Korea governor position ahead of the April term expiration. I recommended academic experts who understood the administration's core economic philosophy. I merely noted that if accountability required it, they could utilize me alongside those experts. I never made a formal request or demand for a position.”

Choi condemned the coordinated online attacks against him as the work of “digital Red Guards”—a coalition of hardcore online fanbases weaponizing presidential posts to execute targeted cyber-attacks against internal critics. Rather than evaluating whether policy critiques held merit, the administration’s defenders sought to delegitimize critics by branding them as self-serving traitors.

The ‘Structural Majority’ Trap and Base Alienation

During an October 19 press conference, President Lee defended his governance approach, reiterating his commitment to building a broad-based coalition that extends beyond the traditional progressive wing to include moderate and reasonable conservative forces. Addressing the discontent among his core supporters, President Lee remarked:

“If anyone claims I betrayed them simply because I became president with support from one side, I have never said I would be a president for only one side. I will be a president for everyone.”

While framed as a call for national unity, the statement alienated many long-time supporters. Core DPK voters argued they never demanded an exclusive president; rather, they expected an administration that prioritized working-class protections and structural reforms over political compromise.

The sense of alienation deepened with controversial personnel choices, most notably the appointment of Kim Ji-yong to head the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency—a key body created to absorb investigative powers as part of long-sought prosecutorial reforms. Kim had previously signed a joint statement opposing disciplinary action against Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol during Mr. Yoon's tenure as Prosecutor General, and had publicly opposed legislation reducing prosecutorial power in 2022. For DPK loyalists who campaigned for prosecutorial reform, appointing such a figure to lead the new agency felt like a direct rollback of key campaign promises.

The political cost of this pivot has been reflected in public opinion polls. According to a Realmeter survey released on October 26, President Lee’s job approval rating stood at 35.3%, with negative assessments reaching 62.3%. The strategy of alienating the core base to capture moderate support has so far failed to produce the broad political consensus the administration envisioned.

Policy Censorship and Market Volatility

The friction between the administration and its economic advisory base extends beyond personnel appointments into core policy decisions. Prof. Choi revealed that a column he regularly contributed to Policy Briefing—the government’s official policy portal managed by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism—was effectively blocked after he refused internal demands to alter his text.

Choi’s rejected column contained a critical analysis of the administration's flagship "3-4-5 Vision" (aiming for 3% potential growth, becoming a top 4 trading nation, and achieving $50,000 per capita income). Choi warned of a potential "Korean-style Nokia risk," arguing that macro growth targets were masking structural vulnerabilities stemming from an over-reliance on the semiconductor sector.

He specifically criticized the Financial Services Commission’s introduction of high-leverage single-stock ETF products, which were rolled out to encourage retail investors to shift capital back from U.S. markets to domestic equities. Choi characterized these products as high-risk tools that exacerbated market volatility. Market data highlighted these vulnerabilities: between June 22 and July 17, when the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index declined 20.2%, South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI index plummeted 25.2%—far exceeding drops in the S&P 500 (0.2%) and Taiwan’s TWSE (1.0%). Major domestic tech giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix experienced single-day drops exceeding 5% on 11 and 10 separate occasions, respectively, compared to just once for Taiwan’s TSMC.

While the administration highlights macroeconomic metrics—such as an expansionary 2026 national budget of 727.9 trillion won (about $530 billion), low-interest relief funds of 3.36 trillion won (about $2.45 billion) for small businesses, and 16.4 trillion won (about $12 billion) in debt restructuring through a bad bank—these top-line figures have not fully insulated working-class households from persistent inflation and high living costs.

What to Watch Next

As the administration navigates these internal and external pressures, several key indicators will determine whether it can stabilize its governing coalition:

  • Approval Rating Trajectories: Watch whether President Lee's approval ratings remain stuck in the mid-30% range. Sustained low polling may force the administration to reconsider its "structural majority" strategy or adjust its outreach to the DPK base.
  • Implementation of Prosecutorial Reforms: Public reaction to key appointments like Kim Ji-yong at the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency will serve as a bellwether for whether the administration can maintain support from reform-oriented voters.
  • Economic Policy Adjustments: Track whether the Ministry of Economy and Finance and financial regulators adjust market stabilization tools and retail investment policies in response to volatility in the semiconductor sector.
  • Legislative Unity within the DPK: Pay close attention to whether reform-minded lawmakers within the ruling party begin openly coordinating with external critics, which could complicate the passage of priority budget items and legislative proposals.
