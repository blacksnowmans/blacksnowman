A House Divided: The Leaked Polls Threatening South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 17, 2026

In the high-pressure corridors of Yeouido—Seoul’s equivalent to Capitol Hill—secrets are the primary currency. But when those secrets involve the potential collapse of the ruling party, the resulting explosion reshapes the entire political landscape. Recently, a leak from within the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), currently the nation’s ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, has sent shockwaves through the government. The revelation is stark: internal polling suggests the party’s support has plummeted into the 20% range, and without a radical shift in internal unity, they could be looking at a catastrophic defeat in the next general election, potentially securing as few as 80 seats.

For a foreign observer, a dip in polling might seem like the standard ebb and flow of democratic politics. However, in the context of South Korean history and the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, these numbers represent an existential threat. The DPK is not just facing a temporary loss of popularity; it is staring down the barrel of a legislative paralysis that could render the "Lee Jae-myung era" a lame-duck period before it even reaches its peak. With internal disciplinary actions now being weaponized against high-ranking members, the party is struggling to decide whether to fix its leaking roof or simply purge those pointing at the holes.

The Ghost of 2008 and the 80-Seat Nightmare

To understand why the number "80" causes such visceral fear within the Democratic Party, one must look back to 2008. In the 18th General Election, the liberal predecessor to today’s DPK was decimated, winning only 81 seats. It was a period of wandering in the political wilderness, a trauma that the party’s leadership has spent nearly two decades trying to avoid repeating. The current internal leaks, first brought to light by the YouTube political commentary channel "Press Needs to Know to Change" (Eon-lon-al-aya-baggun-da), suggest that this nightmare scenario is becoming a mathematical probability.

The internal analysis warns that if the current factional infighting is not resolved, the party’s seat count will "plummet to around 80." This isn't just about losing a majority; it’s about losing the ability to protect the President's agenda or prevent the opposition—the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea's main conservative party—from holding the keys to the kingdom. When a ruling party falls below the 100-seat threshold in the 300-member National Assembly, they lose the power to block constitutional amendments or even stop the impeachment of their own officials.

Critics within the party might be tempted to dismiss these polls as outliers, citing the 2016 election where polls showed the liberals trailing significantly, only for them to pull off a stunning upset. Back then, pollsters relied heavily on landline telephones, which skewed toward older, conservative voters. However, the game has changed. Since the 2020 elections, South Korean pollsters have utilized "safe numbers"—one-time virtual mobile numbers provided by telecom companies—which have significantly increased accuracy. In the 21st General Election, these methods accurately predicted the DPK’s landslide victory of 180 seats. Therefore, when internal DPK polls today show a drop to the 20% range, the leadership knows this is likely a reflection of a real and present danger, not just a statistical glitch.

The Stalled Engine of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

The real concern behind these leaked numbers isn't just the seats themselves, but the "legislative engine" they provide for President Lee Jae-myung. In South Korea's presidential system, a President without a cooperative National Assembly is a general without an army. The leaked analysis explicitly states that a "crushing defeat in the general election would inflict an irreparable, fatal blow to the Lee Jae-myung government’s national momentum."

For the average citizen, this political math translates into stagnant policy. Major initiatives like housing stabilization, labor reform, and welfare expansion require more than just presidential decrees; they require funding and legislation. If the DPK loses its grip on the Assembly, the President's core promises will likely die in committee. Furthermore, there is the matter of "Prosecution Reform"—a signature goal of the DPK. The party fears that a major loss would not only stop these reforms but potentially reverse them, returning the nation’s powerful investigative agencies to their pre-reform state.

"The greatest factor eroding the national momentum of the Lee Jae-myung government is not the offensive from the opposition, but the divisions growing from within the ruling party itself."

The fear of becoming a "long-term opposition party" is palpable. In South Korean politics, momentum is everything. A massive loss in the general election is often the harbinger of a loss in the next presidential race. The internal documents suggest that the party’s base is becoming disillusioned, not necessarily because they support the conservative PPP, but because they are exhausted by the DPK's internal power struggles. For a 30-something voter struggling with inflation and housing costs, the news from the ruling party isn't about economic relief; it's about which faction is currently winning a civil war.

Discipline or Purge? The Case of Choe Min-hee

Against this backdrop of falling polls, a new internal conflict has emerged that perfectly illustrates the party's tension. Kim Min-seok, a prominent leader within the DPK, recently ordered a "direct investigation" (jikgwon gamchal) into Representative Choe Min-hee. Choe is a high-ranking member, a "Supreme Council" member who was elected to her position by the party's rank-and-file members. The decision by Kim to bypass standard procedures and order a direct probe has set off alarms regarding party democracy.

In the DPK's internal structure, a direct investigation by the party leadership is a "heavy-handed signal," according to analysts. It is usually reserved for serious ethical breaches, but its application here is being viewed as a political tool. The controversy centers on the perceived lack of fairness. While Choe is being targeted for a one-time, non-public incident, other party members have engaged in repeated, public, and arguably more damaging behavior without facing similar scrutiny. This has led to accusations of "double standards" and "selective discipline."

The timing of this disciplinary action is particularly sensitive because of the upcoming "nomination" (gongcheon) process. In South Korea, the party leadership holds immense power over who gets to run under the party banner in the next election. If a party member is under investigation or disciplined, their chances of being nominated are effectively zero. By targeting a popular, elected Supreme Council member, the leadership is being accused of "lining up" the party—essentially telling every other lawmaker that if they don't follow the current leadership's line, they will be the next to face an investigation and lose their seat.

The Price of Forced Unity

The immediate result of this aggressive internal policing has been a sudden, chilling silence within the DPK. Previously, there were murmurs of discontent regarding the leadership’s handling of various issues, including controversies surrounding religious groups like Shincheonji. However, once the "sword" of direct investigation was drawn against a figure as high-ranking as Choe Min-hee, the internal debate vanished almost overnight. On the surface, the party looks unified; underneath, it is paralyzed by fear.

This "silence of the legislators" is a dangerous sign for any ruling party. When lawmakers are more worried about their party’s internal disciplinary board than the voters’ needs, the feedback loop between the government and the people breaks. Healthy parties require internal dissent to self-correct. If the DPK uses the fear of the "80-seat disaster" as a pretext to suppress all internal criticism, they risk becoming a monolithic entity that is out of touch with the very public they need to win over.

The irony is that the DPK’s leadership argues these measures are necessary to ensure a "One Team" approach for the election. They claim that in a state of emergency—with support in the 20% range—there is no room for factionalism. But for the voters, the sight of a ruling party purging its own elected leaders suggests a lack of confidence and a preoccupation with power rather than governance. The path to recovery isn't found in silencing the party's own members, but in restoring the public's trust through fair processes and tangible policy results.

What to Watch Next

The DPK is currently at a crossroads. Over the coming months, there are several key indicators that will determine if the party can avoid the "80-seat nightmare":

  • The Outcome of the Choe Min-hee Investigation: If the leadership proceeds with a heavy penalty, expect a significant backlash from the party’s grassroots base, who view her as an elected representative of their will.
  • Nomination Rules: Watch for changes in how the party selects candidates for the next election. If the rules are tweaked to give more power to the central leadership and less to local primaries, it will be a sign that the "lining up" of candidates is in full swing.
  • Public Polling Trends: If the DPK's support remains stuck in the 20% range despite the internal "unification" efforts, it will prove that the public is more concerned with the party's performance than its internal discipline.
  • The Opposition's Move: The People Power Party (PPP) is watching this internal DPK strife closely. If the DPK remains distracted by civil war, the PPP will likely ramp up its legislative offensive to further stall President Lee Jae-myung’s agenda.

The leaked polls are a fire alarm. Whether the Democratic Party uses that alarm to put out the fire or simply tries to hide the bell will decide the fate of the current administration and the legislative map of South Korea for years to come.
