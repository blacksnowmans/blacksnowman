The Paradox of Power: Why Lee Jae-myung’s Administration is Expanding the Prosecutor’s Grip

October 12, 2026

When the political tides turned in South Korea, the public expected a radical departure from the past. Supporters of the new administration, led by President Lee Jae-myung, had spent years witnessing the heavy-handed tactics of a prosecution service that acted as a law unto itself. They assumed that those who had been on the receiving end of such unchecked power would be the most motivated to dismantle it. Instead, the reality of the current political landscape has left many observers stunned, with a growing chorus of critics lamenting that the situation under the current government is, in some ways, even worse than it was during the era of the now-imprisoned former leader, Yoon Seok-yeol.

The Ministry of Justice: A Fortress for Prosecutors

The Ministry of Justice—the very agency tasked with overseeing the prosecution—has become the epicenter of this irony. During recent parliamentary audits by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, it became clear that the ministry is no longer a civilian-led overseer of the legal system, but a bastion for the very people it is supposed to supervise. Key positions, including the Legal Affairs Office, the Planning and Coordination Office, the Criminal Justice Department, and even the Human Rights Bureau, are currently occupied by active-duty prosecutors.

This is a classic case of the “tail wagging the dog.” By populating the Ministry of Justice with 56 concurrent-duty prosecutors, the administration has effectively allowed the subjects of oversight to become the masters of the house. In any functional democracy, the ministry serves as the guardian of legal impartiality, yet here it has become a haven for prosecutorial self-preservation. While the previous administration under Yoon Seok-yeol was notorious for its prosecutorial dominance, it ironically maintained civilian leadership within the Human Rights Bureau. Today, even that last vestige of civilian oversight has been surrendered to career prosecutors.

The sentiment expressed by investigative reporter Jang In-soo resonates deeply: “Prosecutorial reform isn't difficult; it’s actually quite easy. If the president simply makes a firm decision, it is the easiest thing in the world.” Yet, the Lee Jae-myung administration appears to be doing the opposite—walking deeper into the barricades of the prosecutorial establishment rather than tearing them down.

The Shadowy Move to Weaken the Corruption Investigation Office

Beyond the ministry, there is an even more alarming trend developing within the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (the country’s main liberal party). Recent debates led by pro-Lee lawmakers have surfaced proposals that strike at the heart of the country's anti-corruption architecture. Specifically, there are calls to reduce the powers of the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO), an agency designed specifically to keep the executive and powerful elites in check.

The proposal—transferring the authority to investigate high-level politicians from the CIO to a newly proposed “Major Crimes Investigation Agency”—smacks of a political power grab. President Lee campaigned on a platform of strengthening the CIO to ensure that powerful institutions remained accountable. However, now that he holds the reins of power, the very existence of an independent investigative body seems to have become a burden. By shifting investigative jurisdiction to a new agency, the administration appears to be building a “safety valve” to shield itself and its allies from the kind of scrutiny the CIO was empowered to conduct.

The potential appointment of Kim Ji-yong as head of this proposed agency has only fueled these concerns. Critics describe him as a “weather vane” politician who shifts his loyalty to wherever the wind blows. For many, this is a transparent attempt to control the investigative machinery through subservient appointments, essentially recreating the very monopoly of power the public voted to abolish.

Two Consistent Failures: Entrenchment and Petty Retribution

Why is a liberal government pursuing such contradictory policies? Two themes seem to define the Lee administration’s approach to governance. The first is a profound reluctance to challenge the interests of the powerful. Whether it is the prosecution, the giant conglomerates, or the conservative-leaning judiciary, the administration seems to have calculated that maintaining the status quo is safer than risking the backlash that comes with genuine reform. By choosing to act as a partner to these establishments rather than their regulator, the government has traded its reformist mandate for the comforts of the inner circle.

The second theme is a petty, almost pathological obsession with erasing the legacy of the previous Moon Jae-in administration. In an effort to differentiate itself, the government has discarded the very tools and personnel that once formed the backbone of the reform agenda. This self-defeating behavior has created a void where genuine structural change should be. By obsessing over “distancing” itself from its predecessor, the administration is inadvertently falling into the same trap of autocracy it once condemned.

What to Watch Next

The coming months will be a critical test for the Lee Jae-myung administration. As the public becomes increasingly aware of the “prosecutorial entrenchment” within the Ministry of Justice and the legislative maneuvering around the CIO, the administration faces a potential crisis of legitimacy.

  • Personnel Appointments: Watch for whether the administration continues to appoint active-duty prosecutors to civilian-led roles within the Ministry of Justice. A reversal of this trend would be the first sign that they are listening to the public.
  • Legislative Moves on the CIO: Keep a close eye on the Democratic Party’s legislative agenda regarding the Corruption Investigation Office. Any formal move to strip its power will likely be met with intense scrutiny and potential mass protests.
  • The “Gold Standard” of Reform: The public will be looking for a clear commitment to the separation of investigation and indictment powers. If the government continues to prioritize political self-preservation over these structural changes, it risks being labeled a failure by its own supporters, potentially turning the political tides once again.

The administration must now look in the mirror and decide: are they the architects of a more transparent, democratic Korea, or simply the latest tenants of a system that serves to protect the powerful at the expense of the people? The "golden time" for reform is fading, and history has little patience for governments that lose their way so early in their mandate.
