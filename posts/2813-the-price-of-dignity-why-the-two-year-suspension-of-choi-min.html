The Price of Dignity: Why the Two-Year Suspension of Choi Min-hee Shakes the Ruling Party

November 28, 2026

Imagine the atmosphere inside the headquarters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), South Korea’s ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, on the afternoon of November 27. Usually, the halls of the party’s Yeouido base are a hive of activity focused on pushing through the President's legislative agenda or managing the ongoing budget negotiations in the National Assembly. But that afternoon, the air grew heavy as news leaked from a closed-door session of the party’s Ethics Tribunal. The internal watchdog had just handed down a decision that many in the party did not see coming: a two-year suspension of party membership for Choi Min-hee, a high-profile member of the Supreme Council.

For those unfamiliar with the internal mechanics of Korean politics, a "two-year suspension" might sound like a manageable hiatus. In reality, it is a political death sentence. In the DPK, such a suspension triggers an immediate loss of all party positions. Choi, who serves as the Senior Member of the Supreme Council—the party's highest decision-making body—would be stripped of her rank instantly. More crucially, the timing of a two-year ban ensures she would be ineligible for the party’s nomination in the 2028 general elections. In a political system where party nomination is the primary gateway to a seat in the National Assembly, this ruling effectively ends her career in its current form.

The shockwaves from this decision have immediately fractured the party’s facade of unity. While the Ethics Tribunal cited a "damage to the party’s dignity" as the formal reason for the purge, the ambiguity of that charge has led to accusations of factional warfare. With Choi’s allies, including heavyweight lawmaker Jung Chung-rae, calling the move "unacceptable," the Lee Jae-myung administration now faces a significant internal crisis at a time when it needs total cohesion to navigate a difficult legislative season.

The Mechanics of a Political Purge

To understand why this matters, one must understand the power held by the Ethics Tribunal of the Democratic Party of Korea. In the Korean political landscape, parties are highly centralized organizations. The Ethics Tribunal acts as both judge and jury for internal disputes, tasked with maintaining "party discipline." However, the criteria they use are often subjective. The phrase used in Choi’s case—"damaging the dignity of the party"—is a notoriously elastic term that can be applied to anything from a controversial public statement to an private disagreement with party leadership.

The severity of the punishment—two years—is what has set the political world on fire. Usually, a member might receive a "caution" or a short-term suspension of three to six months for verbal gaffes. By leaping straight to a 24-month ban, the Tribunal has sent a message that Choi’s presence in the leadership is no longer tolerated. As a member of the Supreme Council, Choi was one of the few figures with the authority to influence party policy and the selection of candidates for future elections. Removing her shifts the internal balance of power significantly.

The immediate fallout is the loss of her "Senior Supreme Council Member" title. This role is not merely ceremonial; it involves presiding over meetings in the absence of the party leader and holding significant sway over the party’s internal administrative and financial decisions. By stripping her of this post, the Tribunal has effectively decapitated one wing of the party’s leadership. The move has been described by some insiders as "Eupchammasok," a traditional Korean idiom referring to a leader who must tearfully execute a favorite subordinate to maintain order and discipline. However, unlike the ancient proverb, there is very little "tearing" happening among Choi's rivals, who seem content to see her sidelined.

A Factional Tinderbox and the Kim Min-seok Counter-Attack

The ruling has exposed deep-seated rivalries within the DPK, specifically between the supporters of Choi Min-hee and other powerful figures within the party leadership. This is most visible in the reaction of Jung Chung-rae, a prominent lawmaker and a key figure in the party’s "pro-Lee Jae-myung" core. Jung’s public declaration that the ruling is "impossible to accept" is a rare instance of a high-ranking official openly defying the party’s own disciplinary body. It suggests that the consensus that has held the DPK together during the early years of the Lee Jae-myung presidency is beginning to fray.

The conflict has now morphed into a "what-about-ism" battle that threatens to drag other leaders into the mud. Allies of Choi have quickly pointed the finger at Kim Min-seok, another influential DPK lawmaker. They argue that if Choi is being punished for "damaging party dignity," then Kim should be held to the same standard. They have pointed to Kim’s past statements and political maneuvers—specifically his history of shifting alliances, which they label as "anti-party" or "party-splitting" behavior.

A formal petition for Kim Min-seok’s discipline has already been filed with the same Ethics Tribunal. This puts the party in a precarious position: if they punish Kim, they risk a total collapse of leadership stability; if they do not, they prove Choi’s supporters right in their claims of a "double standard." This "tit-for-tat" disciplinary warfare is the nightmare scenario for any ruling party. It turns the internal watchdog into a weapon used by various factions to "sniping" their rivals, rather than an impartial arbiter of conduct.

Governing Under a Divided House

For the broader public and international observers, the internal bickering of the DPK might seem like small-scale political theater. However, the timing makes it a national concern. South Korea is currently in the middle of its most critical legislative month. December is when the National Assembly must finalize the national budget and pass major reform bills that the Lee Jae-myung administration has promised the electorate.

As the ruling party, the DPK holds the responsibility of negotiating with the main opposition, the conservative People Power Party (PPP). These negotiations require a united front and a clear hierarchy. With the Supreme Council now in disarray and senior members like Choi and Jung at odds with the party’s disciplinary wing, the government’s "legislative drive" is losing momentum. If the DPK is too busy fighting itself, it cannot effectively counter the PPP’s attempts to block President Lee’s budget priorities.

There is also the question of the 2028 General Election. While it is years away, the "gongcheon" (party nomination) process in Korea is a constant, looming shadow over every action a lawmaker takes. By effectively banning Choi from the 2028 race, the party has signaled that the "cleansing" of the candidate pool has begun early. This makes every other lawmaker nervous. If a Senior Supreme Council member can be erased for "dignity" issues, no one is safe. This anxiety often leads to lawmakers becoming more focused on their own survival than on the government's policy goals, leading to legislative gridlock.

What to Watch Next

The Choi Min-hee saga is far from over. The coming weeks will determine whether this remains an internal dispute or evolves into a full-blown constitutional and legal battle. Here are the specific developments to watch:

  • The Appeal Process: Choi has already filed for a re-examination of her case. The party’s appellate body will have to decide whether to uphold the two-year ban, reduce it, or scrap it. A reduction to something like a six-month suspension would be a facesaving compromise that allows Choi to keep her 2028 eligibility while acknowledging the "offense."
  • Legal Injunctions: If the party’s internal appeal fails, Choi is likely to take the matter to the civil courts. In South Korea, it is increasingly common for politicians to file "injunctions to stay the effect of disciplinary action." If a court rules that the party's process was unfair or the punishment disproportionate, it would be a massive blow to the Ethics Tribunal's authority.
  • The Kim Min-seok Petition: How the party handles the disciplinary request against Kim Min-seok will be the ultimate litmus test for "fairness." If the petition is dismissed without a serious hearing, expect the "pro-Choi" faction to escalate their protests, potentially boycotting leadership meetings.
  • Presidential Intervention: While President Lee Jae-myung typically stays out of "party affairs" to maintain an image of being above the fray, the paralysis of the Supreme Council may force his hand. Watch for subtle signals from the Presidential Office (the "Yongsan" office, as it is often called, referring to its location) on whether they favor "order" or "mercy."

The Democratic Party of Korea has long prided itself on its discipline compared to the often-fractured opposition. However, the heavy-handed treatment of Choi Min-hee suggests that the pressure of being the ruling party is starting to create cracks in that discipline. Whether this is a necessary "house cleaning" or a self-destructive purge will become clear as the December legislative session draws to a close.
