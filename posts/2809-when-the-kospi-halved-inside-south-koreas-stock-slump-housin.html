When the KOSPI Halved: Inside South Korea’s Stock Slump, Housing Trap, and Pension Dilemma

November 27, 2026

When South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI stock index closed at 4,711 on November 26, it marked a brutal reversal of fortune. Just five months earlier, in June, the market had hit an all-time high of 9,385. Although the index remained above its early-January trough of 4,217, wiping out roughly half of its value in half a year turned a booming equity rally into an acute test for ordinary households, housing policymakers, and the country’s massive public pension fund.

In domestic brokerage circles, the sharp sell-off has been attributed to growing doubts over whether artificial intelligence businesses can actually generate profits, combined with fears of an oversupply in semiconductors—the backbone of South Korea’s export-heavy market. Yet as media researcher Jung Joon-hee, director of the Media Humanities School Haeshigalliji, argued on his November 26 broadcast Jung Joon-hee’s Argument (Episode 258), the gap between official assurances of a solid "real economy" and a stock market plunging far faster than global peers has left investors searching for answers. Market commentators, Jung noted, have offered little more than self-serving, after-the-fact explanations—praising growth myths on the way up and blaming external shocks on the way down.

For readers outside South Korea, the crash matters because it exposes a deeper policy conflict. Under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration, policymakers have sought to steer household wealth out of overheated apartments and into productive equities. Instead, the volatility has revealed how fragile South Korea’s retail-driven stock market remains when ordinary citizens’ wages, housing aspirations, and retirement security are tied directly to equity swings.

The retail investor trap behind the numbers

South Korea has a vast retail investing culture, where salaried workers routinely enter the stock market seeking to supplement stagnant wage growth or afford skyrocketing housing costs. On his November 26 briefing, Jung laid out five structural realities that leave small investors uniquely exposed during downturns:

  • Fragmented scale: Unlike corporations or the government, households are a sprawling collection of small, uncoordinated actors.
  • Information and capital asymmetry: Ordinary investors lack the analytical tools and liquidity of institutional players, meaning individually rational bets often produce collectively damaging results.
  • Stability over yield: For working households, preserving principal matters far more than chasing high returns; once savings are wiped out, recovery is nearly impossible.
  • Housing as shelter, not speculation: Residential real estate should secure living stability and quality of life rather than act as an investment casino.
  • No sacrifice for market liquidity: Capital markets should serve ordinary citizens’ welfare, not rely on retail losses to absorb market risk.

Two widely shared cautionary tales in Korean financial media illustrate what happens when stability breaks down. Chae Sang-wook, who hosts the popular YouTube investing channel Chae Guk-jang’s KOSPI 20,000 Moneyverse, recently recounted losing nearly everything early in his career. Beginning in late 2007, Chae put about 300 million won (roughly $215,000)—money he had saved over six to seven years starting at age 24—into just two stocks. One company was liquidated over losses from KIKO currency derivatives during the global financial turmoil, while the other plunged 90 percent. Chae recalled crying for days, feeling as though six years of labor had been erased, and warned struggling investors to step away from market commentary altogether.

When investors use borrowed money, losses can spiral into decade-long debt traps. According to the YouTube channel Master of Rehabilitation, an office worker in his forties at a major South Korean conglomerate took out brokerage margin loans during the 2008 global financial crisis and ended up 250 million won (about $180,000) in debt. Even after spending five years repaying 100 percent of the principal to secure a court discharge in 2018, he turned to high-interest loans to cover repayments and tried to win back his losses in stocks—ballooning his debt to 430 million won (around $310,000). Despite earning over 7 million won (about $5,000) a month after taxes, he faced wage garnishment and considered working late-night shifts as a designated driver before the Seoul Rehabilitation Court approved a restructuring plan cutting roughly 51 percent of his principal.

Why a stock boom cannot fix South Korea’s housing obsession

The Lee Jae-myung government has championed stock market revitalization—including revisions to the Commercial Act aimed at improving corporate governance and curing the "Korea Discount," the chronic undervaluation of South Korean shares relative to global peers. A central goal of that agenda has been breaking South Korea’s real estate obsession: if household capital shifts from apartments into companies, firms gain growth funding while domestic consumption recovers from heavy mortgage burdens.

Data from this year suggest the market has behaved very differently in practice. Between January and October, South Korean homebuyers declared more than 10 trillion won (roughly $7.1 billion) in housing purchase funds raised by selling stocks, bonds, and cryptocurrencies. Rather than keeping money invested in corporations, retail participants used the stock market as a stepping stone to buy apartments. Even in May, when the KOSPI surged nearly 35 percent in just four weeks, analysts observed that profits were rapidly flowing back into housing due to South Korea’s entrenched belief that real estate never loses value.

The reverse is equally disruptive. During the recent equity slump and tighter lending rules, the share of Seoul home-purchase financing coming from stock and bond liquidations dropped from 9.8 percent to 5.9 percent, freezing upward housing mobility for ordinary buyers. Meanwhile, Jung pointed to an asymmetric exit dynamic inside the stock market itself: when returns look shaky, large institutional and foreign "big hands" exit immediately through stop-losses, short sales, and hedges, while everyday workers remain trapped holding underwater shares. With disposable cash locked in losing portfolios, households cut back on dining, travel, and daily spending—deepening the very consumption slump policymakers hoped to cure.

"When the stock market is judged unstable, whether returns are high or low, big money moves without hesitation, while small money belonging to small investors ends up hopelessly trapped." — Jung Joon-hee, Jung Joon-hee’s Argument

A $130 billion pension shock and the limits of market-driven retirement

The volatility has also engulfed the National Pension Service (NPS), South Korea’s public retirement fund. According to figures from the NPS Investment Management division and financial data provider FnGuide reported by South Korean outlets including Yongin Shinmun and Edaily MarketIn, the early-year equity rally pushed the fund’s domestic stock holdings to 395.1 trillion won (about $282 billion) by the end of February—24.5 percent of its 1,610.4 trillion won ($1.15 trillion) portfolio. That was well above the 2026 year-end target of 14.9 percent and even breached the 19.9 percent upper tolerance cap.

Under standard risk rules, the NPS should have sold shares to rebalance. Instead, in January it postponed rebalancing until late June, and in May raised its domestic equity target weight to 20.8 percent amid concerns that heavy selling would shock the market. That left the pension fund heavily exposed when prices tumbled. Total fund reserves fell from 1,865.6 trillion won at the end of June to 1,684.2 trillion won by the end of July—a one-month paper drop of 181.4 trillion won (about $130 billion), driven largely by a 124.6 trillion won ($89 billion) slide in domestic equities. Cumulative returns sank from 27.22 percent to 14.84 percent over the same month. FnGuide separately tallied that NPS stakes of 5 percent or more across 287 listed companies fell by 182.4 trillion won (about $130 billion) between late June and October 15.

While unrealized paper losses are not permanent, investment analysts have questioned whether political considerations—such as upcoming local elections and medium-term asset allocation talks—led the fund to delay prudent diversification. Jung argued that relying on public pension funds to prop up stock prices blurs the line between "pension fund socialism," the ideal that citizens might guide corporate responsibility as collective owners, and "pension fund capitalism," where workers’ retirement savings are exposed to market volatility while the state shifts retirement risk onto individuals.

Still, not everyone agrees that cutting domestic equity exposure is the answer. Writing in The Fact, columnist Park Soon-hyuk criticized earlier NPS plans to shrink domestic stocks from 15.4 percent down to 13 percent by 2029, which would require selling 26.4 trillion won ($19 billion) over five years. Citing Warren Buffett’s warning against "rearview mirror investing" and the principle of mean reversion, Park noted that while U.S. equities traded at price-to-earnings multiples above 23, South Korean stocks traded below 8—levels seen only during the 1997 Asian financial crisis and 2008 global crisis. Selling off deeply undervalued domestic equities solely based on recent underperformance, he argued, would be equally shortsighted.

Beneath that debate lies a misconception Jung sought to dismantle: the popular slogan that "the era of labor income is over and the era of investment income has arrived." In macroeconomic terms, that trend merely signals that total returns to capital have outpaced total wages—an indicator of widening inequality, not proof that individual workers can safely replace paychecks with stock picking.

What to watch next

As South Korea digests a six-month market halving, three concrete developments will determine whether the downturn stabilizes or deepens economic strain:

  • NPS asset allocation governance: Watch how the National Pension Service’s fund management committee sets its 2027–2031 mid-term allocation plan, and whether it establishes clearer, politically insulated rules for rebalancing domestic equities without triggering forced market sell-offs.
  • Decoupling housing from equities: Monitor whether the Lee Jae-myung administration’s corporate governance reforms succeed in keeping long-term capital in Korean stocks, or whether any equity rebound simply fuels another wave of Seoul apartment purchases.
  • Tech earnings and household consumption: Watch upcoming quarterly earnings from South Korea’s semiconductor and AI supply-chain giants alongside retail spending data; if chip oversupply fears persist, trapped retail savings could weigh heavily on domestic growth into next year.
