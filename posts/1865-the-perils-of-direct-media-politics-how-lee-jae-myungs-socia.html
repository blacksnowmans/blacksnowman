The Perils of Direct Media Politics: How Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Habits and Party Fractures Threaten His Reform Agenda

November 02, 2026

Late at night, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung opened X (formerly Twitter) on his smartphone, scrolled through his feed, and reshared a post originating from FM Korea—popularly known as "Femco"—a male-dominated online forum infamous for its aggressive anti-feminist rhetoric, hostility toward social minorities, and right-leaning online grievance culture. The post complained about Seoul’s public postpartum care center priority rules, claiming that placing multicultural families in the secondary priority bracket—alongside war veterans, single parents, and disabled residents—amounted to "reverse discrimination" against native Korean mothers carrying twins. Attached to the repost was President Lee’s brief endorsement: "Thank you for the good point."

What might have appeared to an outside observer as a routine social media interaction was, in reality, a political flashpoint. By validating a piece of unverified online grievance from a fringe community, the head of state transformed a xenophobic narrative into an active item of government business. Within hours, the presidential office scrambled to defend the post as a "fairness-based correction of policy excess," while government officials began discussing revisions to enforcement decrees. This episode was not merely an isolated slip of the tongue; it revealed a troubling dynamic at the heart of the Lee administration: an unfiltered reliance on direct social media engagement, an army of subservient aides, and a ruling party struggling with internal cowardice and stagnant support.

Amplifying Fringe Grievance: The Welfare Debate and Administrative Failure

To understand the severity of the controversy, one must examine the welfare policies targeted by the online post. Under Mayor Oh Se-hoon, the Seoul Metropolitan Government initiated trial welfare programs—such as safety income pilot schemes and public postpartum care facilities—designed specifically to shield vulnerable populations from systemic poverty. The efficacy of these targeted safety nets is well-documented. In Seoul’s first-stage Safety Income trial involving 484 low-income households, essential food consumption rose by 12 percent, medical spending increased by 28 percent, and self-reported depression levels plummeted by 31 percent. In public health and welfare administration, prioritizing high-risk and disadvantaged groups is standard practice to build overall societal resilience.

However, the Femco post ignored these structural realities, replacing policy data with sensationalist headlines framing multicultural families as privileged freeloaders stripping native citizens of public benefits. Multicultural families in South Korea continue to face severe language barriers, economic marginalization, and systemic discrimination. When the president amplifies a narrative that portrays these marginalized groups as perpetrators of "reverse discrimination," he degrades the public sphere, replacing evidence-based governance with the politics of division.

The Incompetence of the Presidential Staff

Equally alarming was the reaction of the presidential staff. Rather than acting as a deliberate buffer to fact-check information and manage strategic communications, senior aides immediately bowed to the president’s personal social media activity. Lim Po-geun, the presidential secretary for population policy, quickly announced that the administration was reviewing amendments to the Mother and Child Health Act’s enforcement decree to accommodate the online complaint. Not a single advisor stepped forward to inform the president that the forum post was factually misleading and politically dangerous.

"A proper advisor is someone who participates and deliberates together. If they fail to do that, they are not advisors—they are merely household manservants or lackeys," noted one harsh critic of the presidential office's paralysis.

When presidential staff abandon their duty to offer candid advice and instead spend their time inventing post-hoc justifications for executive impulses, policy missteps become inevitable. Instead of correcting public misconceptions and explaining why affirmative welfare policies exist, the administration allowed a vocal internet outrage movement to dictate executive priorities.

The Trap of Unfiltered Media Politics and the Democratic Party's Silence

President Lee’s reliance on direct digital communication has deep roots. Back in October 2018, while serving as Governor of Gyeonggi Province, Lee reflected on his heavy Twitter usage during an interview on TBS News Factory with host Kim Ou-joon. When advised to log off, Lee remarked that responding directly to citizens at night was his "greatest strength and a breathing hole," though he candidly admitted it was simultaneously becoming a "shackle."

During his years as a political outsider battling hostile coverage from mainstream conservative media, direct social media interaction served as an indispensable weapon to bypass traditional gatekeepers and mobilize his core base. But strategies born of outsider survival become dangerous liabilities when brought into the presidential office. In recent months, President Lee has used his personal social media accounts to directly confront coverage from major news outlets, arguing over individual headlines and comment sections. This constant, unmediated combativeness risks eroding the dignity of the executive office and entangling the administration in unending emotional spats.

The Ruling Party's "Silence Cartel"

The root cause of the president's isolated social media battles lies in the passive behavior of the ruling party. The Democratic Party of Korea (DP) has largely retreated into silence. Lawmakers who eagerly defended Lee on camera during primary battles have gone quiet as executive approval ratings stall. Instead of taking the lead in defending administration policies or absorbing political damage on controversial issues, ruling party MPs have ducked for cover to protect their own local electoral interests.

Deprived of an effective party shield, the president feels politically isolated—prompting him to retreat into the familiar habit of late-night social media combat. This dynamic was vividly demonstrated during the DP's recent internal dispute over allegations that the Shincheonji religious group systematically infiltrated the party's August national convention. Despite initial pledges by senior party leaders like Park Sun-won to conduct a thorough investigation, party elders quickly moved to bury the issue, citing risks to member records and party unity. By suppressing truth-seeking to avoid short-term discomfort, the party leadership further undermines its moral authority and public trust.

Stagnant Approval Ratings and the Looming 100-Day Milestone

The strategic missteps of the administration are increasingly reflected in electoral math and polling data. The Democratic Party's victory margin in the recent snap presidential election—which followed the unprecedented constitutional crisis surrounding Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol's military insurrection, subsequent impeachment, and imprisonment—offered a stark warning.

  • 20th Presidential Election (vs. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol): Lee Jae-myung secured 47.83% of the vote.
  • Post-Impeachment Presidential Election: Lee Jae-myung secured 49.42% of the vote.

Despite the historic collapse and removal of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the Democratic Party managed an increase of just 1.5 percentage points. By attempting to court conservative online demographics while sidelining its core progressive base, the party has failed to expand its support among moderate voters, leaving its approval ratings flatlining for over a year.

This stagnation has set the stage for an intense internal political reckoning. November 24 marks the 100th day of the current DP leadership under party chairman Kim Min-seok. Prominent DP lawmaker Jung Chung-rae raised alarm bells during an appearance on Park Si-young TV, declaring that the 100-day mark would serve as a decisive deadline to evaluate the party's trajectory. Jung hinted that he was preparing to raise a "100-day flag" to press for clear accountability and strategic realignment if party ratings failed to recover. With voters increasingly anxious about a potential comeback by the opposition People Power Party, the upcoming 100-day mark will force a high-stakes midterm review of the ruling party's leadership.

Opposition Integration: The January 2027 Deadline

Beyond internal leadership reviews, the broader balance of power within South Korea's progressive camp depends heavily on integration with minor opposition parties, most notably the Rebuilding Korea Party, led by Shin Jang-sik. Lawmaker Jung Chung-rae recently sparked widespread debate by proposing a firm deadline of January 2027 for a complete party merger between the Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party.

While critics within the DP dismissed the proposal as premature pressure, the political calendar makes the January 2027 deadline a practical necessity for the April 2028 general elections:

  • July 2027: The cutoff date for prospective candidates to join the party, as DP rules require at least six months of paid dues to participate in candidate selection primaries.
  • February 2028: Official party primaries begin for general election nominations.
  • January 2027: The absolute deadline for a merger, allowing incoming members six months to build local grassroots support and compete fairly in primary elections.

In the last parliamentary elections, dozens of critical swing districts in the Seoul metropolitan area, South Gyeongsang province, and the Chungcheong region were decided by margins of 1 to 3 percentage points. If progressive votes split between the DP and the Rebuilding Korea Party, conservative opposition candidates will win those tight races. However, entrenched DP incumbents currently oppose an early merger, fearing that high-profile figures from the Rebuilding Korea Party will challenge them in primary races. Protecting incumbent fiefdoms at the expense of broad progressive unity threatens to fracture the coalition before the next national election.

What to Watch Next

President Lee Jae-myung stands at a critical juncture in his presidency. To regain momentum and restore public confidence, the administration and the ruling party must address three key dynamics in the coming months:

  • Presidential Communication Reform: Watch whether President Lee curtails his reliance on direct social media posts and restores formal, institutionalized channels of executive communication and staff vetting.
  • The November 24 Leadership Assessment: Pay attention to how DP Chairman Kim Min-seok responds to internal pressure from lawmakers like Jung Chung-rae as the 100-day deadline approaches, particularly regarding party performance and policy direction.
  • Merger Negotiations with the Rebuilding Korea Party: Monitor whether the DP leadership undercuts incumbent self-interest to establish formal merger talks with Shin Jang-sik’s party ahead of the January 2027 deadline, ensuring a unified front for the 2028 parliamentary elections.
