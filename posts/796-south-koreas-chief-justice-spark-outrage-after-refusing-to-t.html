South Korea's Chief Justice Spark Outrage After Refusing to Take Oath at Parliamentary Audit

October 07, 2026

The scene inside the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee felt less like a routine government oversight hearing and more like a constitutional crisis in slow motion. Lawmakers sat in disbelief as Cho Hee-de, Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of South Korea, flatly refused to take the standard witness oath or answer direct questions. For an outsider, the idea of the head of the judiciary showing up to a parliamentary audit only to refuse the oath sounds baffling. For South Koreans watching live streams, it was a staggering display of defiance that laid bare a deepening war between the nation's courts and its legislature.

The confrontation thrust the judiciary's internal culture, luxury spending habits, and political neutrality into the harsh glare of public scrutiny. As opposition lawmakers pressed the Chief Justice on everything from unspent vacancies to lavish dining funds, Cho retreated behind the traditional shield of judicial independence. But critics argue that the shield has been weaponized—turned from a safeguard for impartial judging into a fortress for institutional impunity.

When the Head of the Courts Rejects Accountability

The parliamentary audit descended into chaos almost immediately when Chief Justice Cho declared that he would neither take the oath nor provide formal testimony. Lawmakers from the progressive minor opposition Progressive Party and the ruling Democratic Party argued that the refusal was a transparent legal dodge to avoid potential perjury charges. When pressed about leaving Supreme Court vacancies unfilled for roughly 200 days—a historic backlog in South Korea's constitutional history—Cho offered evasive answers, prompting one lawmaker to ask whether he was harboring ambitions to follow in the footsteps of jailed former leader Yoon Suk Yeol.

Cho's defense relied on a trio of arguments that stunned legal scholars: he claimed his authority was equivalent to that of the president, denied any knowledge of operational finances, and accused critics of using judicial independence as a mere excuse. To suggest that a career judge appointed through civil service exams holds equal constitutional weight to a directly elected president struck many observers as a direct challenge to South Korea's democratic separation of powers. The friction highlights a growing public anger over courts that appear increasingly detached from democratic norms.

The Cash Distribution and Luxury Dining Scandals

Beyond constitutional clashes, the audit exposed highly questionable financial practices within the Supreme Court administration. Lawmakers zeroed in on tens of millions of won (tens of thousands of US dollars) in regular cash disbursements routed from the Chief Justice's office directly to Supreme Court justices. While court administrators claimed these payments were cleared through standard operational budget shifts, questioning of the Board of Audit and Inspection revealed no such approval existed. Even conservative opposition lawmakers on the committee conceded that the maneuvers amounted to improper budget diversion.

The financial controversies did not stop at cash envelopes. Relentless questioning by the ruling party uncovered records showing thousands of bottles of wine and high-end meals—including luxury omakase dinners costing hundreds of dollars per person—funded through official operational expense accounts. When confronted with records showing roughly 180 million won (about $130,000) worth of wine and meals tied to his residence and office cards, Cho claimed he never touched the credit cards, drank zero alcohol, and knew nothing about the purchases. The defense that overworked judges simply wanted expensive food they had previously missed out on did little to soothe public anger, particularly at a time when average citizens are tightening their belts amid economic pressures.

The Erosion of Public Trust in South Korea's Judiciary

The fallout from the audit reflects a broader, precipitous decline in public confidence in South Korea's legal system. Polls indicate that the judiciary's approval rating has been cut in half over the past year and a half, sliding from 38% down to 19%. Critics argue this collapse is self-inflicted, driven by perceived political biases in high-profile rulings and a leadership style that treats the court as an independent kingdom rather than a public institution accountable to the citizenry.

Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party pointed to controversial rulings issued ahead of past elections and a perceived silence during the turbulent final days of Yoon Suk Yeol's martial law attempt as evidence of a politicized court. They argued that the judiciary risks sinking to the level of a compromised prosecutorial apparatus if its leaders continue to shield themselves behind institutional autonomy while misusing public funds.

What to Watch Next

As the parliamentary audit concludes, the political pressure on Chief Justice Cho is unlikely to fade. Key developments to monitor in the coming weeks include:

  • Formal Investigations: Watch for potential follow-up probes by financial regulators or investigative agencies into the legality of the Supreme Court's cash disbursements and operational expense card usage.
  • Judicial Reform Bills: Anticipate renewed legislative pushes by the ruling Democratic Party to tighten parliamentary oversight over court administration and budget transparency.
  • Internal Dissent: Monitor whether rank-and-file judges and legal organizations issue further public statements demanding accountability or leadership changes within the judiciary.
  • Resignation Pressure: Track whether opposition parties escalate their campaign from committee-level grilling to formal calls for impeachment or voluntary resignation.
