The Death of Traditional Media in South Korea: Why YouTube Outranked Major Networks

September 18, 2026

Turn on a television in South Korea today, and you are immediately met with an unsettling question: What is actually true? Trust in mainstream journalism has hit rock bottom, splintering the public into isolated digital tribes that consume only what they want to believe. This fractured reality was laid bare in a recent joint media credibility survey by SisaIN, a prominent South Korean weekly magazine, and Gallup Korea, one of the country's leading polling agencies. But the numbers do more than just rank winners and losers—they tell a deeper story about how the public relates to the news, and how political devotion in the country is curdling into disillusionment.

The Great Media Realignment: How an Independent YouTube Show Toppled the Giants

For anyone accustomed to South Korea's traditional media establishment, the results of the 2026 credibility survey came as a profound shock. Polling 1,013 adults nationwide, the survey crowned a YouTube-based political talk show, Kim Ou-joon's Humble Factory is Hard, as the country's most trusted current affairs program, capturing 6.3% of public trust. It outperformed every single legacy broadcaster: MBC's Newsdesk trailed at 5.9%, KBS's flagship News 9 sat at 4.9%, JTBC's Newsroom at 2.1%, and SBS's 8 News at a meager 2.0%.

During a broadcast discussing these results, Hong Sa-hun, a veteran journalist turned commentator, joked that the success was all thanks to him, easing the tension in the studio. But the weight of the data is no laughing matter. Decades of institutional authority, capital accumulation, and prime-time broadcasting slots were eclipsed by a digital-native, independent operation. It is an undeniable empirical sign that ordinary people have stopped gathering around traditional television sets to receive the evening news. The center of gravity in South Korean media has permanently shifted.

When legacy outlets fail to do their jobs—trimming their coverage to appease whoever holds power or hiding behind a cowardly mask of "mechanical neutrality" while truth is buried—the public looks elsewhere. This survey proves that trust is not handed down from an ivory tower; it is earned when a platform speaks directly to the frustrations and lived experiences of its audience.

Why the Public Abandoned Legacy Networks for Alternative Platforms

The slide of terrestrial and cable news networks cannot be dismissed as a mere symptom of younger generations abandoning television. The anemic ratings of KBS News 9 (4.9%) and JTBC Newsroom (2.1%) are a cold, harsh judgment on years of compromised independence and hollow public-interest reporting. As these networks tiptoed around political power to protect their institutional interests, YouTube channels stepped into the void, armed with unvarnished commentary and aggressive analysis.

During panel discussions, hosts even joked about how baffling it is that a deeply polarizing figure like Kim Ou-joon could top a credibility index. Yet that irony highlights a deeper fatigue: the public knows that a perfectly neutral, objective media outlet does not exist. Rather than enduring establishment press outlets that pretend to be fair while constantly reading the room for the ruling class, audiences prefer a platform that openly admits its political alignment but remains fiercely committed to attacking entrenched power based on factual reporting.

This poll serves as a reliable compass pointing to where South Korean public opinion is heading. YouTube has ceased to be a playground for independent creators; it has cemented itself squarely at the center of the country's public square. If legacy institutions refuse to acknowledge this reality and cling to archaic broadcast habits, the chasm between them and the public will only widen.

From Fandom Politics to Political Cynicism

The structural transformation of South Korean media mirrors a parallel crisis in its politics. In a widely discussed academic paper titled From Identification to Love to Cynicism: The Developmental Logic and Dynamics of Political Intimacy, Professor Park Koo-yong offers a sharp diagnostic tool for understanding South Korea’s political decay. To disentangle terms that are often thrown around interchangeably—fandom politics, charismatic politics, and responsible politics—Park categorizes the relationship between political leaders and their supporters into two distinct paradigms. The first is the "identification paradigm," more commonly known as fandom politics.

In this phase, total alignment between supporters and a political leader is demanded. It is a politics of absolute belief. The president's inner thoughts become the supporters' inner thoughts; the leader's snap decisions dictate everything, and the base follows blindly. While it projects an aura of impenetrable strength, Park warns that this dynamic is profoundly dangerous over the long term.

When a leader and their supporters erase the distance between them and operate as a single organism, critical thinking vanishes. Animosity toward opposing political camps is dialed to the maximum, and internal dissent is treated as treason. While fandom politics can generate short-term explosive energy, it cannot solve the complex, grinding problems of a democracy. The stronger the demand for blind identification, the faster politics loses its capacity for self-reflection and descends into a vortex of raw emotion.

The Promise of the Recognition Paradigm and Responsible Governance

In contrast, Park’s second model—the "recognition paradigm"—aims for an entirely different political order. Here, the constitution, legal procedures, and policy promises act as the mediums for support and communication. Instead of forcing unconditional identification, this framework preserves a healthy tension between leaders and citizens.

Within this paradigm, the emotional connection between a leader and the public exists on a spectrum. It can start from a place of deep affinity and love, move through friendship, solidarity, and friendly indifference, and ultimately tip over into cynicism and political paralysis if broken. When Park remarked in interviews that he hoped a presidential press conference might at least restore a baseline of "friendship" between the administration and the public, this is what he meant. Citizens should not be mindless courtiers; they are partners demanding accountability, maintaining a critical friendship built on fulfilled promises.

Politics must be the management of state institutions, not an emotional pressure valve. When policy promises are honored and constitutional guardrails respected, citizens feel a sense of security. But if a leader loses their reform-minded footing and betrays public expectations, that relationship plummets past friendship straight into cynicism. Much of the South Korean public today is standing right at that dangerous threshold.

Rebuilding Trust Before the Base Walks Away

The cracks in the relationship between South Korea's leadership and its core supporters are no longer hidden. Fandoms do not turn overnight; they sour because supporters begin to sense, long before anyone else, that a leader has lost their original conviction. The moment a subtle fracture appears in a trusted figure, public sentiment turns with the force of a storm.

What must a leader say to win back a disillusioned base? Vague excuses and superficial apologies no longer cut it. The only viable path forward is an unyielding commitment to core reform agendas—such as sweeping judicial and prosecutorial overhauls—that initially birthed the administration's political momentum.

The public's anger is not born of a demand for perfection, but from a terrifying realization that authenticity has evaporated. Realizing where trust was lost, offering agonizing self-reflection for past missteps, and presenting a concrete roadmap for reform are the only ways to reopen closed hearts. Defensiveness and buck-passing in moments of crisis will only alienate the moderate middle alongside the core base.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this turbulent intersection of media distrust and political disillusionment, several concrete markers will determine whether the current political and media landscape heals or fractures further:

  • The Response to Personnel Shakes: Watch for tangible personnel overhauls within the administration. If leadership continues to double down on controversial appointments despite public outcry, expect political cynicism among the base to deepen rapidly.
  • Mainstream Media Survival Tactics: Pay attention to how traditional networks like KBS and MBC attempt to claw back credibility. Without structural reforms to protect journalistic independence, their ratings slide is likely irreversible.
  • Legislative Reform Deadlines: Monitor whether the ruling bloc delivers on promised institutional reforms—particularly regarding prosecutorial power and checks on executive authority—or treats them as temporary talking points.
  • Polling Trajectories in the Moderate Middle: Keep a close eye on upcoming approval ratings and independent polling trends. The speed at which moderate voters migrate from cautious support to outright cynicism will dictate the government's leverage in upcoming legislative battles.

Ultimately, what ordinary citizens want is remarkably simple: a guarantee that the trust they placed in institutions and leaders will not make them look foolish. Until the political class returns to the fundamentals of responsible governance and the media rediscovers its backbone, South Korea's public square will remain a battleground of cold cynicism.
