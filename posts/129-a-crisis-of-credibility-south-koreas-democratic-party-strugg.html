A Crisis of Credibility: South Korea’s Democratic Party Struggles with Youth Alienation and Internal Strife

September 29, 2026

Watching the latest political developments in South Korea feels like witnessing a slow-motion collision. On one hand, you have a judiciary branch mired in scandal; on the other, the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK)—the primary opposition party—is grappling with a plummeting approval rating among the nation’s youth and an increasingly toxic internal culture. It is a moment of profound vulnerability for the progressive movement, and the current response from party leadership has been, to put it mildly, disappointing.

Judicial Misconduct: The Chief Justice’s Expense Scandal

The credibility of the judiciary, the very bedrock of a functioning democracy, is under fire. A recent investigation into Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae revealed a pattern of behavior that borders on the farcical. Records submitted to the National Assembly claimed that the Chief Justice held a meeting with 38 judicial researchers at a restaurant in Seoul’s Bangbae neighborhood. The catch? The restaurant’s private room, where the meeting supposedly took place, has a maximum capacity of only 19 people.

The financial irregularities go deeper. Records show high-end "omakase" sushi meals and five-star hotel dinners being billed as inexpensive lunches. In one instance, a meal costing over 70,000 won (about $50) per person was logged as 28,000 won (about $20) to fit within the 50,000 won (about $35) limit set by South Korea’s anti-graft laws. To maintain this facade, the court reportedly inflated the number of attendees in their official records.

Beyond these fabricated receipts, the Board of Audit and Inspection discovered that the Supreme Court had been distributing monthly "encouragement bonuses" of 2 million won (about $1,400) in cash to Supreme Court justices and had spent 100 million won (about $70,000) on wine without proper documentation. When the institution tasked with upholding the law treats public funds as a personal slush fund, it is not just a breach of policy—it is a betrayal of the public trust. The DPK is now facing immense pressure to push for impeachment, and any hesitation from the party to hold the judiciary accountable is being viewed by voters as a dereliction of their duty as the opposition.

The Youth Exodus: A "Double-Score" Defeat

Perhaps more alarming for the DPK than the judicial scandal is the party's crumbling support among the younger generation. According to a recent poll by Realmeter, a prominent local polling agency, the DPK has been effectively routed among voters in their 20s. Support for the DPK in this demographic stands at just 20.9%, while the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative party—commands 54.5%.

This is not a sudden shift, but the result of a long-standing disconnect. While party leadership has frequently spoken about "embracing the youth," their policies and communication styles remain trapped in an ivory tower. Younger Koreans, who face a brutal job market and skyrocketing housing costs, have grown weary of empty promises regarding "fairness." They are no longer swayed by rhetoric; they are coldly evaluating whether their lives are actually improving.

The situation is equally dire in the party's traditional strongholds, including the Jeolla provinces, where support is sliding. When the party’s base begins to peel away, the strategy of trying to capture moderate "swing" voters becomes a fantasy. The current decline in approval is a clear signal that the DPK’s approach to party management and its reflexive, often arrogant, political posturing are being rejected by the very people it claims to represent.

The Rhyu Si-min Controversy: A Case Study in Political Hubris

The DPK’s recent reaction to political commentator Rhyu Si-min further highlights the party's internal malaise. After Rhyu made public remarks critiquing the current administration, a poll by NewsTomato found that 58% of the public—including a majority of moderates and even a significant portion of conservatives—viewed his comments as "constructive criticism."

Despite this public sentiment, DPK leader Kim Min-seok chose to distance the party from Rhyu, grouping him with fringe YouTubers and implying that his comments were subject to party disciplinary procedures. This was a catastrophic political error. Rhyu is not even a member of the DPK, yet the party treated his intellectual independence as a threat. This public shunning emboldened external activist groups to file a police complaint against Rhyu for "spreading false information," effectively turning a political disagreement into a legal battle.

By attacking a respected veteran intellectual who simply voiced a common critique, the DPK leadership has signaled that they prioritize ideological purity over open debate. This "purist" mentality is a trap. Politics is not a religious cult where dissenters are purged to maintain the faith; it is a coalition-building exercise. By alienating those who offer honest, if painful, advice, the party is shrinking its own tent and fueling the perception that it is an insular, intolerant institution.

What to Watch Next

The immediate future for the DPK hinges on whether it can move past its current reflex of scapegoating and internal gatekeeping. Here are the key developments to monitor:

  • Impeachment Momentum: Will the DPK move beyond rhetoric and actually file for the impeachment of judicial figures involved in the expense scandal? Failure to act will reinforce the narrative that the party is weak or complicit.
  • Leadership Accountability: Watch for changes in how DPK leadership addresses internal criticism. If they continue to treat intellectual dissenters as enemies, the "youth exodus" will likely accelerate.
  • The "Police Complaint" Fallout: The legal action taken against Rhyu Si-min will likely backfire, painting the DPK as an entity that suppresses free speech. How the party manages the optics of this "lawfare" will be a major test of their political maturity.

The DPK stands at a crossroads. They can either continue to lash out at critics and blame the media for their declining support, or they can perform the uncomfortable, necessary work of internal reform. For a party that claims to be the vanguard of progress, the latter is the only path that leads to long-term survival.
