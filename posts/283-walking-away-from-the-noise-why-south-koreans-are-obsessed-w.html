Walking Away from the Noise: Why South Koreans Are Obsessed with the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Picture yourself stuck in a concrete maze. Outside, your phone buzzes with yet another notification, traffic is gridlocked on the morning commute, and your internal clock is entirely dictated by deadlines, expectations, and the quiet panic of falling behind. It is a modern exhaustion familiar to city dwellers everywhere, but in South Korea, this specific brand of burnout has sparked a massive, enduring cultural phenomenon: an escape to a rugged 800-kilometer (nearly 500-mile) trail cutting across northern Spain.

For thousands of Koreans each year, the antidote to the hyper-competitive pressure cooker of Seoul isn't a beach resort or a luxury spa. It is the Camino de Santiago—specifically the French Way, which begins in the small French border village of Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port. Getting there requires over 20 hours in the air followed by a seven-hour drive, landing travelers at the foot of the Pyrenees. Yet, year after year, tens of thousands of South Koreans make this grueling pilgrimage. To understand why, you have to look past the romantic travel brochures and examine the immense social weight they are trying to leave behind.

The Birth of a Pilgrim: Shedding Status at the Starting Line

The journey begins not with the first step, but at the pilgrim's office in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, where travelers are handed a credential—a pilgrim passport. This document instantly strips away everything that defines a person back home: job titles, university degrees, net worth, and societal rank. In exchange, you are given a single, leveling title: pilgrim.

Significantly, the passport includes a clear designation indicating that you intend to travel on foot rather than by bicycle. It is a deliberate declaration to slow down the pace of life. Around 60,000 people a year start their journey here, and a remarkably high proportion of them are South Korean. The fact that the office provides official Korean-language instruction sheets speaks volumes about how deeply ingrained this pilgrimage has become in South Korea's collective imagination. People arrive carrying invisible wounds: some burned out by the corporate grind, others experiencing major life crises, and many simply seeking to reclaim a sense of basic human dignity by putting one foot in front of the other.

Along the way, the traditional scallop shell—worn like a badge of identity—acts as a silent signal. When strangers pass on the trail and exchange a quiet "Buen Camino," the profound isolation of modern life briefly shatters. Historically a strictly religious obligation to visit the tomb of St. James, today's pilgrimage has morphed into a deeply personalized spiritual quest. Stripped of rigid dogmas, walkers find their religion in the friction of the dirt, the sting of a blister, and the raw wind over the mountains.

The Slow Torture of Time and the Albergue Community

Once on the trail, time ceases to function normally. The frantic rush of urban schedules is replaced by the raw rhythm of the sun: you walk when it rises, you eat when you are hungry, and you sleep when darkness falls. For veterans, this routine is a comforting ritual. For first-timers, the transition can be jarring and outright punishing.

When flat landscapes give way to relentless vertical climbs, grand life plans suddenly become utterly useless. Worrying about your five-year career trajectory or your financial investments won't help you scale a muddy ridge in the Pyrenees. The only thing that matters is the immediate sensory feedback of the ground beneath your boots. Every repetitive step acts like a mental broom, sweeping away the hyper-anxious calculations that define contemporary success.

Every 10 to 20 kilometers, weary travelers find refuge in albergues, traditional pilgrim hostels that have dotted the route for centuries. These spaces function as makeshift communities where barriers drop instantly. Whether it's a father and daughter from South Africa or solo travelers from Seoul, strangers share dinner tables, massage each other's aching knees, and quietly share the day's suffering. In hyper-individualized modern societies, true spaces for communal vulnerability are rare; the cramped bunk rooms of the Camino offer a poignant reminder that nobody is suffering alone.

Confronting the Mirror: Pain as a Master

The physical toll of the Camino is entirely non-transferable. Swollen knees and raw blisters are solitary burdens, forcing travelers to confront their own fragility. Colombian mothers hauling heavy camping gear, or solo hikers struggling under oversized packs, quickly realize that their heavy loads are physical manifestations of the stubborn emotional baggage they refuse to drop in daily life.

Encountering your own raw self is rarely comfortable. Cowardice, impatience, selfishness, and deep-seated insecurities—traits easily masked behind professional facades and polite social masks in ordinary life—are laid completely bare under the Spanish sun. Yet, there is a profound liberation in finally acknowledging those flaws. Surviving 800 kilometers demands a level of self-forgiveness that short vacations can never unlock.

At the same time, the trail faces mounting friction. The very popularity that draws millions has introduced the shadow of overtourism. Historic villages now struggle with mounting litter, and some transient visitors treat the sacred route as a mere novelty rather than a living community, threatening the delicate balance between sustainable travel and commercialization.

What to Watch Next

If you are watching South Korea's cultural relationship with the Camino de Santiago from the outside, several key shifts are worth tracking:

  • The Evolving Demographics of Burnout: Initially popular among older travelers with time on their hands, the demographic has rapidly shifted toward younger South Koreans in their 20s and 30s seeking escape from extreme youth unemployment and corporate burnout.
  • Sustainable Tourism Backlash: Look for local European municipalities and South Korean travel agencies to face increasing pressure to address trail erosion, waste management, and respectful tourism practices.
  • The Integration Back Home: Pay attention to how travel memoirs, television shows, and essays centered on the Camino continue to shape mainstream Korean publishing and wellness culture, redefining what "success" looks like for a generation exhausted by the rat race.

Ultimately, the true destination of the Camino is never Santiago itself. For the thousands of South Koreans who trade their city skylines for dirt paths every year, the real test begins the moment they return home—trying to hold onto the quiet rhythm of a single step in a world that demands you run.
