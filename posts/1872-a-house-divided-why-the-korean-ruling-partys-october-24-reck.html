A House Divided: Why the Korean Ruling Party’s October 24 Reckoning Matters

November 02, 2026

Step inside the halls of the National Assembly in Seoul, and you can feel the tension mounting within South Korea's ruling center-left Democratic Party of Korea (DPK). Having reclaimed power and installed President Lee Jae-myung in the Blue House following the dramatic impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the ruling coalition was expected to move forward with a unified reform agenda. Instead, the party finds itself trapped in an uncomfortable paradox: holding executive power while suffering from deep internal fissures and stagnant public support.

At the center of this storm sits Kim Min-seok, a key figure in the party leadership whose political survival now hinges on a looming October 24 deadline. Coupled with a bitter controversy over alleged ties to Shincheonji—a high-profile, secretive religious group that frequently becomes a flashpoint in Korean politics—the leadership faces an existential question. Will it offer genuine reform and apologies for internal political maneuvering, or will it slide further into factional isolation?

The October 24 Deadline and the Rhetoric of Approval Ratings

In political analyst circles, including popular political commentary outlets like Park Si-young TV, October 24 has taken on outsized significance. It marks the date when key public opinion polls and internal performance metrics will be delivered to the Democratic Party leadership. In South Korean politics, party approval ratings are not merely cosmetic numbers; they are the ultimate report card that determines whether a leadership team retains the authority to manage candidate nominations for upcoming elections.

Earlier, the leadership under Kim Min-seok set a clear benchmark: a 10 percentage point surge in party approval ratings. However, as the deadline drew near and polls remained stubbornly flat, the leadership's rhetoric began to shift. What was once presented as an explicit target has been quietly reframed as merely a "firm commitment to try our best."

This linguistic pivot has drawn sharp criticism from both political rivals and political commentators inside the party. For many rank-and-file members, watching leadership play word games to evade accountability feels uncomfortably familiar. It echoes the bitter public fatigue experienced during the presidency of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, when absurd rhetorical maneuvers were used to downplay major political failures. When political leaders treat explicit promises as mere figures of speech, public trust erodes rapidly.

If the October 24 figures fail to demonstrate a meaningful rebound, Kim Min-seok will face aggressive questioning from both mainstream media and internal party critics. With public sentiment remaining cool, excuses will no longer suffice to hold off calls for fundamental structural change.

The Shincheonji Controversy and Factional Smear Campaigns

Compounding the pressure over polling figures is an extraordinary internal dispute surrounding an emergency party caucus on Shincheonji. To understand why this issue cuts so deeply, one must grasp the place Shincheonji holds in the South Korean public imagination. As a secretive fringe religious organization that has repeatedly found itself in the public crosshairs during national crises, any alleged association with the group carries severe electoral stigma.

During the ruling party's recent national convention, rumors were systematically circulated alleging links between Shincheonji and Jung Chung-rae, a prominent party leader and rival faction figure. At the time, these claims functioned as a potent smear campaign designed to cloud voters' judgment and derail Jung's candidacy. The strategy highlighted a dark side of party politics: the willingness of internal factions to weaponize unverified religious accusations to preserve their own political standing.

Now, as details emerge regarding how those allegations were manufactured, the scandal has turned back on the current leadership. Lawmakers are demanding a full accounting of how private data was accessed:

  • Unlawful Access: Who authorized the retrieval and screening of confidential membership registries containing thousands of personal records?
  • Political Weaponization: How did internal party data end up being deployed as a weapon during an internal leadership race?
  • Accountability: Will the leadership offer an official apology to Jung Chung-rae and the party rank-and-file for allowing black propaganda to disrupt democratic processes?

The unauthorized inspection of personal data is not just a violation of internal party rules; it borders on legal misconduct. For many party members, a failure by Kim Min-seok's leadership to thoroughly investigate this breach and deliver a clear apology will render internal reconciliation impossible.

Constitutional Constraints: Mergers and the Early Convention Trap

When major South Korean parties face prolonged polling slumps, they typically look to external fixes, such as political mergers or emergency leadership committees. In the current context, rumors have repeatedly surfaced about a potential merger between the ruling Democratic Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller progressive party led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk that shares a reformist agenda.

Yet a merger appears highly implausible under current conditions. The Rebuilding Korea Party maintains its own distinct ideological identity and dedicated supporter base. It has little incentive to act as a lifeline for a ruling party leadership struggling with internal discipline. Furthermore, Kim Min-seok currently lacks the political capital required to negotiate such an alliance on favorable terms.

This leaves the leadership facing a structural dilemma embedded within the Democratic Party’s own constitution:

The Eight-Month Rule: According to party regulations, an Emergency Response Committee (비대위)—the standard mechanism used to replace failing party leaders in South Korea—cannot be established within eight months of a general election.

This rule creates a high-stakes scenario. If public support continues to decline and leadership authority collapses past this deadline, the party cannot simply appoint a temporary caretaker committee. Instead, it would be forced to trigger an emergency national convention to elect entirely new leadership. Such a move would plunge the ruling party into an intense power struggle, distracting from President Lee Jae-myung’s policy initiatives and handing political momentum to the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party.

Prosecution Reform vs. Internal Collapse: What to Watch Next

The core frustration among many ruling party supporters is that this internal friction is taking attention away from major legislative goals. The public voted for President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party to push forward structural reforms—most notably, establishing special counsel investigations into past cases of fabricated prosecutions under previous administrations, and repairing economic governance following the political instability of the previous regime.

Instead of driving this agenda forward from a position of strength, the party leadership is bogged down defending its own position. If the ruling party loses control of the political narrative, its ability to pass key reform bills will be severely compromised.

In the coming days and weeks, foreign observers and political analysts should monitor three specific indicators:

  • The October 24 Polling Numbers: Watch whether public approval for the Democratic Party shows any real recovery, or if stagnant numbers prompt open calls for Kim Min-seok's resignation.
  • The Shincheonji Investigation Results: Pay attention to whether the leadership orders a transparent, independent inquiry into the illegal access of member data during the party convention, or attempts to minimize the incident.
  • Legislative Momentum on Special Counsel Bills: Track whether the ruling party can regain its footing to pass key reform measures targeting past prosecutorial abuses, or if internal dissent halts its legislative calendar.

The post-impeachment political landscape in South Korea was built on a promise of accountability and institutional renewal. For the ruling Democratic Party, the crisis surrounding the October 24 deadline is a decisive test: it must either confront its internal dysfunctions head-on, or risk seeing its governing mandate slip away.
