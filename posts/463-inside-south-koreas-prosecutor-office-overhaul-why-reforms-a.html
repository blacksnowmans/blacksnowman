Inside South Korea's Prosecutor Office Overhaul: Why Reforms Are Stalling Under Lee Jae-myung

September 08, 2026

Standing in the current political climate of South Korea, it is hard not to feel a sense of déjà vu. After a hard-fought change in administration, the public expected a decisive break from the past—a sweeping, irreversible dismantling of old power structures. Yet, the machinery of state seems to be running on the exact same fuel. The fiery rhetoric promising reforms that "not even a returning conservative administration could roll back" has quietly faded into the background.

Nowhere is this more apparent than in the recently proposed organizational restructuring of the prosecution service. While officially billed as a clean separation of investigative and indicting powers, a closer look reveals a labyrinth of loopholes designed to keep prosecutorial influence intact. As President Lee Jae-myung’s approval ratings slide downward without a break, it is worth examining the uncomfortable mechanics behind South Korea's stalled political transformation.

The Illusion of Reform: Same Staff, New Labels

The Ministry of Justice recently announced its organizational blueprint for the new Public Prosecution Office, a structural shift supposed to strip prosecutors of their direct and supplementary investigative powers. On paper, the mandate sounds revolutionary. In practice, the numbers tell a different story.

Despite dropping investigative responsibilities, the total headcount of the newly rebranded office remains virtually untouched at 8,412 personnel, including 2,292 prosecutors. Critics point out that downsizing the workload while keeping the entire bureaucracy intact effectively neutralizes the reform. When an institution keeps its massive physical footprint, it retains the capacity to flex its muscles whenever it chooses.

This is nothing more than a cosmetic re-labeling. True structural reform requires transforming the DNA of an institution, not simply swapping out the nameplate on the door. By preserving their core privileges under a different banner, prosecutors are signaling that they view this as a temporary inconvenience rather than a permanent loss of power.

From Forensic Department to Forensic Planning Bureau

One of the most revealing alterations in the restructuring plan involves the agency's forensic divisions. Originally slated to be transferred entirely to an independent investigative authority, the high-level Forensic Science Department was instead downsized into a three-division "Forensic Planning Bureau" led by a senior prosecutor.

The justification from legal authorities is that prosecutors still need secondary verification capabilities to secure convictions when other agencies hand over evidence. However, when the boundary between gathering evidence and prosecuting cases blurs, old investigative habits tend to creep back in through the back door. This suspicion is underscored by a rushed, high-budget tender for a new building for the National Digital Forensic Center—a project with a total price tag of about 57.4 billion won (roughly $42 million), heavily backed by a preliminary emergency tender worth about $22 million (30 billion won). For an organization supposedly stripped of direct investigative powers, investing heavily in state-of-the-art forensics raises obvious questions about their true intentions.

The "Judicial Control" Contradiction

Perhaps the most jarring addition to the blueprint is the creation of a "Judicial Control Bureau" across 18 regional prosecution offices. The stated mission of this new body is to review police decisions not to forward cases for prosecution, checking for shoddy police work. The underlying problem is institutional posture. Prosecutors are administrative public officials, not members of the judiciary. Yet, by embedding the word "control" into the title of a new oversight body, the prosecution service is signaling its enduring view of local police as subordinates rather than equal partners in law enforcement. Because these changes are being pushed through via executive enforcement decrees rather than parliamentary votes—a legislative bypass method previously criticized under former conservative administrations—critics argue that democratic norms are being sidelined to preserve bureaucratic turf.

Personnel Controversies and Slipping Approval Ratings

This institutional reluctance to change coincides with a broader leadership and personnel crisis within the current administration. Recent polling data from September 2026 places President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating at 37.4%, while negative assessments have surged to 59.5%.

Discontent is particularly acute among younger voters—traditionally sensitive to issues of fairness and anti-elitism—who feel alienated by political appointments that seem to reward entrenched insiders rather than merit. When politicians and allied figures with substantial personal assets and institutional backing are lined up for high-profile positions, it triggers a deep sense of relative deprivation among young adults struggling in a competitive job market. Ignoring these warnings from the electorate risks turning a temporary polling dip into a permanent political crisis.

The Cost of Factionalism and Inner-Circle Politics

Beyond institutional friction, the administration faces internal pressures from its most vocal supporters—often referred to pejoratively as pro-Lee loyalists who monetize their proximity to power. While publicly projecting fierce loyalty to the president, these inner-circle figures frequently generate controversies through indiscreet media appearances and public squabbles.

When minor internal disputes escalate into indiscriminate mutual accusations—such as high-profile loyalists filing complaints against presidential aides—the ruling bloc begins to look less like a disciplined reform government and more like a faction consumed by infighting. President Lee's administration cannot afford to be weighed down by allies who treat executive power as a personal commodity rather than a public trust.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea navigates this political turbulence, several concrete indicators will determine whether the administration can right the ship:

  • The Finalization of Enforcement Decrees: Watch whether the Ministry of Justice backtracks on the controversial Judicial Control Bureau and forensic bureau restructuring following public pushback.
  • Cabinet and Staff Reshuffles: Pay close attention to upcoming personnel appointments; any sign of a pivot toward technocratic merit rather than factional loyalty will signal a shift in political strategy.
  • Legislative Pushback: Monitor how the ruling Democratic Party handles future compromises with the opposition regarding prosecutional independence and budgetary allocations for forensic infrastructure.

The core promise of any reform-minded government is self-restraint—the willingness to dismantle the very instruments of power that past regimes abused. If the current administration continues to rely on administrative workarounds and insider protection, it risks squandering the historic mandate that brought it to power.

What was the change in government supposed to achieve, after all?
