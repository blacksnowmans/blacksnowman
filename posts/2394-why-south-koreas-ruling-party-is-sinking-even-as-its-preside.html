Why South Korea’s Ruling Party Is Sinking Even as Its President Rebounds

November 16, 2026

In mid-November 2026, South Korea’s ruling party found itself fighting a war on multiple fronts—and losing ground on the one that matters most at the ballot box. According to a weekly survey for the second week of November by Realmeter, one of South Korea’s major polling agencies, President Lee Jae-myung’s approval rating edged upward. Yet in the same poll, his ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) fell to its lowest support level since taking power.

Normally, a sitting president and the ruling party rise and fall together. When they diverge, it usually signals trouble inside the party itself. Major South Korean broadcasters such as Yonhap News TV and YTN, alongside daily newspaper Kyunghyang Shinmun, have pointed to an intensifying internal clash between factions loyal to President Lee and those aligned with senior party figure Jung Chung-rae. Meanwhile, prominent progressive commentator Kim Eo-jun’s YouTube broadcast has attributed the slump to accumulated frustration among core voters over the government’s "pragmatism" line and a bitter party disciplinary dispute—all unfolding alongside a tense constitutional standoff with Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae and the Supreme Court.

A Split Verdict in the Polls: A Rising President and a Sinking Party

The Realmeter numbers highlight a shifting landscape within South Korea’s liberal-progressive bloc rather than a wholesale flight to conservatives. While the DPK hit a post-transition low, the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, reform-oriented progressive party—climbed from 6.9% to 8.7%, putting it within striking distance of the psychologically significant 10% mark.

Observers cited across Korean media and online political commentary interpret this movement as an internal reshuffling of progressive voters. Rather than abandoning the governing camp altogether, voters disillusioned by the DPK’s infighting appear to be migrating toward a rival party projecting a sharper, more uncompromising message. That dynamic is especially visible on judicial issues. Following a parliamentary audit of the Supreme Court, the Rebuilding Korea Party unveiled a second impeachment motion against Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae listing six grounds for removal. Commentators on Kim Eo-jun’s broadcast have argued that if confrontations with the judiciary deepen while the DPK remains mired in internal discipline battles, the smaller party’s gains could accelerate.

For President Lee’s administration, the divergence carries practical risks. Even with personal approval ticking slightly upward, a weakened ruling party makes it harder to sustain legislative momentum in the National Assembly, where the government needs a unified coalition to pass budgets and reform bills.

Inside the Democratic Party’s Factional Clash Over Discipline

The immediate spark for the DPK’s internal turmoil was a leaked remark from a closed-door party gathering held the previous month. On November 12, reports surfaced that DPK Supreme Council member Choi Min-hee had told attendees that President Lee had once opposed the impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was later impeached, removed from office, and imprisoned—on the grounds that winning over political moderates was essential to winning the presidency and taking power.

As backlash rippled through the party, Choi issued apologies on Facebook on November 15 and at a Supreme Council meeting on November 16, blaming a lapse in memory and confused phrasing while expressing regret for causing trouble to President Lee. Yet even as she apologized, she went on the offensive, demanding an investigation and preventive measures regarding allegations raised earlier by DPK leader Kim Min-seok during the party’s leadership convention—specifically, claims of a secret three-way alliance involving anti-Lee figures, divisive elements, and Shincheonji, a controversial religious group that has repeatedly drawn scrutiny in South Korean politics.

On November 15, party leader Kim Min-seok received an internal ethics inspection report on Choi and referred her case to the Central Party Ethics Tribunal. The nine-member body, which includes external figures and is chaired by lawyer Lee Chang-hwan, a Kim appointee, is widely seen in Seoul political circles as a step toward heavy disciplinary action.

Factional Lines Harden

The move to the Ethics Tribunal quickly turned a verbal misstep into an open factional confrontation between the pro-Lee wing and the pro-Jung Chung-rae wing:

  • Protests over party leadership tactics: Supreme Council members Lee Seong-yoon and Han Min-su, both aligned with the pro-Jung faction, boycotted a Supreme Council meeting and held a press conference protesting the ethics inspection.
  • Pushback from leadership allies: Lawmaker Yeom Tae-young, who supported Kim Min-seok during the party convention, condemned the boycott as factional behavior that undermines party discipline. Lawmaker Hong Gi-won similarly called on colleagues to halt internal strife and focus on the public.
  • Pressure from pro-Lee groups: Outside pro-Lee organization The Minjoo Innovation Council and freshman lawmaker group The Mincho demanded strict punishment and Choi’s resignation from the Supreme Council. After President Lee remarked that "the hardest thing is an arrow flying from behind my back," his close aide, lawmaker Kim Nam-jun, shared the comment and directly targeted Choi, insisting the president must not be distorted or slandered with falsehoods.

Still, criticism of the leadership’s handling has also come from within the party’s own ranks. Several senior DPK lawmakers and commentators on Kim Eo-jun’s broadcast argued that because Choi had already admitted her error and apologized, the leadership should have resolved the matter politically—such as through a formal apology at the Supreme Council—rather than escalating it to an ethics tribunal and turning it into a zero-sum battle.

Notably, the ruling party is not alone in its internal dysfunction. South Korea’s main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP) under leader Jang Dong-hyuk, is simultaneously embroiled in its own severe infighting, including allegations of a "fabricated disciplinary action" aimed at expelling lawmaker Han Dong-hoon and lawsuits filed by lawmakers Bae Hyun-jin and Cho Kyung-tae challenging party sanctions. Both major parties, analysts note, are struggling to resolve internal disputes through politics rather than punitive measures, deepening voter fatigue across the spectrum.

Pragmatism vs. Principle: Why Core Supporters Are Growing Restless

Beneath the week-to-week drama of ethics hearings and boycotts lies a deeper vulnerability: the alienation of the DPK’s core base. On Kim Eo-jun’s YouTube broadcast, critics of the party’s current trajectory framed the polling drop as the result of months of unmet expectations regarding prosecutorial reform and key government appointments—concerns that re-elected DPK lawmakers have also acknowledged are building among grassroots supporters.

"I voted for Lee Jae-myung because I believed he would at least uphold fairness, justice, and the principles promised by the Democratic Party."

According to commentary aired on the broadcast, loyal supporters had tolerated tough economic realities, including rising housing prices, because they believed the administration remained committed to structural reform and clear principles. Critics argued that the government’s emphasis on "pragmatism"—ostensibly meant to broaden appeal among moderate voters and stabilize governance—has failed to deliver tangible results while serving instead as a justification to sideline dissenting party voices.

"President Lee turned his back on supporters first, which is why he was hit by arrows. Yet they call that pragmatism."

The warning directed at the presidential office and DPK leadership was blunt: "If you push away even uncomfortable voices for your own politics, who on earth are you going to fight the next election with?" Whether the administration can reconcile pragmatic governance with the expectations of its core electorate remains an open and pressing question.

The Supreme Court Standoff and What Comes Next

While the DPK wrestles with internal unity, an equally consequential battle is playing out between the governing bloc and the judiciary. At the center is Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, whose tenure has been marked by repeated clashes with the ruling camp.

A focal point of recent criticism is a motion filed by former minister Choi Sang-mok seeking to recuse Judge Lee Jin-kwan from presiding over his trial. Although lower courts dismissed the recusal request, the Supreme Court has left the matter unresolved for six months—far longer than the typical one-month timeframe. On his broadcast, Kim Eo-jun alleged that Chief Justice Cho is overturning Judge Lee’s insurrection-related rulings to signal to the broader judiciary that those decisions were flawed, and suggested Cho could stall until regular judicial personnel transfers early next year to reassign Judge Lee away from the case. Kim also raised suspicions that Cho had influenced the sudden presidential candidacy of former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, asking, "If the head of the judiciary engages in politics to that extent, shouldn’t he be impeached?"

That mistrust traces back to the turbulent 2025 presidential election cycle and subsequent appointment disputes:

  • The 2025 election law ruling: On March 26, 2025, an appeals court acquitted then-DPK leader Lee Jae-myung in a Public Official Election Act violation case. Prosecutors appealed the next day, and the Supreme Court referred the case to its full bench on April 22 before voting 10–2 on May 1 to overturn and remand the acquittal. Shortly afterward, then-Acting President and Prime Minister Han Duck-soo resigned and entered the presidential race. Following Lee’s victory in the June 3, 2025 presidential election, the Seoul High Court halted his trial under Article 84 of the Constitution.
  • The 2026 justice nomination clash: On August 18, 2026, Chief Justice Cho nominated Kim Seong-su and Son Bong-gi to succeed retiring Supreme Court Justices Lee Heung-gu and No Tae-ak. Controversy erupted over whether Cheong Wa Dae (the presidential office) had been properly consulted on Son’s nomination. The presidential office requested a re-nomination on August 28, which Cho refused on September 22, citing a lack of constitutional basis.

Chief Justice Cho and several legal observers reject the governing bloc’s accusations. Testifying at the Supreme Court’s parliamentary audit on October 6 without taking a witness oath, Cho argued that "it is unconstitutional for the National Assembly to interfere in the Chief Justice’s nominations," maintaining that neither the president nor parliament can intervene in the nomination process. Regarding the December 3 insurrection crisis, Cho asserted that "the Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional before anyone else, before any other institution." Some legal experts have likewise contended that an en banc review of Lee’s election law case was a predictable judicial procedure and that refusing to re-nominate a justice cannot easily be classified as a constitutional violation.

For now, the DPK has stopped short of backing the Rebuilding Korea Party’s impeachment push. Instead, in late October, the ruling party opted to prioritize a parliamentary investigation covering five areas: the election law remand process, the Supreme Court’s response during the December 3 insurrection crisis, the justice re-nomination dispute, allegations involving business promotion expenses and cash encouragement payments, and trial delays involving Kim Keon-hee.

What to Watch Next in Seoul

Whether the Democratic Party can arrest its slide in the polls—and reclaim the reform initiative from the Rebuilding Korea Party—will hinge on several concrete developments in the coming weeks:

  • The Ethics Tribunal’s ruling on Choi Min-hee: Heavy sanctions could further inflame the pro-Lee and pro-Jung divide, whereas a measured resolution could test whether party leader Kim Min-seok can restore internal calm.
  • The Rebuilding Korea Party’s 10% threshold: Upcoming weekly polls will show whether the smaller party breaks into double digits, signaling deeper structural erosion in the DPK’s base.
  • Former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo’s detention deadline: Han faces charges of playing a key role in insurrection, and the approaching expiration of his detention period could prove pivotal. If he is released, calls within the governing camp to impeach Chief Justice Cho could quickly reignite.
  • The DPK’s five-part parliamentary investigation: As lawmakers press ahead with inquiries into the Supreme Court, the clash between legislative oversight and judicial independence is set to remain a defining fault line in South Korean politics.
