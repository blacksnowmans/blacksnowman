The Price of a Conspiracy: How "Shincheonji" Allegations Backfired on South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 03, 2026

The atmosphere inside the headquarters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) was thick with tension as an internal investigation team prepared to release its final report. For months, a shadow had loomed over the party’s internal politics, cast by one of the most radioactive labels in South Korean public life: Shincheonji. To be linked to this secretive religious group is often a political death sentence in Korea, and the party’s own leadership had used that label to target internal rivals. But as the findings were read aloud, the grand conspiracy of a "triple secret alliance" between cult members and party rebels vanished into thin air, leaving the accusers—most notably Representative Kim Min-seok—scrambling to contain the fallout.

The investigation, led by Representative Park Sun-won, concluded that there was no evidence whatsoever of a coordinated infiltration by Shincheonji during the party’s recent national convention. The claims, which suggested that tens of thousands of cult members had joined the DPK to influence the leadership race against the interests of current President Lee Jae-myung, were revealed to be baseless fabrications. What was meant to be a shield for the party leadership has instead become a sword over their heads, raising fundamental questions about the integrity of the ruling party’s top brass and the toxic influence of partisan "cyber-wreckers" on YouTube.

The Weaponization of a Religious Cult

To understand why these allegations were so explosive, one must understand the unique status of Shincheonji (the Shincheonji Church of Jesus). To many outside Korea, it is known as the group at the center of the country’s first major COVID-19 outbreak in 2020. Domestically, however, it is viewed with deep suspicion as a "predatory cult" that allegedly infiltrates social and political organizations for its own ends. In the hyper-competitive world of the Democratic Party of Korea, the ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, accusing a rival of being backed by Shincheonji is the ultimate form of "red-tagging" or character assassination.

Kim Min-seok, a high-ranking member of the DPK’s Supreme Council, was the primary architect of this narrative. During the leadership race, he repeatedly claimed that Shincheonji forces had systematically joined the party to form an "anti-Lee Jae-myung" faction. This wasn't just a casual observation; it was a powerful frame intended to delegitimize any dissent within the party. By labeling critics as cult-linked infiltrators, Kim and his supporters effectively turned internal policy debates into a holy war against an external enemy.

However, the investigation team’s report systematically dismantled these claims. Allegations that 15,000 or 30,000 Shincheonji members had registered in specific districts were found to be "factually groundless." No evidence of coordinated leadership or communication between the religious group and any party faction was discovered. The "triple secret alliance" that Kim Min-seok had warned about was revealed to be a ghost, a political phantom conjured to consolidate power and silence opposition during a critical transition for the party.

A Proxy Apology and the Crisis of Leadership

In politics, the manner of an apology often matters as much as the content. When the investigation's results made Kim Min-seok’s position untenable, the public expected a direct, televised statement from a man who has long championed "transparency" and "open politics." Instead, the party was treated to what critics are calling a "backroom apology." Kim did not stand before the cameras; he sent a spokesperson, Kim Seong-hoe, to deliver a written statement.

This "proxy apology" has ignited a firestorm of criticism from both the public and his colleagues. Representative Lee Hye-yeon pointed out the hypocrisy of fueling a massive conspiracy theory for months only to hide behind a spokesperson when the truth came out. Former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook also weighed in, noting that in a situation where clear damage was done to the reputations of innocent party members, the only acceptable response was a "frontal breakthrough"—a direct and humble apology to the victims—rather than a cowardly retreat.

The resentment is particularly high among the rank-and-file party members who were branded as potential cultists or "anti-Lee" saboteurs simply for questioning the leadership's direction. For a party currently holding the presidency, this internal fracture is more than just a PR problem. It suggests a leadership style that relies on fear and labeling rather than consensus-building. If the party’s top officials are willing to use such extreme tactics against their own members based on flimsy YouTube rumors, it calls into question their ability to govern the nation with a steady hand.

The Legal Shadow and the $1.2 Million Question

The fallout from the Shincheonji scandal is now moving from the court of public opinion to the court of law. Legal experts and several DPK lawmakers, including Representative Lee Ji-eun, have pointed to Article 52 of the Political Parties Act. This law states that anyone who publishes false information with the intent to prevent a candidate from being elected can face up to five years in prison or a fine of up to 10 million won (about $7,200). By spreading the Shincheonji narrative during a national convention, Kim Min-seok may have crossed a legal line that carries significant consequences.

Adding to the pressure is a growing controversy over party finances. Attention has shifted to a special "Task Force for Prosecution Reform" that operated under Kim’s influence. The group reportedly spent about 1.7 billion won (approximately $1.25 million) in party funds. Critics are now demanding a full audit of this budget, questioning whether such a large sum was used effectively or if it was diverted to bolster the influence of specific factions. The lack of visible results from the task force, combined with the collapse of the Shincheonji allegations, has created a "double whammy" of ethical and financial suspicion.

The role of partisan media has also come under fire. The investigation highlighted how certain YouTube channels and fringe news outlets acted as an echo chamber for Kim’s claims, inflating numbers and creating a sense of panic among the party’s base. This "information pollution" is a growing problem in Korean politics, where the line between investigative journalism and partisan propaganda has blurred. By validating these rumors, high-ranking politicians like Kim Min-seok give them a veneer of legitimacy that they do not deserve, causing real-world damage to the democratic process.

Ghosts of the Past: Betrayal and Factionalism

For many veteran observers of Korean politics, Kim Min-seok’s current predicament feels like a case of history repeating itself. Kim was once a rising star often referred to as a "political prodigy," but his career has been dogged by accusations of opportunism. The most famous instance occurred in 2002, during the presidential campaign of the late Roh Moo-hyun. Kim famously defected to a rival camp in the middle of the race, a move that earned him the permanent ire of the "pro-Roh" base and the nickname "The Frozen Man"—referring to his political career being put on ice for years afterward.

The current scandal has revived these old wounds. Critics argue that Kim's use of the Shincheonji conspiracy is proof that his political "DNA" hasn't changed—that he is still a politician who prioritizes his own survival and power over party loyalty or truth. Some have even compared his current influence within the DPK to the "Gyeonggi Dongbu Alliance," a notorious hard-left faction that was accused of using secretive, undemocratic tactics to seize control of the now-defunct United Progressive Party years ago.

The danger for the Democratic Party of Korea is that this "culture of suspicion" could become permanent. If every internal disagreement is met with a McCarthy-esque search for "hidden enemies" or "cult infiltrators," the party will lose its ability to attract moderate voters. President Lee Jae-myung’s administration is currently focused on major domestic reforms and navigating a complex geopolitical landscape; the last thing it needs is a civil war triggered by baseless conspiracy theories from its own Supreme Council.

What to Watch Next

The "Shincheonji intervention" story has ended as a factual matter, but its political life is just beginning. In the coming weeks, there are several concrete developments that will determine the future of the DPK leadership:

  • Legal Challenges: Watch for "injunctions to suspend duties" filed by disgruntled party members against Kim Min-seok. If these move forward, they could effectively paralyze his role in the party leadership.
  • Ethics Committee Referrals: There is growing pressure for the DPK’s internal ethics body to investigate not only the Shincheonji claims but also the 1.7 billion won ($1.25 million) expenditure by the prosecution reform task force.
  • The "YouTube" Backlash: As the investigation proved the YouTubers wrong, there may be a shift in how the party interacts with these hyper-partisan channels. Whether the leadership distances itself or continues to court them will be a key indicator of the party's health.
  • Internal Factional Realignment: With Kim Min-seok weakened, other leaders within the "pro-Lee" camp may move to distance themselves from him to protect the President’s image. Keep an eye on figures like Representative Jung Chung-rae and Choi Kang-wook as they navigate this fallout.

Ultimately, this episode serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of conspiracy-driven politics. In the race to consolidate power, it is tempting to use the most powerful weapons available—even if they are built on lies. But as Kim Min-seok is discovering, those weapons have a tendency to explode in the hands of the person holding them. For the Democratic Party of Korea, the path forward requires a return to evidence-based debate and a rejection of the "politics of labeling" that has caused so much unnecessary damage.
