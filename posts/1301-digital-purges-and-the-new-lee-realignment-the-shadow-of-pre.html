Digital Purges and the "New Lee" Realignment: The Shadow of President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Wars

October 19, 2026

Imagine, for a moment, the president of a major global economy sitting late at night, glowing screen in hand, not reviewing intelligence reports or trade agreements, but engaging in a "keyboard battle" with critics on social media. In South Korea, this is no longer a hypothetical scenario. President Lee Jae-myung, the head of the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DP) administration, has recently sent shockwaves through the political establishment—not by passing a landmark bill, but by "shouting out" a radical pro-government YouTube channel and effectively "pointing the coordinates" for his digital supporters to attack his own allies.

This is not merely a case of a politician being overly active online. To outside observers, it may look like a minor spat, but within the context of Korean politics, it signals a sophisticated and ruthless effort to reshape the progressive landscape. By leveraging aggressive YouTube influencers and his massive social media following, President Lee is accused of conducting a "digital purge" to silence internal critics and consolidate power under a banner known as the "New Lee Jae-myung" faction. The fallout is threatening to fracture the very liberal coalition that brought him to power, raising deep concerns about the health of South Korean democracy.

The YouTube Shout-out and the Targeting of an Icon

The controversy began when President Lee, known for his direct and often combative style of communication, publicly endorsed a YouTube channel titled "Minju is a Police Station." In the hyper-partisan world of Korean digital media, this channel is not a neutral observer. It is a vanguard for the most radical wing of the "New Lee" movement, known for its extreme intolerance toward any dissenting voices within the liberal camp. For a head of state to grant such a channel a public "shout-out" is an unprecedented breach of political norms, signaling to his millions of followers which voices are currently "in" and which are "out."

The most shocking development, however, is the channel's primary target: Rhyu Si-min. To a foreign reader, Rhyu Si-min is perhaps the most influential liberal intellectual in South Korea. A former Minister of Health and Welfare and a close confidant of late President Roh Moo-hyun, Rhyu has spent decades as a pillar of the progressive movement. Yet, the channel promoted by the President has begun reviving old, discredited allegations against him. Specifically, they have brought back the "SillaJen" narrative—a complex stock manipulation scandal involving a biotech firm that conservative prosecutors and media used to harass Rhyu years ago.

The irony is thick: a channel endorsed by a liberal president is now using the same "smear" tactics once employed by the conservative establishment to demand that Rhyu be "sent to prison." The shock among the liberal base is profound. If someone as storied as Rhyu Si-min can be targeted as a "moral failure" or a "traitor" simply for offering constructive criticism, then no one in the party is safe.

This move has already drawn fire from other corners of the progressive alliance. Cho Kuk, the leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party—a smaller, more ideologically focused liberal party—lashed out on Facebook, stating that the current tactics have "crossed the line by a wide margin." The sense of betrayal is palpable; the very people who fought to end the era of political persecution are now seeing those same tools turned inward.

The "New Lee" Blueprint: A Deliberate Political Realignment

While the President’s defenders might dismiss these SNS interactions as "authentic communication," evidence suggests a much more calculated strategy is at play. On October 16, internal rumors and "information sheets" circulating within the ruling Democratic Party revealed what is being called the "Pro-Lee Political Realignment Scenario." According to these reports, the leadership of the pro-Lee faction has a specific "under-drawing" for the future of the party: the systematic exclusion of non-mainstream or critical voices.

The goal is reportedly to make life so uncomfortable for internal reformers and critics that they choose to leave the party voluntarily. By mobilizing the "fandom"—the President’s most ardent and sometimes aggressive online supporters—the leadership can launch "indiscriminate bombings" of criticism against anyone who deviates from the official line. This creates a binary political landscape: you are either "Pro-Lee" or "Anti-Lee."

This strategy of polarization is a dangerous gamble. While it may consolidate the President's grip on the Democratic Party in the short term, it erodes the party’s "dynamic diversity," a trait that has historically been the DP's greatest strength. Scholars have long warned that "fandom politics" can distort the public sphere, creating echo chambers where confirmation bias reigns supreme. When a president uses this energy to hunt down "internal enemies," politics ceases to be about negotiation and starts to be about "annihilation."

Weaponizing Morality: The New Face of Political Suppression

Professor Choi Bae-geun, a prominent economist and frequent political commentator, has provided a chilling analysis of why this specific tactic—attacking morality—is so effective. On his "Choi Bae-geun TV" live stream, he argued that these SNS attacks are not intended to win an argument, but to commit "political murder."

"This isn't just a temporary strike. What are they aiming for? They are aiming for a strike on morality," Choi explained.

In South Korean progressive politics, morality is not just a personal trait; it is the primary source of political capital. Liberal politicians and intellectuals derive their authority from the claim that they are the "clean" alternative to the perceived corruption of the old guard. If a critic can be labeled a "hypocrite" or a "moral failure" through associations with financial scandals like SillaJen, their ability to speak truth to power is neutralized. Their criticisms are no longer seen as principled stands, but as the "excuses of a criminal."

Professor Choi also noted a disturbing parallel to South Korea’s dark past. During the military dictatorships of the 20th century, the state used "red-tagging"—labeling anyone who disagreed with the government as a communist or a North Korean spy—to silence dissent. Today, Choi argues, we are seeing a "Lee Jae-myung-style red-tagging." Instead of "communist," the new label is "radical reformist" or "unrealistic extremist." Anyone who demands more rigorous reform or criticizes the administration’s shortcomings is branded with a label that makes them an outcast within their own camp. It is a sophisticated form of "labeling" designed to ensure that the general public stops listening to them entirely.

The Danger of Power Asymmetry and Cyber-Lynching

The most troubling aspect of this phenomenon is the sheer asymmetry of power. On one side, you have the President of the Republic, a man who commands the entire administrative and executive power of the state, along with millions of digital followers. On the other side, you have individual intellectuals, professors, or minor lawmakers. When the President "points the coordinates" at an individual, it is not an invitation to a debate; it is a signal for a "digital lynching."

Professor Choi Bae-geun has explicitly called this "SNS violence." Unlike the physical violence of the past—where police or intelligence agencies might have used force—this "new kind of violence" uses the anonymity and speed of the internet to destroy a person’s reputation and mental well-being. When the President shares a link or expresses "discomfort" with a person, thousands of hate comments and malicious rumors are unleashed instantly. This creates a "chilling effect" where even the most seasoned commentators think twice before speaking their mind.

There is a sense of "déjà vu" in these events. Figures like Chu Mi-ae, the former Justice Minister who was a central figure in the push for prosecutorial reform, and Lawmaker Kim Yong-min have faced similar treatment. When Chu Mi-ae stood her ground on matters of principle, she was met not with policy rebuttals, but with personal attacks. Critics labeled her an "elite" who had lost touch with the common people, using frames like "luxury residences" to undermine her moral standing. It is the same playbook now being used against Rhyu Si-min: take a symbol of reform, find a way to scratch their moral surface, and then let the digital mob do the rest.

According to statistics from the National Police Agency, cases of cyber-defamation and insult have skyrocketed in recent years. When the highest office in the land becomes the source of such "coordinate-spotting," the impact is magnified a thousandfold. It moves the conversation away from the "common good" and into a dark alley of personal vendettas.

What to Watch Next

The current situation suggests that the "New Lee Jae-myung" realignment is not an accident, but a feature of the current administration's survival strategy. However, history shows that power built on the exclusion of allies is inherently fragile. As President Lee continues to rely on SNS "keyboard battles" rather than institutional systems, the following developments will be crucial to watch:

  • The Reaction of the "Allies": Watch for whether the Rebuilding Korea Party and other minor progressive factions begin to distance themselves further from the Democratic Party. If the "digital lynching" continues, the liberal coalition could split before the next major election cycle.
  • The Exit of Reformers: Keep an eye on non-mainstream DP lawmakers. If the "New Lee" scenario of forced defections begins to materialize, we may see a significant exodus of the party’s more moderate or independently-minded members, potentially leading to the formation of a new political entity.
  • Public Fatigue: While the "fandom" is vocal, the general public often tires of constant internal strife. If the President is seen as more interested in online spats than in the "bread and butter" issues of the economy and social welfare, his approval ratings—and his ability to govern—may suffer a terminal blow.

The true strength of a democracy is measured by how it treats its critics, especially those within its own ranks. If the "New Lee" era is defined by the silencing of voices like Rhyu Si-min and the weaponization of social media, it may find that it has won the party, but lost the soul of the movement that put it in power. For now, the "keyboard battles" continue, but the cost to South Korea’s political culture is mounting every day.
