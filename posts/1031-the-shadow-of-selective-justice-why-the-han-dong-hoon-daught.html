The Shadow of Selective Justice: Why the Han Dong-hoon Daughter Scandal Won’t Go Away

October 12, 2026

For nearly two years, a cloud of suspicion has hovered over the academic credentials of the daughter of Han Dong-hoon, a prominent member of the opposition People Power Party (the main conservative political bloc in South Korea). What began as a series of allegations regarding fabricated research papers and ghostwritten academic projects has morphed into a litmus test for the integrity of South Korea’s investigative agencies. After a lengthy, criticized police investigation that resulted in no formal charges, a recent remark by the acting Commissioner General of the Korean National Police Agency has breathed new life into the case, signaling that a re-investigation may finally be on the table.

To understand why this matters, one must look past the technicalities of academic admissions and see the case for what it is: a mirror reflecting the uneven application of justice in a country that prides itself on egalitarian education, yet often bows to the influence of those with power and resources.

A Masterclass in "Selective Blindness"

From May 2022 to December 2023, the police conducted what many observers describe as a textbook case of stalling. During this 20-month period, the investigative process departed from standard procedures in ways that defy common sense. While the individuals who filed the original complaints were summoned for questioning six separate times, the key subject of the investigation—Han’s daughter—was only asked to provide a written statement once. As opposition lawmaker Yoon Kun-young pointedly asked during a parliamentary audit, “Is there any other case in this country where the police only grill the accuser while giving the accused a free pass?”

The pattern of the investigation suggests a lack of intent to uncover the truth. The police did not execute a single search warrant. Instead, they sent formal requests for information to 11 institutions. When three of those organizations—including the National Assembly and various academic databases—failed to respond, the police simply let the matter drop, eventually labeling the absence of evidence as a lack of regulatory grounds to proceed. Using the bureaucratic silence of an institution as a legal justification for closure is not just a failure of investigation; it is an act of administrative obstruction.

Central to the controversy is the allegation that a Kenyan ghostwriter, identified as "Benson," was the true architect of the research papers in question. Despite public interviews where the alleged author claimed credit for the work, the accused has remained largely silent for over four years. For a political figure who has frequently demanded "fairness" and "justice" from his rivals, this silence creates a glaring image of hypocrisy that continues to erode public trust.

The Tale of Two Investigations: 131 vs. 0

The outrage surrounding this case is fueled by a stark comparison with how the South Korean justice system has treated others. Take the case of Cho Min, daughter of former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, who faced a massive, multi-front investigation that included raids on over 70 locations. Or consider the investigation into Kim Hye-kyung, wife of President Lee Jae-myung, where police conducted an 11-hour search of the Gyeonggi Provincial Office. In the latter case, nearly 131 search warrants were issued.

In contrast, the investigation into Han Dong-hoon’s daughter saw zero search warrants issued and zero witnesses summoned. The contrast—131 to 0—is not merely a statistic; it is a profound symbol of inequality. At the time of the initial probe, Han was the Minister of Justice, wielding immense influence over the prosecution service. It is a widely held belief that investigative agencies, fearing the power of the then-ruling establishment, dared not pursue even the most basic investigative steps.

This "selective justice" is toxic to a democracy. When the law is enforced with a sledgehammer against political opponents but becomes a paper tiger when applied to the children of the elite, the concept of the "rule of law" loses its meaning. The perception is that parental capital and elite networks are being used to build an insurmountable "spec" (academic credential) cartel, effectively locking out ordinary students who compete on merit alone.

The Fall of Institutional Credibility

The controversy deepened in June 2024, when Woo Jong-soo, then-head of the National Office of Investigation, publicly claimed that his agency had "conducted numerous witness interviews." It was later revealed that this was a complete fabrication; the number of witnesses interviewed was, in fact, zero. That the nation’s top investigative official could stand before the press and the legislature and utter a verifiable lie is a dark stain on the current state of South Korean law enforcement.

This era of partiality was, in many ways, the legacy of the administration led by former President Yoon Suk-yeol, who was eventually impeached and is currently imprisoned. During his tenure, the justice system was often viewed as a weaponized tool used to protect allies and target enemies. While the current administration under President Lee Jae-myung is now in power, the culture of "looking the other way" for those with the right connections remains a deep-seated institutional rot that requires systematic pruning.

What to Watch Next

The path forward is now marked by a glimmer of accountability. Kim Jong-cheol, the acting Commissioner General of the police, recently indicated at a parliamentary audit that while he believes the original process followed "law and principle," he would not rule out a comprehensive re-investigation if new facts are brought to light. Furthermore, potential audits by the Ministry of Justice may uncover the extent to which the original investigation was suppressed.

For those watching the Korean political and legal landscape, the key milestones are:

  • Formal Re-investigation Requests: Whether legal activists or parliamentary committees force the police to open a new case file based on the discrepancies in the original record.
  • Institutional Audits: The findings of the Ministry of Justice regarding the police’s handling of the 2022-2023 investigation.
  • Public Scrutiny: Continued pressure from civil society to ensure that "selective justice" is not swept under the rug as a remnant of the previous, disgraced administration.

True justice requires the courage to admit when the system has failed. If South Korea is to move past the era of the "parental-chance" cartel, the judiciary and police must prove that the law, indeed, applies to everyone—even those who once held the keys to the kingdom.
