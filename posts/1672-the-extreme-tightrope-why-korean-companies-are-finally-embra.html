The "Extreme Tightrope": Why Korean Companies are Finally Embracing Parental Leave

October 29, 2026

For many working parents in South Korea, the morning routine is not just a flurry of activity—it is a high-stakes, "extreme tightrope" walk. Balancing the relentless demands of a competitive corporate culture, often defined by mandatory overtime and the unspoken pressure to prioritize the office over the home, leaves many parents feeling as though they are perpetually burning out. For a long time, the notion of a company that truly supports parents felt like a myth, a piece of PR fluff designed to bolster recruitment rather than reflect reality.

However, recent shifts in how top-tier organizations approach human resources suggest that the tide may be turning. On October 28, the government highlighted two organizations—one private and one public—that have moved beyond lip service to produce measurable, structural changes in how they support families. For a country grappling with a record-low birth rate and a deeply ingrained culture of long working hours, these cases offer a glimpse into what a sustainable future might actually look like.

The Cultural Shift at HK inno.N

The most striking news came from HK inno.N, a major pharmaceutical and healthcare subsidiary of the Kolmar Korea group. The company was recently honored with a ministerial award from the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family (the cabinet-level agency responsible for policies regarding family support and gender equality) for being an "enterprise good for raising children."

While industry awards are common, the data behind this recognition is staggering: an 82 percent usage rate for male parental leave. In a country where "father" and "parental leave" are rarely used in the same sentence without a qualifier about career suicide, this statistic represents a massive cultural earthquake. Historically, male parental leave in Korea has been a "paper policy"—a right that exists in the labor code but is effectively neutralized by the fear of professional retaliation or social stigma.

HK inno.N’s achievement demonstrates that the success of these policies depends entirely on the company's internal culture. It is not enough to simply have a policy; the workplace must provide a "soil" where male employees feel safe to step away for caregiving duties and confident that they can return to their positions without penalty. This is a clear signal to the rest of the business sector: childcare is no longer a "mother's burden," and companies that actively encourage paternal involvement are positioning themselves as the workplaces of the future.

Beyond Perks: Trust as Corporate Strategy

When companies discuss "supporting childcare," it is easy to assume they mean one-off bonuses or holiday gifts. However, the success at HK inno.N proves that these superficial benefits are dwarfed by something far more valuable: trust. When employees feel they can use institutional benefits without fear of judgment, the entire organization becomes more resilient.

The outdated Korean model—where overtime is treated as a virtue and weekend work is seen as a rite of passage—is rapidly becoming a liability. It is no longer possible to retain high-quality talent or address the national demographic crisis using the management strategies of the 1990s. In the current labor market, companies that actively welcome and support parenting staff are the ones that will survive and thrive. This must move beyond a few large corporations; the government and society at large need to provide stronger incentives for small-to-medium enterprises and startups to mirror this success, as these changes are not an act of charity, but a critical investment in corporate longevity.

The Persistent Reality of the "Eye-Reading" Culture

The fact that HK inno.N’s 82 percent usage rate is newsworthy is, paradoxically, a testament to how difficult it remains for most employees in Korea to take leave. In many workplaces, employees are still forced to "read the room" (눈치) regarding their supervisors' expectations. Until there are tangible penalties for companies that systematically suppress the use of parental leave, and aggressive support for those that normalize it, these success stories will remain the exception rather than the rule.

The Public Sector’s Leading Role

The shift is not limited to the private sector. The Ulsan Port Authority, a state-owned enterprise responsible for the management and development of the Ulsan port, was also recognized with an award from the Ministry of Personnel Management (the central government agency overseeing civil service and public administration).

Public institutions often suffer from rigid, hierarchical structures, making the Ulsan Port Authority’s successful implementation of flexible working hours and comprehensive family-support systems particularly notable. By proving that even government-run organizations can modernize their internal operations, they set a new baseline for the entire country. When the public sector takes the lead, it creates a "demonstration effect," pushing private companies to match these standards or risk losing their competitive edge in recruitment.

The role of state-owned entities in this context is to act as a buffer for the life cycles of their employees. Young employees working in public agencies are just as vulnerable to the pressures of parenting as their private-sector peers. By rooting out the "old-fashioned practices" of viewing vacations or parental leave as "pie in the sky," these agencies are doing more than managing logistics—they are helping to preserve the social fabric of the country.

The Road Ahead: What to Watch

As the administration under President Lee Jae-myung continues to grapple with the country’s existential demographic crisis, the focus must shift from simply providing cash subsidies to changing the fundamental environment of the workplace.

  • Policy Enforcement: Watch for whether the Ministry of Personnel Management and the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family transition from merely handing out awards to conducting more rigorous audits of firms with suspiciously low parental leave usage.
  • Corporate Competition: As the labor market becomes increasingly tight, expect a "race to the top" among corporations trying to brand themselves as "parent-friendly." Companies that fail to adapt will likely see an exodus of young talent.
  • The "Village" Concept: The old saying goes that "it takes a village to raise a child." In modern Korea, the workplace is the village. The next phase of policy must center on the "right to disconnect"—ensuring that when a parent leaves the office, they are not expected to be reachable, allowing them to focus fully on the home.

The news from October 28 is a positive sign, but it remains a small flicker in a vast landscape. The ultimate goal is for these "parent-friendly" workplaces to cease being news and start being the common, unremarkable standard. For a nation where the sound of children's laughter is becoming increasingly rare, the most revolutionary innovation is a simple, empathetic hand extended from the workplace to the family.
