South Korea’s Political Collision: Special Prosecutor Nominees and Cabinet Confirmations Trigger National Assembly Gridlock

November 27, 2026

The corridors of South Korea’s National Assembly in Seoul were charged with palpable tension on November 26 as two distinct political flashpoints collided. On one front, the ruling liberal coalition finalized its nominees for a high-profile Special Counsel tasked with investigating alleged "fabricated indictments" by the state prosecution. On the other, President Lee Jae-myung formally submitted confirmation hearing requests for five top executive appointments, including the chief of the national intelligence agency and four cabinet ministers, while a contentious confirmation hearing for the country's top tax administrator was already underway.

For international observers watching South Korea’s political landscape, November 26 offered a vivid snapshot of how legislative friction, judicial battles, and executive vetting converge in Seoul. The simultaneous push for a special prosecutor and the launch of high-stakes cabinet vetting have drawn sharp battle lines between the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP). As both sides brace for an intense legislative battle, the developments signal a turbulent winter for South Korean governance.

Choosing the Prosecutor to Investigate the Prosecution

The selection of nominees for the "Fabricated Indictment Special Counsel" represents a major step in the ruling coalition’s broader efforts to scrutinize past prosecutorial actions. On November 26, the ruling Democratic Party of Korea recommended attorney Lee Im-sung, a former prosecutor, while its minor liberal ally, the Rebuilding Korea Party, recommended attorney Lee Jeong-hoon, a former judge. Under South Korea’s special prosecutor framework, these two recommendations narrow the field to a final pairing, placing the ultimate appointment decision directly in the hands of President Lee Jae-myung.

The stark difference in background between the two candidates underscores competing strategic philosophies within the liberal camp regarding how the special counsel team should be led:

  • Lee Im-sung (Recommended by the DPK): A former prosecutor whose background offers deep institutional knowledge of investigative techniques, internal prosecutorial procedures, and evidence gathering. Proponents argue a former prosecutor possesses the practical expertise needed to navigate complex case files.
  • Lee Jeong-hoon (Recommended by the Rebuilding Korea Party): A former judge whose career has focused on judicial deliberation, legal interpretation, and courtroom procedure. Supporters suggest a judicial background offers a more neutral, balanced framework for assessing whether past prosecutorial actions breached legal boundaries.

The contrast forces President Lee Jae-myung to make a strategic choice: prioritize aggressive investigative experience by picking a former prosecutor, or lean toward legal deliberation and procedural balance by selecting a former judge. Whichever candidate receives the presidential mandate will immediately begin building the special prosecutor team, setting the baseline tone, scope, and initial timeline for the upcoming investigation.

Past Stances and the Shadow of Presidential Protection

No sooner were the two legal figures nominated than their past public records became flashpoints for intense political scrutiny. South Korean media quickly highlighted that both Lee Im-sung and Lee Jeong-hoon have previously voiced criticisms regarding impeachment proceedings. Notably, reports surfaced that DPK nominee Lee Im-sung had previously joined a public declaration opposing the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Jo Hee-de—a detail that appears at odds with the aggressive stance taken by the ruling party and its allies toward the judiciary in recent months.

Compounding the controversy are the nominees' legal views on the potential withdrawal of indictments against President Lee Jae-myung. Because the special counsel is mandated to examine allegations of prosecutorial overreach and "fabricated" cases, the legal positions held by the nominees regarding cases involving the current president carry immense political sensitivity. Critics argue that any special prosecutor assessing systemic prosecutorial misconduct must demonstrate absolute independence to maintain credibility.

The main opposition People Power Party (PPP) responded aggressively to the nominations by declaring a total boycott of the special counsel process. Conservative lawmakers frame the entire initiative as a political maneuver designed to build a "bulletproof shield" around President Lee Jae-myung and neutralize ongoing judicial risks facing the administration. From the PPP’s perspective, a special prosecutor nominated exclusively by the ruling coalition lacks cross-party legitimacy and serves as an instrument of political defense rather than impartial justice.

Yet, concern is not confined entirely to the opposition. Within the ruling Democratic Party, subtle anxieties over political backlash have begun to surface. Party insiders and political analysts warn that pushing forward with a special prosecutor while simultaneously pursuing judicial impeachments risks alienating moderate voters who may perceive the strategy as overreaching. As debate over party responsibility intensifies, the ruling coalition faces the delicate challenge of executing its investigative agenda without undermining broader public trust in state institutions.

A Cabinet Under Fire: The Nomination of Kang Kum-sil and Four Ministers

While the special counsel debate escalated, executive business moved forward at the presidential office. On November 26, President Lee Jae-myung officially signed and submitted confirmation hearing requests to the National Assembly for five high-level nominees. Anchoring the list is Kang Kum-sil, nominated to lead the National Intelligence Service (NIS)—South Korea’s premier foreign intelligence and security agency—alongside four cabinet minister nominees overseeing key government departments.

In South Korea’s presidential system, senior executive appointments must undergo formal legislative vetting before standing committees. While National Assembly confirmation reports for cabinet ministers and the NIS chief are technically non-binding on the president's ultimate power to appoint, a hostile or failed confirmation process can severely damage a new administration’s political momentum and public standing.

The opposition PPP immediately targeted Kang Kum-sil, demanding that the president withdraw her nomination even before committee hearings were scheduled. The NIS director position is uniquely sensitive, demanding strict political neutrality, profound national security judgment, and administrative capability. Opponents signal that they will use Kang’s upcoming confirmation hearing as a primary battleground to challenge the administration's broader security vision and political objectivity.

With five major nominees entering the legislative vetting pipeline at once, parliamentary committees are preparing for a series of high-intensity hearings. For the ruling party, securing a smooth passage for these nominees is critical to solidifying administrative control and driving its policy agenda forward. For the opposition, the hearings represent a prime opportunity to probe the administration's policy direction and moral integrity.

Tax Service Clashes and the Imminent Polling of Power

The political friction was already visible on November 26 inside the committee rooms of the National Assembly, where lawmakers held a full-day confirmation hearing for An Deok-soo, the nominee to head the National Tax Service (NTS). As the agency tasked with managing nation-wide tax administration, enforcement, and corporate audits, the NTS chief holds immense influence over South Korea’s economic landscape.

During the hearing, discussions sharply divided along party lines:

  • Ruling Party Focus: DPK lawmakers highlighted An Deok-soo’s stated commitment to tax administration reform, technical expertise, and administrative efficiency, presenting him as a qualified candidate capable of modernizing fiscal collection.
  • Opposition Focus: PPP lawmakers aggressively questioned An regarding his political neutrality and pressed him on real estate-related allegations, arguing that ethical standards and impartiality are non-negotiable prerequisites for overseeing the nation’s tax apparatus.

Because lawmakers remained divided over his responses, the committee postponed the decision on whether to adopt An’s confirmation report, scheduling further deliberations for November 30. The outcome of the November 30 meeting will serve as an early barometer for the broader confirmation season. If the committee fails to reach a consensus on An Deok-soo, it will set a contentious precedent for the upcoming hearings of NIS nominee Kang Kum-sil and the four prospective cabinet ministers.

What to Watch Next in South Korean Politics

As November draws to a close, the simultaneous unfolding of special prosecutor appointments and cabinet confirmation battles has set South Korea’s political calendar on a collision course. To understand how this high-stakes standoff will resolve, three specific milestones warrant close attention in the days ahead:

  • The Presidential Special Counsel Selection: President Lee Jae-myung’s formal decision between former prosecutor Lee Im-sung and former judge Lee Jeong-hoon will determine the structural leadership of the special prosecutor team. Watch for how the chosen nominee addresses past impeachment criticisms and questions of investigative neutrality upon taking office.
  • The November 30 NTS Confirmation Report Decision: The National Assembly's scheduled meeting to vote on or defer An Deok-soo’s confirmation report will reveal whether the ruling and opposition parties can achieve functional compromise on executive appointments, or if legislative gridlock will fully entrench itself.
  • Scheduling of the NIS and Cabinet Hearings: The alignment of hearing dates for NIS chief nominee Kang Kum-sil and the four cabinet minister nominees across parliamentary committees will dictate the tempo of political warfare in Seoul heading into December.
