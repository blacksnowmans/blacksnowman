Why South Korea’s Prosecution Reform Has Ground to a Halt

September 07, 2026

When scrolling through political commentary on South Korean YouTube, the frustration is often palpable. It isn’t just the partisan rhetoric; it’s the glaring contradictions beneath the surface. A recent, impassioned demand—"If they won't reform the prosecution, we'll force them to!"—highlights the current state of South Korean politics. As figures like former lawmaker Choi Kang-wook sound the alarm and prominent political commentator Rhyu Si-min critiques the administration, the mask has slipped. The question is no longer just about policy; it is about why a party with a legislative majority is hesitating, and who is truly pulling the strings of power behind the scenes.

The Strange Bedfellows of Legacy Media and YouTube

As former Justice Minister Cho Kuk has pointed out, the current South Korean media landscape is surreal. Many pundits who once used legacy media platforms to relentlessly attack and mock President Lee Jae-myung are now, under his administration, styling themselves as his most loyal defenders. It is a bizarre self-contradiction: those who once privately maligned the President are now positioning themselves as his "true" guardians, all while turning their blades against former President Moon Jae-in and his loyalist faction.

As veteran political commentator Kim Ou-joon famously pointed out, the irony is thick. The very people who poured their energy into electing Lee are now being branded as "anti-Lee" by newcomers who once tore him apart. This shift is difficult to explain without acknowledging the hidden agendas of influential decision-makers. You cannot simply repackage yesterday’s enemies as today’s allies when their history of hostility is so well-documented.

From a political strategy perspective, this erratic shifting of alliances only muddies the waters. The strategy of trying to win over centrist voters by alienating the party's own core base and manufacturing internal enemies is a mathematical failure. By fracturing the coalition that brought President Lee to power, the administration has effectively lost both sides of the aisle.

The Mirage of "Centrist Expansion"

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Former critics of President Lee are now branding themselves as his "new" supporters, driving internal division.
  • The attempt to chase "centrist" votes by alienating the traditional base has backfired, causing voters to drift away from both sides.
  • Fixating on political engineering rather than the core values of the electorate risks squandering the administration’s momentum.

True "centrist expansion" is not a political performance of swinging left or hiring figures from the opposition. As political consultant Park Dong-won suggests, it requires sijung (timing) and jeokjung (hitting the mark)—a pragmatic approach to governance. Instead, both conservative and liberal factions are chasing opinion poll numbers like a shell game, ignoring the genuine desires of the public and leading themselves into political ruin.

When Lee Jae-myung was gaining popularity, his outreach was effective because it was rooted in pragmatism and a clear vision of the times. After taking office, however, that flexibility vanished, replaced by an approach that actively undermines internal cohesion. Chasing the illusion of the center while alienating one's own base is an act of political self-harm.

The Retreat of Prosecution Reform

Rhyu Si-min hits the nail on the head regarding the current administration’s hesitation. Why did the legacy media remain silent when Rep. Jeong Seong-ho was being considered for the ministerial post, but react with fury when Rep. Kim Seung-won was proposed? It is because they know exactly where the blade of prosecution reform is aimed. Supporters are now expressing a grim, self-deprecating sentiment: "The only way to finish this reform is to have someone from the prosecution do it themselves."

As Rep. Kim Yong-min has argued, South Korea needs irreversible structural reform that cannot be undone by a change in power. Yet, the current proposal for the new "Public Prosecution Service" retains the existing prosecutors and investigators, essentially leaving a back door open for the "political prosecution" to rise again. It is deeply perplexing that a President who emerged from constitutional institutions—and who understands firsthand the dangers of unchecked prosecutorial power—would be the one to slow down this reform.

A Mirror Image of the Previous Administration

🔥 A Question of Procedure

If the current administration uses administrative decrees (sihaengryeong) to maintain power despite legislative intent, how does it differ from the governing style of the previous Yoon Suk-yeol administration? If the Democratic Party, which holds a majority, tries to keep the prosecution's hands tied via administrative workarounds rather than systemic change, it is a direct challenge to democratic procedure.

For months, the government essentially sat on its hands. While the Prime Minister and cabinet ministers passed the buck, a draft plan emerged—one that would have been impossible without presidential approval. Even though the administration eventually corrected course following inter-party consultations in early March, the initial confusion was a self-inflicted wound. The delusion that one can bypass procedure and control the pace of reform just because they hold power is exactly what leads to isolation and disaster.

The Rebranding of Power

The prosecution has shown remarkable skill in survival. While ostensibly handing over investigative powers, they have quietly rebranded the "Scientific Investigation Bureau" to a new "Legal & Scientific Planning" office. By keeping capabilities like digital forensics and evidence analysis under their control, they retain the ability to steer cases to their desired outcomes under the guise of "verification."

Even more alarming is the creation of a "Judicial Control Department." The prosecution is, by nature, an administrative agency, not a judicial one. Yet, by positioning themselves as a supervisor over the police, they reveal an authoritarian mindset—treating the police not as partners, but as subordinates. They are effectively cutting off the prosecution's limbs while sharpening its eyes and ears to maintain influence over the flow of power and wealth.

Editor’s Closing Thought: When the Reformers Become the Establishment

Politics loses trust not through grand conspiracies, but when leaders quietly break the small promises they made to the people. Prosecution reform was the defining demand of the public for decades. The administration was given power to complete this mission, yet they are now using loopholes and bureaucratic rebranding to keep the system intact. The decline in the President’s approval rating is not a mystery; it is the result of public disillusionment and the fear that the administration is undermining the very democratic processes it claimed to protect.

If the architects of reform become the protectors of the status quo, the administration’s future is grim. The public has been patient, but they will not be fooled indefinitely. There is still time, but instead of calculating political odds, the administration must return to the principles and common sense they once championed. Power is only a borrowed tool; the moment it is wielded for selfish ends, it invites its own destruction.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on the upcoming local elections and the party convention. The influence of online grassroots activists and party members is challenging the old, top-down "crony" politics of Yeouido (the home of the National Assembly). If the leadership continues to ignore this shift, the internal friction will only intensify, potentially leading to a broader political realignment.
