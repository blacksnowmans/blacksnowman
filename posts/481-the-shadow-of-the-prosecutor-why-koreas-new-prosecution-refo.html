The Shadow of the Prosecutor: Why Korea's New Prosecution Reform Is Raising Alarm Bells

September 09, 2026

For decades, the South Korean public has campaigned for a singular goal: to curb the immense power of the prosecution. In July, the country finally reached a historic milestone—a law mandating the complete separation of investigative and prosecutorial powers, ending 72 years of consolidated authority. It was intended to be the final chapter in a long struggle to return power to the people, following a lineage of legal reforms dating back to 1954.

However, the celebration has been short-lived. Critics argue that the Ministry of Justice, through a new organizational plan for a newly created "Public Prosecution Office," is attempting to bypass the legislature’s intent. By using administrative enforcement decrees, opponents claim the government is effectively "resurrecting" the old prosecution under a different name. Kim Yong-min, a lawmaker from the Democratic Party of Korea (the main liberal opposition party), recently called an emergency press conference, demanding the immediate withdrawal and full review of this new plan.

The Trojan Horse: A Rebranding of Power

The Ministry of Justice claims its new organizational structure reduces the staff of the new Public Prosecution Office by 21.4%. On the surface, this sounds like a victory for reform. But a closer look reveals that this is, at best, an accounting trick. Once you account for the 1,761 personnel being transferred to the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (a separate, independent investigative body), the actual reduction in force is a mere 238 people.

The most glaring issue is the retention of roughly 7,000 investigators. If the new office is meant solely to focus on prosecution and courtroom litigation, why retain an entire army of investigators? Furthermore, the Ministry is brazenly requesting to increase the number of prosecutors by 281. Critics argue that if the agency’s role is now limited to legal oversight and prosecution, the organization should be downsized and redesigned according to actual workload requirements, not bolstered to preserve its former investigative influence.

The intent, according to opposition lawmakers, is clear: the Ministry wants to keep the human and material infrastructure of the old prosecution intact, waiting for a shift in the political winds to restore its former power. By keeping these investigators on the payroll, they can effectively maintain "investigative command" over other agencies, subverting the spirit of the reform.

The Audacity of the 'Judicial Control Department'

Perhaps the most controversial feature of the new plan is the creation of a "Judicial Control Department" within each local prosecution office. The Ministry claims this department will review cases that police decide not to forward for prosecution. In a functional democracy, "judicial control" is the purview of the courts—they ensure that both investigation and prosecution remain within the bounds of the law.

For the prosecution—a body that has been the subject of intense public scrutiny for its own past abuses—to label its internal review unit as a "Judicial Control Department" is being viewed by many as an act of profound arrogance. It is, in essence, the accused attempting to act as the judge. Critics argue that this is not oversight; it is an attempt to institutionalize the prosecution’s authority to "choke" or direct other investigative agencies, effectively maintaining the status quo under a new, authoritative-sounding banner.

"The attempt to use sub-legal administrative decrees to defy the mandate of the people must be halted immediately. It is a return to the authoritarian habits of the past, where administrative power was used to manipulate the judicial system and neutralize the legislature."

The 'Two-Roof, One-Family' Danger

The risks are compounded by the personnel being tapped to lead these institutions. There is growing concern that both the newly formed Serious Crimes Investigation Agency and the Public Prosecution Office will be headed by former prosecutors. This creates a "two-roof, one-family" dynamic—a situation where, despite the legal separation of the two organizations, they remain deeply intertwined through their leadership’s shared institutional background.

The recent nomination of Kim Ji-young as the first head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency has intensified these fears. Kim is a former senior official at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office who has historically opposed efforts to reduce the prosecution’s reach. Placing former prosecutors at the helm of both the investigative and prosecutorial agencies effectively recreates the "prosecutorial cartel" that the reform was intended to dismantle. If the heads of both organizations share the same past, the structural separation becomes little more than a "yo-sik haeng-wi" (a mere formality or window-dressing).

What to Watch Next

The public debate is now focused on the looming September 9 deadline for the legislative notice of this plan. Opposition parties and civil society groups are making four concrete demands:

  • Immediate postponement: The government should pause the implementation to allow for a total redesign of the organizational structure.
  • Scrap the "Judicial Control" units: The plan to create internal control departments should be discarded in favor of strengthening actual courtroom capabilities.
  • Remove "Serious Crime" departments: The creation of these departments within the prosecution office is seen as a loophole for constant intervention in external investigations and should be deleted from the plan.
  • Implement independent oversight: The government must establish robust, external oversight mechanisms that allow other agencies to check against abuses of power, rather than concentrating that power within the prosecution.

The fight for judicial reform in Korea has always been about one thing: moving toward a society where the law applies equally to all, rather than serving as a tool for a privileged, self-regulating cartel. As the government moves toward its deadline, the question is whether it will listen to these concerns or continue to push through an administrative maneuver that risks hollows out the very reforms the public fought so hard to win.
