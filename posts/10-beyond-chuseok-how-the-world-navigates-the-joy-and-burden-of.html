Beyond Chuseok: How the World Navigates the Joy and Burden of Holidays

September 24, 2026

There is a specific, heavy silence that often settles over South Korea as the Chuseok holidays approach. Chuseok, our harvest festival, is traditionally a time for families to reunite under the full moon to honor their ancestors and share a meal. Yet, for many, it is synonymous with suffocating traffic, exhausting domestic labor, and the social anxiety of invasive questions from relatives. It is easy to view these struggles as uniquely Korean—a byproduct of our hyper-competitive society and lingering patriarchal norms. However, a recent discussion featuring guests from Iran, Pakistan, and Canada reveals that while the cultural packaging differs, the core experience of the "holiday" is a universal, often conflicted, human endeavor.

Nowruz and the Persian Art of Symbolic Renewal

In Iran, the most significant holiday is Nowruz, the Persian New Year. Like Chuseok or the Lunar New Year, it is tethered to the cycle of nature, arriving in March to mark the beginning of spring. It is a period for religious reflection, poetry, and a profound communal desire for prosperity and health.

The most striking aspect of Nowruz is the Haft-sin table. Families curate seven symbolic items that begin with the Persian letter "S," such as garlic or apples, to invite good fortune. Perhaps most relatable to a Korean observer is the ritual of the "golden coin." Elders gift coins—or chocolate versions of them—to the younger generation. Seeing these Iranian families engage in this tradition felt remarkably similar to the familiar sight of Korean elders handing out crisp banknotes to their nieces and nephews. It is a reminder that the instinct to offer financial blessings, paired with words of wisdom, is a global language of love.

What sets Nowruz apart is its grounding in the transition of seasons. While modern, fast-paced life often disconnects us from nature, Iranians gather under the moonlight to welcome the spring. For those of us who have forgotten the value of pausing to observe the rhythm of the year, there is much to envy in this ritual of stepping away from the weight of daily existence to reset alongside the blooming world.

The Eid Tradition and the Warmth of Community

If Nowruz is about the cycle of nature, Eid—the Islamic holiday celebrated in Pakistan—is a masterclass in communal endurance and hospitality. Eid follows the month of Ramadan, and after the initial morning prayers, the home becomes a hive of activity. Women prepare vast amounts of traditional food, and for three or four days, the home is a revolving door for relatives and neighbors.

A particularly poignant detail is the preparation of "Eidi," or gift money. Much like Korean families who visit banks to secure fresh, crisp 10,000 won (about $7.50) bills for Chuseok, Pakistani elders prepare pristine new currency to delight the children. The effort taken to secure these physical tokens of affection speaks to a deep-seated need to maintain social bonds. In a world where nuclear families and individualism are increasingly the norm, the Pakistani custom of spending days in close proximity to extended family serves as a blunt reminder of what we may have lost: the feeling of being a piece of a larger, sheltering whole.

We often frame holidays through the lens of "stress" or "fatigue." While the inequalities of domestic labor and the pressures of these gatherings are valid critiques, the Pakistani example suggests that when we dismiss these gatherings as "annoying," we risk drifting into a deeper, colder isolation. The fatigue of maintaining relationships is the price we pay for the warmth of belonging.

A Pragmatic Approach: The Canadian Holiday Model

In Canada, the holiday experience offers a different set of lessons. While Thanksgiving in Quebec is a "golden weekend"—a set time in October for family meals—the true intensity of family gathering is reserved for Christmas. However, the Canadian approach tends to be more pragmatic and, in some ways, more communal in its logistics.

Rather than confining themselves to a single matriarch’s kitchen, Canadian families often rent cabins or large spaces to camp, play games, and engage in shared activities together. This shift in environment is crucial. It moves the holiday from a domestic chore to a shared experience. Furthermore, there is a visible trend toward more egalitarian labor; the days of one gender shouldering the burden of cooking and cleaning are rapidly fading in favor of a "help each other" ethos. For South Korea, where debates over gender roles in holiday labor often lead to family friction or even divorce, this evolution in Western family structures provides a blueprint for a more equitable future.

What Lies Ahead: Reimagining the Ritual

Whether it is the Iranian Haft-sin, the Pakistani Eidi, or the Canadian camping trip, every culture struggles with the tension between the obligation to gather and the exhaustion of doing so. We are not alone in our complaints; family gatherings are inherently explosive environments where life milestones are judged and hierarchies are enforced.

However, the value of the holiday remains in its capacity to serve as a check on our survival. It is a periodic "wellness check" on those we love. The most important trend across these cultures is that the younger generation is no longer accepting the "old ways" blindly. They are asking, "Why must this be so painful?" and finding new ways to celebrate—be it through travel, shared labor, or simply setting boundaries.

What to watch for: As South Korean society continues to evolve, look for a shift away from traditional, labor-intensive home gatherings toward more "neutral" spaces, such as hotels or recreational facilities, which act as a great equalizer. Watch also for the rise of "holiday alternative" cultures, where families increasingly prioritize quality time over strict adherence to ancestral rites. The goal for the coming years should not be the total abolition of holidays, but the radical redesign of them. We must preserve the connection while stripping away the cruelty. After all, the true measure of a holiday is not the spread on the table, but the genuine care in the eyes of those sitting around it.
