From the Assembly Floor to Gwanghwamun: South Korea’s Opposition Launches Filibuster and Street War over Special Counsel Bill

November 06, 2026

Inside the main chamber of South Korea’s National Assembly in Yeouido, Seoul, the atmosphere turned electric as the Speaker of the National Assembly introduced the Special Counsel Bill on Fabricated Indictments. Lawmakers from the main conservative opposition party, the People Power Party (PPP), rose to their feet in unified protest. Holding up placards printed with angry condemnation, shouts echoed across the chamber floor. Within minutes, the PPP triggered a filibuster—an unlimited debate mechanism designed to block legislative action—with PPP Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik rushing to the podium to denounce the proposed law as an "unconstitutional parliamentary coup."

To understand the intensity of this confrontation, international observers must look at the transformed political landscape in Seoul. Following the historic impeachment and removal of Yoon Suk-yeol, the liberal Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) assumed power under President Lee Jae-myung. The ruling coalition is now pushing forward sweeping reform measures, including independent prosecutors to investigate alleged abuses of investigative authority, weaponized indictments, and prosecutorial misconduct during Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency. For the conservative PPP, now relegated to opposition status, the special counsel bill represents a direct political attack aimed at dismantling their remaining influence. For the ruling party, it is framed as a long-overdue accounting to restore judicial integrity after years of controversial political prosecutions.

A Legislative Gridlock: Filibusters and Chamber Walkouts

The core of the current battle is the Special Counsel Bill on Fabricated Indictments. The proposed legislation mandates an independent prosecutor to re-examine high-profile cases handled by the prosecution service during Yoon Suk-yeol’s tenure, specifically targeting claims that prosecutors systematically manufactured charges against political opponents while protecting political allies.

When the bill was formally brought to the floor, PPP Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik took the podium to lead his party's filibuster, declaring that conservatives would fight the measure "even if the heavens split in two." In his opening marathon address, Jeong argued that the special counsel is nothing more than a customized "defense law firm" built solely to insulate President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling party from legal scrutiny.

The parliamentary standoff quickly spilled over into other legislative business. Demonstrating their strategy of total non-cooperation, PPP lawmakers staged a collective walkout during discussions on an amendment to the Agricultural Cooperatives Act. By abandoning committee rooms and plenary sessions over critical rural policy legislation, the opposition signaled its intent to halt routine governance in an effort to extract concessions from the ruling coalition.

Taking the Fight to the Streets: The Conservative Rally at Gwanghwamun

Recognizing that their minority status limits their ability to block legislation indefinitely inside parliament, the People Power Party has announced a major shift to street politics. Opposition leaders announced plans to mobilize supporters for a massive outdoor rally on Saturday, December 7, centered at Gwanghwamun Plaza in downtown Seoul, with plans to expand protests toward the presidential office in the coming weeks.

Street mobilization holds immense historical weight in South Korea. Gwanghwamun Plaza is the traditional epicenter of Korean civic movement, famous for the massive candlelit demonstrations that brought down past administrations. However, when a major establishment party abandons parliamentary deliberation in favor of street rallies, it underscores an extreme level of partisan polarization.

This outdoor strategy relies on re-energizing the conservative base and linking arms with right-wing political figures and online commentators. By framing the Lee Jae-myung administration’s reform measures as an authoritarian overreach, the opposition seeks to build public pressure. Critics, however, warn that taking to the streets to block an independent investigation into past prosecutorial abuses risks turning civic spaces into polarized arenas of hostility, bypassing democratic legislative processes.

Collateral Damage: Budget Delays and Financial Backlash

The partisan warfare has completely disrupted the National Assembly’s Special Committee on Budgets and Accounts, throwing next year’s national budget review into uncertainty. Rather than scrutinizing state spending allocations for President Lee Jae-myung’s key economic policies, committee sessions have devolved into shouting matches over the special counsel bill, leading to repeated suspensions.

This legislative paralysis coincides with intense public scrutiny over unresolved financial liabilities stemming from Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency. Under South Korean law, political parties must return public election expense reimbursements under specific conditions of invalidation or campaign violations.

While the PPP routinely criticizes the Democratic Party over a disputed 43.4 billion won ($31.5 million USD) campaign fund issue, the conservative opposition itself faces mounting public pressure regarding a 39.7 billion won (about $29 million USD) reimbursement obligation linked to Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency and its disastrous political conclusion. For many citizens, the opposition's refusal to address its own massive financial liabilities while bringing budget reviews to a standstill severely damages its credibility when accusing the government of fiscal recklessness.

Internal Fractures and the Shadow of Yoon Suk-yeol

Beneath the PPP's aggressive public front lies deep anxiety over the lingering legacy of Yoon Suk-yeol. Currently imprisoned as his criminal appeal trials proceed, the former leader remains a deeply divisive figure within the conservative movement.

The special counsel investigation threatens to uncover further evidence regarding controversial prosecutorial actions and abuse of authority during Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency. Some moderate conservative lawmakers worry that Floor Leader Jeong Jeom-sik’s hardline rhetoric—which includes calls to "cancel the regime"—will alienate moderate voters who supported the transition of power to President Lee Jae-myung.

By tying the party’s survival entirely to blocking investigations into Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency, the PPP leadership risks backing itself into a corner. If the filibuster is broken and an independent counsel uncovers definitive proof of fabricated indictments or unlawful prosecutorial interference, the political fallout for the conservative party could be catastrophic.

What to Watch Next

As South Korea enters an intense political confrontation heading into the end of the year, several key milestones will reveal whether the political deadlock will deepen or resolve:

  • The Filibuster Closure Vote: Under National Assembly rules, a filibuster can be forcibly ended after 24 hours if three-fifths (180 members) of the assembly vote for closure. Watch whether the ruling Democratic Party and aligned parties can successfully rally the required votes to bring the Special Counsel Bill to a final vote.
  • Turnout at the December 7 Gwanghwamun Rally: The size and enthusiasm of the PPP’s street demonstration will show whether the conservative base is willing to sustain an extended outdoor campaign, or if public fatigue with legislative paralysis will limit its impact.
  • The Statutory Budget Deadline: With fiscal reviews stalled in committee, watch whether party leaders can negotiate a compromise before statutory deadlines expire, or if core public funding initiatives under President Lee Jae-myung will remain stalled in committee.
  • Developments in Yoon Suk-yeol’s Appeal Trial: Upcoming decisions and testimony in Yoon Suk-yeol’s ongoing judicial proceedings could reshape the political landscape, either strengthening the ruling party's push for special counsel investigations or fueling further opposition resistance.
