Packing for the Camino de Santiago: What South Korean Hikers Know About Surviving the 800-Kilometer Trek

September 07, 2026

For many travelers, the Camino de Santiago is far more than a long-distance hike. Spanning roughly 800 kilometers (about 500 miles) from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in the French Pyrenees to Santiago de Compostela in northwestern Spain, the historic pilgrimage route has become a massive sanctuary for reflection, physical endurance, and mental reset. While some travelers opt to walk the final 100 kilometers from Sarria simply to earn their official completion certificate, those who brave the punishing climbs of the Pyrenees from the French starting point understand the deeper value of the full journey. Yet, standing in a room surrounded by gear, prospective pilgrims inevitably face a wave of anxiety: What do I actually need, and how much weight can I carry before my back gives out?

In South Korea, where outdoor recreation has exploded into a massive cultural phenomenon, seasoned hikers and long-haul backpackers have meticulously debated and refined the art of Camino packing. Drawing from their trials and errors, South Korean travel logs offer a pragmatic, highly tactical masterclass on what items make or break a month-long trek across Europe.

Daily Recovery and the Battle Against Muscle Fatigue

When you walk anywhere from 10 to 40 kilometers a day, your body will inevitably protest. The arches of your feet and your calves absorb relentless shock, building up deep, stubborn knots. Among South Korean pilgrims, a simple, low-tech tool has emerged as an absolute non-negotiable: a palm-sized massage ball, similar to the massage rollers widely popular in local fitness culture. Weighing almost nothing, a rubber release ball slips easily into a side pocket. Reaching an albergue (a pilgrim hostel) after a grueling day and rolling it under your feet while sitting on the edge of a bunk offers immediate, targeted relief. Priced affordably—typically between 4,000 and 9,000 won (about $3 to $7)—it delivers unmatched value per gram of weight.

Equally critical is anticipating pharmaceutical differences abroad. Pilgrims often experience a rude awakening upon reaching a Spanish pharmacy looking for adhesive medicated patches (the kind commonly sold in South Korea for sore muscles). European pharmacies generally stock topical gels rather than adhesive sheets. Navigating a language barrier while trying to explain deep joint pain to a pharmacist can be exhausting. Smart travelers pack specialized patches, painkillers, and personal joint supports directly from home rather than wasting precious energy searching foreign towns.

The Core Rule of Camino Gear: Local Adaptability

Many first-time pilgrims load their backpacks with excessive supplies out of anxiety, turning their bags into instruments of torture. Packing for the Camino requires a strict division between what you must bring from home and what you should source locally. Items tied to cultural habits, like specific pain relief patches, should be packed. Bulky items like walking poles or rain gear, however, can easily be bought on the ground in Europe.

Surviving the Spartan Realities of Pilgrim Hostels

The albergues that house weary walkers every night are communal by design, packing anywhere from four to twenty strangers into a single room. A symphony of loud snoring, restless tossing, and pre-dawn packing noises makes quality sleep a rare commodity. For light sleepers, earplugs are not an optional comfort; they are a vital survival tool. Lacking proper rest leads to chronic fatigue, which quickly drains the psychological resilience needed to finish the trail.

Power management presents another daily trial. Charging outlets next to bunk beds are scarce in many rustic hostels, and public charging areas in hallways invite theft when you cannot monitor your device. A reliable 10,000mAh portable power bank is essential for keeping navigation apps running without risking a lost phone. For those looking to cut dining costs and warm their stomachs after days of bread and cheese, compact travel gear like a foldable electric kettle has become a cult favorite. Boiling water in a dorm room to make a quick hot drink or boil eggs transforms a lean budget into a comforting daily ritual.

The Comfort of Home: Food and Organization

While Spanish cuisine is celebrated worldwide, weeks of heavy European meals can leave travelers longing for the comforting warmth of a savory, broth-based dish. South Korean pilgrims have popularized packing freeze-dried soup blocks—lightweight cubes that dissolve in hot water to create an instant, steaming soup. Eating a familiar, comforting broth on a chilly evening does more than fill an empty stomach; it provides a profound psychological anchor when homesickness strikes.

Equally important is how you organize the interior of your pack. Because traditional backpacks load from the top rather than opening flat like a suitcase, searching for a single item can easily force you to empty your entire bag onto a hostel floor. Experienced hikers swear by packing cubes or simple zip-lock bags to categorize clothes, electronics, and medical supplies. Maintaining a hyper-organized pack eliminates daily friction and preserves mental peace over a 40-day itinerary.

Knowing What to Buy on the Ground

One of the most common beginner mistakes is buying expensive hiking gear at home before departure. High-end trekking poles, for instance, are notoriously difficult to pack in airplane carry-ons and subject to strict airline baggage restrictions. Instead, many travelers head straight to Decathlon—the ubiquitous European sporting goods retailer—upon arrival to pick up sturdy trekking poles and long, poncho-style rain covers designed to drape over both the hiker and a bulky backpack for 30,000 to 40,000 won (about $22 to $30).

Similarly, eye protection is paramount. Because the Camino generally tracks from east to west, morning walks keep the sun at your back, but late-morning hours expose hikers to relentless lateral glare. Transition lenses or a wide-brimmed sun hat are mandatory equipment to prevent ocular fatigue from compounding physical exhaustion.

What to Watch Next

As interest in long-distance walking routes continues to grow among travelers seeking alternatives to fast-paced tourism, watch for shifting trends in lightweight gear design and airline baggage policies regarding outdoor equipment. More importantly, observe how modern travelers increasingly view travel not as a checklist of sights, but as an exercise in intentional minimalism—stripping away excess to focus entirely on physical endurance and inner quiet. For anyone planning their own departure, the ultimate takeaway from the trails of northern Spain is clear: success relies less on perfecting your gear list and more on cultivating the patience to accept whatever the road brings your way.
