Inside South Korea's Never-Ending Battle Over Prosecution Reform

October 06, 2026

Picture a legal system where a single institution writes the rules of an investigation, hunts down the suspects, collects the evidence, decides whether to formally charge them, and even asks the court for arrest warrants. For decades, this has been the reality in South Korea, where the prosecution service wields a sweeping monopoly over both investigating crimes and prosecuting them. To critics, it is a structure that invites unchecked power and political abuse. To defenders, it is a necessary anchor of state stability.

Now, this long-standing fault line in South Korean politics has cracked open once again. At the center of the latest uproar is a debate over "supplementary investigation rights"—the power for prosecutors to step back into active investigative work after the police have handed over a case. What sounds like a dry procedural dispute is actually the frontline in a bitter war over the future of South Korean democracy, pitting reform-minded politicians against entrenched institutional power and exposing sharp divisions even within the country's opposition parties.

The Monopolized Power of South Korea's Prosecutors

In modern democracies, the separation of powers is treated as an unshakeable baseline. Investigators gather facts; lawyers trained in the state's legal code decide whether those facts cross the line into criminality. Yet South Korea’s prosecution service has traditionally held both roles simultaneously. Lawmakers like Park Eun-jung, a prominent critic of the system and a member of the Rebuilding Korea Party, have long argued that this concentration of authority makes structural abuse inevitable. Under the current framework, prosecutors not only direct investigations but also hold the sole power to indict, drop charges, request warrants, and close cases.

Reformers argue that true democratic accountability requires splitting these functions cleanly: police and dedicated investigative agencies should handle fieldwork, while prosecutors should be strictly confined to reviewing whether a case meets the threshold for indictment. Yet prosecutors have fiercely defended their right to conduct "supplementary investigations," arguing that they need direct access to the investigative process to ensure cases are ironclad before they reach a courtroom.

Critics, however, point out that this power is routinely used to preserve institutional leverage rather than secure justice. When prosecutors retain the ability to meddle in ongoing files or dig deeper themselves, the theoretical neutrality of the charging process evaporates. The core demand of South Korea's prosecution reform movement has always been simple: turn prosecutors from active detectives back into legal arbiters. But translating that principle into law has proven remarkably difficult.

The Illusion of Data and the Reality of Supplementary Investigations

To understand why reformers are so skeptical of the prosecution's defense of its powers, one only has to look at the numbers. Out of roughly 1.4 million criminal cases handled annually in South Korea, prosecutors formally request that police conduct supplementary investigations in about 90,000 instances. But when it comes to the supplementary investigations prosecutors perform themselves, reliable data is remarkably sparse.

Reformers argue that the prosecution’s insistence on keeping these powers is largely a defensive bluff. In practice, much of what prosecutors label as "supplementary investigation" amounts to little more than bureaucratic paperwork, administrative record-stretching, or minor warrant requests—tasks that could easily be supervised from a distance rather than handled through direct investigation. If the goal is ensuring case integrity, critics argue that the burden should rest on police and independent investigative bodies, with prosecutors acting strictly as legal gatekeepers.

The debate is not about administrative efficiency; it is about whether an elite legal agency should be allowed to keep its thumb on the scales of justice under the guise of technical necessity.

By clinging to direct investigative authority without transparent metrics to justify it, the prosecution service fuels public suspicion. In a modern democratic state, administrative power cannot be sustained by institutional habit alone. It requires empirical justification—something the prosecution's defenders have consistently failed to provide.

Cracks in the Opposition: Mixed Signals from Political Leaders

While the argument for separating investigation from prosecution has long united the progressive camp—including figures like independent-minded lawmakers and prominent commentators—translating that unity into a coherent legislative strategy has hit severe roadblocks. The fault lines became glaringly apparent following a recent social media post by Lee Jae-myung, leader of the Democratic Party, South Korea's main liberal opposition party.

For years, hardline reformers—including members of the Rebuilding Korea Party and progressive lawmakers like Choo Mi-ae and Yong Min-in—have insisted that prosecutors should only hold the power to *request* supplementary work from police, rather than conducting it themselves. They envision a restructured system featuring a dedicated investigative agency under the Ministry of Justice, paired with a separate prosecution office restricted entirely to indictments.

However, Lee's recent public comments suggested a more compromising approach, hinting at institutional adjustments that would still permit a degree of supplementary investigative authority within the prosecution framework. This stance triggered immediate backlash and confusion within the reform coalition. Critics argued that leaving any room for prosecutors to conduct direct investigations defeats the entire purpose of the overhaul, reducing a systemic transformation into a toothless compromise.

This political friction highlights a recurring weakness in South Korea's reform movements: when faced with the messy realities of governance and immediate political pressures, politicians often retreat from radical institutional redesign in favor of expediency. For a public weary of watching the legal system bend to political winds, these mixed signals from the opposition are deeply frustrating.

What Lies Ahead for South Korea's Legal System

The battle over supplementary investigation rights is ultimately about more than statutes and agency charts. It is a referendum on whether South Korea can successfully dismantle what many citizens refer to as the "Republic of Prosecution"—a system where state power is concentrated to an unhealthy degree in an unelected, unaccountable elite.

As the debate continues to bounce between the National Assembly, party headquarters, and public forums, the stakes remain exceptionally high. True reform will require resisting the temptation to settle for half-measures and institutional workarounds. If South Korea is to build a truly equitable judicial system, the line between investigating a crime and judging a suspect must be drawn in stone, not written in shifting political sand.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on upcoming legislative proposals from both the Democratic Party and smaller progressive blocs like the Rebuilding Korea Party. Specifically, watch whether draft bills explicitly strip prosecutors of direct investigative authority or leave loopholes for "supplementary" work. That wording will tell the real story of whether South Korea's politicians are serious about structural reform or simply shuffling titles on an organizational chart.
