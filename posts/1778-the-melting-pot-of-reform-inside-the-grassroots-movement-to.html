The Melting Pot of Reform: Inside the Grassroots Movement to Shield the Lee Jae-myung Administration

October 31, 2026

The humid air at Mukgye Yangjok—a quiet spot removed from the bustling center of Seoul—did not dampen the spirits of the hundreds who gathered there. They had traveled from across the country, not for a holiday, but for a "political retreat" organized by Park Si-young TV, one of the most influential pro-government media channels in South Korea. For the outside observer, it might look like a simple fan meet-up for a YouTuber. But in the context of South Korean politics today, this gathering represents the "furnace" of the progressive movement: the core supporters who believe they are the last line of defense for President Lee Jae-myung’s government.

The event, titled "What Shall We Do?", arrives at a critical juncture. Despite the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) holding the presidency and a significant presence in the National Assembly, the atmosphere among the rank-and-file is not one of triumphalism. Instead, it is a mixture of urgency and anxiety. The supporters gathered here are grappling with a paradox: while they won the ultimate prize of the presidency after the impeachment and imprisonment of Yoon Suk Yeol-ssi, they feel the "system"—the judiciary, the bureaucracy, and even elements within their own party—is still working against their reform agenda. To understand the current state of South Korean progressivism, one must look past the formal halls of power in Yeouido (Seoul’s political district) and into these grassroots "universes" where political strategy is debated with the fervor of a religious revival.

The Rise of the Digital Agora and the "Park Si-young TV Universe"

To understand why this retreat matters, one must first understand the role of Park Si-young. A veteran political consultant, Park has built a massive digital platform that serves as a primary source of news and strategy for the DPK’s core base. In South Korea, where traditional media is often polarized, YouTube channels like Park Si-young TV have become more than just media outlets; they are political organizers. They function as a "Digital Agora," a space where supporters coordinate their messaging and hold their own elected officials accountable.

The retreat featured a "who’s who" of progressive voices, including investigative journalists like Jang In-su and politicians like Representative Kim Young-hwan. The rapport between the speakers and the audience was electric. When Lee Myung-sook, a journalist known for his sharp commentary, was jokingly voted "most handsome" in an informal popularity poll, the laughter that erupted served as a brief respite from the heavy political discourse. But the levity was a backdrop to a serious mission: synchronizing the "fandom" with the party’s legislative goals.

This "fandom politics" is a double-edged sword that defines the current era. To critics, it represents a populist surge that can stifle internal debate. To those at the retreat, however, it is the only way to ensure that the "Imperial Presidency"—a term often used in Korea to describe the concentration of power in the Blue House—is actually used to enact the will of the people. They argue that when the formal systems of democracy fail to deliver reform, the energy must come from the streets and the digital space. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, this core base is the "engine" of its governing power, providing the public pressure necessary to push through controversial reforms in the face of a fierce opposition from the People Power Party (PPP), the country's main conservative party.

A Party Divided: The "New Lee Jae-myung" Supporters and the Old Guard

One of the most significant tensions discussed at the retreat is the internal friction within the Democratic Party. Since Lee Jae-myung’s rise to the presidency, a new wave of supporters has joined the party. These are often referred to as "New Lee Jae-myung" (or "New-Lee") supporters. They are generally younger, more digitally active, and more uncompromising in their demand for reform than the party’s traditional establishment.

The retreat highlighted a growing concern that the DPK leadership, led by figures like Party Chairman Kim Min-seok, must do more to integrate these new members. There were sharp warnings against "exclusionary politics." Some within the party’s "old guard" have suggested that the party should focus on moderate voters, even if it means distancing themselves from the more radical demands of the "New-Lee" base. The voices at the retreat were clear: this is a recipe for disaster. They argue that the party’s strength comes from its unity, and that "throwing away half the supporters" to appease the center is a failing strategy.

"Politics is the process of gathering strength and winning hearts, not a 'subtraction' game where you cut off your comrades because they think differently."

This internal struggle is about the very definition of "representation." If the party fails to reflect the passion of its most active members, it risks a "collapse of representation," where the leadership and the base are moving in different directions. For President Lee to succeed, the party must act as a "melting pot," merging the experience of the veteran politicians with the raw energy of the new activists. The slogan heard throughout the retreat, "Let’s protect the Democratic Party," was as much a message to the party’s own leadership as it was to the opposition. It was a demand for a party that is "wide-armed" enough to hold all its reformers together.

The Judicial Wall and the Crisis of Democratic Accountability

Perhaps the most contentious topic at the retreat was the state of the South Korean judiciary. Despite the change in government, many progressives believe the courts remain a stronghold of the "Old Guard." The name of Cho Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, was frequently invoked as a symbol of a system that lacks "democratic accountability."

The core supporters of the DPK are deeply suspicious of the judiciary’s role in high-profile political cases. They point to what they perceive as unfair rulings and "manufactured investigations" by the prosecution—a legacy they associate with the era of Yoon Suk Yeol-ssi. There is a profound sense that while the presidency was won at the ballot box, the "judicial power" remains unelected and unresponsive to the public’s desire for justice. This has led to calls for radical transparency and even institutional changes to how judges are held responsible for their rulings.

This frustration extends to the DPK’s own lawmakers. Speakers at the retreat criticized "complacent" members of the National Assembly who, once they have secured their seats (often referred to as "wearing the badge"), become hesitant to push for aggressive prosecutorial or judicial reform. The supporters see this as a betrayal of the mandate they gave the party. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, the judiciary is often seen as a "bottleneck" that can stall or invalidate key policies. The retreat served as a warning to DPK lawmakers: if they do not hold the judiciary accountable, the supporters will hold the lawmakers accountable at the next primary.

Sustainable Activism: The "Yangjaecheon Strategy" for the Long Haul

Political engagement in South Korea is famously high-intensity, and the retreat addressed a growing problem: "political burnout." The constant cycle of news, internal party strife, and legal battles has left many supporters feeling exhausted and angry. This is where the event took a more reflective turn, focusing on how to maintain a movement for the long term.

Journalist Kang Ji-ho offered a surprisingly practical piece of advice that resonated with the crowd. He spoke about his routine of walking along the Yangjaecheon (a popular stream and park area in southern Seoul) for an hour every day. He described it as a way to "lower the angle of his anger." This "Yangjaecheon walk" became a metaphor for the emotional wisdom needed in politics. To fight a "long war" against established interests, one cannot live in a state of constant, white-hot fury.

  • Reflective Activism: Moving beyond blind anger to strategic, thoughtful action.
  • Emotional Regulation: Recognizing that "political stress" can lead to apathy if not managed.
  • Unity over Purity: Resisting the urge to turn on allies over minor tactical differences.

This focus on "reflection" (seongchal) is seen as a key differentiator between the progressive movement and its opponents. By looking inward and acknowledging the contradictions within their own camp, these activists believe they can build a more resilient and intellectually grounded movement. It is a recognition that the success of the Lee Jae-myung presidency depends not just on winning elections, but on the mental and emotional health of the people who power the campaign.

What to Watch Next: The Road to the General Elections

As the retreat at Mukgye Yangjok concluded, the focus shifted toward the future. The energy gathered here is intended to be channeled into a "Yeouido Alliance"—a proposed massive collaborative broadcast featuring all the major progressive YouTube and independent media voices. This would be an attempt to bypass traditional media and speak directly to the public on a scale never seen before in Korean politics.

In the coming months, there are several key indicators of whether the "melting pot" of this retreat will successfully influence the national stage:

  • DPK Nominations: Watch how the party handles the primary process. If "New-Lee" candidates successfully challenge established incumbents, it will be a sign that the grassroots energy from retreats like this is being converted into institutional power.
  • Judicial Reform Legislation: Look for whether the DPK-controlled Assembly introduces new bills aimed at increasing the accountability of judges and prosecutors. The level of "aggression" in these bills will be a direct reflection of the pressure from the base.
  • The "Unity" Narrative: Observe the rhetoric of Chairman Kim Min-seok and President Lee Jae-myung. Will they adopt the "inclusive" language demanded by the retreat, or will the internal factionalism between the old guard and the new fans continue to spill into the open?

The "Park Si-young TV Political Retreat" was a reminder that in South Korea, politics is a high-stakes, participatory drama. For these core supporters, protecting the Democratic Party is not just about supporting a political organization; it is about protecting the very tool they believe will fix a broken system. Whether their "furnace" of passion will forge a new, unified party or simply burn through the existing structures remains the central question of the Lee Jae-myung era.
