Cracks in the Firewall: Why South Korea Is Watching Germany's Far-Right Surge

October 16, 2026

When political commentators in Seoul survey international headlines, European parliamentary maneuvering often feels worlds away from the high-stakes, hyper-polarized battles of South Korean domestic politics. Yet over recent weeks, an unusual degree of attention among Korean analysts has settled on the eastern states of Germany. What unfolded across state elections last September—and the subsequent polling projecting toward the 2029 federal elections—is no longer being treated in Seoul as distant foreign trivia. Instead, it is being scrutinized as an urgent case study in how fringe, grievance-driven politics can breach the core institutions of an advanced democracy.

At the center of this scrutiny is the dramatic ascent of the Alternative for Germany (AfD). In state after state, this far-right formation has disrupted a postwar settlement engineered specifically to keep extremism out of executive power. For South Korean observers, who are currently living through their own profound institutional realignments and intense civic polarization, the questions being asked in Berlin resonate with uncomfortable clarity: What happens when historical taboos are no longer enough to contain populist resentment? And what happens when the formal rules of coalition-building force democratic parties into impossible moral compromises?

Coalitions, Compromise, and the Arithmetic of German Power

To understand why the latest regional ballots sent shockwaves through the continent, one must first understand the structural machinery of the German republic. Germany functions under a layered federal framework comprising the central federal government (the Bund) and sixteen distinct state parliaments (the Landtage). Under its proportional electoral system, parliamentary seats are distributed to reflect each party's share of the popular vote.

This design was deliberately chosen after the devastation of World War II. By denying any single faction easy total control, proportional representation guarantees pluralism, but it also imposes a permanent requirement for compromise. Winning the most votes on election night does not confer an automatic mandate to govern. Unless a party secures an outright legislative majority—a rare feat in modern Germany—it must negotiate pacts, stitch together coalition platforms, and compromise on core campaign pledges to reach the threshold of power.

For decades, this machinery operated with predictable stability. Power rotated between, or was shared by, two mainstream pillars:

  • The Union bloc: The center-right alliance of the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Bavaria’s Christian Social Union (CSU).
  • The Social Democratic Party (SPD): The traditional center-left force that championed social welfare and labor.

Whether in opposition or in grand coalition with one another, these two historic forces formed an unspoken duopoly. Voters accepted the horse-trading because it delivered economic growth, international respectability, and institutional predictability. Today, however, that consensus is buckling under accumulated public fatigue, persistent regional grievances, and deep discontent with established political elites.

The Historical Taboo: How the 'Firewall' Kept Extremism at Bay

The linchpin of postwar German stability was never purely structural; it was ethical. Built on the painful collective memory of the Nazi era and the catastrophic collapse of the Weimar Republic, Germany’s political establishment adopted a non-negotiable cordon sanitaire known as the firewall principle (Brandmauer).

The premise of the firewall was simple and unyielding: no mainstream party would ever govern with, form a coalition alongside, or rely on the legislative backing of the far right. Regardless of how many protest votes an extremist party managed to harvest at the ballot box, the doors to executive authority remained bolted. The firewall was viewed not merely as an ordinary tactical maneuver, but as the moral baseline of German constitutional democracy—an institutional immune system designed to prevent fascism from ever using democratic instruments to dismantle democracy from within.

For years, this taboo held firm. Fringe agitators could enter state legislatures or the federal parliament to make speeches, but they were permanently locked out of actual governance. Yet a firewall only functions as long as the mainstream parties collectively hold enough legislative ground to form viable alternative majorities. Once the margins shrink, the cost of keeping the wall intact rises exponentially.

The September Earthquake in the East

That arithmetic collapsed during the state elections held last September across three German states. Rather than functioning as marginal protest vehicles, the far-right AfD scored decisive victories, claiming first place in two of the three contested states and displacing the long-entrenched mainstream parties.

The scale of the shift was particularly dramatic in the state of Saxony-Anhalt:

  • The AfD captured a staggering 43.8% of the vote.
  • This represented a near-doubling of the party’s support compared to the previous state election in 2021.

A similar political earthquake occurred in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, where the AfD pushed aside the historically dominant Social Democrats to finish in first place. These were not narrow, statistical flukes. They were sweeping repudiations of the status quo, fueled by simmering economic anxieties, perceived cultural marginalization, and sharp divisions over immigration policy.

Yet, because the AfD still fell short of an absolute majority of legislative seats, the results created a governing paralysis. To pass budgets and form cabinets, parties must assemble coalitions. With the AfD holding the largest bloc of seats, mainstream parties are confronted with a toxic dilemma: either enter unprecedented, unwieldy coalitions of ideological opposites that alienate their own voter bases, or quietly begin normalizing communication with the far right. In several regional corridors, whispers of loosening the firewall to ensure basic legislative function have already begun to surface. Once political survival supplants historical principle, the taboo ceases to protect the system.

The 2029 Horizon and the Warning for Seoul

The state-level victories are not isolated anomalies confined to the former East. Long-term forecasting paints an even bleaker picture for the federal landscape. Recent hypothetical polling modeling the 2029 federal parliamentary election shows the AfD surging to 30% support nationally. This figure puts the far-right party more than ten percentage points ahead of the traditional center-right Union alliance.

For South Korean observers watching this unfold, the parallels are hard to miss. While South Korea’s institutional framework differs sharply—featuring a direct presidential system rather than a parliamentary coalition model—the underlying social dynamics bear an eerie resemblance. In Korea, as in Europe, established parties have struggled to address widening wealth disparities, regional decline, and deep generational anxieties. When mainstream politics appears unresponsive or trapped in perpetual institutional gridlock, populist movements step into the void, turning alienation into organized political resentment.

The lesson drawn by Korean analysts is stark: democratic norms do not erode because extremist movements suddenly discover persuasive arguments; they erode because mainstream institutions lose the public's confidence, leaving the electorate receptive to the politics of exclusion.

The German experience demonstrates that legal frameworks, proportional mechanics, and postwar moral consensus cannot indefinitely defend a system if the public feels unrepresented. When historical remembrance is treated as an abstract lecture rather than backed by effective governance, the rhetoric of anger inevitably finds an audience.

What to Watch Next

As Germany approaches its next federal cycle, analysts in Seoul and around the world will be watching several critical indicators:

  • State Government Formations: Watch how coalition negotiations unfold in Saxony-Anhalt and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. Any formal or informal reliance on AfD votes to pass state legislation will signal the practical demise of the firewall.
  • Mainstream Strategy and Rhetoric: Observe whether the CDU/CSU and SPD attempt to reclaim lost ground by adopting harder lines on immigration and cultural issues, or whether they attempt to build broader cross-party pacts to defend democratic norms.
  • The Path to 2029: Monitor national tracking polls to see if the AfD’s projected 30% support solidifies into durable party loyalty or recedes as economic and geopolitical conditions evolve.

Germany has long stood as an exemplar of historical reckoning and structural democratic stability. If its political firewall gives way under the weight of populist anger, the reverberations will travel far beyond Europe—offering a sobering warning to democracies everywhere that institutions are only as strong as the public trust that sustains them.
