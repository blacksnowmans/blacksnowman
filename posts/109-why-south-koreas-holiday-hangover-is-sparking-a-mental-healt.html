Why South Korea's Holiday Hangover is Sparking a Mental Health Reckoning

September 28, 2026

The turkey or the mooncakes have been cleared away, the guests have gone home, and the house is finally quiet. For many people, however, the silence does not bring relief. Instead, it brings a crushing physical and emotional exhaustion that lingers long after the suitcases are unpacked. In South Korea, where major traditional holidays like Chuseok or Lunar New Year demand intense family obligations, travel, and labor, this phenomenon is widely recognized as "holiday syndrome." But as discussion around the condition deepens, it is becoming clear that what millions experience is far more than a simple case of post-holiday fatigue. It is a systemic collision of outdated social expectations, unequal domestic burdens, and a cultural stigma surrounding mental health that refuses to break.

The Anatomy of Holiday Stress: In-Laws, Husbands, and Domestic Inequality

When analysts and mental health advocates look at the root causes of holiday stress in South Korea, the findings are remarkably consistent—and deeply revealing. Topping the list of grievances is the dread of visiting the spouse's family, a friction point so well-documented it borders on cliché. Yet, beneath the familiar tropes lies a severe structural flaw in traditional family dynamics. Close behind the resentment toward in-laws is a far more damaging friction: resentment toward husbands.

During a high-stress holiday, the partner who is supposed to be an ally often behaves like an outsider, or worse, an adversary. While modern marriages exist on paper, deep-seated patriarchal habits tend to resurface under the roof of the husband's parents. Husbands frequently retreat into authoritarian or passive roles, leaving their wives isolated, while expecting them to navigate the unspoken rules of the extended family. To an outside observer, these tensions might look like minor domestic spats. To the person trapped in them, they feel like matters of survival.

This dynamic exposes an old, unresolved wound in Korean society: the glaring inequality of domestic labor during major holidays. While men often gather in the living room to watch television or rest, women are expected to spend days hunched over kitchen counters preparing elaborate meals for dozens of relatives. When exhausted spouses point out this glaring disparity, they are too often met with dismissive refrains about tradition—the classic "that's just how things have always been." This refusal to acknowledge systemic unfairness acts as the primary greenhouse for holiday-induced depression, turning a calendar event into an annual exercise in alienation.

When Exhaustion Becomes Depression: The Distortion of Mental Fatigue

When holiday stress crosses the line into clinical depression, the psychological toll alters how a person experiences reality. Mental health experts note that when the brain's neurochemicals are depleted by chronic exhaustion, a household chore that normally feels like a 10 out of 10 suddenly registers as a 100. The danger of holiday burnout is not just physical fatigue; it is the total collapse of emotional resilience.

A brain running on empty begins to misinterpret ordinary social cues as active threats. In a crowded room full of relatives—many of whom may not even be particularly well-liked—a depressed individual can easily slip into profound paranoia and isolation. If other family members are laughing and chatting while the exhausted spouse sits in silence, the brain's defense mechanisms often leap to a painful conclusion: everyone in the room is allied against them. This heightens feelings of persecution and traps the individual in a suffocating loop of anxiety and anger.

Crucially, this state cannot be cured by simply "toughing it out" or relying on willpower. Depression is a physiological depletion of brain function, not a character flaw. Yet, forcing individuals to push through these episodes under the guise of family harmony only deepens the psychological scarring.

Beyond Talk Therapy: The Pragmatic Role of Medical Intervention

When the psychological weight becomes unmanageable, finding the right kind of help is essential, yet fraught with barriers. Basic psychological counseling certainly has its place. Talking through pent-up resentment can provide an initial release valve, and therapists often act as objective filters to determine whether a person is experiencing mild situational blues or severe clinical depression.

However, counseling alone has a hard ceiling. When a person's neurotransmitters are entirely exhausted, telling them to "think positively" is practically useless. This is where the lingering social stigma surrounding psychiatric medication in South Korea does the most damage. Many people recognize that they need medical intervention, yet hesitate for fear of judgment or a vague, superstitious dread of psychiatric drugs.

Modern psychiatric treatment, however, is far more practical than popular culture admits. For someone trapped in a loop of trauma and past grievances, the goal of medication is simple: to grant the brain "the freedom of oblivion." When a person’s chest is tight with panic and their mind is replaying distressing arguments on an endless loop, a well-prescribed pharmaceutical can act as a circuit breaker. Experts note that the right medication can quiet heart palpitations and grant a suffering person eight hours of uninterrupted peace—a much-needed reset for an overheated mental engine. Utilizing medical help is not a sign of weakness; it is a rational strategy for self-preservation.

Facing the Endless Cycle: Preparing for the Next Holiday Wave

The end of one holiday rarely brings genuine rest; it merely signals the countdown to the next. For working professionals and overburdened spouses in South Korea, the relief of surviving Chuseok is immediately undercut by the approaching wave of year-end gatherings, company banquets, and Christmas obligations. Without adequate time to recover, individuals are constantly dragged back into a cycle of burnout.

This relentless social calendar forces a difficult reckoning. As individuals emerge from the fog of holiday-induced depression, they face a stark choice, as one mental health specialist noted: do they find the courage to face social disapproval and simply decline to participate, or do they retreat into the familiar misery of compliance?

There is no easy answer, but the agency must belong to the individual. Breaking the cycle requires radical honesty about personal limits. Whether that means refusing to attend gatherings that demand undue sacrifice, insisting on an equal division of labor within the marriage, or taking the step to consult a medical professional, change is no longer optional. Holiday syndrome is not a personal failure; it is a glaring warning sign from a society whose traditional expectations are outliving their sustainability. Listening to that warning—and choosing self-preservation over forced appearances—is the only way to survive the calendar year with one's sanity intact.

  • Structural inequality: Deeply entrenched patriarchal expectations around family visits and domestic labor remain the core drivers of holiday stress.
  • Neurological impact: Severe burnout and depression distort perception, magnifying everyday tasks and social interactions into sources of intense anxiety and isolation.
  • Practical medicine: Psychiatric medication is not a sign of moral weakness, but a functional tool to give an exhausted brain much-needed rest and stability.
