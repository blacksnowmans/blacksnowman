The Price of a Power Strip: Lee Jun-seok’s Practical Rebellion Against the Status Quo

November 06, 2026

Imagine a standard office power strip—the kind you buy for your home office for about $36 (50,000 KRW). Now, imagine that same plastic strip being sold to a government office for $144 (200,000 KRW). In a small booth at a legislative fair inside the National Assembly, Rep. Lee Jun-seok, the leader of the New Reform Party (a centrist splinter party), held up these two identical items. It was a visual indictment of a procurement system that Lee argues has become a sieve for taxpayer money.

To the uninitiated, a debate over office supplies might seem trivial. But in the context of South Korean politics, where the ruling Democratic Party holds a massive majority and the main opposition People Power Party (the conservative party formerly led by Lee) is struggling to find its footing, this "politics of the mundane" is Lee Jun-seok’s latest weapon. By focusing on the "Narajangteo" (the Korea ON-line E-Procurement System, or KONEPS) and its inflated prices, Lee is signaling a shift away from the grand ideological battles that usually dominate Seoul’s news cycle, moving instead toward a practical, "common sense" reformist agenda.

However, Lee’s activities on November 5 were not limited to hardware. He also pivoted to deliver a scathing warning to the ruling Democratic Party, cautioning them that their current legislative dominance could lead to the same ruin that befell the previous administration. In a political landscape still reeling from the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, Lee’s words carry the weight of someone who has seen the inside of the conservative machine—and watched it explode.

The KONEPS Paradox: Why Taxpayers Pay Quadruple

The "Narajangteo" system, managed by the Public Procurement Service, was designed to be a transparent digital marketplace where government agencies could buy everything from pencils to fighter jet components. In theory, it eliminates corruption by digitizing the bidding process. In practice, Lee Jun-seok argues, it has created a sheltered "cartel" where vendors can charge exorbitant prices simply because the buyer is a public entity using "blood tax" (a common Korean term for taxpayer money).

During the New Reform Party’s legislative fair, Lee and Supreme Council member Lee Gi-in demonstrated how the system fails. When an item is registered on the state procurement portal, it often becomes immune to the price competition of the open market. Because government officials are often required to buy through this portal to satisfy audit requirements, they end up paying four times the retail price for basic electronics.

This isn't just about a few power strips. The public procurement market in Korea is worth tens of billions of dollars annually. If a 400% markup is even remotely common across the board, the scale of the "tax leak" is astronomical. For Lee, this is the perfect issue: it appeals to the fiscally conservative base of the opposition, the tech-savvy youth who are sensitive to market prices, and the general public frustrated by the high cost of living. By bringing physical products to the National Assembly, Lee bypassed abstract economic data and went straight for a "show, don't tell" approach that resonated across social media.

A Warning to the Ruling Majority: The Ghost of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol

While Lee used the procurement issue to showcase his practical side, he used the Democratic Party’s push for a "Special Counsel Act on Fabricated Indictments" to showcase his political intuition. The Democratic Party, the current ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, is moving aggressively to pass legislation that would investigate the prosecution for allegedly "fabricating" cases against their leaders.

Lee Jun-seok’s response was a sharp reminder of Korea’s recent history. He warned the Democratic Party that "unbridled power eventually destroys itself." He drew a direct parallel to the era of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose administration collapsed in a spiral of martial law declarations and constitutional crisis, leading to his impeachment and current incarceration. Lee pointed out that those who were once "sycophants" to Mr. Yoon’s power were swept away the moment the tide turned.

His critique is that the Democratic Party is currently acting with the same "arrogance of power" that Mr. Yoon did. By using their legislative majority to push through special counsel bills that target the judicial system, Lee argues they are inviting a massive public backlash in the next election. He even cited the political shift in the American South, where a once-dominant party lost its grip because it stopped listening to the people, as a warning that the Democratic Party’s stronghold in the Gyeonggi South region (the "semiconductor belt") is not guaranteed.

For a foreign audience, this highlights the extreme volatility of Korean politics. The transition from a "ruling" party to a "disgraced" one can happen with breathtaking speed. Lee Jun-seok, who was purged from the People Power Party during Mr. Yoon’s reign, is now positioning himself as the only politician with the foresight to warn the new winners about the old mistakes.

Strategic Footprints in the Semiconductor Belt

Lee’s warnings are backed by tactical maneuvers on the ground. A key development is the activity of Rep. Lee Ju-young, a member of Lee’s New Reform Party, who is preparing to open a local office in the Suwon-Jung district. Specifically, she is targeting the Maetan-dong area near Ajou University Hospital.

Suwon-Jung is not just any district; it is the heart of Gyeonggi South, an area populated by young IT professionals, researchers, and healthcare workers. This is the "swing" demographic of Korea—voters who are less concerned with the old regional rivalries of the past and more concerned with healthcare policy, education, and the tech economy. By establishing a physical presence here, the New Reform Party is attempting to move from being a "party of the internet" to a "party of the neighborhood."

This geographic strategy is essential for Lee Jun-seok’s long-term survival. In the Korean electoral system, small parties often struggle to win local districts, usually relying on proportional representation. If the New Reform Party can flip a seat in the "semiconductor belt," it would prove that there is a viable "Third Zone" in Korean politics that can break the duopoly of the Democratic Party and the People Power Party. Lee’s frequent mentions of US political dynamics suggest he is looking for a way to "realign" the Korean electorate, moving toward a coalition based on generational and professional interests rather than traditional party loyalty.

Tax Justice and the "New Weapon" Politics

Finally, Lee has extended his focus to the concept of "Tax Justice." He recently took aim at Jigwang, a prominent figure who recently resigned from a foundation board amidst allegations of significant tax arrears. Lee has called for the National Revenue Service to strengthen its disclosure systems, arguing that high-profile individuals and those with social influence should not be allowed to evade taxes while ordinary citizens struggle.

This is part of a broader strategy that political analysts call "New Weapon Politics." Instead of fighting on the battlefields chosen by the big parties—such as constitutional amendments or grand foreign policy—Lee picks "small" issues that have high symbolic value. Tax evasion by the elite and the $144 power strip are two sides of the same coin: they both represent a system where the "insiders" benefit at the expense of the "outsiders."

By framing himself as the guardian of the "blood tax," Lee is attempting to reclaim the mantle of "true" conservatism—one that is focused on fiscal responsibility and the rule of law—while distancing himself from the "corrupt" or "incompetent" labels that have dogged the People Power Party since the fall of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. Whether he can convince the traditional conservative base in places like Daegu to join his "New Reform" movement remains the biggest question in his political career.

What to Watch Next

  • The Audit of the Public Procurement Service: Watch for whether Lee Jun-seok brings more "hardware" to the National Assembly Finance Committee. If he can force a formal investigation into KONEPS pricing, it will be a major win for his "practical reform" brand.
  • The Special Counsel Act Stalemate: As the Democratic Party pushes the Special Counsel Act on Fabricated Indictments, pay attention to whether Lee Jun-seok’s New Reform Party votes with the opposition (People Power Party) or tries to negotiate a third way. This will reveal his current leverage in the National Assembly.
  • The "Yeongtong-gu" Experiment: Rep. Lee Ju-young’s success in establishing a base in Suwon will be a litmus test. If the New Reform Party’s polling in the Gyeonggi South region begins to rise, it may trigger a "Third Zone" movement that could reshape the 2027 presidential landscape.
  • Conservative Realignment: Look for signals from the Daegu and Gyeongbuk political circles. As the People Power Party continues to struggle, the calls for a "conservative union" led by Lee Jun-seok may grow louder, though Lee will likely demand a total purge of the "Yoon Suk-yeol loyalists" before agreeing to any merger.
