The Cracks in the Foundation: Cracking Down on South Korea’s Real Estate Fraud

November 11, 2026

For the average South Korean, the dream of homeownership has long been synonymous with patience—years of dutifully building up "subscription points" (a government-mandated scoring system that prioritizes those who have lived without a home for years) and carefully navigating strict mortgage regulations. But while honest citizens have been playing by the rules, a sprawling underground network has been systematically gaming the system. Recent data from the National Office of Investigation (the primary investigative body under the National Police Agency) reveals a staggering reality: over the past year, authorities have caught 3,565 individuals engaged in widespread real estate crimes, from price-fixing syndicates to fraudulent housing applications.

This isn't just about a few bad actors; it is a fundamental assault on housing equity. The Lee Jae-myung administration, having made housing stability a core pillar of its policy agenda, has responded by extending this special crackdown for another year. As the government transitions into its third phase of intensive investigations, it is clear that the goal is no longer just to stop individual crimes, but to dismantle the culture of "real estate infallibility" that has skewed the market for decades.

A Deep Dive into the 3,565 Arrests

The scale of the crackdown is unprecedented, with 3,565 suspects hauled in for crimes ranging from illicit land speculation to unauthorized real estate brokering. Perhaps most telling is that 25% of these cases involved illegal agricultural land speculation. In South Korea, there is a constitutional principle known as "land to the tiller," which mandates that only those actively farming can own agricultural land. Yet, the investigation uncovered a persistent pattern of urban dwellers—investors with no intention of farming—buying up farmland using shell names or subdividing plots to flip them for massive profits.

This speculation is more than a legal violation; it is a parasitic drag on the economy. By treating farmland as a speculative asset, these individuals drive up costs for actual farmers and drive out young people looking to start their careers in agriculture. Furthermore, the authorities targeted "price-fixing" operations—where groups of residents in online chat rooms conspire to keep property listings above a certain price—and "false registration" scams, where buyers report a high-price sale to the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (the government agency overseeing housing policy) to artificially inflate market value, only to cancel the contract once the public record has been deceived.

The Erosion of the Subscription System

Perhaps the most infuriating aspect of these revelations is the 354 cases of fraudulent housing subscriptions. The Korean apartment subscription system is designed to provide a "housing ladder" for those most in need. However, the investigation found 334 cases of "fake address registration," where applicants moved their official residence to a high-demand area to gain priority, despite living elsewhere. In one egregious instance, a person who already owned three homes had their child register a fake address to pretend they were a "homeless" applicant, successfully winning a new apartment allotment.

Equally scandalous was the behavior of high-income professionals. In several instances, dual-income physician couples were caught "faking resignation" from their jobs—simply to qualify for the special supply quotas intended for lower-income, newlywed couples. By falsifying income records, these wealthy individuals stripped opportunities away from families who truly needed the support. These aren't just administrative errors; they are calculated, predatory acts that prioritize greed over the social contract.

The Third Phase: A Broader, Sharper Net

With the announcement that the special investigation will continue for another year, the National Office of Investigation is shifting its focus to the "structural" side of these crimes. The third phase of the crackdown will target 10 core sectors, including construction site corruption, large-scale development schemes, and the illicit activities of "mobile brokers" (informal agents who gather and sell information on high-value properties).

The government's decision to extend this crackdown sends a powerful signal: the era of lax enforcement is over. By focusing on the money trail and the organizations behind these crimes, the police aim to do more than just levy fines—they want to ensure that the profits from these crimes are clawed back. This is a vital step in restoring trust in a market that has been so heavily manipulated that even statistical data has become suspect.

What to Watch Next

For those looking to enter the market or participate in housing subscriptions, the landscape has fundamentally changed. Here is what to watch for in the coming months:

  • Increased Administrative Scrutiny: Expect the government to tighten the cross-referencing of health insurance and income data for all subscription applicants. Any discrepancy is now highly likely to trigger a criminal investigation.
  • Market Normalization: With authorities keeping a close eye on "contract cancellations" following high-price filings, the practice of faking sales to drive up neighborhood prices will likely become much riskier. If you see a sudden spike in a specific apartment complex’s price without a corresponding surge in actual transactions, treat it with extreme skepticism.
  • Legal Consequences: As the administration tightens the screws, we should expect to see more "zero-tolerance" sentencing. This includes not just fines, but the permanent revocation of the right to apply for future housing, a penalty that can effectively blacklist an individual from the primary market for a decade or more.

While the shadow of the past—including the systemic issues that persisted during the years under the previous administration, now characterized by the imprisonment of the disgraced former leader, the individual known as the "civilian" Yoon Suk-yeol—still hangs over the nation's real estate, these new, aggressive measures represent a necessary correction. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, the goal is simple: ensure that the housing market functions for the people who live in the homes, not the syndicates that profit from the scarcity of the dream.
