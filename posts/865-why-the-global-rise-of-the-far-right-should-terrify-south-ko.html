Why the Global Rise of the Far-Right Should Terrify South Korea’s Progressive Government

October 09, 2026

If you look at the current state of global politics, it feels as if the machinery of democracy is grinding to a halt. In Brazil, President Lula da Silva’s administration is struggling to find its footing after electoral setbacks. Across Europe, far-right movements are surging in nations where the political Left held power for decades. It is far too convenient to dismiss this trend as a passing fad or a generic "conservative wave."

The hard, uncomfortable truth is that the rise of the far-right is a reaction to the incompetence and perceived failures of progressive governments that, once in power, failed to meaningfully alter the lives of ordinary citizens. South Korea is not immune to this tide. With the Democratic Party of Korea (the country’s main liberal party) now in power under President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea can no longer afford to watch the crises in Brazil and Europe as a distant spectator. The warning signs are already at our door.

The Failure of the Left: Lessons from Brazil

The struggles of Brazil’s Lula reflect a haunting lesson he himself articulated back in 2017: "The failure is not theirs; it is ours." Lula argued that while left-wing movements are often adept at winning elections by championing democracy, they frequently become subservient to their supposed enemies once they hold the reins of power. Caught in the pressure of the media, financial systems, and the demands of conservative intellectuals and corporations, these governments often alienate the very base that brought them to office.

In South Korea, the reality for the Lee Jae-myung administration mirrors this dynamic. Winning an election is not synonymous with reforming a nation. If the government succumbs to the pressures of conservative media, the judicial establishment, and massive corporate interests, it risks turning its most loyal supporters from disappointed bystanders into an angry opposition. When a government stalls on reform to appease the establishment, the rot begins from the roots.

Furthermore, Brazil’s experience shows that half-measures lead to disaster. Lula’s return to power has been hampered by his inability to dismantle the deep-seated corruption within the judiciary and the political class. When a progressive administration fails to cut out the cancer of corruption—as seen in Brazil’s Petrobras scandal—it loses its moral authority. The public, feeling that "all politicians are the same," eventually turns to populist, far-right alternatives. This is a law of political physics: if the Left fails to clear the path of inequality and corruption, that failure becomes the fertilizer for the far-right.

The British Blueprint: A Move Away from Neoliberalism

Is there a way for progressive governments to survive this hostile landscape? The recent moves by the British Labour Party offer a potential roadmap. A prominent figure within the party, Burnham, recently declared that "the status quo cannot be the goal of politics," signaling an end to the era of privatization and deregulation that has dominated Western governance since the days of Margaret Thatcher.

Burnham’s approach is refreshingly concrete. Recognizing that housing is a fundamental human right rather than just a commodity, the party has committed to a massive expansion of public housing owned by local governments. By banning the sale of these units to the private sector—a policy that exacerbated the UK’s housing crisis—they aim to create a permanent buffer against the speculative volatility of the private market.

For South Korean youth, who face the crushing burden of Seoul’s astronomical housing costs and systemic "jeonse" (long-term deposit rental) fraud, this is a vital lesson. Relying solely on market mechanisms to solve housing crises has proven to be a failure. Without the state taking the lead in housing supply, welfare policies simply result in higher household debt.

Beyond housing, the British Labour approach includes reclaiming public control over essential resources like water and energy. If private companies prioritize profit over the fundamental needs of the people, the state is prepared to renationalize these systems. Furthermore, they are launching a "National Care Service," fully funded by taxes and provided free at the point of use. This demonstrates that true welfare is not found in rhetoric, but in the institutional commitment of state budgets.

The Trap of Performative Politics and PC Culture

To understand why the far-right is gaining ground in Canada, Europe, and the U.S., we must address the "elephant in the room": the perceived arrogance of the progressive elite. In many Western nations, progressive parties have become synonymous with "political correctness" (PC) at the expense of addressing the material struggles of the working class.

When parties obsess over ideological purity or identity-based policies while the average citizen struggles with rising costs of living, crime, or job insecurity, they lose their connection to reality. The public is not necessarily turning against democratic values; they are turning against a political class that views their concerns as "prejudiced" or "backward."

South Korea’s 20-something generation is watching the Democratic Party with a similarly cold gaze. They often perceive the party not as a champion of reform, but as an entrenched establishment that has enjoyed power for decades. When party members are perceived as avoiding real reform to protect their own interests, they are viewed no differently than their conservative counterparts in the People Power Party. If the Democratic Party continues to prioritize image over substance, it will inevitably face the same backlash that has humbled progressive movements globally.

What to Watch Next: The Test of Reform

South Korea stands at a critical juncture. Following the impeachment and imprisonment of the former leader, Yoon Suk-yeol, the Lee Jae-myung administration carries the heavy responsibility of charting a new course. The question remains: does the Democratic Party have the genuine will to complete the necessary reforms, or will it remain trapped in the cycle of political compromise?

Key markers for the coming months:

  • Internal House Cleaning: Watch how the Democratic Party handles internal figures who act as obstacles to reform. If the party continues to harbor "migratory" politicians who prioritize personal survival over the party's platform, it will lose its identity and its mandate.
  • Concrete Economic Policies: Look for whether the government shifts from vague slogans to tangible interventions—specifically in housing and public services—that directly alleviate the burden on low-income families and the youth.
  • Judicial and Media Reform: These are not optional tasks; they are essential for the survival of the current administration. Any sign of "cooperative" politics with the establishment will be interpreted as a betrayal by the party’s base.

The era of being the "underdog" is over for the Democratic Party; it is now the establishment. If it cannot prove its ability to deliver substantial, life-changing reforms, it will eventually face the same judgment it once leveled at its opponents. Reform is painful and invites resistance, but it is the only path that ensures the survival of both the government and the democratic process itself.
