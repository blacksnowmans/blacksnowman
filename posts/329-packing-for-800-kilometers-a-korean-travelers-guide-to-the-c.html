Packing for 800 Kilometers: A Korean Traveler's Guide to the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

Six years ago, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic shattered a long-held plan: walking the Camino de Santiago. To soothe the disappointment at the time, I shaved my head and walked South Korea’s Jeju Olle trail instead. That memory remains vivid, but this time is different. I am on the verge of taking my first steps on the French Way, an approximately 800-kilometer trek starting from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in France and ending at the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela in northwestern Spain.

Packing bags the night before departure always brings a mix of nervous laughter and quiet disbelief ("We leave tomorrow... this is huge, haha"). Yet beneath the anxiety lies a profound sense of anticipation. The Camino is much more than a long walk; it is a therapeutic journey across hundreds of kilometers designed to confront personal limits and recover a sense of inner peace lost in daily life. Yet behind the romantic imagery lie very physical barriers: blisters, muscle aches, and unpredictable weather. Preparing for this trek is an exercise in realism, an inventory of the gear meant to sustain a traveler over nearly 500 miles.

The Geometry of Survival: Packs, Bags, and Over-Tourism

The backpack is an extension of the pilgrim's body. For this journey, I pulled out a 36-liter Deuter pack, a German brand well-known to hikers, which I also used during my Jeju Olle trek. Its proven durability inspires confidence, but the real art lies in weight distribution and organization.

At the very bottom of the pack rests a lightweight sleeping bag from Naturehike (model NH LW180), a staple brand for budget-conscious travelers. September in northern Spain brings sharp temperature drops, making a sleeping bag non-negotiable when sleeping on the cold stone floors of public pilgrim hostels, known as albergues. Weighing just 700 grams (about 1.5 pounds), it serves as a portable sanctuary in unfamiliar territory.

The Shadow of Over-Tourism

While the transformation of the Camino de Santiago into a global path of recovery is a welcome phenomenon, it has brought severe consequences. Local infrastructure is overburdened, and litter has become a pressing issue along the routes. The weight in a pilgrim's pack must include a sense of responsibility for the ground they cover. Relying on disposable items for convenience runs counter to the spirit of the trail; minimizing gear is the true mark of a sustainable pilgrim.

When it comes to clothing, the cardinal rule is avoiding greed. My wardrobe is pared down to the absolute minimum: two long-sleeve shirts, two pairs of long pants, two pairs of socks, and two sets of underwear. This combination is optimized for alternating daily washes. Layered with a lightweight padded jacket and a windbreaker, it is enough to withstand volatile mountain weather.

To maintain communal harmony in packed hostel dormitories, every garment is sorted inside mesh laundry pouches. Rustling through plastic bags in the dark is a quick way to disturb sleeping roommates. Pre-sorting gear preserves precious sleep and ensures a rapid departure before dawn.

Strategic Weatherproofing and First-Aid Infrastructure

One of the most crucial late additions to my pack is a 170-gram poncho. While I initially planned to rely solely on a windbreaker, veteran pilgrims stress that sudden downpours on the trail are dangerous variables that can quickly lead to rapid heat loss and hypothermia. A proper poncho covers both the walker and the backpack entirely.

Alongside lightweight sandals for resting tired feet at the end of the day, I packed a traditional string market bag gifted by my mother. It folds down to nothing yet proves invaluable when buying groceries in local village shops. Everyday domestic resourcefulness often outshines high-end mountaineering gear on the trail.

First-aid supplies are strictly portioned into airtight zipper bags. Beyond basics like Tylenol, digestive medicine, and anti-diarrhea pills, the kit includes sewing needles and thread for draining blisters, Tiger Balm, and medicated patches. Earplugs and an eye mask are equally vital; snoring in shared albergue rooms is an inescapable reality. Small touches—such as safety pins used instead of bulky clotheslines, and character-printed tissues gifted by friends—occupy the corners of the pack, offering small comforts amid physical exertion.

Carrying the Goodwill of Friends and Family

The most important item on any long journey is not manufactured gear, but state of mind. A month before departure, after realizing my passport had expired despite my initial confidence, a sharp-witted friend named Jun-young intervened just in time for me to secure a fresh replacement.

Gifts from other friends carry heavy sentimental weight. Heon gave me an Apple AirTag to track the bag—my first truly high-tech accessory. Yerin attached a secure strap to my phone to prevent drops, and Sion supplied protein bars. These tokens of support will act as fuel during the 800-kilometer trek.

A card containing pocket money from acquaintances carried a note explaining that 10% of the purchase value went toward treating children with pediatric cancer. Knowing that goodwill is embedded in these funds makes spending them feel sacred. Someone else’s benevolence helps power my steps, while offering hope for a life elsewhere. On this path, no one walks entirely alone.

An Editor's Reflection: The time that stopped six years ago has begun to flow again. Packing for this journey drives home how little we actually need to survive and move forward. Trimming the weight of a backpack is an exercise in shedding the unnecessary weight of daily life. Some might call it reckless, but the recklessness of youth transforms into the fondest memories of later years. I hope this walk is a time to move healthily, observe deeply, and encounter my true self. If these notes serve as a small lantern for anyone else dreaming of Santiago, that is more than enough.

What to Watch Next

As I finalize my departure with 200 euros in cash tucked away—and joke about keeping it safe from pickpockets—it is finally time to step onto the world’s longest walking path. Over the coming weeks, the focus will shift from preparation to execution. Observers of overseas travel trends and adventure tourism should watch for how European pilgrimage routes manage the mounting pressure of post-pandemic tourist surges, and whether stricter regulations on sustainable trail usage are implemented in rural Spain.

For now, the packs are closed, the goodbyes are said, and the road from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port awaits.
