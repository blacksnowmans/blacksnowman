Internal Strife and Shifting Power: The Friction Between South Korea’s President and the Gyeonggi Governor

October 13, 2026

Politics in South Korea is a volatile ecosystem, defined as much by shifting alliances as it is by policy. Recently, the relationship between South Korean President Lee Jae-myung—a former leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's main liberal party—and Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae has become the epicenter of a widening political fracture. To the casual observer, their disputes appear to be about budget allocations, but digging deeper reveals a complex web of generational power dynamics, the influence of aggressive online support bases, and a struggle over the future direction of the liberal movement.

The Rise of "New Lee" and the Fragmentation of Support

The DPK’s internal cohesion is currently fraying. For years, the party’s support base was anchored by "Gaettal" (a term for die-hard, largely younger female supporters of Lee Jae-myung). However, that community has undergone a radical transformation. Following internal controversies involving former party figures, a new, more militant wave of supporters has risen to fill the void, often dubbed the "New Lee" faction.

Tensions have reached a breaking point between these newer, online-community-based supporters and the party's traditional base. The situation was exacerbated on October 11 when President Lee Jae-myung personally retweeted a post from a prominent, aggressive social media user that used derogatory language to describe Governor Choo Mi-ae. This validation from the highest office in the land effectively signaled that open vitriol against perceived dissenters within the party is acceptable.

The impact has been profound. Even senior DPK figures, such as Supreme Council member Kim Min-seok, have been forced into uncomfortable positions, attempting to appease these aggressive online factions while struggling to maintain a coherent party platform. When the President’s own support base prioritizes toxic, abusive rhetoric over rational debate, the broader health of South Korean democratic discourse suffers.

Misinformation and the War Over Local Budgets

A primary point of contention has been the alleged "slashing" of public welfare budgets in Gyeonggi Province under Governor Choo Mi-ae. Critics—specifically those within the "New Lee" online sphere—have accused the Governor of cutting essential funding for childcare and maternity services.

However, the facts tell a different story. The previous administration had left a "budgetary hole," having only allocated funding for nine months of essential welfare programs rather than a full year. Upon taking office, Governor Choo—a former judge and a veteran politician—identified and eliminated unnecessary event-based expenditures. She redirected these funds to fill the gap, providing a supplementary budget of about $260 million (354.3 billion won) to secure critical services for the public.

Despite this, the "New Lee" faction has framed her fiscal discipline as an assault on the underprivileged. Such attacks often rely on personal smears, suggesting that someone with her background cannot empathize with the poor. These narratives ignore Choo’s own history as the daughter of a tailor in Daegu, and highlight how easily political factions can weaponize misinformation to undermine allies who do not fully align with the current leadership's agenda.

Power Dynamics and Long-standing Complications

To understand the friction between President Lee and Governor Choo, one must look at the hierarchy of the DPK. For years, Choo Mi-ae has been a titan within the party—a senior figure who served as the Minister of Justice and played a pivotal role in the liberal movement long before Lee became President.

There is a palpable psychological tension at play. Despite Lee’s ascension to the presidency, Choo remains a figure who does not defer to him or hesitate to provide blunt criticism, such as her opposition during the appointment process of Kim Ji-yong as the head of the proposed Investigation Bureau. For a President who has navigated a long and often hostile path to power, Choo’s independence appears to be a source of personal discomfort. What should be a professional evaluation of policy has devolved into a power struggle, fueled by emotional baggage and a rigid sense of hierarchy.

The Danger of "Sidal" Populism and the Path Forward

The current political environment is increasingly susceptible to what some call "sidal" (soda/refreshing) populism—the craving for immediate, emotional gratification at the expense of long-term strategy. Nowhere is this more dangerous than in the discourse surrounding the ongoing trials of political figures. Whenever rumors surface that Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae might accelerate legal proceedings involving President Lee, some supporters cheer for "justice."

This is a strategic mistake that threatens the entire liberal camp. If the judiciary is used as a weapon for political retribution, the precedent set will ultimately be used against the movement itself. The DPK must look back to the values upheld by former Presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in: procedural democracy, common sense, and the rule of law.

The "New Lee" faction’s tendency to view internal dissent as "internal betrayal" and their reliance on smear campaigns are pushing the party toward self-destruction. The path toward restoration requires the DPK to distance itself from the toxic populism currently clouding its judgment.

What to watch next:

  • Party Reorganization: Watch for whether DPK leadership attempts to rein in the more aggressive online factions, or if they continue to cater to them to maintain short-term control.
  • Policy Realignment: Monitor the next provincial budget cycle in Gyeonggi to see if Governor Choo’s fiscal reforms are vindicated, which would force a reckoning regarding the misinformation campaigns leveled against her.
  • Judicial Independence: Keep a close eye on the Supreme Court's handling of pending political cases; any perception of political influence in the courtroom could trigger a national crisis, regardless of the target.

Ultimately, the liberal movement in South Korea stands at a crossroads. It must decide whether it wants to be a movement defined by the loudest voices on social media or one anchored in the principles of democratic integrity. The survival of the party’s long-term vision depends entirely on that choice.
