How AI Disappeared From South Korea’s Future, and What’s Coming Next

October 02, 2026

Every autumn in South Korea, as the air turns crisp and office workers begin drafting their company's business plans for the upcoming year, bookstore windows and YouTube feeds fill with a familiar title: Trend Korea. Published by Seoul National University professor Kim Randoo and his research team, the book has long served as a cultural barometer for Asia's fourth-largest economy. When the latest edition hit shelves on September 30, readers eagerly scanned its forecast for the year ahead. But something strange caught their attention.

For the past few years, artificial intelligence had dominated the list. Last year's predictions were packed with terms like "AX (AI Transformation) organizations" and "human-in-the-loop." Yet in the new report, the word "AI" vanished entirely from the ten core keywords. Readers raised their eyebrows. How could a technology supposedly reshaping everything suddenly disappear from a trend list?

History suggests this is a natural evolution. Back in 2007 and 2008, when smartphones were just starting to flood the market, trend reports were filled with direct terms like "wireless evolution" and "ultra mobile." But by 2009—once mobile devices became the invisible air people breathed—the word "mobile" dropped out of the keywords completely. It had become too fundamental to market as a novelty.

AI has followed the exact same trajectory. After a brief spotlight, it has settled into the background, becoming the default operating system of modern business. No company brags about using AI anymore, because failing to use it means going out of business. The real story for the coming years is not the technology itself, but the psychological, organizational, and social fallout of an economy running entirely on invisible code.

The Fatigue of an Over-Stimulated Society

Walk down a street in Seoul today, and you are unlikely to hear the standard greeting, "How have you been?" Instead, young people are increasingly likely to ask: "Anything fun happen lately?"

The question itself is an admission of exhaustion. South Korea is drowning in an unprecedented wave of listless apathy, despite being one of the most hyper-connected societies on earth. Short-form video apps flood users' eyes with endless dopamine triggers 24 hours a day, yet a profound sense of boredom has taken root. Experts refer to this not as the traditional boredom of scarcity—having nothing to do—but as "busy boredom": a state of cognitive overload where the brain shuts down from too many stimuli.

This burnout permeates daily life, spawning a lexicon of fatigue: people joke about being in an *ultaegi* (looks burnout), *chaetaegi* (book burnout), or *babtaegi* (meal burnout). Studies show that the proportion of adults experiencing clinical depression has crossed the 11% mark. In corporate offices, even as AI drives nominal efficiency upward, employee engagement is plumbing new depths as a handful of workers shoulder the cognitive load of fixing machine errors.

At the same time, this exhaustion has turned interpersonal decision-making cold and transactional. The concept of Life ROI (Return on Investment) has ruthlessly invaded romance, marriage, and education. University students now ask professors point-blank whether they can skip the final two classes and still pass. Young married couples are intentionally delaying official marriage registrations with government agencies to game housing lottery point systems or maximize single-mother financial subsidies. When disposable incomes shrink and the future feels perpetually uncertain, the emotional appeals of romantic sacrifice no longer hold up. Every minute and every won spent must be strictly accounted for.

Subverting Structure in a Low-Growth Economy

With traditional economic growth slowing down, South Korean businesses are finding that superior function alone is no longer enough to open wallets. To break through the stagnation, companies are turning to Form Factor Shift—a strategy of slightly twisting the physical form, structure, or behavior of a product rather than inventing a brand-new technology.

Examples are everywhere in daily life. Shampoo, traditionally sold in plastic bottles of liquid, is being redesigned into solid tiles to bypass airline liquid restrictions and plastic waste. Toothpaste now comes in chewable solid tablets. In automotive design, Kia Motors has introduced PBVs (Purpose-Built Vehicles) that allow the interior structure to shift easily between passenger and cargo modes depending on the user's immediate need. LG’s StandbyME—a portable television mounted on a rolling wheel stand—completely upended how consumers watch content by changing the physical form of the screen.

This desire for lower friction has also birthed the Have-a-Go Economy. Driven by the ethos of Facebook’s famous motto, *“Done is better than perfect,”* lowered execution barriers mean individuals can now accomplish tasks that once required entire departments. Former television writers are using generative AI to independently produce, voice, and market audiobooks single-handedly. Housewives are navigating civil lawsuits without lawyers by leveraging AI assistants. Rather than spending months perfecting a business plan, the winning strategy is to launch immediately and fix things based on real-world feedback.

Yet this culture of infinite archiving—dubbed the Homo Archivarius phenomenon—carries dark risks. Armed with smartphones where storage costs are practically zero, people record, track, and upload every moment of their lives to the cloud. But convenience comes at the cost of privacy. Recently, military personnel using the fitness tracking app Strava inadvertently leaked the classified locations of aircraft carriers simply by jogging on deck. As smart glasses and ambient recording devices enter the mainstream, the freedom to save everything means privacy is evaporating in real time.

Organizational Evolution and the Human Edge

Despite years of pouring capital into AI transformation projects, corporate executives in Seoul face a frustrating paradox: why hasn't overall office productivity visibly accelerated?

Economist Erik Brynjolfsson’s productivity J-curve theory explains the lag. New technology rarely boosts productivity immediately; it often stagnates before a sudden, explosive upward break. Most companies remain stuck in the valley of that curve because their use of AI remains isolated to individual tasks—drafting emails, summarizing meeting notes, or writing PowerPoint outlines. The underlying corporate approval processes remain just as bureaucratic as they were a decade ago.

To break through, organizations must adopt what forecasters call Vibe Walking: restructuring internal workflows so AI agents can collaborate directly across departments, while human workers step back to act as orchestrators rather than manual typists.

Marketing strategies are shifting just as rapidly. With customer acquisition costs skyrocketing and brand loyalty at an all-time low, companies are abandoning expensive customer-poaching campaigns in favor of Re:tention strategies. Rather than locking customers in with aggressive contracts, successful brands build ecosystems so seamless—like Coupang’s hyper-fast logistics model—that users simply cannot imagine going back to a slower alternative.

Simultaneously, shifting demographics are forcing businesses to pay attention to structural life transitions. Generation X is hitting mid-life and entering menopause en masse. Once treated as a taboo private issue, menopause is increasingly being reframed as a natural halftime in a 100-year lifespan. Global corporations are beginning to introduce menopause leave policies, recognizing that physical and organizational support is essential as employees juggle eldercare, children, and career transitions all at once.

Taste Capital in the Age of Synthetic Content

When artificial intelligence can write essays, generate illustrations, and complete 80% of a product design in seconds, what remains uniquely valuable about being human?

The answer, according to the latest Korean trend analysis, is Taste Capital. In modern consumer surveys regarding aesthetic procedures like dermatology or plastic surgery, patients increasingly rank the doctor’s "aesthetic sense" above raw technical skill or medical credentials. Young consumers evaluate a clinic’s interior design and curated art objects to determine whether the practitioner shares their specific taste.

Taste has become a form of capital. In a market flooded with AI-generated mediocrity, the final 20% of a product's execution—what experts call the "human edge"—depends entirely on a trained eye. Developing this taste requires deliberate practice: visiting exhibitions, actively taking notes on why certain works resonate, internalizing cultural context, and refining one's perspective over time.
