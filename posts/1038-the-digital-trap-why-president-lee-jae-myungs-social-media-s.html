The Digital Trap: Why President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Strategy is Backfiring

October 12, 2026

The essence of democracy is communication. Yet, the success or failure of a government often hinges on who that communication is directed at, the language used, and the context in which it occurs. In South Korea, the social media activity of President Lee Jae-myung—a former leader of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the nation's current ruling liberal party—has become a flashpoint for intense political debate. Is social media a genuine bridge between a leader and the people, or is it a detonator accelerating internal divisions? As the political landscape grows increasingly volatile, we must examine the substance behind the noise.

The DPK’s Plummeting Approval Ratings: A Warning from the Public

Recent polling from Realmeter, a prominent South Korean research firm, has delivered a sobering blow to the DPK. The party’s approval rating has dropped for two consecutive weeks, hitting 35.2%. A seven-percentage-point decline in such a short period is highly unusual and suggests that the public believes the DPK, despite holding the responsibility of governance, is drifting without a clear course.

The rise of the Rebuilding Korea Party (a progressive minor party) and the narrowing gap between the DPK and the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), are not merely signs of voters switching sides. They are symptoms of a spreading cynicism; voters who feel let down by the DPK are either looking for alternatives or withdrawing from political participation altogether. Despite the party leadership's pledge to boost approval ratings within their first 100 days, their current silence and lack of substantive response are only deepening the crisis.

Whispers of potential electoral defeat in the Seoul mayoral race have already begun to circulate, signaling that the party’s internal momentum is at an all-time low. Voters are waiting for reflection and renewal, yet what echoes from within the party is a stifling atmosphere where criticism of leadership—including Representative Kim Min-seok—is discouraged. This suppression of debate is hardly the hallmark of a party focused on retaining power.

Presidential Social Media: Direct Communication or Emotional Outburst?

President Lee Jae-myung’s social media usage remains a constant source of controversy. His recent remarks, implying that those who have "lived a life of luxury as high-ranking officials" cannot understand the lives of ordinary citizens, were widely interpreted as a veiled attack on former Gyeonggi Province Governor Choo Mi-ae. Using metaphors like "high-ranking pigs" without naming names is a tactic many critics find cowardly.

While the presidential office’s public relations senior secretary, Seong Ki-hong, argues that the public has a "right to know the President’s thoughts without editorial intervention," the content itself is the issue. If the public requires a team of analysts just to decipher the President’s social media posts, it is not communication—it is a riddle. Social media should be a channel for explaining policy and building empathy, not a digital arena for venting grievances against specific broadcast networks or political rivals.

There is a bitter irony in watching those who once criticized the late President Roh Moo-hyun’s communication style as "lacking dignity" now blindly defending the current President’s social media outbursts. If the President’s posts only increase political fatigue and administrative confusion, they are doing more to foster conflict than to advance his own political agenda. Aides must pivot from enabling these emotional outbursts to crafting clear, persuasive policy messages.

Furthermore, rumors regarding "shadow organizations"—unelected groups allegedly consulted on key messages before official channels are briefed—are deeply troubling. If, as some lawmakers suggest, the President is consulting with non-official entities on sensitive matters, it threatens to isolate him from his own cabinet and the public, undermining the fundamental structure of his administration.

The Judiciary and the "Impeachment" Question

A critical point of tension in the current political climate is the potential for "resumed trials" under Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae. Article 84 of the South Korean Constitution states that "the President shall not be charged with a criminal offense during his tenure except for insurrection or foreign aggression." The core legal debate is whether "charged" covers only indictment or the entirety of a trial process. Many legal experts argue that a trial itself should be prohibited during the presidency.

If Chief Justice Cho attempts to bypass this by pushing forward with trials, it would be an infringement upon both the status of the President and the constitutional separation of powers. Many analysts believe this is a political maneuver by the judiciary to reshape the courts to their liking ahead of next year's general elections. While the judicial system’s potential overreach could theoretically serve as grounds for impeachment, the internecine power struggles and self-preservation instincts within the judiciary make any such outcome highly unpredictable.

The Missing Pillars of Governance

To successfully navigate the remainder of his term and secure the future of his political movement, President Lee and the DPK require three things: tangible improvements in the lives of citizens, a compelling and unified political vision, and the end of internal factionalism. Currently, the administration is struggling on all three fronts.

The DPK must confront why its base is fracturing. The party's failure to address the exodus of supporters toward the Rebuilding Korea Party, combined with a leadership that remains silent in the face of falling approval ratings, reveals a dangerous level of complacency.

Editor’s Note: The most disheartening aspect of this political stalemate is the transformation of communication from a bridge into a weapon. We learned from the era of President Roh Moo-hyun that leadership must be about reaching out, not settling scores. When legal scholars use the Constitution as a shield to protect their own cartels, it forces us to question whether the law truly remains equal for all. It is time for the political sphere to stop using the language of combat and return to the substance of public service.

We are standing at a turning point. Politics fueled by anger and division will never reach a stable destination. As we look ahead, the priority must shift from 140-character barbs on social media to the concrete realities of the people's livelihoods. The question remains: can the ruling party suppress its internal fighting long enough to return to the essence of democratic governance?

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the DPK’s upcoming internal reorganizations and whether the presidential office takes concrete steps to professionalize its communication strategy—or if it continues to rely on the current, volatility-prone model of direct social media confrontation.
