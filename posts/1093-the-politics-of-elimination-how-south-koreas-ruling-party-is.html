The Politics of Elimination: How South Korea’s Ruling Party is Turning Reform into a Tool for Purges

October 13, 2026

Turn on political YouTube in South Korea today, and you will feel a sharp, freezing draft. Figures who were once celebrated as comrades in the great crusade for reform are now being discussed in chillingly martial terms. Across progressive channels and factional meetings, the consensus has hardened: "No more appeasement. It's time to crush them."

On the surface, this is framed as the completion of prosecutorial reform and basic institutional housekeeping. But look closer, and you can sense a quiet, deliberate tightening of the grip—an effort by the ruling camp to stamp out dissenting voices both inside and outside the government with absolute, synchronized discipline. As South Korea navigates this aggressive charge under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, a difficult question hangs over Seoul's political class: Is this the final, necessary phase of democratic reform, or is it simply a familiar, darker shade of the abuse of power?

Targeting the Bureaucracy and the Party Ranks

The first tremors of this hardline turn were felt not in the National Assembly, but in the quiet, dusty offices of regional administration. Shortly after taking office, the new head of the Gwangju Regional Office of Small and Medium Ventures targeted a specific internal department for a high-profile audit. For a routine bureaucratic handover, it was an aggressively unusual move—a signal to the entire civil service that the current government intends to keep the bureaucracy on a very short leash, hunting for any sign of ideological drift.

This intolerance for internal friction extends straight into the heart of the Democratic Party of Korea, the country's liberal ruling party. Senior party figures have begun openly berating any remaining institutional moderation. Kim Min-seok, a key leader in the Democratic Party, took to progressive media to air grievances about prosecutors and officials who have not fallen entirely in line, complaining that his office is flooded with demands from angry supporters asking why internal critics are being left alone. It is an open warning to figures like Prosecutor Im Eun-jung—veterans of the old legal establishment who have not moved fast enough to satisfy the party's base—that their grace period has expired.

This intolerance is rapidly curdling into factional warfare. Hardliners are openly demanding disciplinary action against figures like former Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae, with some party members even circulating signature campaigns, or yeonpanjang—a historically loaded practice of collective political pressure. While billed as a house-cleaning to root out the lingering cultural habits of the conservative era, these tactics increasingly resemble ideological loyalty tests. When a ruling party begins treating internal debate as a security threat, the line between institutional reform and a political purge blurs into nothingness.

Targeting the Opposition and the Judiciary

The pressure campaign is not confined to internal party discipline; it has expanded into a full-scale judicial and political offensive against prominent figures associated with the opposition or critical factions. At a recent parliamentary audit by the National Assembly’s Public Administration and Security Committee, Democratic lawmaker Chae Hyun-il targeted Lee Jong-keun, a former senior prosecutor turned defense attorney. Lee is accused of violating the Attorney Act by representing a key figure in the massive "V-Global" cryptocurrency fraud scandal—a case that Lee had previously supervised while serving as a senior prosecutor under the Ministry of Justice.

What makes this case significant is its trajectory. After previously being closed by police without formal charges, the case was suddenly resurrected through civic group complaints and relentless coverage by pro-government media channels, culminating in a parliamentary audit where lawmakers openly demanded a reinvestigation. The National Investigation Headquarters readily agreed, setting the wheels of justice spinning once again.

The pattern is unmistakable. When legal mechanisms are selectively revived based on political utility rather than universal principle, the rule of law is hollowed out into a weapon of convenience. This dynamic is ensnaring a wide circle of high-profile figures, including independent-minded politicians and commentators like former Minister Choo Mi-ae, Representative Park Eun-jung, and political commentator Rhyu Si-min. On progressive talk shows, pundits no longer bother to veil their intentions, openly declaring that figures like Rhyu "cannot be appeased—they have to be suppressed."

This is no longer standard parliamentary hardball. It is the systematic delegitimization of any voice that refuses to sing from the same hymnal, utilizing the machinery of the state to grind political adversaries into silence.

Centralizing Power and the Shadow of Feudal Loyalty

This urge to centralize control is equally visible in regional politics, particularly in the Honam region—the traditional liberal stronghold in Jeolla province. Rumors surrounding the potential appointment of former South Jeolla Governor Kim Yung-rok as the next presidential chief of staff are frequently packaged as a move toward national unity and cross-factional cooperation. But beneath the surface rhetoric lies a meticulous bid to lock down regional political hegemony.

The architecture of this control was laid out during past local elections and party conventions, where key regional heavyweights forged alliances that funneled support straight into the leadership camp of Kim Min-seok. The appointment of lawmaker Shin Jeong-hoon to head the party’s nomination reform task force is the final piece of the puzzle. By placing a trusted loyalist in charge of the machinery that hands out electoral nominations for upcoming local elections, the party leadership is effectively inoculating itself against internal dissent.

In a healthy democracy, political parties function as melting pots for diverse regional and ideological interests. But under this top-down model, the Democratic Party risks calcifying into a vertical hierarchy where survival depends entirely on proximity to the core leadership. When the nomination process is effectively privatized to reward only loyalists, local democracy withers at the root.

The Erosion of Democratic Norms

Whenever the opposition challenges these appointments, the ruling camp reaches for a familiar, well-worn shield: "the president’s inherent constitutional authority over personnel." It is true that the executive holds broad appointment powers under South Korea's presidential system. But that power was never meant to be absolute, nor was it designed to construct an insular echo chamber.

The National Assembly’s confirmation hearings exist precisely because history has shown that when leaders surround themselves only with those who look like them, think like them, and never talk back, governance quickly curdles into factional rule. Dismissing all criticism as the whining of sore losers does not strengthen an administration; it isolates it.

The path of politics built on intimidation always begins with small, justified-sounding purges—a bureaucratic audit here, a party disciplinary hearing there. But as history demonstrates, a society that demands absolute silence from its critics soon discovers that it has smothered the very vitality that kept it free.

What to Watch Next

  • The Launch of the New Investigative Agency: Watch closely how the newly proposed, powerful anti-corruption investigative bodies are staffed. The placement of key investigative roles will reveal whether these agencies function as independent arbiters of the law or as extensions of the ruling party's internal security apparatus.
  • Upcoming Regional Nominations: Pay attention to how the Democratic Party’s nomination reform task force, led by Shin Jeong-hoon, handles local and regional candidates. This will be the ultimate litmus test for whether internal party democracy has been entirely subordinated to central loyalty.
  • Judicial Actions Against Critics: Monitor whether high-profile investigations into figures like Lee Jong-keun and allied commentators expand into broader indictments, which would signal that the current political pressure has graduated from rhetorical intimidation to systemic legal exclusion.

South Korea’s democratic institutions have weathered deep crises in the past, largely because citizens and lawmakers refused to let the mechanisms of the state be captured by any single faction. As the current administration tests the limits of executive power and party discipline, the ultimate question is not whether the ruling camp can successfully silence its opponents. It is whether the broader public will accept a political landscape where dissent is treated as a crime, and where the fragile art of democracy is replaced by the quiet, heavy atmosphere of enforced conformity.

At the end of a politics ruled by fear, what choices will we ultimately be forced to make?
