The Price of Progress: Seoul’s Shrinking Green Spaces and the Legacy We're Squandering

September 30, 2026

Walking through the dense thickets of high-rise apartment complexes that define the Seoul skyline, a haunting question often arises: will the patches of greenery visible between these concrete giants actually survive to support the future of our children? Behind the scenes, the city is grappling with a controversial policy known as the "Urban Park Sunset System," which has ignited a fierce debate over unchecked urban development. This isn’t just about replacing trees with buildings; it is a profound, short-sighted act of eating the "seed grain"—the natural assets our descendants need to survive—to satisfy our immediate hunger for land and revenue.

The Historical Necessity of Urban Greenery

For millions of years, humans lived as part of the natural world, but the Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions fundamentally trapped us in artificial environments. The 19th century was particularly brutal for the urban working class; in Manchester, roughly 57% of children from poor families died before reaching age five, and factory workers in Liverpool had an average life expectancy of just 15 years. These aren't just grim statistics; they are the price humanity paid for the lack of urban planning. When London’s Hyde Park transitioned from a royal hunting ground to a public space, it wasn't an act of mere benevolence. It was a recognition that nature is a life-support system. Parks are not luxury aesthetics; they are essential infrastructure, as vital as sewage systems or electrical grids.

New York’s Central Park serves as the gold standard for this philosophy. When it was built, the city council famously noted that "if we don't build parks now, we will have to build more mental hospitals later." They understood that in a city without nature, humans wither. Central Park was an engineering marvel—tucking roads underground to protect the pedestrian experience—that provided the psychological and physical sanctuary necessary for Manhattan to eventually become the world’s financial hub. Had New York chosen to build housing on that land instead, it would likely be nothing more than a bleak concrete jungle today.

The Reality in Numbers: As of 2020, the Urban Park Sunset System—a policy that releases long-designated park land from its status if not developed by the city—has removed 15.08 square kilometers of green space across South Korea. Based on official land values, this represents a loss of over $2.1 billion (3 trillion won), and once compensation costs are factored in, the true financial burden to the state exceeds $6.4 billion (9 trillion won). Seoul sits at the epicenter of this crisis. Every time a green space intended for future generations is sacrificed to become "developable land," we are essentially taking out an ecological loan that our children will be forced to repay.

Yongsan Park and the Future of Ecological Infrastructure

South Korea has historically lagged in proactive urban planning. We lack the royal foresight that gifted Hyde Park to London or the 150-year-old master plan that birthed Central Park. However, we have a unique opportunity now that the Yongsan district—long occupied by foreign forces, from the Mongols to the Japanese and the U.S. military—is returning to Korean hands. This 900,000-pyeong (roughly 735-acre) tract of land is not just a park; it is the city's lungs, essential for mitigating the urban heat island effect and creating natural airflow corridors. To discuss this land in terms of "housing supply" is to repeat the catastrophic mistakes of the past.

The government often justifies re-zoning park land for commercial use, hoping to secure profits—sometimes in the range of $70 million (100 billion won)—to fix short-term budget deficits. This is akin to selling off the furniture in your house to pay for dinner. The Seoul Housing and Communities Corporation (SH), the state-owned developer, often cites the need for school sites or infrastructure as reasons to convert green space, but these are often self-serving justifications. Legality does not equal morality; if an administrative process threatens the survival of future generations, it must be halted and put to a rigorous public debate.

The JYP Headquarters Case: A Failure of Vision

The situation involving the land in front of the proposed JYP Entertainment headquarters in Godeok-dong is a prime example of "so-tam-dae-sil" (suffering a great loss while chasing a small gain). The original design for the JYP building was a masterclass in eco-friendly architecture, intended to use an atrium to facilitate natural light and ventilation, serving as a "K-pop mecca" that would have drawn creative energy to the area. Because the administration refused to compromise, opting instead for a rigid, high-density commercial development, the project now teeters on the brink of collapse.

Many believe that simply attracting large corporations will revitalize a city. In reality, the priority must be to create the environment in which those corporations can thrive. The JYP project’s synergy with the local community was dependent on the park adjacent to it. By prioritizing immediate tax revenue over long-term environmental value, the city has successfully stifled a project that could have become a cultural anchor.

Moving Forward: The Role of Private and Public Partnership

The government’s tendency to treat every plot of land as a commodity to be auctioned off for revenue is a relic of state-planned economies. A mature society should instead be one where private capital and local citizens participate in creating spatial value. Rather than the government imposing top-down "social mix" policies that rarely work, it should provide the public goods—like parks—that allow diverse groups to coexist and prosper naturally.

As the old saying goes, "A farmer would rather starve to death than eat his seed grain." High housing prices and budget shortfalls are serious problems, but they are not excuses to cannibalize our future. True, mature leadership looks beyond the next election cycle. If we continue to sacrifice our green spaces, we are not just failing to protect the environment; we are failing to provide our children with the basic social savings they need to thrive. Without these green "savings," the next generation will find themselves living in a sterile, parched city, spending the money we "saved" on their own mental health care.

What to watch next: Keep a close eye on the development plans for the Yongsan Park site and any further amendments to the Seoul City zoning ordinances regarding "sunset" land. If civil society groups begin to coalesce around a "Green New Deal" for Seoul, it could force the city to choose between short-term commercial optics and the long-term sustainability of the capital.
