Anthropic’s $100 Billion IPO: A High-Stakes Test for the Global AI Economy

October 27, 2026

Imagine the tension in a trading room in Yeouido, Seoul’s financial heart, as the news broke across the terminals: Anthropic is officially going public. The ticker symbol "ANT" flashed across screens, signaling that the developer of the "Claude" AI model had filed its S-1 registration statement with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). This isn't just another tech listing; it is a seismic event. If Anthropic hits its target of raising up to $100 billion, it will bypass Elon Musk’s SpaceX to become the largest initial public offering (IPO) in history. For the global markets, and especially for Korea’s tech-heavy economy, this is the moment where the "AI hype" finally meets the cold, hard reality of a balance sheet.

For years, generative AI companies have operated in the shadows of private venture capital, absorbing billions of dollars with the promise of changing the world. Now, the veil has been lifted. Anthropic, widely considered the most formidable rival to OpenAI, has stepped onto the stage of the Nasdaq. The reaction in New York was immediate and bullish, but beneath the excitement lies a set of numbers that should give every investor pause. This IPO is the ultimate "stress test" for the industry. It asks a simple but terrifying question: Can these massive language models actually become profitable businesses, or are they just black holes for capital?

The Audacity of the $100 Billion Goal

The scale of Anthropic’s ambition is difficult to overstate. By aiming for a $100 billion capital raise, the company is positioning itself as the "Emperor" of the AI sector. To put that in perspective, $100 billion is roughly equivalent to the entire market capitalization of some of South Korea’s largest conglomerates outside of the top tier. It is a figure that dwarfs traditional industrial giants and sets a new benchmark for the "Magnificent Seven" era of tech dominance.

The choice of the ticker "ANT" is almost poetic, especially for a Korean audience. In the Korean stock market, individual retail investors are affectionately—and sometimes disparagingly—called "ants" (gaemi). While the ticker refers to the company's name, it inadvertently signals a call to arms for retail investors worldwide to join the AI revolution. However, this is no small-scale venture. Anthropic’s confidence stems from the technical superiority of Claude, which many developers now prefer over ChatGPT for its nuance and safety-first architecture.

This massive capital injection is intended to fuel a "war of attrition." In the world of Large Language Models (LLMs), size matters, and size costs money. Anthropic is not just competing on algorithms; it is competing on the ability to buy up every available H100 chip from Nvidia and secure the massive amounts of electricity needed to run them. By going public now, Anthropic is attempting to lock in its lead before the market’s appetite for AI risk potentially cools.

The Financial Paradox: Explosive Growth vs. Terrifying Losses

When the S-1 filing went public, analysts immediately dove into the numbers, and what they found was a stark illustration of the "AI trap." In the first half of this year, Anthropic recorded a staggering $16.3 billion in revenue. For a startup that didn't exist a few years ago, this is explosive growth. It proves that there is a real, massive market for enterprise AI. Companies are willing to pay billions for the productivity gains promised by Claude.

However, the other side of the ledger is where the alarm bells start ringing. During that same six-month period, Anthropic reported a net loss of $80.7 billion. To put that in terms of Korean currency, that is a loss of over 110 trillion won—a figure that exceeds the annual budget of many mid-sized nations. For every dollar Anthropic earns, it is spending roughly five dollars. This is a business model that, in any other industry, would be seen as a flight to bankruptcy.

Where is the money going? It is being swallowed by the "infrastructure monster." The cost of training state-of-the-art models has moved from the millions to the tens of billions. Between cloud computing fees paid to giants like Amazon and Google (who are also major investors in Anthropic) and the purchase of high-end GPUs, the "cost of goods sold" for intelligence is currently unsustainable. The public market will now have to decide if it believes Anthropic’s promise that these costs will eventually scale down, or if the "loss-to-revenue" ratio will remain a permanent fixture of the AI landscape.

A Shift in the Global Power Dynamics

Anthropic’s move to the Nasdaq is also a strategic strike against OpenAI. While Sam Altman’s OpenAI has struggled with a complex corporate structure—balancing its non-profit roots with a profit-driven arm—Anthropic has maintained a clearer, albeit still mission-driven, corporate identity. By going public, Anthropic gains "clean" capital and a transparent valuation that OpenAI currently lacks. This could give Anthropic a significant advantage in the "war for talent," as it can now offer liquid stock options to the world’s top AI researchers.

This matters deeply for the Korean tech ecosystem. Korea’s current government, led by President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party of Korea, has been vocal about "AI Sovereignty." The administration has pushed for Korean companies to develop their own indigenous LLMs to avoid total dependence on Silicon Valley. However, the Anthropic filing serves as a cold shower for those ambitions. If an American giant with $16 billion in revenue is still losing $80 billion, can Korean firms like Naver or Kakao—or even the hardware giants like Samsung—afford to stay in the software race?

The "Lee Jae-myung government" has prioritized science and technology as the nation's new growth engine, but the sheer scale of Anthropic’s losses suggests that the price of entry into the top-tier AI club may be too high for anyone but the absolute largest global players. This may force a pivot in Korean strategy: instead of building the "brains" (the models), Korea may double down on being the "foundry" and "memory" (the chips) that these money-burning brains require to function. The People Power Party, the main conservative opposition, has often criticized the current administration's spending on tech projects, and these numbers will likely fuel further debate in the National Assembly about how much taxpayer money should be risked on "K-AI" development.

The End of the "VC Party" and the Dawn of Accountability

For the last three years, AI startups have lived in a "liquidity party" provided by venture capitalists. In the private markets, you can sell a vision. In the public markets, you have to sell a path to profitability. Anthropic’s IPO marks the official end of the hype phase and the beginning of the accountability phase. Quarterly earnings calls will now force Anthropic’s leadership to explain, in detail, how they plan to turn an $80 billion loss into a profit.

There is a growing "AI bubble" sentiment in global markets, reminiscent of the dot-com era. Critics argue that we are over-investing in the "supply" of AI before the "demand" has fully materialized. Anthropic’s performance on the Nasdaq will be the primary indicator of whether that bubble continues to expand or begins to hiss. If the stock price sags after the initial IPO pop, it will send a chilling message to the hundreds of other AI startups waiting in the wings.

However, there is a more optimistic view. Some see this as the "Amazon moment" for AI. In its early years, Amazon lost massive amounts of money while building the world's most dominant logistics and cloud infrastructure. Investors who stayed the course were rewarded. Anthropic is betting that "intelligence" is the ultimate commodity, and that once the infrastructure is built, the marginal cost of providing that intelligence will drop to nearly zero, leading to unprecedented profit margins. The "ANT" ticker is a $100 billion bet on that specific future.

What to Watch Next

As Anthropic moves toward its first day of trading, there are three critical factors to watch:

  • The SEC Review: Given the unprecedented size of the IPO and the complexity of Anthropic’s relationship with its major investors (Amazon and Google), the SEC’s scrutiny will be intense. Any delays here could signal regulatory nerves about the stability of AI valuations.
  • The "OpenAI Response": Will Sam Altman be forced to accelerate OpenAI’s own path to a public listing or a massive restructuring? Anthropic has stolen the march on the public markets; OpenAI cannot afford to be left behind in terms of liquidity.
  • The Korean Supply Chain: Watch the stock prices of SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics. As Anthropic prepares to spend its $100 billion "war chest," much of that money will eventually flow into the pockets of the companies that make High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) chips. In a strange twist, Anthropic’s massive losses are, in part, a massive subsidy for the Korean semiconductor industry.

The "AI Emperor" is finally putting on its clothes and stepping out for the world to see. Whether those clothes are made of gold or thin air will be the most important story in the global markets for the remainder of the year.
