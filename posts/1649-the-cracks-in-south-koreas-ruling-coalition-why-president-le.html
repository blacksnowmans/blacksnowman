The Cracks in South Korea’s Ruling Coalition: Why President Lee Jae-myung is Losing His Base

October 29, 2026

Inside the headquarters of the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK) in Yeouido, Seoul's political heart, the atmosphere has turned distinctly grim. Fresh polling data circulating through party offices carries a message that senior leadership can no longer afford to dismiss as mere noise from opposition critics. For months after President Lee Jae-myung took office following the dramatic downfall and imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was impeached for his botched insurrection attempt—the ruling party operated with the momentum of a historic political shift. Today, that momentum is stalling. A growing wave of voter fatigue, internal factional warfare, and an eroding base now threaten to undermine the administration’s legislative agenda well before the next general election.

To understand why this matters to international observers, one must look past the routine turbulence of South Korean party politics. The DPK is not just managing standard mid-term blues; it is confronting a fundamental misalignment between its core progressive supporters and the presidency it elevated to power. When a ruling party’s approval ratings begin to disintegrate among its own loyalists, its ability to push through major economic reforms, manage complex regional foreign policy, and project stability inevitably falters.

A Dead Cross in the Progressive Stronghold: What the Numbers Reveal

In South Korean politics, the fate of the ruling party is inextricably linked to the standing of the president. When public approval of the presidency dips, voters do not typically isolate their frustration to the executive branch; they punish the party in parliament. Recent polling data underscores a worrying trend for the DPK leadership under Party Chairman Kim Min-seok.

Comparing Automated Response System (ARS) polling from mid-September (Sept 18–19) to mid-October (Oct 16–17) reveals a clear negative shift. Overall positive evaluations of Chairman Kim’s job performance dropped from 33.1% to 31.0%, while negative evaluations climbed from 61.3% to 63.4%. While a two-percentage-point shift across the general electorate might seem modest, the breakdown across ideological lines tells a far more alarming story.

Among self-identified progressives—the very bedrock of the ruling coalition—positive approval for Kim’s leadership plummeted by 4.7 percentage points, falling from 45.9% to 41.2%. At the same time, negative ratings among progressives surged by 7.4 percentage points, climbing from 45.1% to 52.5%. This shift represents a classic "dead cross," where negative sentiment outpaces positive sentiment within the party's core base. Meanwhile, support among moderate voters slipped from 34.6% to 33.6%, and conservative approval edged down from 14.8% to 13.7%. When a governing party loses the enthusiasm of its base while failing to win over moderates, its legislative momentum hits a wall.

Toxic Legacies of the Convention: Stigmas, Raids, and Echo Chambers

The roots of this current disillusionment date back to the DPK’s recent internal national convention. Political primary battles are inherently contentious, but the tactics deployed during the race left deep, unhealed wounds within the party rank and file. Winning factions aggressively framed party rivals using extreme smears, labeling intra-party opponents as "factionalists" or attempting to tie them to controversial fringe religious groups like Shincheonji.

While those tactics secured short-term victory for the current party leadership, they alienated nearly half of the party’s broader support base. Rather than offering olive branches or unifying gestures after the votes were counted, the winning leadership doubled down on an exclusionary management style. Worse still, the party leadership stands accused of resorting to high-handed tactics—including targeted search and seizure raids against internal critics and media commentators who voiced dissent.

"Power obtained by demonizing one's political opponents trapped the leadership in a vicious cycle: to maintain legitimacy, it must constantly hunt for new internal enemies."

For many progressive voters, these prosecutorial tactics evoke troubling memories of the heavy-handed governance seen during Yoon Suk-yeol's tenure. By treating internal criticism as betrayal or labeling dissenters as "watermelons"—a derogatory term used by party hardliners to accuse colleagues of being secret conservatives—the leadership has created a climate of fear. This atmosphere has begun to infect the Presidential Office itself. Rather than receiving blunt, objective assessments of public sentiment, President Lee is increasingly surrounded by loyalist aides who filter out harsh truths, dismissing falling poll numbers as bad media coverage rather than a signal for course correction.

The Ghost of 2003: How Liberal Fractures Threaten the Governing Mandate

South Korea’s modern political history offers a stern warning about the dangers of liberal self-sabotage. Modern Korean political analysts often point to the early months of the late President Roh Moo-hyun’s administration in 2003 as a prime example of political miscalculation.

At the time, President Roh chose not to exercise his veto power over a special counsel investigation into illicit money transfers to North Korea that had occurred under his liberal predecessor, Kim Dae-jung. While Roh’s decision was driven by a commitment to legal transparency, its political fallout was catastrophic. It deeply fractured the progressive camp, triggering a split that led to the creation of the Uri Party and leaving Roh with a crippled "quarter-strength government" (4분의 1 정권). Though the liberal camp later managed a temporary recovery during parliamentary elections following a conservative impeachment push, the administration spent years burning political capital trying to repair its broken base, ultimately losing the momentum needed for structural reforms.

The lesson for President Lee Jae-myung today is stark: good intentions and ideological purity cannot substitute for coalition cohesion. When a ruling bloc dismantles its own base through internal purges or tactical arrogance, no amount of executive authority can compensate for the lost political energy. If the DPK allows its current internal feuds to deepen, it risks repeating the fragmentation that crippled past liberal administrations.

From Target of Prosecution to Chief Executive: The Demands of Power

Not long ago, Lee Jae-myung was widely viewed as one of the most persecuted figures in modern South Korean political history. Having survived aggressive prosecutorial investigations and physical threats surrounding the political crisis of the previous regime, his rise to the presidency was driven by a powerful wave of public sympathy and collective defense from the political left.

However, the political math changes completely the moment a candidate takes the oath of office. Lee is no longer the political dissident fighting an oppressive apparatus; he is the head of state, wielding the full authority of the South Korean government. The electorate no longer views him through the lens of a victim requiring protection, but as the executive responsible for economic stability, governance, and national unity.

When a leader in supreme power continues to react to criticism with defensive aggression or grievance, it undermines presidential stature. The public expects a president to govern with magnanimity—to absorb criticism, engage with rival factions, and deliver concrete policy outcomes. Retaining a combative posture while holding the reins of power alienates moderate voters and exhausts even dedicated supporters who want to see effective governance rather than perpetual political warfare.

What to Watch Next: The Rebuilding of the Progressive "Mother Ship"

As South Korea approaches its next major electoral tests, the Democratic Party of Korea stands at a critical crossroads. To regain its footing, the leadership must move from an insular strategy to a broad-tent strategy that restores unity across the progressive and moderate spectrum.

Here are the key indicators to watch in the coming months:

  • Internal Reconciliation Gestures: Watch whether Party Chairman Kim Min-seok and President Lee make concrete efforts to engage internal party critics, halt aggressive disciplinary actions, and tone down the rhetoric against non-mainstream factions.
  • Presidential Office Staffing and Tone: Pay close attention to potential reshuffles within the Presidential Office. Replacing sycophantic advisors with seasoned political strategists willing to deliver candid assessments will be a primary signal that the administration acknowledges its polling crisis.
  • Legislative Strategy on Key Reform Bills: Track whether the DPK uses its parliamentary majority to force through partisan bills or shifts toward building broader legislative consensus to re-engage moderate voters.
  • Base Sentiment in Key Primary Polling: Monitor whether progressive approval ratings for party leadership recover from the current sub-45% levels. A failure to reverse the "dead cross" among core progressives will signal deep trouble for the ruling party’s ground game in upcoming general election campaigns.

Power in a democracy is inherently fragile. For President Lee Jae-myung and the Democratic Party, the recent decline in public support is not an unexplainable anomaly, but a clear warning from the electorate. Rebuilding trust will require more than defensive rhetoric; it will require the humility to listen to critics, embrace former rivals, and remember that holding power demands a far broader vision than acquiring it.
