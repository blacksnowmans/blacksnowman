Power Struggles and Policy Retreats: The Democratic Party’s Identity Crisis

September 07, 2026

Watching the news regarding South Korea's main liberal opposition party, the Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), has become an exercise in frustration. Where once there was a palpable energy to reform the nation and build a better future, there is now only the exhausting spectacle of backroom power-brokering and intense internal factionalism. It begs the question: is this the democracy we fought so hard to achieve?

A single, embarrassing text message leaked to the press recently laid bare the party’s current state. The mere fact that internal discussions revolve around which specific cliques—such as those loyal to Representative Jung Chung-rae—hold the most sway is a devastating blow to the party’s supporters. While staffers scramble to appease power brokers and politicians trade secrets behind the anonymity of Telegram, the party has lost sight of its mandate. This is not the refined art of power sharing; it is a petty, insular scramble to protect one’s own tribe.

The Rot Within: Democracy in Crisis

Factionalism is nothing new for the DPK, but every electoral cycle brings empty promises of "cleansing the factions," only for the party to return to the same status quo. When senior four-term lawmakers are forced to speak out in criticism, it is a clear symptom of a structural failure in the party’s internal democratic systems.

A political party is supposed to be a forum for negotiation, compromise, and the refinement of public policy. Instead, it feels as though the DPK has devolved into an organization where "who you know" matters far more than your convictions. This toxic culture of "line-drawing"—aligning oneself with a powerful patron—is the inevitable result of a vertical, opaque decision-making structure. The growing internal calls for a "one-member, one-vote" system are a desperate attempt to break this cycle of patronage.

The irony is particularly sharp when it comes to the party’s stance on judicial reform. Supporters backed these initiatives under the belief that the DPK would build a fairer society. Yet, when the party is seen as fundamentally unfair in its own internal dealings, its moral authority evaporates. The DPK must realize that the greatest threat to its future isn't the opposition—it’s the internal collapse of trust. When a party stops being a vehicle for public service and becomes an arena for raw power struggles, the public inevitably turns away.

A Warning to the Party

The DPK should stop labeling its internal conflicts as "political pluralism." What we are witnessing is the naked face of factional politics, completely devoid of democratic procedure. True democracy begins with checking those who seek to monopolize power and engaging in transparent, honest dialogue.

Property Policy: A Retreat from the Public Good

The DPK’s recent shifts regarding the Comprehensive Real Estate Tax (a tax levied on high-value property owners) have been deeply disappointing. By rolling back tiered taxation based on residency and maintaining deductions for owners of expensive homes who do not actually reside in them, the party has left many wondering: who exactly are they representing?

Many ordinary citizens who supported the DPK in previous elections are asking why the party’s policy direction seems to favor the wealthy. To put the scale in perspective: for properties with an assessed value exceeding 2 billion KRW (about $1.4 million), the tax deductions alone amount to tens of billions of KRW (hundreds of millions of dollars). Relieving the tax burden on such assets, under the guise of "public livelihood," is a difficult argument for the average citizen to swallow.

Every time the DPK retreats on these policies, the dream of homeownership for the working class drifts further out of reach. When property policy is manipulated to protect the interests of the wealthy, the market inevitably trends toward greater polarization. By folding to pressure, the DPK isn't winning over new supporters—it is merely alienating the base that once believed in its commitment to equality.

Young People, Debt, and the End of the "Jeonse" Dream

In the Seoul metropolitan area, where apartment prices routinely exceed 1 billion KRW (about $700,000), the life of a young person is a bleak equation. Even with a loan of 600 million KRW (about $420,000), managing interest rates of 5% to 8% is crushing. When a young couple spends 3.5 to 4 million KRW (about $2,500–$2,800) a month just on interest, it is nearly impossible to imagine starting a family or maintaining a decent quality of life.

The government and the DPK often talk about "increasing supply," but for most young people, this offers little relief. The current system feels less like an opportunity and more like a silent mandate to live as a debtor for the rest of one’s life. Furthermore, we are witnessing the sunset of the Jeonse system—the uniquely Korean deposit-based rental model. While the media often portrays the decline of Jeonse as a tragedy, it may actually be a necessary correction to a speculative housing culture that relied on excessive leverage.

As South Korea transitions toward a monthly rental-heavy market—similar to Germany or Japan—without robust legal protections for tenants, the Rent-to-Income Ratio (RIR) for young people will likely soar past 40%. The DPK’s preoccupation with internal infighting shows a disconnect from this reality. Jeonse is no longer the ladder it once was; it is a time bomb. Acknowledging this reality is the first step toward crafting a future where the youth aren't sacrificed for the sake of the property market.

Editor’s Perspective: The Values Left Behind

I still remember the values championed by former Presidents Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun: the protection of the vulnerable, the oversight of powerful institutions like the prosecution and the media, and the participation of an aware citizenry. It is difficult to find that same spirit in the DPK today. The "art of power sharing" that Roh so deeply contemplated was rooted in procedural legitimacy, dialogue, and compromise—not the "rice bowl" fighting we see now.

It is true that the failures of the Moon Jae-in administration’s real estate policies continue to haunt the party. But rather than offering sophisticated, cold-blooded alternatives, the DPK seems paralyzed by fear of offending the wealthy, leading to a policy retreat that helps no one. If a political party cannot improve the lives of its citizens and chooses instead to protect its own vested interests, it has effectively negated its own reason for existing.

We did not vote for a party that serves as an agent of tax cuts for the rich. We wanted a force that, however imperfect, would reach out to the working class and the youth trapped at the edge of the cliff. For the DPK to recover, it must dismantle its corrupt culture of patronage and return to the fundamental goal of ensuring housing stability for the people. In an era where a $700,000 apartment is the norm, what exactly does the DPK stand for? If they cannot answer that, they may find themselves left behind by history.

What to watch next: Keep an eye on the party's upcoming internal leadership nominations. If the DPK continues to prioritize factional loyalty over policy competence, it will signal a permanent retreat from the values that once made it a viable champion of the people.
