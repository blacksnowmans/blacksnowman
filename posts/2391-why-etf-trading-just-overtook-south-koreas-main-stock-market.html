Why ETF Trading Just Overtook South Korea’s Main Stock Market—and Why It’s a Warning Sign

November 16, 2026

Imagine opening a brokerage app on your commute home in Seoul and doing a double take at the day’s trading tallies. For the first time on record, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) turned over more money in a single day than the entire KOSPI, South Korea’s main stock market and home to corporate giants like Samsung Electronics and Hyundai Motor.

Listen to office workers or everyday retail investors talk about the market right now, and the shift is just as jarring. Nobody is trading tips about quarterly earnings surprises or a manufacturer’s new product line. Instead, every conversation circles the same two anxieties: how far US Nasdaq futures slid overnight, and whether it is time to move whatever cash is left into American index funds or high-yield dividend products.

Last month, average daily ETF trading in South Korea hit 20.381 trillion won (about $14.5 billion), edging past the KOSPI’s 20.36 trillion won (also roughly $14.5 billion) for the first time in history. On paper, that milestone sounds like a coming-of-age story for modern fund investing. Up close, it looks far more like a warning siren for a stock market whose core liquidity is drying up.

An Optical Illusion of Drying Liquidity

At first glance, South Korea’s ETF industry appears to be enjoying a golden age. As of January 12, total net assets in domestically listed ETFs reached 315.68 trillion won (about $225 billion). Globally, ETF assets topped $17 trillion by the end of September 2025 after growing at an average annual clip of 20 percent over a decade, and South Korea’s growth rate has been more than double that global pace.

Strip away the headline assets, however, and the trading-value milestone tells a darker story. During past bull runs, daily turnover on the KOSPI easily cleared 50 trillion won (over $35 billion). Recently, that figure has broken below 20 trillion won to hover around 17 to 18 trillion won ($12 billion to $13 billion), dipping as low as 14 trillion won ($10 billion) on quiet sessions—an evaporation of nearly 60 percent.

Over the same stretch, daily ETF trading actually shrank too, falling roughly 40 percent from around 38 trillion won ($27 billion) to the 20 trillion won range. ETFs did not overtake the main board because fund trading exploded; they overtook it because individual stock trading collapsed even faster. As longtime market watchers in Seoul have lamented, a bourse where index wrappers out-trade the companies inside them starts to look less like a stock market and more like a fund bazaar.

Look at individual stock order books, and the chill is unmistakable. Through a prolonged market pullback, even companies posting respectable earnings have seen their shares slide by double digits in a matter of days. Rather than risk picking single stocks, investors have retreated into sector baskets for protection, while asset managers have rushed to feed the habit. By the end of last year, 1,058 ETFs were listed in South Korea—nearly half the roughly 2,700 listed companies. Active ETFs, where managers adjust holdings rather than passively track an index, surged 66.9 percent in a year to 91.14 trillion won ($65 billion) as of January 2. Across South Korea’s five largest asset managers alone, investors can choose among 42 AI-themed ETFs, 34 semiconductor funds, and five humanoid-robot products.

That convenience creates a vicious cycle. As money crowds into low-fee ETF baskets, order books for individual KOSPI stocks grow thinner. With fewer buyers and sellers resting in the queue, even modest sell orders jolt share prices, scaring away remaining stock pickers. It is as if a grand department store has gone dark across its luxury halls and main retail floors while every shopper crowds into the food court.

How Seoul Became a Mirror of Overnight Nasdaq Futures

One of the clearest symptoms of this hollowed-out liquidity is how tightly South Korean shares now shadow New York. Years ago, if US Nasdaq futures slipped 1 percent overnight, strong local earnings or momentum in chips and autos could cushion the blow in Seoul, limiting the drop or even keeping the KOSPI flat.

Today, traders describe the relationship as a twin market. Even if a Korean manufacturer beats earnings forecasts or signals an industry turnaround, a 0.3 percent dip in Nasdaq futures during Asian trading hours is enough to pull the Seoul index down at the exact same angle. Worse still, South Korea’s stock market closes at 3:30 p.m. When Wall Street rallies in the evening or during regular US hours, Seoul’s shutters are already down, leaving local investors unable to price in the rebound in real time. The result is a lopsided timing mismatch: Seoul absorbs daytime weakness tied to futures moves while missing live overnight rallies.

Why has the domestic market lost its independent footing? Because price discovery has passed from fundamentals-driven active investors to mechanical passive money tracking global index derivatives. Previously, when foreign program selling hit Seoul in lockstep with US futures, domestic institutions and deep-pocketed local investors stepped in to buy the dip, forming a buffer. With local liquidity depleted, that counterweight has vanished, leaving the market exposed to one-way passive flows. Inside Yeouido, Seoul’s financial district, analysts gripe that the exchange has been reduced to an overnight secondary monitor for New York—or, as one commentator put it with stinging exaggeration, that South Korea is trading like America’s "51st state."

A Tale of Two Flagships and the Basket Volatility Trap

Nowhere is the flight of domestic confidence clearer than in the rivalry between South Korea’s two dominant ETF flagships, each sitting at roughly 21 trillion won ($15 billion) in net assets. Samsung Asset Management’s KODEX 200, which tracks South Korea’s benchmark KOSPI 200 index, holds about 21.6 trillion won ($15.4 billion). Mirae Asset’s TIGER US S&P 500 stands just behind it at 20.99 trillion won ($15 billion) as of the 13th.

On the surface, domestic and American index funds look evenly matched. Follow the actual cash flows, however, and the balance breaks sharply in one direction. While KODEX 200 recently attracted around 5 trillion won ($3.6 billion), TIGER US S&P 500 acted as a capital black hole, pulling in 8.38 trillion won (nearly $6 billion) in net inflows. Exhausted by years of the "Boxpi"—a rueful Korean nickname for the KOSPI’s habit of staying trapped inside a narrow trading box—and bruised by repeated selloffs, local investors are packing their capital and sending it across the Pacific.

Even the money staying in domestic index ETFs carries structural risks. South Korea’s equity market is famously top-heavy: at one point, semiconductor giants Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix accounted for 60 to 70 percent of total index weight, though recent market declines have pulled their combined share down to 48 to 49 percent. When basket ETF trading dwarfs single-stock volume, any wobble in one or two bellwethers triggers automatic buying or selling across every stock in the basket at once. Instead of spreading risk, ETFs in a thin market can amplify indiscriminate swings that ignore individual company fundamentals.

Double-Leverage Bets and Covered-Call Shelters

Among the Korean retail investors who have not fled to US markets, trading behavior has split into two extremes: desperate double-down bets and defensive income hunting.

On the aggressive side, money keeps flowing into leveraged ETFs that promise twice the daily return of an index. While below the 10-trillion-won-plus peaks of past bull markets, leveraged funds still drew roughly 4 trillion won (about $2.9 billion) in net inflows last month. Many traders caught in losing positions are turning to "pattern play"—holding spot shares of Samsung Electronics or SK Hynix while simultaneously flipping leveraged index ETFs to scalp short-term rebounds.

Hard data shows how punishing that strategy has been:

  • An analysis of 615 domestically listed Korean-equity ETFs from January 2020 through June 2026 found that retail buying coincided with positive returns just 12.5 percent of the time on KODEX Leverage, 13.8 percent on KODEX KOSDAQ150 Leverage, and 17.2 percent on TIGER Leverage—meaning retail buyers were caught in falling markets roughly eight or nine times out of ten, compared with a 55.8 percent alignment rate for foreign investors on KODEX Leverage.
  • Since the COVID-19 pandemic, cumulative retail net purchases of ETFs jumped from 6.2 trillion won ($4.4 billion) to over 20 trillion won ($14.3 billion), while monthly retail ETF turnover climbed from around 15 trillion won to 60 trillion won ($43 billion)—with 60 to 70 percent of that volume concentrated in leveraged and inverse derivative funds.
  • A study of roughly 136,000 retail brokerage accounts revealed that stripping out all ETF holdings and trades actually improved investors’ monthly returns and risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe ratio). While plain diversified ETFs helped portfolios, overconfidence and holding onto losing leveraged and inverse bets dragged overall returns down.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, risk-weary savers are fleeing into yield. More than 10 trillion won (over $7 billion) has poured into bond and bond-mixed ETFs so far this year. Covered-call ETFs have also surged in popularity. Think of a covered call like a bottle with a cap: the investor owns the underlying stocks (the bottle) and sells away the right to gains above a set price (the cap) in exchange for upfront option premiums paid out as cash. In sideways or choppy markets, that strategy shines, generating monthly payouts near 2 percent; TIGER Dividend Covered Call, for example, posted a one-year distribution yield of roughly 24 percent, topping its category. The catch is that covered calls lag during powerful bull rallies because the upside is capped, and high payouts do not protect principal if the underlying stocks suffer a steep drop.

What to Watch Next in Seoul’s Hollowed-Out Market

Restoring health to South Korea’s stock market will take more than another wave of themed ETF launches. Over the coming months, three concrete indicators will show whether Seoul can break out of its liquidity trap or sink deeper into fund-driven volatility:

  • Whether KOSPI cash-equity turnover recovers: Watch whether daily trading on the main stock board climbs back above 20 trillion won toward healthier historical levels, narrowing the gap with ETFs and rebuilding depth in individual stock order books.
  • Whether domestic institutional buffers return: Monitor if major local buyers—such as pension funds or market-stabilization mechanisms—step back in to counter one-way foreign program selling tied to US Nasdaq futures.
  • Whether retail flows normalize: Track whether outflows into US index trackers like TIGER US S&P 500 cool down, and whether local retail traders pull back from 2x leveraged derivatives that magnify losses and market swings alike.

Until money returns to buying actual Korean businesses rather than flipping index wrappers or hedging against overnight New York futures, ETFs out-trading the KOSPI will remain a symptom of market fatigue—not a cause for celebration.
