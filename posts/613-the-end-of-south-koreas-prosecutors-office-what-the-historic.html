The End of South Korea's Prosecutors' Office: What the Historic Overhaul Means

October 03, 2026

For nearly eight decades, the South Korean prosecution service held a near-monopoly on state power, functioning simultaneously as master investigators, grand inquisitors, and gatekeepers of the courtroom. To outsiders, it looked like a standard legal institution. To generations of South Koreans, it was a terrifyingly unaccountable political weapon—an elite caste whose loyalty was to its own institutional self-preservation above all else. Now, that era is officially over. But as the dust settles on a sweeping structural reorganization, the messy reality of what comes next reveals that changing a sign on a government building is much easier than changing a culture of impunity.

The dissolution of the traditional prosecution system, following years of relentless political friction, has left legal experts and the public staring down a fractured landscape. Rather than a clean break with the past, the transition has exposed deep anxieties about administrative competence, institutional foot-dragging, and whether genuine reform has truly taken root—or if the old guard is simply learning how to wield power under new names.

Two Agencies, Two Attitudes: The Public Face of a Divided Bureaucracy

The contrasting births of South Korea's two new investigative and legal bodies—the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency (SCIA) and the Prosecution Service (responsible purely for indictments and trials)—tell the story of an institution unwilling to let go of its past. While the newly minted SCIA has stepped into the spotlight with grand opening ceremonies and public briefings, the successor prosecution agency has chosen a path of sullen obscurity. It opted for a quiet, closed-door internal transition without even putting up a proper exterior signboard.

This reluctance to face the public reflects a deep-seated grievance within an elite corps that views the loss of its direct investigative supremacy not as a democratic correction, but as a defeat. On the eve of the old prosecution service's final day, prosecutors were reportedly spotted taking sentimental photographs outside their soon-to-be-demolished headquarters. To critics, these scenes underscored a bitter truth: for decades, the organization treated the nation's criminal justice system as a private fiefdom rather than a public service.

Yet, splitting a 78-year-old monolith into independent investigative and prosecuting arms has also brought immediate, practical chaos. Observers and opposition lawmakers have warned of an impending vacuum in public safety. With leadership appointments delayed and staffing shortages rippling through the field, ordinary citizens are left vulnerable to the growing pains of a poorly synchronized rollout. Internal government proposals to maintain large numbers of investigative personnel within the prosecution-adjacent offices have even sparked anger among reform-minded politicians, who ask an obvious question: If the prosecutors keep their staff and digital forensics units, have their investigative teeth truly been pulled?

The Culture of the Inquisitor: Badges of Honor Built on Human Ruin

To understand why a mere structural split is causing such fierce debate, one must look at the internal culture that dominated the old prosecution service for decades. In private gatherings among veteran prosecutors, professional prestige was rarely measured by courtroom eloquence, meticulous fact-finding, or the protection of civil liberties. Instead, careers were built on high-profile, politically sensitive scalps.

It is an open secret in legal circles that senior prosecutors would casually trade boasts like, "I'm the guy who put Hong Sa-hun away," or "I’m the one who led the raid on Cho Kuk's home." These aren't remembered as moments of sober legal duty, but as combat medals earned in partisan warfare. When an organization turns the humiliation of political figures and the aggressive violation of personal privacy into its highest institutional achievements, it ceases to be an agency of justice and becomes a political hit squad.

This distorted heroism explains why targeted investigations and "fishing expeditions" routinely accompanied changes in national leadership. Prosecutors relied on labyrinthine case files and procedural exhaustion to pressure judges and wear down defendants. Without confronting and dismantling this toxic meritocracy, structural reorganization risks becoming a cosmetic exercise where the players simply change uniforms.

A Long, Painful Road: From Roh Moo-hyun to Lee Jae-myung

The formal dismantling of the traditional prosecution system is the culmination of a political marathon that spans multiple progressive administrations. The push began under former President Roh Moo-hyun, whose tragic death following intense prosecutorial pressure turned systemic reform into a generational mission for the liberal bloc. It continued through the presidency of Moon Jae-in and finally reached a legislative conclusion under the administration of Lee Jae-myung.

Yet, this historic victory comes bundled with profound institutional regret. Looking back, even architects of past reform efforts have admitted severe miscalculations. Former President Moon Jae-in famously expressed deep regret over his appointment of Yoon Suk Yeol as Prosecutor General, admitting that the administration had been dangerously myopic in its pursuit of reform. Critics, including prominent commentators, have pointed out that treating reform as a matter of personal trust in a charismatic leader—rather than an ironclad system of checks and balances—was a catastrophic mistake that nearly handed the state over to the very forces it sought to tame.

The lesson learned is stark: reform cannot rely on the goodwill of individuals. It must be locked into rigid institutional constraints that leave no room for executive overreach, regardless of who occupies the Blue House.

The Threat of Reversal and the Need for Judicial Oversight

Reform in South Korea has always carried the risk of a swinging pendulum. If conservative forces return to power in future elections, their playbook is entirely predictable: they will likely attempt to weld the SCIA and the prosecution service back together, restoring direct investigative powers under the guise of restoring "public safety" and fixing administrative gridlock.

To prevent this rollback, structural separation alone is not enough. Simply moving investigative power from prosecutors to a new agency just shifts the risk of abuse unless external guardrails are established. Legal scholars have pointed to international models—such as Germany’s mandatory judicial review of indictments—as essential blueprints for South Korea's future. Without an independent judicial filter to review and screen charges before they reach trial, the risk simply migrates to a new office.

Furthermore, administrative and specialized domains, such as tax and fair trade violations, will require diversified oversight to prevent any single bureau from accumulating unchecked leverage over citizens and corporations.

Ultimately, the dismantling of the old prosecution service is not about revenge against a powerful institution; it is about restoring a basic, boring baseline of democratic governance. Power that is concentrated without oversight will always rot. As South Korea steps into this new legal reality, the ultimate defense against the return of a politicized police state will not be the benevolence of its leaders, but the relentless, unblinking vigilance of an informed public.
