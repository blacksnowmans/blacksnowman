Why South Korea's Young Men are Giving Up on Marriage

September 11, 2026

Walk down any street in Seoul today, and you will see a generation quietly stepping back not just from marriage, but from dating altogether. Turn on Korean television or YouTube, and algorithms are flooded with deep-dives and video essays dissecting a stark reality: the rapid surge of young men who have permanently closed the door on marriage. Dismissing this as a symptom of inflated personal standards or modern selfishness misses the larger, structural earthquake shaking Korean society. Among thirty-somethings, living alone is no longer an unconventional phase; it is the default blueprint for the future. The question worth asking is how marriage in South Korea transformed from an expected rite of passage into such a formidable, high-stakes endurance test. Stripping away the cultural noise and looking directly at the data reveals that opting out is not impulsive—it is the result of cold, calculated survival math.

The Impossible Entry Barrier: Real Estate and Stagnant Wages

Conversations in modern South Korean matchmaking agencies bear little resemblance to the experiences of past generations. When a matchmaker gently asks a female client if she would consider starting out in a modest one-room studio apartment because a male candidate, despite being otherwise stable and well-employed, hasn't managed to secure housing yet, the reaction is often disbelief and blunt rejection. In the era of parents and grandparents, starting small in a cramped room with a briquette stove and slowly working your way up to a better home was an accepted narrative. Today, however, simply buying an older, 24-pyeong (roughly 850 square feet) apartment in Seoul requires anywhere from 600 million to 700 million won (about $450,000 to $530,000). Even after maximizing every available loan—a practice colloquially known as "scraping the bottom of the soul"—a young couple still needs at least 150 million to 200 million won ($115,000 to $150,000) in cash just to cross the threshold.

Consider an entry-level worker earning an annual salary of 35 million won (about $26,000) who hopes to marry at age 33 or 34. Earning 3.5 million won a month, after paying 500,000 to 600,000 won in rent, leaves roughly 2.2 million won. Saving every single won of that remaining amount for 96 consecutive months without taking a single day off for leisure would barely yield 200 million won. The game is mathematically rigged before it even begins. This financial squeeze is compounded by the country's hyper-competitive job market and obsession with higher education. Since the early 1990s, university enrollment rates have soared, trapping young people in classrooms, mandatory military service for men, and years of grueling job preparation. According to data from employment portals like Incruit, the average age of a new conglomerate hire climbed steeply from 25.1 in 1998 to 30.9 by 2018. When men are economically pressured to secure a top-tier corporate job before even thinking about marriage, the starting line is pushed back by years.

Compounding this is the widening chasm between South Korea's conglomerates, known as *chaebols*, and its small-to-medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Data from the Small and Medium Business Research Institute indicates that the average wage at SMEs dropped from 71.7% of a conglomerate worker's salary in 1999 to just 59.4% in 2019. Young adults entering the workforce at an SME face soaring inflation while earning less than half of their corporate peers. The fear is no longer just about personal survival; it is the grim realization that bringing a child into this environment means inheriting a cycle of inescapable scarcity.

The Post-Marriage Reality: Becoming an Economic Engine

For those who manage to clear the financial hurdles and enter married life, the destination is far from a fairy tale. Many married men describe their post-nuptial reality not as a sanctuary, but as an extension of relentless labor. Taking a closer look at a typical household budget for a man earning an annual salary of 60 million won (about $45,000) paints a sobering picture: roughly 1 million won vanishes immediately into mortgage repayments, another million goes toward basic infant care and formula, and household living expenses, insurance, and credit card bills swallow up an additional 1.5 million to 2 million won.

It is common for married men in South Korea to subsist on a monthly personal allowance of just 300,000 won (about $230)—an amount dryly joked to be less than a teenager's pocket money. While a single person earning 5 million won a month can spend that income entirely on themselves, a married person earning the exact same amount often sees every coin funneled back into the household, leaving zero discretionary funds. The moment children enter the picture, expenditures multiply exponentially. Premium English-language kindergartens routinely cost 1.8 million to 2 million won per month. Parents throw themselves into this grueling private education race out of a singular, driving fear: that their child will fall behind while everyone else surges ahead.

By the time children reach middle and high school, private tutoring costs can easily consume up to 2 million won a month per child. Men find themselves transitioning into little more than "money-making machines," navigating their forties and fifties having completely lost their personal identities. This economic pressure intersects with shifting domestic expectations. In a society where dual-income households make up nearly 60% of the total, men are expected to contribute heavily to housework and childcare despite carrying the burden of primary financial provision. When exhausted men return from long hours at the office only to face accusations of not pulling their weight at home, the psychological burnout becomes absolute.

The Shadow of Divorce and the Zero-Sum Game of Assets

South Korea’s divorce rate has climbed to rank among the highest in Asia. While many couples maintain appearances while raising children, long-suppressed conflicts often erupt once children reach adulthood, leading to a wave of "twilight divorces." For a generation that grew up watching their parents' relationships fracture, the threat of legal dissolution is never abstract. Marriage no longer feels like a sacred life milestone, but a potential prelude to bitter litigation and asset warfare.

Under South Korean civil law, property division upon divorce is designed to split assets accumulated during the marriage according to each partner's contribution. In marriages lasting over a decade, courts frequently award homemaking and childcare contributions a 40% to 50% share of the total assets. From the perspective of men who spent decades grinding through the corporate world, this legal framework presents a terrifying risk: a lifetime of accumulated wealth can be halved in a single legal proceeding. Conversely, women often find themselves fighting to ensure their domestic labor is properly recognized. When the legal architecture of marriage resembles a zero-sum game focused on asset protection rather than shared happiness, remaining single becomes a rational defense mechanism.

The Rise of the Single Life and the Late-Stage U-Turn

In the 1990s, remaining unmarried past the age of thirty meant wearing a social scarlet letter—stigmatized as an anomaly or someone with personal flaws. Today, that cultural script has been rewritten. With individual self-actualization prioritized and robust infrastructure built for single-person households, "voluntary singlehood" has established itself as a respected lifestyle. Publications and foreign media alike point to this cultural realignment, rather than just economic policy failures, as the core driver behind South Korea's plunging birth rates.

Yet, an interesting paradox occurs later in life. Individuals who adamantly champion the single life through their thirties sometimes experience a late-stage u-turn in their forties and fifties. Surrounded by friends battling illnesses or facing the quiet reality of aging alone, a profound realization sets in. Human beings are rarely islands, and the existential dread of aging without a companion prompts some to re-enter the marriage market late in life. Complete avoidance of risk, it turns out, is its own kind of isolation.

What Comes Next

  • Housing Costs: Seoul's real estate market remains the single largest deterrent to household formation, requiring targeted public housing interventions rather than temporary subsidies.
  • Wage Inequality: The productivity gap between conglomerates and SMEs must narrow to restore a sense of economic security for young entry-level workers.
  • Legal Frameworks: Rebalancing the psychological and financial anxieties surrounding family law and asset division will be critical to rebuilding trust in the institution of marriage.

Pointing fingers at young men and branding them as selfish or lacking in responsibility is a superficial dodge of a systemic crisis. When an apartment in Seoul is permanently out of reach, raising a child consumes an entire paycheck, and the legal system introduces the constant fear of losing everything in a divorce, marriage stops being a choice and starts resembling a severe penalty. Fixing South Korea's demographic and social crisis requires more than appealing to patriotism or moral duty—it requires fundamentally rebuilding the economic and social floor so that building a shared life no longer feels like walking a tightrope over an abyss.
