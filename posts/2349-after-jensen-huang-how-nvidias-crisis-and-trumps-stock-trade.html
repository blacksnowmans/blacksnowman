After Jensen Huang: How Nvidia’s Crisis and Trump’s Stock Trades Are Rattling South Korea’s Tech Giants

November 15, 2026

Inside the boardrooms of Pangyo and Suwon—South Korea’s equivalent of Silicon Valley—the mood turned from cautious optimism to quiet dread almost overnight. For years, South Korean chipmaking titans Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix operated under an unspoken assumption: as long as Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang was driving the global artificial intelligence boom, demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips would remain bottomless. Huang was not just a corporate executive; he was the charismatic face of a global supply chain that tied American AI design directly to East Asian advanced manufacturing.

That sense of certainty has shattered. Following Huang’s sudden resignation after 33 years at the helm, the global semiconductor market has descended into severe turbulence. For South Korea, the crisis is not merely about a change of leadership in Santa Clara. It has exposed the fragile web of geopolitics, smuggling networks, and backroom political dealings that underpin the hardware powering the AI revolution. As Washington scrambles over trade restrictions, Beijing threatens critical mineral retaliation, and allegations surface regarding Donald Trump’s personal stock trades ahead of regulatory shifts, South Korean tech giants are finding themselves exposed on all fronts.

The Vacuum at the Top of the Global AI Empire

Jensen Huang’s departure abruptly ended one of the longest and most dominant tenures in modern technology history. Under his leadership, Nvidia transformed from a graphics card maker for video games into a multi-trillion-dollar titan controlling the bedrock of generative AI. His exit immediately triggered market wild swings, sending rival chipmakers like AMD and Broadcom higher as investors weighed whether Nvidia could sustain its near-monopolistic grip without its singular leader.

The leadership transition inside Nvidia has quickly devolved into confusion. Rumors surfaced that the board had approached Rene Haas, the chief executive of British chip designer Arm, though Nvidia’s board publicly denied any official contact. Meanwhile, market observers have raised the prospect of Colette Kress, Nvidia’s chief financial officer, stepping up to manage the fallout. Inside the company, critics point to an insular culture cultivated under single-man rule, arguing that the absence of a clear, established successor has exposed internal vulnerabilities.

The panic reached bizarre lengths when a letter claiming to represent an executive mass resignation began circulating online. An investigation revealed the document was a hoax compiled from letters written during OpenAI’s management crisis a year earlier. Yet the fact that such a hoax managed to rattle global markets highlights just how fragile market confidence in Nvidia has become.

For South Korean memory chip leaders Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, as well as the nation’s major telecommunications carriers, this corporate vacuum creates immediate danger. Both chipmakers have tied their capital expenditure and next-generation HBM production directly to Nvidia’s product roadmap. Nvidia’s upcoming earnings report on November 17 is now viewed across Seoul’s financial markets as a critical stress test for the entire global AI investment landscape. If Nvidia’s internal shockwaves slow down its chip rollouts, South Korean suppliers will feel the pain first.

China’s $3.5 Billion Workaround and the Rare Earth Threat

While Nvidia struggles with its internal crisis, the reality on the ground in East Asia demonstrates just how difficult it is for governments to control advanced hardware. Despite aggressive export controls enacted by the U.S. Commerce Department aimed at cutting off Beijing from advanced AI processors, Chinese tech companies and research institutes have continued to acquire high-end Nvidia hardware at a massive scale.

Reports indicate that active AI projects inside China relying on Nvidia hardware have surpassed $3.5 billion (4.6 trillion won). To bypass U.S. sanctions, buyers have leveraged a shadowy network of shell companies, third-country intermediaries, and mislabeled shipments. For Chinese tech giants, running cutting-edge large language models without Nvidia’s graphics processing units (GPUs) remains virtually impossible. The strict sanctions have not stopped the flow of chips; they have simply driven the trade underground, pushing black-market prices to record highs.

Key Takeaway: U.S. export bans have failed to freeze Chinese AI development. Instead, an estimated $3.5 billion in Nvidia-powered AI projects remain active in China through diverted supply lines, proving the limits of unilateral technology blockades.

This widespread evasion has sparked anger in Washington, where hardline lawmakers are calling for an outright ban on all semiconductor sales to Chinese entities. However, Beijing has made it clear that further tightening will carry heavy consequences. Chinese trade officials and state-aligned commentators have signaled that if the U.S. escalates hardware blockades, China will deploy its ultimate leverage: strict export controls on rare earth elements.

Because China controls the vast majority of the global processing capacity for rare earth minerals—materials indispensable for manufacturing microchips, military hardware, and electric vehicle motors—a resource war would devastate global electronics supply chains. South Korean chipmakers, which rely heavily on imported raw materials to feed their domestic fabrication plants, find themselves caught directly in the crossfire of this escalating tit-for-tat.

Trump’s Timing: $5 Million Stock Buys Ahead of Regulatory Shift

Adding a layer of intense political controversy to an already volatile market are recent disclosures surrounding Donald Trump’s personal investments. Revelations have surfaced showing that prior to easing or lifting certain export restrictions on AI chips bound for China last year, Trump purchased up to $5 million (about 6.7 billion won) worth of Nvidia stock.

The timing of the purchases has drawn fierce ethical criticism from market regulators and political opponents. Following the relaxation of those export rules, Nvidia’s share price surged, generating massive returns for early buyers. Critics argue that using executive policy decisions—framed publicly as matters of national security—to influence stock movements represents a glaring conflict of interest.

The controversy has reopened debates in Washington regarding whether senior political figures and public officials should be banned from trading individual stocks altogether. For international market participants, the scandal reinforces a troubling reality: U.S. trade policy regarding high-tech chips is not governed solely by strategic security principles, but can be heavily influenced by political expediency and private interests.

For South Korea's tech sector, this unpredictability poses a major strategic headache. When rules can be rewritten based on political whims, long-term capital allocation becomes a high-stakes gamble. South Korean firms must plan decade-long factory investments without knowing whether Washington's trade rules will shift overnight.

Separating Scandals from Strategy at the US-China Summit

As preparations continue for an upcoming summit between Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping, diplomats on both sides are working to manage expectations. Foreign policy insiders indicate that recent personal scandals and espionage rumors surrounding Jensen Huang will be strictly excluded from the official bilateral agenda. U.S. negotiators prefer to avoid getting bogged down in personal controversies, choosing instead to focus on broader trade and economic balances.

However, leaving the personal drama off the table does not mean Washington is softening its stance. U.S. officials maintain that technology export controls on strategic AI chips remain non-negotiable. The summit is expected to preserve the structural divide between the two superpowers, keeping U.S. hardware restrictions firmly in place while Beijing quietly prepares its raw-material counter-strategy.

In response to the mounting global uncertainty, South Korea’s government has stepped up its monitoring efforts. The National AI Strategy Committee—a presidential advisory body tasked with steering South Korea's technology policies under President Lee Jae-myung’s administration—has convened emergency review meetings to assess the impact of Nvidia’s executive transition and the ongoing U.S.-China technology friction.

What to Watch Next

As the global semiconductor market navigates this period of friction, several key developments will determine the industry's direction in the coming months:

  • Nvidia’s Earnings and Succession Plan (Nov 17): Financial markets will scrutinize Nvidia’s upcoming quarterly report not just for revenue figures, but for concrete details regarding leadership succession and supply chain guidance.
  • South Korean Supply Chain Diversification: Watch whether Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix accelerate efforts to build independent AI memory architectures and diversify their customer bases beyond Nvidia to reduce reliance on a single partner.
  • U.S. Congressional Action on Trading Restrictions: Fallouts from Trump’s stock trade controversy could re-energize legislative efforts in Washington to ban individual stock trading by high-ranking politicians and policy setters.
  • China’s Rare Earth Export Controls: Any further tightening of U.S. chip sanctions by the Commerce Department will likely trigger immediate retaliatory restrictions from Beijing on critical raw materials, directly affecting manufacturing lines in South Korea and Taiwan.

The era of undisputed market leadership under a single vision is over. As political maneuverings overwrite market fundamentals, South Korea’s tech giants must navigate an increasingly fractured global stage where technological excellence alone is no longer enough to guarantee survival.
