The Ghost of Yalta in Shenzhen: Why a US-China-Russia Summit Leaves South Korea on Edge

November 15, 2026

On November 19, in the booming southern Chinese megacity of Shenzhen, three men who control the world’s most formidable nuclear arsenals will gather around a single table. The white-hot spotlight of global diplomacy will fall on U.S. President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin as they convene for an unprecedented trilateral summit on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) forum. They will lock arms to debate nuclear disarmament, the escalating conflict in Iran, and the restructuring of global trade corridors.

Yet, for all the diplomatic theater unfolding in Shenzhen, it is what will not happen that has sent a shiver through foreign policy circles in Seoul. White House officials officially confirmed that there are no plans for a high-profile meeting between President Trump and North Korean leader Kim Jong Un during this trip. The specter of a dramatic breakthrough in U.S.-North Korea relations has evaporated, leaving Korean diplomats to reckon with a far more perilous reality: a high-stakes negotiation among superpowers where South Korea’s security interests risk being bargained away in secret.

For observers outside the Korean Peninsula, it might seem like standard realpolitik—great powers doing what great powers have always done. But in Seoul, the Shenzhen gathering triggers a deep-seated historical anxiety. South Korean diplomats are openly invoking the ghost of the 1945 Yalta Conference, fearing a modern echo of the day Allied powers carved up post-World War II spheres of influence without a single Korean in the room. As Northeast Asia’s security landscape shifts beneath their feet, South Korean leaders face a daunting calculation: how to maintain strategic leverage when the world’s behemoths decide to rewrite the rules among themselves.

Three Superpowers, One Room: The Unprecedented Shenzhen Summit

The announcement from Washington confirming the November 19 trilateral summit marks a watershed moment in 21st-century diplomacy. Never before have the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia sat down in a structured three-way format of this scale. The gathering arrives at a moment of intense fragmentation, where trade conflicts, regional wars, and strategic rivalries have pushed the international order to its breaking point.

The agenda awaiting Trump, Xi, and Putin is breathtakingly broad. Discussions are expected to center on nuclear arms reduction, neutralizing the risk of wider war in the Middle East around Iran, and recalibrating the mechanics of global economic hegemony. For Washington, Beijing, and Moscow, the trilateral format offers a rare venue to forge transactional compromises that stabilize their respective spheres of interest. But for the rest of the world—and particularly for mid-sized powers nestled between these giant states—the meeting carries an ominous undertone.

In diplomatic corridors across Seoul, the summit is being watched with tense vigilance. The fear is not merely that these three nations will fail to reach an agreement, but rather that they will. When superpowers reach compromises behind closed doors, they inevitably trade concessions. If global security is treated as a zero-sum game of spheres of influence, the sovereign interests of smaller neighboring nations are often the first chips sacrificed on the table.

Sidelined Nukes and Chinese Reassurances: Where North Korea Fits In

Heading into the Shenzhen summit, speculative trade in diplomatic rumors had reached a fever pitch over whether President Trump might attempt a surprise encounter with Kim Jong Un. That speculation was abruptly shut down by White House officials, who confirmed that no North Korea-U.S. summit is scheduled. While low-probability wild cards are never entirely ruled out in international relations, the official stance confirms that Washington's current priority lies squarely with the major power dynamics of Beijing and Moscow.

This reality leaves North Korean nuclear diplomacy frozen in place. As talks remain stalled, Pyongyang continues to refine its arsenal in isolation, raising the baseline military threat facing South Korea. Being relegated to an afterthought in big-power diplomacy gives Pyongyang little incentive to return to serious denuclearization negotiations, while creating space for North Korea to deepen its own strategic maneuvering behind the scenes.

China, meanwhile, has been actively working to manage perceptions in Seoul. During recent diplomatic exchanges, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi took explicit steps to brief South Korean officials on the recent visits of Kim Jong Un and senior Workers' Party official Kim Song Nam to China. Beijing was eager to frame these interactions not as a deepening military alliance, but strictly as routine economic cooperation.

However, analysts in Seoul remain deeply skeptical of Beijing's narrative.

"Beijing presents its relationship with Pyongyang as mere commerce, but in Northeast Asia, economic lifelines are inherently political tools," notes one senior diplomatic strategist in Seoul. "China keeps North Korea afloat to use as a buffer and a bargaining chip against Washington, while telling Seoul not to worry."

By keeping North Korea economically viable under the guise of trade, China ensures Pyongyang remains a persistent thorn in the side of the U.S.-South Korea alliance—even as it offers comforting verbal assurances to Seoul.

Realpolitik in Action: $30 Billion Tariff Deals over High-Stakes Scandals

While the trilateral summit dominates headlines, the bilateral track between Washington and Beijing in Shenzhen is moving with equal speed. Ahead of their face-to-face meeting, U.S. and Chinese negotiation teams formally launched their joint Trade Committee to lay the groundwork for economic stabilization. On the table is a tangible compromise: a proposed $30 billion tariff reduction package alongside commitments for expanded cross-border agricultural trade.

For a global economy battered by inflation and supply-chain fragmentation, a truce between the world’s two largest economies is welcome news. But what is most telling is what both sides agreed to remove from the agenda. Sensational intelligence reports and corporate espionage controversies—including foreign media frenzy over Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and alleged Chinese intelligence scandals—have been explicitly excluded from the summit agenda by American officials.

Washington’s decision to quietly park sensitive intelligence and security scandals outside the conference room doors underscores the triumph of cold, transactional realpolitik. When immediate economic relief and trade stabilization are required, even high-profile national security friction can be set aside. For South Korea—an export-reliant economy heavily dependent on the free flow of global trade—this shift toward pragmatism presents a double-edged sword. While reduced U.S.-China tariff friction alleviates market stress, it also demonstrates that principles can easily yield to transactional deals when the price is right.

Seoul’s Countermove: President Lee Jae-myung’s Quest for Diplomatic Autonomy

Faced with the threat of being sidelined, South Korea’s diplomatic establishment has moved into high gear. Senior officials at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, including Vice Foreign Minister Cho Hyun, have voiced strong warnings against any superpower arrangement that resembles a "21st-century Yalta Conference." The message from Seoul is unambiguous: South Korea’s security sovereignty and the strategic architecture of the Korean Peninsula cannot be decided in a room where South Korea is not represented.

To assert South Korea’s voice, President Lee Jae-myung, leader of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, is undertaking a high-stakes diplomatic itinerary around the APEC gathering. President Lee is scheduled to visit the Philippines before heading to China, where the presidential office is finalizing details for a bilateral summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping.

For President Lee, the meeting with Xi represents a critical opportunity to push back against big-power marginalization. Seoul intends to leverage the bilateral summit to demand that Beijing play a responsible, constructive role in maintaining stability on the Korean Peninsula and curbing North Korean provocation. Simultaneously, President Lee seeks to safeguard South Korea’s economic interests and secure equitable treatment for Korean enterprises operating within Chinese supply chains.

South Korea’s foreign policy posture under President Lee emphasizes pragmatic, national-interest-driven diplomacy. Navigating between a long-standing security alliance with the United States and intense economic ties with China requires relentless balancing. As the trilateral summit in Shenzhen reorders the global chessboard, Seoul’s objective is clear: ensure that South Korea remains an active player shaping the regional order, rather than a pawn managed by foreign powers.

What to Watch Next

As the Shenzhen summits unfold, international observers should monitor three critical indicators to gauge the regional fallout:

  • The Shenzhen Trilateral Communiqué: Pay close attention to whether Trump, Xi, and Putin issue a joint statement. Any specific phrasing regarding nuclear non-proliferation or regional security boundaries will signal how far the three powers are willing to carve out formal spheres of influence.
  • The Lee-Xi Bilateral Summit: Watch for concrete commitments from President Xi Jinping regarding North Korea. If China offers only generic statements on peace while expanding economic support for Pyongyang, South Korea will face mounting pressure to bolster its own independent deterrence capabilities.
  • Pyongyang’s Reaction to Exclusion: With a Trump-Kim meeting officially off the table in Shenzhen, monitor whether North Korea responds with fresh missile tests or military demonstrations to force its way back onto Washington’s priority list.

The gathering in Shenzhen serves as a stark reminder that in an era of great-power rivalry, national interests dictate everything. For South Korea, surviving the diplomatic waves generated by Washington, Beijing, and Moscow will require every ounce of strategic clarity, nimble maneuvering, and firm resolve it can muster.
