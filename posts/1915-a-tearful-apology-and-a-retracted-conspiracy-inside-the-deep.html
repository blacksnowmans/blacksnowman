A Tearful Apology and a Retracted Conspiracy: Inside the Deepening Rift Within Korea’s Ruling Democratic Party

November 03, 2026

Standing before his party's lawmakers inside the National Assembly caucus room in Seoul, Kim Min-seok could barely hold back his emotions. As the chairman of South Korea’s ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), Kim had summoned the meeting to deliver a humiliating admission. The explosive conspiracy theory he had championed for months—a claim that his internal party rivals had forged a secret pact with an infamous religious sect—was completely unfounded.

"There was no evidence of a three-way secret alliance," Kim confessed, his voice reportedly wavering as he offered a public apology to those targeted by his claims. "I wish to express my deepest apologies to anyone who suffered unintended harm."

For a ruling party currently holding both the presidency under President Lee Jae-myung and a powerful parliamentary majority, the spectacle of its own leader surrendering to an internal investigation was a dramatic low point. What began months ago as a hardball primary tactic has backfired, exposing deep factional fractures, eroding public trust, and forcing the party leadership into damage-control mode.

The Anatomy of a Smear: Shincheonji and DPK Factional Warfare

To understand why this apology has sent shockwaves through Korean politics, one must first understand the political weight of the name "Shincheonji." Shincheonji is a secretive, fringe religious group in South Korea that has drawn intense public scrutiny and hostility over the years, particularly after being linked to a massive cluster of COVID-19 infections in 2020. In the hyper-charged arena of South Korean politics, accusing a fellow politician of colluding with Shincheonji is the political equivalent of throwing radioactive mud.

The controversy dates back to July during the Democratic Party's national convention. Kim Min-seok, then vying for the party leadership, launched a scathing campaign frame. He publicly alleged the existence of a "three-way secret alliance" comprising anti-mainstream party figures, divisive factionalists, and the Shincheonji organization. Though Kim refrained from naming every target directly, political insiders universally understood the attack was aimed at his fierce primary rival, former party leader Jung Chung-rae.

For months, the allegation paralyzed party unity. It triggered bitter online disputes among loyal supporters and led to the formation of a special internal task force dedicated to auditing party membership and investigating the claims.

The task force’s final verdict, delivered at the recent general assembly, was decisive: while the presence of individual church members within general party registries was confirmed, there was absolutely zero evidence showing any covert alliance between political figures and the religious group. Kim’s central premise was an absolute fabrication.

In South Korean electoral jurisprudence, courts have historically set a high bar for criminal defamation during campaign season, acknowledging that political rhetoric often mixes exaggerated claims with hyperbole. However, the political fallout within a party structure follows a very different logic. By weaponizing an unsubstantiated conspiracy theory against his own colleagues, Kim compromised internal trust and squandered valuable political capital.

Retribution and Collapsing Polls

Unsurprisingly, the apology failed to placate Kim’s internal rivals. Immediately following the caucus meeting, former party chairman Jung Chung-rae launched a scathing counteroffensive. Refusing to address Kim by his official title as party leader, Jung pointedly referred to him merely as "the candidate," signaling a refusal to recognize his legitimate authority.

"Come out in public and apologize directly to the party members," Jung demanded, calling for immediate accountability. "He must publicly clarify what kind of political responsibility he intends to take regarding his future."

Jung was joined by Supreme Council member Han Min-soo, who argued that leveling baseless accusations that divide the party demands concrete consequences, not just a scripted reading of regrets. For the anti-leadership faction, the Shincheonji episode is not an isolated mistake, but symbol of an autocratic and exclusionary leadership style that has dominated the party since the summer convention.

Meanwhile, loyalists aligned with Kim have attempted to build a defensive perimeter around the embattled leader. They argue that because the task force confirmed Shincheonji members did exist in peripheral capacities, Kim’s original warning was not entirely wrong, and that the party should focus on unity rather than eating its own. But this defense has only deepened the impasse, dividing the DPK into two bitter camps: those who want to sweep the scandal under the rug, and those demanding a political reckoning.

This internal civil war comes at a devastating cost to the party's public standing. Recent polling data paints a grim picture for the ruling party:

  • Overall Party Support: The DPK’s national approval rating plummeted to 35.5%, marking a steep 7 percentage point drop within a single week.
  • Core Demographics: Support among voters in their 40s—historically the bedrock of the liberal coalition—experienced a staggering drop of 17 percentage points.

Political analysts attribute this collapse directly to voter fatigue over internal infighting. While Korean households face persistent economic headwinds and real estate anxieties, the ruling party's executive leadership appeared entirely consumed by personal rivalries and conspiracy theories.

Legislative Gambits: Pivot to High-Stakes Confrontations

Faced with a rapidly eroding base, the DPK leadership is turning to a familiar play from the South Korean political playbook: driving aggressive legislative battles in the National Assembly to force party unity and distract from internal scandal.

The party has announced plans to force a floor vote on a controversial special prosecutor bill targeting alleged "fabricated prosecutions" by state prosecutors. By framing the legislative agenda around judicial reform and pushing back against state investigative powers, the leadership hopes to rally its core supporters and redirect the political narrative away from Kim Min-seok's personal credibility crisis.

However, this strategy carries significant risks with moderate voters. The party has already been forced to recalibrate its aggressive posturing on other judicial fronts. Plans to launch a broad parliamentary investigation into Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae have hit a wall of public skepticism. Recent national polls revealed that public opposition to impeaching or aggressively investigating Chief Justice Cho outweighs support by a margin well outside the error limit. Recognizing the danger of a backlash, DPK floor leaders have quietly signaled a more cautious, deliberative approach toward the judiciary.

The ruling party now finds itself walking a precarious tightrope. Pushing too hard on controversial special prosecutor bills risks alienating moderate voters who view it as a partisan smokescreen. Yet stepping back risks infuriating party hardliners who demand relentless warfare against the opposition.

What to Watch Next

The resolution of the Shincheonji controversy marks the end of a conspiracy theory, but the beginning of a prolonged leadership crisis within South Korea's ruling party. As the political landscape reshapes itself, here are the key dynamics to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • Kim Min-seok’s Position: Watch whether Jung Chung-rae and his allies escalate their demands for Kim to step down from the party leadership, or if a formal compromise on party governance can be brokered.
  • The Special Prosecutor Vote: Pay close attention to the upcoming National Assembly plenary session. The passage of the "fabricated prosecution" special prosecutor bill will serve as a crucial test of whether the DPK can maintain legislative discipline despite its deep internal divisions.
  • Polling Recovery in the 40s Demographic: Track whether the DPK can stem the bleeding among middle-aged voters, or if the loss of support signals a broader structural shift in public sentiment away from the ruling coalition.
  • Opposition Dynamics: Observe how the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party (PPP), attempts to exploit the DPK's civil war—even as the PPP copes with its own structural vulnerabilities and declining support in traditional strongholds like the Daegu-Gyeongbuk (TK) region.

Ultimately, Kim Min-seok’s tearful retraction has laid bare a fundamental truth of modern Korean politics: internal smears may offer short-term tactical advantages during primary fights, but when the dust settles, the political price of governing through division is almost always paid by the party itself.
