Cracks in the Coalition: Why South Korean President Lee Jae-myung’s Approval Ratings Are Tumbling

October 26, 2026

When an administration’s public disapproval rating crosses 60%, the political ground beneath it begins to shift from routine partisan tension to structural crisis. For South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, that threshold has now been decisively breached. A recent survey conducted by public opinion pollster Realmeter placed negative assessments of the president’s performance at 62.3%, dragging his approval rating down into the precarious 35% range. For an administration elected on promises to dismantle judicial overreach and stabilize the state in the tumultuous aftermath of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment and imprisonment, such figures are not merely a mid-cycle slump—they represent a compounding loss of faith among moderates and an alarming fatigue within the ruling party’s own core base.

To an outside observer, a president with a commanding parliamentary majority and a demoralized opposition should enjoy clear legislative sailing. Yet South Korean politics is defined by an extraordinarily sensitive feedback loop between online activist communities, factional power plays, and immediate public sentiment. Today, the signs of disaffection are visible everywhere: in the plummeting live metrics of pro-government YouTube channels, in disastrous by-election polling numbers from the provinces, and in public policy disputes over municipal budgets where administrative pledges have collided with bureaucratic reality.

When the Base Tunes Out: The YouTube Metrics Behind the Slump

In South Korea, political YouTube broadcasting is not a marginal hobby; it functions as a central organizing hub and an ideological barometer for political parties. Channels aligned with the Democratic Party mobilize voters, frame legislative talking points, and police ideological boundaries. When those channels begin to lose their audiences, party strategists take it far more seriously than an ordinary dip in television ratings.

Consider the recent trajectory of Minti TV, a prominent pro-Democratic live-streaming channel that initially launched to massive fanfare, clocking roughly 55,000 concurrent live viewers during its inaugural broadcast. Those numbers recently evaporated. During a broadcast where party representative Kim Min-seok did not appear, concurrent viewership fell sharply to 19,500 in the first half of the program before crawling back to 23,800 in the second half. Observers noted the peculiar dynamic: viewers were actively tuning out when party elites appeared to offer rote defenses of party leadership, yet returned when the broadcast shifted away from official party talking points.

This viewer attrition—shedding more than half of its audience—illustrates a deeper psychological shift among ruling party supporters. As highlighted during the 2026 Korean Association for Communication and Information Studies conference, partisan digital channels thrive on high-conviction "filter bubbles" that shield loyalists from external criticism. However, when internal dysfunction spills into the open and leadership remains silent, the bubble does not strengthen—it bursts. Democratic supporters are no longer offering automatic, unconditional engagement. When party leaders avoid hard questions or fail to articulate solutions to stalling reform agendas, online activists exercise their only immediate veto: they switch off the stream.

The Gangneung Alarm: A Twenty-Point Deficit

If online streaming metrics demonstrate disquiet among party loyalists, electoral polling shows a far more catastrophic hemorrhage among the general public. Nowhere is this more apparent than in Gangneung, a coastal city in Gangwon Province gearing up for a crucial National Assembly by-election next year.

The seat opened after former conservative lawmaker Kweon Seong-dong was stripped of his parliamentary status. In a contest that ought to have offered the ruling Democratic Party a prime opening to expand its reach, the latest Realmeter polling revealed a lopsided contest: the candidate for the main conservative opposition, the People Power Party, commanded 52.0% support, while the Democratic Party’s candidate languished at just 29.8%—a cavernous deficit of over 22 percentage points.

Gangneung has historically leaned conservative, but it is by no means an electoral dead zone for liberals; previous local elections recorded turnout as high as 63.3%, reflecting an active, contested electorate. To trail by more than twenty points in a national by-election signals that the Democratic Party has lost all traction with independent and moderate swing voters. The perception that the ruling party has grown arrogant, consumed by internal maneuvering rather than bread-and-butter economic issues, has turned a winnable contest into a referendum on the administration's perceived incompetence.

Unfinished Reforms: The Firefighter Budget Crisis

The growing disenchantment is not merely an aesthetic reaction to political squabbling; it is driven by unresolved policy contradictions. A prime example is the ongoing controversy surrounding the financing of South Korea’s firefighting services, brought into the spotlight by Provincial Governor Choo Mi-ae.

Six years ago, South Korea made headlines by reclassifying its municipal firefighters as national public servants, a move intended to standardize equipment, boost safety, and eliminate regional disparities in emergency services. However, the legal transition occurred without an overhaul of the underlying purse strings. Today, local governments still shoulder roughly 90% of the financial burden for firefighting operations, while the central government contributes only a fraction.

"Politicians get hurt, and politicians die. I do not care about that. If my sacrifice can help resolve this problem on a larger scale, I welcome it," Governor Choo declared, challenging the central administration to stop pushing financial responsibilities onto cash-strapped provinces.

Public sentiment on this issue is unequivocal. A Realmeter survey found that 77.7% of respondents believe the central government ought to bear the financial burden for firefighters. A research report from the National Assembly Legislative Research Service confirmed the systemic imbalance: while the absolute number of firefighters expanded significantly after the status change, both budgetary mechanisms and unified command structures remain trapped in an unfinished transitional phase. When rural provinces with shrinking tax bases struggle to buy adequate gear for their emergency personnel, voters view it as a failure of state capacity. For an administration promising effective, progressive governance, the spectacle of a central government skimping on public safety has inflicted serious self-inflicted damage.

"Stifling, Truly Stifling": Factional Crossfire and Legislative Paralysis

At the center of this gathering storm sits President Lee Jae-myung himself, whose public expressions of frustration have begun to lay bare the fissures between the presidential office and his allies in the National Assembly. Recently, President Lee vented his impatience with parliamentary gridlock in unvarnished terms, remarking:

"Ah, the National Assembly is truly stifling, stifling."

While President Lee engaged in sharp factional combat during his previous tenure as party leader—most notably clashing with senior figures such as lawmaker Jung Chung-rae—his current public exasperation with the legislature highlights an administration struggling to enact its core agenda. Instead of maintaining legislative discipline to advance judicial overhaul and clear away the remaining institutional wreckage of the previous era, the Democratic Party has become mired in internal witch-hunts.

Recent attempts by party figures to revive unproven controversies surrounding the Shincheonji religious organization, combined with mutual accusations of internal sabotage, have struck many voters as an aimless distraction. Former lawmaker Choe Kang-wook and other internal figures have argued that the party cannot recover until it confronts these missteps directly, disciplines rogue factions, and offers a transparent accounting to its supporters. When the ruling party appears more interested in scoring points against internal rivals than passing substantive legislation, the presidential office finds its political capital rapidly depleted.

What to Watch Next

President Lee Jae-myung’s administration has arrived at an early inflection point. To reverse this downward trajectory and rebuild public confidence, observers should watch three concrete battlegrounds in the weeks ahead:

  • The Local Subsidy Realignment: Watch whether the central government and the Ministry of Economy and Finance move to adjust the Local Grant Tax allocation system to absorb municipal firefighting costs, as demanded by Governor Choo Mi-ae and supported by nearly four-fifths of the electorate.
  • Democratic Party Floor Strategy: Look for whether the party leadership can halt public infighting over fringe controversies and refocus parliamentary committee chairs on stalled livelihood and regulatory legislation.
  • By-Election Candidate Selection in Gangwon: As the Gangneung by-election approaches, track whether the Democratic Party doubles down on party loyalists or pivots toward reform-minded, centrist-friendly figures capable of chipping away at the People Power Party’s commanding lead.

Voters in South Korea are notoriously unforgiving of governing coalitions that substitute factional discipline for concrete policy outcomes. Unless the Lee administration and its legislative majority acknowledge the warning signals flashing from their own digital base and regional battlegrounds alike, the drop to 35% may prove to be a plateau rather than a floor.
