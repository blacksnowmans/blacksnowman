The Paradox of Reform: Why the Head of South Korea’s New Investigative Giant is Under Fire

October 14, 2026

Imagine a high-stakes hearing room in the National Assembly in Seoul. On one side sits a seasoned prosecutor-turned-lawyer, Kim Ji-yong, nominated to lead the most powerful new law enforcement agency in the country’s history. On the other sits Representative Park Joo-min of the Democratic Party of Korea, the ruling party currently spearheading a radical overhaul of the nation’s justice system. The tension is not merely about a job appointment; it is about the soul of a reform movement that has spent decades trying to dismantle what many call the "Prosecutor Republic."

South Korea is currently undergoing a structural transformation of its legal landscape. By October 2026, the Prosecution Service—an institution that for 78 years held a near-monopoly on both investigating crimes and bringing them to court—will be officially abolished. In its place, two new entities are rising: the Prosecution Office (under the Ministry of Justice), which will handle only indictments, and the Major Crimes Investigation Office (MCIO), which will inherit the heavy-duty investigative powers. The MCIO is designed to be a massive organization, slated to house over 2,800 personnel, including 2,567 specialized investigators, to tackle corruption, economic crimes, and state-level offenses.

The controversy centers on why Kim Ji-yong, a man who spent his career within the very Prosecution Service being dismantled—and who actively fought against these reforms just years ago—is now the top choice to lead the MCIO. For critics, his nomination feels like hiring the captain of a sinking ship to design the new fleet. The debate over his appointment has laid bare the deep-seated "black box" of Korean prosecutorial culture and the ideological tug-of-war defining the President Lee Jae-myung administration’s attempt to finalize the "separation of investigation and prosecution."

The Black Box of Prosecutorial Decision-Making

One of the most jarring revelations from the recent confirmation hearings was the lack of transparency in how the Prosecution Service makes its most sensitive decisions. When questioned by Representative Park Joo-min about whether records existed regarding internal directives or dissenting opinions in high-profile cases, Kim Ji-yong’s response was telling: "I understand that no separate records were kept."

To an outside observer, this might seem like a minor administrative lapse. However, in the context of South Korean politics, it is a structural shield against accountability. For decades, the Prosecution Service operated under the "Prosecutor-as-One" principle (geomsa-dongilche), a legacy of the colonial and military dictatorship eras that demanded absolute hierarchy. While a "right to object" was legally introduced to allow junior prosecutors to challenge illegal or unethical orders from their superiors, Kim admitted he had never once used it during his tenure.

The stakes of this silence are best illustrated by the "Kim Hak-ui illegal exit ban" case. Kim Hak-ui, a former Vice Minister of Justice, was embroiled in a massive bribery and sexual misconduct scandal. When investigators tried to prevent him from fleeing the country, the Prosecution Service—then under the influence of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol (who was later impeached and is currently imprisoned)—allegedly used its power to target the very officials who stopped Kim Hak-ui, rather than focusing on the Vice Minister’s crimes. Kim Ji-yong was in the chain of command that approved these retaliatory indictments.

Representative Park used a powerful metaphor to describe this: "If a thief enters a house to steal, and you, wanting to stop him but ultimately opening the door for him, are what we call an accomplice in legal terms." The argument is that by signing off on politically motivated indictments without leaving a paper trail of dissent, high-ranking officials like Kim provided the "legal" cover for the Prosecution Service to act as a political weapon. For a man seeking to lead a new, transparent agency, this history of "silent approval" is a significant red flag.

A Career Built on Opposing the Reforms He Now Seeks to Lead

Perhaps the most ironic aspect of Kim Ji-yong’s nomination is his past public stance on the MCIO’s very existence. In 2022, while serving as a senior official at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office (SPO), Kim was a leading voice against the "complete deprivation of investigative power" (often called geomsu-wanbak in Korea). At the time, the SPO released documents filled with alarmist rhetoric, suggesting that if prosecutors lost their direct investigative powers, criminals would go free and citizens' rights would vanish.

Some of these documents even claimed that if the reforms passed, suspects would remain trapped in detention even if found innocent, or that people wouldn't be able to attend their parents' funerals while under investigation—claims that ignored existing legal safeguards like stay-of-execution or bail. Kim, as a key figure in the SPO’s criminal department, was responsible for this messaging. He also argued that it was unconstitutional for anyone other than a prosecutor to request warrants, a stance known as "warrant monopoly."

Fast forward to his confirmation hearing, and Kim’s tone has shifted 180 degrees. He now says he "accepts the new structure" where the MCIO investigates and the Prosecution Office requests warrants. When pressed by the Democratic Party on whether his change of heart was simply a bid for power, Kim’s response—that he now sees "some parts were mistaken"—struck many as the height of bureaucratic opportunism. For the Lee Jae-myung administration, which views the MCIO as the culmination of power-branch reform, appointing a leader who recently called the agency's foundation "unconstitutional" risks undermining the entire project's legitimacy.

The Ghost of the "Prosecutor Republic" and the Stakes of the MCIO

To understand why this matters to the average Korean citizen, one must look at the human cost of the old system. Under the previous regime of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the Prosecution Service was frequently accused of "target-based investigations," where the agency decided on a target first and then dug until they found a crime—a process known as "dust-shaking."

The MCIO was created specifically to end this. By housing investigators under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety and keeping the lawyers/prosecutors under the Ministry of Justice, the system creates a "check and balance" that never existed before. The goal is a "lean and professional" investigative body that focuses on evidence rather than political outcomes. According to surveys by the Korean Institute of Criminology and Justice, nearly 60% of citizens want the investigative system improved, and that number rises significantly among those who have actually been victims of crime.

"Power that is not recorded is destined to be corrupted." The failure to document the decision-making process within the prosecution’s hierarchy is not an administrative error; it is a design feature intended to evade future responsibility. Appointing someone who thrived in that culture of silence to lead a reformist agency is, at best, a gamble and, at worst, a betrayal of the reform’s intent.

The concern is that if Kim Ji-yong is appointed, he will bring the "Prosecutor Culture" with him. This includes unofficial directives, closed-door meetings, and the habit of protecting the organization's interests over the public's. Groups like the People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy (PSPD) and the Lawyers for a Democratic Society (Minbyun) have voiced strong opposition, arguing that a successful reform requires a leader who believes in the philosophy of the change, not just someone looking for their next high-ranking post.

What to Watch for Next

The nomination of Kim Ji-yong has put the Lee Jae-myung administration in a delicate position. While the government wants the MCIO to be operational as soon as possible to stabilize the new judicial system, the backlash from its own base and civil society is growing. Here is what to monitor in the coming weeks:

  • The "Accomplice" Narrative: Watch if the Democratic Party continues to push the "accomplice" angle regarding past political indictments. If more evidence of Kim's involvement in suppressing internal dissent surfaces, his nomination may become untenable.
  • Internal MCIO Staffing: Regardless of who is head, the recruitment of the 2,567 investigators will be a major indicator of the agency's direction. Will they be former prosecution investigators, or will they come from diverse legal and forensic backgrounds?
  • The Final Abolition Timeline: As we approach the October 2026 deadline for the Prosecution Service’s closure, the MCIO must prove it can handle complex cases without the "efficiency" of the old, centralized power. Any early failures will be seized upon by the opposition People Power Party to argue for a return to the old ways.

Ultimately, the MCIO is meant to be a gatekeeper of justice. But as the old saying goes, you cannot give the keys to the gate to the person who spent their career trying to tear the fence down. Whether Kim Ji-yong can prove he is a reformed reformer, or if he remains a "prosecutor at heart," will determine the success of South Korea's most ambitious democratic experiment in decades.
