The Trial of Memory: Why South Korea’s New Top Investigator Faces a Firestorm in Parliament

October 14, 2026

The air inside the National Assembly’s hearing chamber was unusually cold when Representative Jung Chung-rae of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea leaned toward his microphone and leveled a direct question at the nominee sitting before him: "Do you feel the weight of the seat you are occupying right now? Do you understand its historical significance?"

On the witness stand, Kim Ji-yong, nominated to serve as the inaugural director of South Korea's newly created Major Crimes Investigation Agency, gave the polite, practiced nod expected of a veteran legal bureaucrat. He murmured that he did. But behind the formal answer hung a heavy silence. This confirmation hearing was never going to be an ordinary vetting exercise. For the lawmakers questioning him and the public watching the broadcast, the proceeding summoned memories that are still raw across the country: the street protests across Gwanghwamun, Namtaeryeong, and Hannam-dong, where citizens gathered with light sticks through freezing nights to resist what they viewed as a prosecutorial dictatorship, ultimately overcoming the martial law insurrection attempted by Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi.

Those mass demonstrations were not merely about unseating a leader; they were a collective demand to dismantle the unchecked power of the state prosecution service and reclaim popular sovereignty. Yet as the government prepares to launch the institutional centerpiece of that promise, the choice for its first commander has triggered disbelief. Instead of an uncompromising reformer, the administration put forward Kim Ji-yong—a career insider whose past reflects the very prosecutorial culture that the public sought to dismantle.

A Historic Overhaul: Splitting the Monolith of Prosecutorial Power

To grasp why this nomination has ignited such fury, one must understand the tectonic shift currently underway in South Korea’s legal system. For over seven decades, South Korea’s state prosecution service operated with an extraordinary concentration of authority. Unlike prosecutors in most Western democracies, who primarily assess evidence brought by police and argue cases in court, Korean prosecutors held both complete investigative authority and a monopoly over indictments. They could initiate investigations, direct the police, issue warrants, conclude probes, and decide unilaterally whether to send a target to trial.

Over decades, this dual monopoly turned the prosecution into the country's most feared political weapon. Ambitious prosecutors engaged in targeted investigations, selective indictments, and collateral probes to pressure political opponents while shielding their own. It was this institutional fortress that paved the path to power for figures like Yoon Suk-yeol-ssi, whose tenure ended in impeachment and imprisonment following his illegal attempt to impose martial law.

The legislative answer to this structural abuse is an unprecedented overhaul scheduled to culminate on October 2, 2026. Under the new legal framework, the traditional Supreme Prosecutors' Office is being abolished. In its place will stand two entirely separate institutions: an Indictment Office, tasked strictly with prosecuting cases, and the Major Crimes Investigation Agency, an independent body that inherits the authority to investigate high-stakes corruption, corporate malfeasance, and systemic abuse of power. The goal is the absolute separation of investigation and indictment—a structural firewall to ensure no single agency can manufacture a case from beginning to end.

Because the Major Crimes Investigation Agency will command the investigative firepower once wielded by elite prosecutors, its first director will set the agency's culture, standards, and institutional DNA. The public expected a figure who had openly resisted the old regime. Instead, they were presented with Kim Ji-yong.

The Career Technocrat and the Burden of Proof

The opposition to Kim is not centered on garden-variety personal corruption or financial scandal. Rather, lawmakers and civic groups are asking a far more existential question: Why him?

Throughout his decades climbing the ranks of the prosecution service, Kim never publicly objected to the service’s political overreach. He never authored a dissenting memorandum that made waves, never submitted a letter of resignation in protest of executive meddling, and never stood with the civil society movements advocating for judicial reform. When pressed by Representative Jung on whether he had ever pushed back against the institutional walls of the prosecutor's office, Kim offered a standard bureaucratic defense: he had always performed his duties faithfully as a public servant.

To his critics, that answer is precisely the problem. South Korea’s legal history is filled with competent technocrats who kept their heads down, carried out questionable orders, and justified their compliance as professional duty. While reformers risked their careers to challenge internal cartels, compliant officials were rewarded with plum assignments. In the eyes of reform advocates, an agency built to purge the remnants of prosecutorial overreach cannot be entrusted to someone whose chief qualification appears to be survival within that very system.

The 2019 Travel Ban and the Politics of Retribution

The hearing quickly moved from abstract principles to specific, documented controversies. Central to the grilling was the July 2021 indictment of Lee Kwang-chul, a former presidential secretary for civil affairs. At the time, Kim was serving in a pivotal post as the Director of Criminal Affairs at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office.

The roots of that indictment go back to late night on March 22, 2019. Kim Hak-ui, a former vice justice minister implicated in serious sex offenses and bribery allegations, attempted to board an overseas flight under cover of darkness to escape the country. Realizing the suspect was at the boarding gate, justice officials moved rapidly to impose an emergency travel ban.

Records and internal memos from the Supreme Prosecutors' Office presented during the hearing revealed that senior leadership was fully aware of the emergency intervention. At 11:35 p.m. that night, Bong Wook, then Deputy Prosecutor General, texted Prosecutor General Moon Moo-il confirming that Kim Hak-ui had been stopped and that directives had been issued to coordinate with Lee Seong-yun, then head of the Anti-Corruption and Violent Crimes Department, to ensure all legal procedures were met. The top echelon of the prosecution had effectively supervised and sanctioned the stop.

Yet months later, the institutional winds shifted. The prosecution service reframed the emergency intervention as an "illegal travel ban." Instead of questioning the superiors who greenlit the decision, prosecutors launched an aggressive probe against the working-level officials and Blue House aides who had acted to stop a fleeing sex-crime suspect. Lee Kwang-chul was indicted in 2021. It took five agonizing years of courtroom battles before South Korea’s Supreme Court definitively cleared Lee of all charges, issuing a total acquittal.

During the hearing, Representative Yoon Kun-young confronted Kim with this history. As the Director of Criminal Affairs at the time, Kim sat in the direct chain of command that approved the indictment. When asked to account for an indictment that the highest court has now ruled meritless, Kim claimed that he had internally opposed the charge. But Yoon pressed the sore spot: where was the paper trail?

"A public official speaks through the record," lawmakers reminded the nominee. "Show us the dissenting opinion you signed. Show us the formal objection you logged."

There was none. Kim had to concede that no written dissent existed. Cornered on whether he bore responsibility for an indictment that consumed five years of an innocent person's life, Kim conceded that he could not say he had no responsibility, offering that "personally, I have the courage to apologize." But as critics pointed out, converting institutional culpability into a private, hypothetical apology avoids the very accountability that a rule-of-law democracy requires.

The Anatomy of Selective Justice

The Kim Hak-ui saga exposed the inner workings of what critics long termed the "Prosecutor’s Republic": a dual standard where leadership figures who issued verbal instructions walked away untouched, while administrative subordinates and political targets bore the brunt of criminal charges. Kim Ji-yong was not a bystander; he held supervisory responsibility during that process.

His admissions during the hearing—that he viewed the final indictment at the time as "somewhat unfair" and regretted that he "could not see his opinion through to the end"—did little to soothe his detractors. If a senior prosecutor knew an indictment was legally flawed and driven by institutional bias, yet permitted his department to advance it anyway, does that passivity qualify him to run the nation's premier independent investigative body?

True judicial reform is not achieved merely by redesigning organization charts, changing office stationery, or moving desks into a new building. It demands leadership that has demonstrated the fortitude to say no to institutional self-preservation. For the citizens who marched through tear gas and sub-zero temperatures, the appointment of an accommodating insider to direct the new Major Crimes Investigation Agency feels less like a breakthrough and more like a retreat.

What Comes Next

The ball is now in the political arena. As the National Assembly wraps up the confirmation proceedings, the immediate focus turns to whether the legislative committee will adopt a formal confirmation report, and if so, what reservations it will include.

  • The Assembly's Assessment: Lawmakers from the ruling Democratic Party of Korea find themselves caught between supporting an administration nominee and heeding the intense pushback from grassroots civic movements who view Kim's appointment as an unacceptable compromise.
  • The Executive Decision: President Lee Jae-myung faces an early test of his administration's reform bona fides. Pushing ahead with Kim’s appointment risks alienating the coalition that brought down the martial law regime, while withdrawing the nomination would trigger an immediate search for a credible, independent legal reformer capable of passing parliamentary muster.
  • The October 2026 Clock: With the statutory deadline of October 2, 2026 rapidly approaching for the abolition of the old prosecution service and the formal standing up of the Major Crimes Investigation Agency, any prolonged vacancy at the top threatens to disrupt the delicate institutional transition.

How the administration handles the growing discontent over Kim Ji-yong will signal whether South Korea's new criminal justice framework will deliver a clean break from decades of prosecutorial overreach, or whether the old habits of bureaucratic compliance will simply find a new home under a different name.
