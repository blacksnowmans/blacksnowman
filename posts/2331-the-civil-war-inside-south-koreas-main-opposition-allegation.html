The Civil War Inside South Korea’s Main Opposition: Allegations of Sabotage and the Fight for the People Power Party

November 14, 2026

Imagine walking into the halls of South Korea’s National Assembly on a chilly Wednesday in mid-November. Usually, the air would be thick with debates over the budget or critiques of the sitting government. But on November 13, the atmosphere inside the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative opposition—was far more volatile. Instead of preparing a unified front against the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, members of the party were effectively at each other's throats. The halls weren't filled with policy talk, but with whispers of "criminal prosecution," "fabricated discipline," and "external interference."

The People Power Party is currently embroiled in an unprecedented internal conflict that threatens to tear the conservative establishment apart. At the heart of this storm is a deepening rift between the current party leadership, led by Representative Jang Dong-hyeok, and a faction loyal to the former interim leader, Han Dong-hoon. What began as a disagreement over the party’s direction has spiraled into a legal and ethical quagmire involving allegations that disciplinary procedures against party members were "rigged" by outside forces. For an opposition party tasked with checking the power of the Democratic Party’s Lee Jae-myung government, this internal meltdown is more than just a political soap opera; it is a crisis of democratic legitimacy.

The Smoking Gun: Allegations of Fabricated Discipline

The primary catalyst for the current explosion is a series of allegations concerning the party’s internal disciplinary process. In a healthy political party, the ethics committee serves as a neutral arbiter to maintain order. However, explosive reports—most notably an exclusive by the major broadcaster MBC—suggest that the PPP’s disciplinary measures were anything but neutral. The allegation is that external figures, not belonging to the party’s formal leadership, were dictating the severity of punishments for high-profile members.

The "pro-Han" faction (supporters of Han Dong-hoon) claims that disciplinary actions against Han and his allies were orchestrated to neutralize them politically. Specifically, reports emerged that specific "sentences"—such as a one-year suspension for Representative Bae Hyun-jin or the total expulsion of Han Dong-hoon—were being discussed and passed down by outside influencers before the ethics committee even met. If true, this suggests the PPP is functioning not as a democratic institution, but as a vehicle for shadow interests.

The legal stakes here are incredibly high. In South Korea, interfering with the official business of a political party can lead to charges of "obstruction of business" or violations of the Political Party Act. Members of the pro-Han faction, including Representative Park Jeong-hun, have upped the ante by calling for a full-scale internal investigation. They have openly mentioned the possibility of criminal prosecution for those involved in the alleged manipulation. By framing this as a legal matter rather than a mere political spat, the pro-Han faction has effectively backed the current leadership into a corner. They are demanding a transparent probe into who, exactly, was pulling the strings from the outside.

Jang Dong-hyeok’s Counteroffensive and the Anti-Han Alliance

Jang Dong-hyeok, the current leader of the People Power Party, has not taken these accusations lying down. On November 13, his strategy became clear: consolidate power by reaching out to veteran heavyweights who share a common rival in Han Dong-hoon. Jang held a high-profile meeting with Representative Ahn Cheol-soo, a veteran politician and former presidential candidate known for his "centrist" brand of conservatism.

Ahn Cheol-soo has recently been one of the most vocal critics of Han Dong-hoon’s potential return to a leadership role. By meeting with Ahn, Jang is attempting to build a "firewall" against the pro-Han faction. Publicly, the two leaders claimed their meeting was about intensifying the struggle against the Lee Jae-myung administration’s "tyranny." However, political observers see it as a clear tactical move to shore up Jang’s leadership. If Jang can align the party’s "non-Han" heavyweights, he can frame the pro-Han faction’s calls for an investigation as "leadership shaking"—a common Korean political term for internal sabotage intended to topple a sitting leader.

The rhetoric has turned surprisingly ugly. Jang has gone as far as to describe the actions of his internal critics as "depraved politics" (패륜의 정치). This is a heavy-handed term in the Korean context, implying a betrayal of the basic moral order. By using such inflammatory language, Jang is signaling that he is no longer interested in mediation or compromise. He is moving to suppress dissent by labeling it as morally bankrupt. However, this "hardline mode" risks alienating neutral members of the party and the public, who see the leader of the main opposition spending more energy fighting his own party members than addressing the country's economic or social issues.

Using Youth Politics as a Shield for Factional Warfare

Amidst this turmoil, the party leadership is attempting to project an image of "business as usual." On the afternoon of November 13, Jang Dong-hyeok attended the inauguration of the 2026 National Youth Local Council Members Association. The event was filled with the usual political theater: cheering crowds, "fighting!" slogans, and photo opportunities with young politicians. Superficially, it was about preparing the next generation for the 2026 local elections.

However, the timing and tone of the event suggest a different purpose. In Korean politics, "youth" is often used as a convenient backdrop for leaders seeking to regain a sense of moral authority. By surrounding himself with young local council members, Jang is attempting to demonstrate that he still commands the "official" apparatus of the party. In his speech, he argued that the "tyranny" of the Lee Jae-myung government was hurting the youth most of all, effectively trying to redirect the party's internal anger toward an external enemy.

"We must stop the administration’s overreach, for the youth are its greatest victims," Jang told the crowd.

The irony of this statement was not lost on critics. While Jang spoke of protecting the future, the party he leads is bogged down in the "old politics" of disciplinary manipulation and factional purges. For many young voters—who are increasingly disillusioned with both the ruling Democratic Party and the opposition—the sight of the PPP leadership using a youth event to distract from a corruption scandal is deeply cynical. Instead of empowering these young leaders, the party risks teaching them that politics is merely a game of survival and backroom deals.

A Party Paralyzed by Legal Risks and Poor Public Perception

The People Power Party’s crisis is exacerbated by the fact that many of its key figures are facing their own "judicial risks." This is a particularly sensitive topic in South Korean politics right now. The current president, Lee Jae-myung, ascended to power while his predecessor, Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, was ousted by impeachment and is currently serving time in prison. This history of "legalized politics" means that court rulings have a disproportionate impact on party stability.

Currently, several high-ranking PPP members, including Floor Leader Choo Kyung-ho and Representative Bae Hyun-jin, are awaiting various court decisions or are involved in ongoing legal disputes. If the court rulings go against these figures, the current power balance within the party will collapse, potentially giving the pro-Han faction the opening they need to force a change in leadership. The party is essentially in a state of "suspended animation," waiting for judges to decide its fate.

The public is watching this with growing disdain. Recent polling data suggests that disapproval of Jang Dong-hyeok’s leadership has climbed above 60%. For a leader of the main opposition, these numbers are catastrophic. They indicate that even conservative voters are losing faith in the party’s ability to act as a credible alternative to President Lee Jae-myung’s government. When the opposition is seen as more corrupt or disorganized than the government it is supposed to be criticizing, the entire democratic system loses its balance.

What to Watch Next: The Breaking Point of the PPP

The coming weeks will determine whether the People Power Party can survive in its current form or if it is headed for a formal split—a "psychological divorce" that could lead to the creation of a new conservative party. There are three key indicators to watch:

  • The MBC Follow-up Reports: The allegations of external interference in the disciplinary process are currently the "live wire" of this conflict. If more concrete evidence—such as phone logs or transcripts of meetings with external fixers—is released, Jang Dong-hyeok’s position may become untenable, and the calls for a criminal investigation will become impossible to ignore.
  • The "Anti-Han" Coalition’s Solidarity: Watch whether other party heavyweights join the Jang-Ahn alliance. If more veteran lawmakers come out against Han Dong-hoon, the party will effectively be split into two warring camps of roughly equal size, leading to total legislative paralysis for the opposition.
  • Court Rulings for Key Lawmakers: Decisions regarding Representative Bae Hyun-jin and other figures will act as a "stress test" for the party. A guilty verdict for a member of the current leadership could trigger an immediate internal coup by the pro-Han faction.

The tragedy of the current situation is that while the People Power Party fights over its internal hierarchy, the actual business of governance is being neglected. In a democracy, the opposition is the "government-in-waiting." Right now, the PPP looks less like a government-in-waiting and more like a house on fire. If the party cannot resolve the "fabricated discipline" scandal with transparency, it risks not just losing the next election, but losing its soul as a democratic institution.
