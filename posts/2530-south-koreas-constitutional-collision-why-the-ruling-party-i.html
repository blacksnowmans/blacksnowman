South Korea’s Constitutional Collision: Why the Ruling Party is Moving to Impeach the Chief Justice

November 20, 2026

The atmosphere inside the National Assembly in Seoul on the evening of November 19 was one of controlled fury. Members of the Democratic Party of Korea, the nation’s ruling party under President Lee Jae-myung, had been ordered to remain on standby within the legislative grounds, awaiting a verdict from the Supreme Court. When the news finally broke, it was not the hammer blow of justice they had expected, but a reprieve for a man they consider a central figure in a dark chapter of recent history.

The Supreme Court overturned the 15-year prison sentence previously handed to Han Duck-soo, the former Prime Minister who served under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol. By remanding the case to a lower court with instructions that essentially declared him partially innocent, the judiciary signaled a pivot that the ruling party could not ignore. Within hours, the Democratic Party (DP) declared "judicial death" and announced an unprecedented move: the formal pursuit of impeachment against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae.

This is not merely a disagreement over a legal interpretation. It is a full-scale collision between the legislative and judicial branches of the South Korean government. For the first time in the country’s constitutional history, the ruling party is moving to remove the head of the judiciary, accusing him of providing a "get-out-of-jail-free card" to those who conspired to subvert the democratic order during the turbulent final days of the previous administration.

The Verdict that Sparked a Rebellion

To understand why the Democratic Party is reaching for the "nuclear option" of impeachment, one must understand the weight of the case against Han Duck-soo. Han served as the top administrative official under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, the former president who was impeached and removed from office before being imprisoned. The core of the legal battle stems from the declaration of emergency martial law and the subsequent actions characterized as an "insurrection" by the current government and its supporters.

For the DP, Han Duck-soo was not a bystander; he was a key facilitator who remained silent or actively assisted while the constitutional order was being dismantled. A lower court had agreed, sentencing him to 15 years in prison. However, the Supreme Court’s decision on November 19 argued that certain aspects of the charges lacked sufficient legal grounding for a conviction, effectively opening a path for Han to receive a significantly reduced sentence or even partial exoneration.

The reaction from the DP was visceral. Representative Lee Hoon-gi called the ruling a "judicial death sentence," suggesting that the court had prioritized legal technicalities over the survival of the republic. The sentiment within the party is that the judiciary is failing in its role as the final bulwark of the constitution. By showing leniency to a high-ranking official of the former regime, the DP argues, the court is inviting future leaders to gamble with the law, knowing that the judiciary might eventually bail them out.

The Impeachment Gambit and the "Opportunist" Critique

Shortly after the verdict, Kim Min-seok, the leader of the Democratic Party, held an emergency briefing. His rhetoric was steeped in historical weight. He characterized the Supreme Court’s decision as an "excuse for historical opportunists," drawing a direct line between the current judiciary and those who served the interests of colonial occupiers or military dictators in Korea’s past.

The DP’s argument is that Jo Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, has led the judiciary into a state of "opportunistic compromise." By failing to issue a stern punishment for what the DP defines as insurrectionist behavior, the court is seen as hedging its bets rather than upholding the law. "If we do not hold the accomplices of constitutional destruction accountable," Kim warned, "no future official will feel the weight of their duty to protect the constitution during a national crisis."

The decision to move forward with impeachment was finalized during an emergency general meeting of DP lawmakers. While some voiced concerns about the political blowback of such a drastic move, the prevailing view was that the National Assembly, as the representative body of the people, has a constitutional mandate to check a judiciary that has gone astray. The party has already moved to form a dedicated "Jo Hee-dae Impeachment Task Force" and plans to adopt the impeachment motion as official party policy by November 20.

A "Parliamentary Coup" or Necessary Accountability?

Across the aisle, the People Power Party (PPP), South Korea’s main conservative opposition party, reacted with equal intensity. They view the impeachment move not as a quest for justice, but as a "parliamentary coup" and an act of pure political revenge. The PPP argues that the DP is attempting to bully the judiciary into submission, ensuring that all future rulings align with the ruling party’s political narrative.

Jeong Jeom-sig, a prominent lawmaker for the PPP, argued that the Supreme Court’s ruling was a return to "law and principle." He accused the DP of acting like "thugs" who resort to violence—in this case, legislative violence—whenever a court ruling does not go their way. From the opposition’s perspective, the DP has spent months building a narrative of a "grand insurrection" to justify its hold on power, and the Supreme Court’s refusal to fully validate that narrative has sent the ruling party into a tailspin.

Han Dong-hoon, another influential figure in the PPP, warned that this move would "paralyze the judiciary and turn South Korea into a backward nation." He described the impeachment pursuit as a "suicide bomb," suggesting that the DP is willing to destroy the independence of the courts to achieve a short-term political victory. The opposition’s defense rests on the sanctity of judicial independence: if a judge can be removed simply for a ruling that the majority party dislikes, then the rule of law in Korea is effectively over.

The Strategic Landscape and the Path Forward

The Democratic Party does not stand alone in this fight. The Rebuilding Korea Party, a smaller but influential liberal party, has already signaled its intent to join the impeachment push. This creates a formidable "pan-liberal" front in the National Assembly. With the DP holding a commanding majority, the passage of an impeachment motion against the Chief Justice is a very real possibility.

However, passing the motion in the National Assembly is only the first step. The final decision would rest with the Constitutional Court of Korea. This body would have to determine whether Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae committed a "grave violation of the Constitution or the law" in the course of his duties. This is a high bar to clear. Critics of the impeachment move point out that a judicial opinion—even one that is unpopular or arguably flawed—rarely meets the legal threshold for impeachment.

The stakes for President Lee Jae-myung’s administration are high. If the impeachment fails, it could be seen as a massive overreach that alienates moderate voters and strengthens the opposition. If it succeeds, or even if it merely stalls the judiciary, it sends a chilling message to every judge in the country. It signals that the era of judicial deference is over and that the courts are now a front line in the country's hyper-polarized political war.

What to Watch Next

The next few days will be critical for the stability of South Korean governance. Here are the specific developments that will determine the direction of this crisis:

  • The Party Vote (Nov 20): Watch for the internal cohesion of the Democratic Party. While the leadership is committed, the margin of support during the general meeting will indicate how much appetite there is for a prolonged fight with the courts.
  • The Language of the Motion: The specific legal grounds cited in the impeachment motion will be scrutinized. The DP must move beyond political rhetoric and identify specific legal failures by Chief Justice Jo to have any hope of success at the Constitutional Court.
  • Public Opinion: South Koreans are generally protective of democratic institutions but are also highly sensitive to perceptions of "judicial elitism" and "political bullying." Polls over the coming week will likely influence how aggressively both sides pursue their agendas.
  • The Constitutional Court’s Temperature: While the court won't speak officially until a motion is filed, the reactions of legal scholars and retired justices will provide a preview of how the high court might view the case.

The move to impeach Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae is more than a news cycle; it is a stress test for South Korea’s 1987 constitutional framework. Whether it results in a more accountable judiciary or a broken system of checks and balances remains to be seen, but the events of November 19 have ensured that the relationship between the bench and the ballot box will never be the same.
