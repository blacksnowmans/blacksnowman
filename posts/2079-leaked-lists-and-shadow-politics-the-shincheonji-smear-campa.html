Leaked Lists and Shadow Politics: The Shincheonji Smear Campaign Roiling Korea’s Ruling Party

November 07, 2026

Behind closed doors in South Korea’s National Assembly, a single sheet of paper can ignite a factional war. Ahead of a high-stakes party convention, whispers began circulating inside the ruling Democratic Party of Korea that a secret list had surfaced—one purportedly proving that Shincheonji, the controversial religious movement widely viewed in Korea as a fringe pseudo-Christian sect, had systematically infiltrated the party's membership ranks. What began as private briefings quickly spilled into public accusations of an unholy coalition between moderate establishment figures and the secretive sect. Yet beneath the sensational headlines lies a far darker and familiar pathology of Korean politics: the weaponization of illicitly leaked state files, egregious privacy violations, and brutal factional purges masquerading as internal reform.

To understand the explosive nature of this controversy, outside observers must understand the uniquely radioactive status of Shincheonji in Korean society. Formally known as the Shincheonji Church of Jesus, the group became a household name globally during early 2020, when its secretive, crowded services in Daegu triggered South Korea's first massive COVID-19 super-spreader outbreak. In domestic politics, any alleged association with Shincheonji is the political equivalent of a lethal scarlet letter. Accusing an opponent of harboring Shincheonji ties does not merely spark debate—it is designed to destroy their political career on the spot.

A Shaky Dossier and the Farce of Ten Recruits

The controversy centers on a mysterious cross-referenced dataset that allegedly matched Democratic Party voter rolls against confiscated member registries of Shincheonji spanning from 2015 to 2021. Yet, despite claims that this list proved an existential threat to the party, almost no senior lawmakers dared to touch it. Among the pro-Chung Cheong-rae faction, only Representative Choi Min-hee was reported to have privately reviewed the material. Her colleagues declined to even examine it, arriving at a swift conclusion: whether the contents were accurate or false, possessing and reading such an unverified, unattributed document was inherently unlawful.

Rather than bringing the dossier into the open and verifying its chain of custody, Representative Choi kept the document shrouded in secrecy while utilizing its supposed findings to launch broadsides against internal party rivals. When the concrete numbers behind the sensational allegations leaked out, however, the grand conspiracy dissolved into unintentional comedy. In Seoul’s Mapo district—a key constituency with hundreds of thousands of registered voters—the controversial dossier showed a grand total of 10 to 15 Shincheonji members registered between 2015 and 2021.

Parading a handful of names across an entire metropolitan district as evidence that a shadowy cult has seized control of the ruling party is political scaremongering at its most cynical. Instead of demonstrating a massive subterranean infiltration, the meager figures exposed the absurdity of the claims. To sift through tens of thousands of party members only to point at roughly a dozen names reveals that this was never a serious security audit, but a pre-engineered fishing expedition designed to fit an existing factional narrative.

State Leaks and the Ghost of Authoritarian Surveillance

The real scandal of the Shincheonji list is not the negligible number of names it contains, but how those names were obtained in the first place. Under South Korea’s strict Personal Information Protection Act, an individual's religious affiliation is categorized as sensitive personal data. Cross-referencing an external religious registry with an internal political party roster without explicit individual consent is a flagrant violation of the law. Party members never signed up to have their private religious histories weaponized by party brass.

More critically, private citizens or individual legislative offices have no lawful way to acquire comprehensive, nationwide Shincheonji membership logs covering 2015 to 2021. A registry of that scope exists in only one place: inside the archives of state investigative authorities, who seized the databases during historic COVID-19 quarantine raids in Gyeonggi Province or during previous specialized prosecutorial task force inquiries. This points to a deeply troubling reality: elements within state investigative agencies or joint investigation headquarters likely leaked confidential state files to selected politicians to meddle in internal party elections.

"An investigative agency handing over classified personal data to selected politicians is not an act of whistleblowing—it is an assault on constitutional due process that belongs to an era of authoritarian surveillance."

For veteran political watchers, the maneuver carries an eerie sense of deja vu. It mirrors the notorious "prosecution-media collusion" playbook seen during the tenure of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, where friendly reporters were fed sensitive target dossiers by state prosecutors to corner political adversaries. Waving unverified, leaked dossiers behind closed doors against figures like Representative Chung Cheong-rae relies on psychological warfare: you convince the target that the state machinery has already compromised them, forcing them into submission before any evidence is formally presented in court.

Factional Warfare Inside the "New Lee" Camp

This manufactured crisis has sharply accelerated fault lines within the "New Lee" camp—the loyalist coalition supporting President Lee Jae-myung. Inside this wing, senior figures are now turning on one another over how to navigate the fallout. Lawmaker Kim Min-seok has found himself caught in the crossfire as different media channels and partisan influencers debate his handling of the smear campaign.

Faced with growing scrutiny over the tainted source material, key proponents of the allegations have aggressively demanded formal police investigations. On the surface, calling for a law enforcement probe sounds like a bid for truth. In reality, it serves as a classic delay tactic. The moment an official police report is filed, politicians can deflect any press inquiries by declaring that they cannot comment on an active investigation, buying months of breathing room while law enforcement remains paralyzed, unable to release intermediate findings.

Yet this exit strategy has backfired among hardline partisan commentators. Some factional voices are now attacking Kim Min-seok directly, deriding his management as weak and accusing him of shielding political rivals, while others demand a relentless purge of anyone remotely tied to the alleged list. The lesson is as old as politics itself: when a faction resorts to dubious, illegal dossiers to destroy internal rivals, the fire inevitably spreads back to consume the arsonists. Distracting the party with phantom smear campaigns not only fractures internal cohesion, but it also directly undermines the policy momentum of President Lee’s administration.

The Credibility Deficit of the Dossier's Champions

The credibility of any political crusade is inseparable from the trustworthiness of the people championing it. Representative Choi Min-hee’s central role in brandishing the secret list has brought renewed attention to her long record of contentious positions and ethical liabilities. Long before this controversy, Choi drew fierce public criticism for promoting dubious pseudo-scientific health theories in her published work—advocating extreme, unproven childcare methods that led critics to associate her with the radical anti-medicine "Anaki" movement.

This pattern of placing fringe beliefs ahead of rigorous empirical evidence has carried over into her legislative work. Her political rhetoric has routinely crossed the line into hostility, from making violent verbal threats against intra-party dissidents to delivering personal attacks against North Korean defector-turned-lawmaker Park Choong-kwon. More recently, as chair of the Science, ICT, Broadcasting, and Communications Committee, Choi has been battered by allegations raised by the conservative People Power Party regarding wedding gift money collected from agencies subject to her committee’s direct oversight—an affair that has triggered formal legal complaints.

It is the height of political hypocrisy to lecture colleagues on party purity and backroom cult ties while stonewalling questions about potential conflicts of interest during committee audits. A lawmaker who evades accountability for her own conduct cannot expect the public to believe she is conducting an impartial, high-minded crusade for procedural transparency.

What to Watch Next

As the party convention nears, the fallout from this botched political operation will test the institutional maturity of South Korea's governing party. Observers should keep a close eye on several key developments in the coming weeks:

  • Tracing the Leak: Watch whether formal criminal complaints are lodged regarding the origin of the Shincheonji files. If prosecutors or police are forced to investigate the chain of custody, the focus will shift squarely to identifying which state investigative bodies leaked classified citizens' data to legislative offices.
  • The Internal Democratic Party Backlash: Look for how rank-and-file party members and grassroots organizations react. If members begin demanding audits into whose personal data was cross-referenced and unlawfully viewed, party leadership under President Lee will face mounting pressure to formally reprimand the lawmakers involved.
  • Factional Realignment: Monitor whether the fractures within the "New Lee" bloc solidify into permanent divisions between moderate institutionalists like Kim Min-seok and aggressive factional hardliners, potentially redrawing the lines of influence across the National Assembly.

Democracy under the Lee Jae-myung administration requires institutional legitimacy and strict adherence to the rule of law. Attempting to destroy intra-party rivals through unverified, illegally obtained dossiers does not modernize Korean politics—it drags it backward into the darkest habits of state-sponsored intrigue.
