Seoul’s 94-Week Housing Rally Stalls, Pushing Korea’s Real Estate Divide Into Gyeonggi

November 28, 2026

Walk into a real estate brokerage in Jamsil, one of southeastern Seoul’s flagship apartment districts, and the phones are largely quiet. Sellers have slashed asking prices by hundreds of millions of won on urgent listings, yet prospective buyers are walking away—telling agents they will wait until prices fall another 200 million won (about $140,000) before making an offer, according to field reporting by South Korean outlet Dailian.

Across the capital, a nearly two-year run-up in apartment prices has hit a wall. Weekly price growth in Seoul’s apartment market slowed to 0.01 percent, effectively flatlining at its lowest reading in 94 weeks, while unsold listings jumped by 20,000 in just three months to surpass 80,000 units, according to Hankook Ilbo. What looked only months ago like a fear-driven scramble to buy before prices climbed further has turned into a standoff between anxious sellers and sidelined buyers.

For foreign observers, South Korea’s apartment market is more than a construction story. High-rise apartment complexes are the primary vehicle for household wealth, social status, and retirement security in a country where roughly half the population lives in the greater Seoul metropolitan area. As strict lending rules cool the capital’s core, the affordability squeeze is not disappearing—it is reshaping what size homes Koreans can buy and pushing 43,000 residents a year out of Seoul and into surrounding Gyeonggi Province.

From Panic Buying to an 80,000-Unit Standstill

On paper, a 0.01 percent uptick still looks like growth. On the ground, multiple Korean outlets report that the market has already tipped into decline. According to Christian Daily and Sisa Journal e, nine of Seoul’s 25 autonomous districts have now turned negative. Crucially, the drop is no longer confined to outer neighborhoods: key districts along the Han River belt, including Yeongdeungpo and Gwangjin, have reversed course, and declines are widening across the affluent Gangnam Three Districts—Gangnam, Seocho, and Songpa—long seen as the bellwether of Korean property wealth.

To understand why the turn feels so abrupt, it helps to understand how Korean buyers finance homes. During the 94-week rally, many younger buyers practiced what Koreans call yeongkkeul—literally "scraping together one’s soul" by maxing out mortgages and unsecured loans to get onto the property ladder before prices ran away. According to Maeil Business Newspaper and Dong-A Ilbo, tighter government mortgage regulations combined with interest rate pressures have abruptly choked off that borrowing power.

Once the psychological floor broke, market dynamics flipped. In past cycles, discounted urgent sales—known locally as geupmae—were snapped up within days. Now, with more than 80,000 apartments sitting on the market, those discounted listings are becoming the new price ceiling, pulling neighborhood valuations lower. Negotiating leverage has shifted decisively from homeowners to cash-holding buyers, and as long as inventory keeps piling up, Seoul’s correction could last longer than many homeowners anticipate.

Shrinking Homes and the Cash Wall in New Developments

The financing squeeze is also rewriting South Korea’s decades-old definition of a standard family home. For generations, the centerpiece of Korean apartment living was the 84-square-meter unit (about 904 square feet of interior floor space), nicknamed the "national standard size." Today, buyers are abandoning it—not by choice, but by necessity.

According to Shin-A Ilbo and EToday, competition in Seoul’s cheongyak system—the government-regulated housing subscription lottery used to allocate newly built apartments—is now 3.7 times higher for smaller 59-square-meter units (about 635 square feet) than for 84-square-meter homes. Sports Q similarly notes sharp polarization, with mid-to-large units struggling to fill quotas while compact apartments sell out quickly.

Demographic shifts, including rising numbers of single-person households and dual-income couples without children, have played a role. Yet price is the decisive driver. In Seoul, a compact "25-pyeong" apartment (roughly 82 square meters of gross floor area, typically corresponding to a 59-square-meter interior) topping 1 billion won (about $715,000) has become routine. Meanwhile, broadcaster JTBC reports that micro-apartments surged by more than 100 million won (about $71,500) in just one or two months as loan limits and climbing jeonse prices—Korea’s unique rental system in which tenants hand over a massive lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent—pushed desperate buyers into the smallest available units.

Even government price caps no longer guarantee access for ordinary households. A premium report by Maeil Business Newspaper on the newly offered Kyunghee Palace Efit complex in central Seoul illustrates the cash barrier:

  • Pre-sale sticker shock: An 84-square-meter unit at Kyunghee Palace Efit is priced at 2.57 billion won (about $1.84 million)—still below the neighboring flagship Kyunghee Palace Xi at 2.75 billion won (about $1.96 million), offering theoretical upside to lottery winners.
  • Upfront cash wall: Because of strict lending limits, winning the right to buy still requires at least 770 million won (about $550,000) in immediate cash just to move in.
  • Rush to smaller units: Locked out of family-sized homes, eligible applicants crowded into the complex’s smaller 59-square-meter special-supply units at a competition ratio of 52.7 to 1.

In practice, critics argue that even price-capped new developments have become a closed market for wealthy buyers with substantial liquid assets, leaving younger, lower-scoring applicants fighting over shrinking square footage.

The Lee Jae-myung Administration’s Gyeonggi Gamble

Faced with unaffordable prices and scarce land inside Seoul, President Lee Jae-myung’s administration has turned to large-scale suburban supply. According to OBC News and Weekly Hankook, 99 percent of the government’s newly designated housing land in the capital region sits outside Seoul in Gyeonggi Province, totaling 103,000 planned homes. Officials are pushing for groundbreakings by the end of the year, aiming to avoid the sluggish supply rollout seen during Yoon Suk Yeol’s time in office.

At the same time, the administration is sharpening regulatory tools inside the capital, expanding authority to designate strict land transaction permission zones—areas where buyers must obtain government approval and commit to living in the home—to the Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport.

Yet this two-track approach of tight regulation in Seoul and mass building in Gyeonggi has drawn pointed criticism from regional researchers and media outlets. Both the Gyeonggi Research Institute and industry outlet Sanup Ilbo, alongside ibs News, warn that building tens of thousands of homes without matching metropolitan transit lines or local job centers risks creating giant bedroom communities where workers face three-hour round-trip commutes into Seoul. News Creator further notes that several designated sites are located in areas where housing prices have actually fallen, suggesting land was chosen for administrative convenience rather than genuine demand.

Without urban redevelopment inside Seoul or rapid rail links connecting new suburbs to central job hubs, critics warn that outer-ring supply risks leaving Gyeonggi oversupplied while making central Seoul an even more exclusive enclave for the wealthy.

Tax Pressures at the Center, Price Surges to the South

Even as Seoul’s overall market cools, holding costs are adding fresh downward pressure on high-end neighborhoods. According to Money Today, Seoul recorded the largest increase in homeowners subject to South Korea’s Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax (jongbuse), an annual levy on high-value property. Because assessments have risen, the tax is no longer hitting only multi-homeowners in Gangnam; it now reaches single-homeowners in middle-class neighborhoods such as Wangsimni and Dangsan. Media Day notes that analysts are watching the first quarter of next year closely, when tax-burdened owners may list more properties at a discount.

Yet cooling Seoul has not stabilized the broader capital region. Instead, analysts point to a classic "balloon effect," in which demand squeezed out of Seoul inflates prices in job-rich hubs to the south. According to Jaekyung Ilbo, apartment prices in Dongtan, a planned city south of Seoul, have surged 23 percent this year, with one property jumping 64 million won (about $46,000) in two days. Further south in Pyeongtaek, apartments near Jije Station have crossed the 1 billion won (about $715,000) mark, and Jije-dong has climbed 15.4 percent despite the national slowdown—buoyed by major local employers such as Samsung Electronics’ semiconductor campus.

The result is an increasingly lopsided market. For buyers priced out of Seoul, southern Gyeonggi’s semiconductor corridor has become the new frontier—bringing the same affordability anxieties to commuters who thought moving south would offer relief.

What to Watch Next

Whether Seoul’s 0.01 percent plateau marks the start of a prolonged correction or merely a pause before another leg up will hinge on three developments in the months ahead:

  • First-quarter tax selling: Watch whether rising Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax bills push hesitant Seoul homeowners to cut prices on the city’s 80,000-unit backlog early next year, particularly in Gangnam and Jamsil.
  • Interest rates and loan rules: Any shift in Bank of Korea rate expectations or government mortgage caps will quickly test how much pent-up demand remains on the sidelines.
  • Transit and urban supply follow-through: Watch whether the Lee Jae-myung administration pairs its year-end Gyeonggi groundbreaking push with concrete transit investments and faster redevelopment inside Seoul—or whether southern Gyeonggi’s job hubs absorb even more displaced demand.
