The Art of Letting Go: Essential Lessons from the Camino de Santiago

September 07, 2026

When you first decide to walk the Camino de Santiago—the ancient 800-kilometer (roughly 500-mile) pilgrimage across northern Spain—the immediate instinct is panic. You look at the vast expanse of the route and feel an overwhelming urge to pack everything you own. But those who have completed the journey share a singular, haunting piece of advice: "The secret to the Camino is how light you can pack. It is absolutely critical."

For a first-time traveler, the Camino is not a journey of addition; it is a brutal, transformative process of subtraction. If you want to survive the walk, you have to learn that every extra item in your pack is a weight you will carry on your back for every single meter of the journey.

Your Feet: The Beginning and the End

Many beginners make the mistake of assuming that expensive, high-end hiking boots are the key to comfort. In reality, the most important factor is familiarity. There is no such thing as a "perfect" boot—only a boot that your feet have already accepted. Walking 800 kilometers in a brand-new pair of boots is a recipe for a medical emergency.

When buying footwear, go half a size to a full size larger than your normal shoe. Your feet will swell significantly after days of continuous walking. If your boots fit perfectly at the store, they will become instruments of torture on the road. Furthermore, the "double-sock" method is the industry gold standard: wear a thin, moisture-wicking toe sock underneath a thicker trekking sock. This layer of friction protects the skin and is far more effective at preventing blisters than any cream. If you feel a "hot spot" forming, stop immediately, take off your shoes, and air out your feet. It is the cheapest and most effective maintenance you can perform.

Once you reach your albergue (a specialized hostel for pilgrims), your day isn't over. Do not settle for simple slippers. Instead, pack sandals that provide heel support. After a day of heavy-duty boots, your feet need to recover in a structured environment that allows the muscles to relax properly.

The Golden Ratio of Weight

For a summer trek on the Camino Frances (the most popular route), your total pack weight—including water and equipment—should ideally hover around 6kg (about 13 lbs). Even if your scale says 5.2kg at home, once you add a liter of water and a few snacks, you will hit that 6kg mark. You will feel every single 100 grams of that pack within three hours of your first day.

The Philosophy of the Pack

A 40-liter backpack is more than enough for a summer journey. The most vital feature isn't the brand, but the hip belt. If you are carrying the weight on your shoulders, you will fail. A proper hip belt shifts that load to your pelvis, allowing you to walk for hours without crippling back pain.

When it comes to clothing, the rule is simple: wear one outfit and carry only one change. You will be washing your clothes every single day; bringing more is a burden, not a convenience. Bring four to seven clothespins to hang-dry your gear. It sounds trivial, but there is a profound, simple joy in starting the day with fresh, dry clothes that makes the physical exhaustion of the walk manageable.

Many travelers pack a "rain cover" for their bag, but seasoned pilgrims often view them as useless. A heavy-duty, durable poncho that covers both you and your pack is significantly more effective. Disposable plastic ponchos will tear within minutes in the Spanish wind; invest in quality gear that will last the distance.

Health and the Minimalist Kit

Injuries are inevitable on the Camino. Your medical kit should be focused and light. Kinesiology tape is far more versatile than a heavy orthopedic brace—it provides physical and psychological support for tired knees without the bulk. As for toiletries, do not over-pack. A single bar of soap can handle hair, face, and laundry. If you run out, buy more at a local market. Every gram you strip away is a gram your body doesn't have to lift.

Communication and hydration are also key. You do not need to carry liters of water; the Camino Frances is well-serviced with fountains every one to two hours. A 500–700ml bottle is plenty. If you are worried about water quality, bring a bottle with a built-in filter. Finally, carry cash in small denominations (5, 10, and 20 Euro notes). Small village shops will often struggle to break a 50 Euro note, and you don’t want to be stranded without the ability to buy food.

Walking at Your Own Pace

The greatest obstacle on the Camino is not the terrain; it is the "speed of others." Young, athletic travelers will pass you, and the temptation to speed up to match them is natural. However, the pilgrims who successfully reach Santiago are those who listen to their own bodies. If your knee hurts, stop. If you find a village you love, stay another night. There is no prize for being the first to arrive.

In the end, the Camino is not a race—it is a conversation with yourself. We live in a world obsessed with productivity, competitive career paths, and the constant fear of falling behind. The Camino is a rare, radical space where you are finally allowed to exist at your own speed. The physical preparation—carefully selecting your socks, shortening your gear list, and testing your boots—is really just a rehearsal for a larger lesson: that when you let go of the things you don't need, you finally have the space to carry the things that actually matter.

What to watch for next:

  • Physical prep: Start walking in your chosen shoes at least a month before departure.
  • The "Mental" Pack: As you pack, ask yourself if each item is a "need" or a "security blanket." If it's the latter, leave it at home.
  • Route Research: Familiarize yourself with the elevation profiles of the upcoming week, but stay flexible enough to ignore your itinerary if your body demands a rest day.
