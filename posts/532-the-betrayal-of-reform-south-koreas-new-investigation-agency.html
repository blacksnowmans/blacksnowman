The Betrayal of Reform: South Korea's New Investigation Agency and the Return of the Pro-Yoon Prosecutors

September 30, 2026

Picture the scene: a room full of political strategists, progressive commentators, and party faithful who spent years enduring the hardline conservatism of the previous administration, now suddenly forced to watch the architects of that very suppression step smoothly into positions of power under a progressive government. Outside, the rhetoric is all about structural reform and breaking old institutional habits. Inside, the actual job offers are going to the exact same prosecutors who once spearheaded high-profile investigations against the democratic camp.

As South Korea prepares to launch the Serious Crime Investigation Agency (SCIA) this October—a body designed with a total staff of 2,874, including 2,567 investigators, meant to strip direct investigative powers away from the traditional prosecution service—a fierce political storm has erupted. Far from representing a clean break with the past, the new agency's prospective leadership has triggered a devastating critique: it looks less like a reform and more like a curated collection of pro-Yoon Suk Yeol prosecutors. And the most startling indictment of this reality has come not from the political opposition, but from within the progressive camp itself.

When Reformers Hand the Keys Back to the Old Guard

The controversy broke wide open when Park Eun-jung, a lawmaker with the Rebuilding Korea Party who has long stood on the front lines of prosecution reform, appeared on a popular political commentary program. She did not mince words. In a direct challenge to the current administration's personnel policy, Park bluntly demanded: "Just openly declare that you are going to walk hand-in-hand with the pro-Yoon prosecutors."

Her frustration was rooted in specific names being floated for top leadership roles within the new agency. Among them is Kim Ji-yong, a former high-ranking prosecutor who historically opposed separating investigative and indicting powers, signed a petition against the disciplinary action taken against former President Yoon Suk Yeol, and was involved in the controversial indictments of rival officials. Placing figures like Kim at the helm of an agency meant to root out corruption and economic crimes makes a mockery of the reform mandate.

The rationale offered by some government and ruling party insiders is that these individuals possess unmatched investigative "expertise." But this argument falls flat for critics. Have former special investigation prosecutors ever truly shown a unique aptitude for cleaning up systemic corruption, or are they simply angling to inherit the exact same levers of power they held under previous conservative administrations? Stripping investigative functions from the traditional prosecution service only to hand them to the very architects of the old regime is not structural reform; it is merely a corporate succession of institutional privilege.

The inclusion of figures like Lim Hyun, a senior prosecutor widely recognized as a core member of the "public security faction" loyal to Yoon Suk Yeol, only deepens these fears. Lim previously served as the public security policy officer at the Supreme Prosecutors' Office during the height of the conservative administration, where he was involved in politically sensitive investigative lines—including the Ulsan election interference case, which ultimately resulted in full acquittals for the accused. When prosecutors who drove aggressive, politically charged indictments are allowed to glide into elite posts at a nominally independent agency, it proves that the old boys' networks of school ties, regional loyalties, and shared political ambition survive any change of regime.

The Cracking of Progressive Solidarity and Cognitive Dissonance

Park Eun-jung’s critique went beyond administrative appointments; it struck directly at the psychological toll being exacted on the progressive grassroots. There is a baseline of political morality that a reform movement must maintain. Aligning or compromising with the forces that crushed democratic procedures and trampled dissent over the past few years is not "unity"—it is self-negation.

Park also took aim at prominent progressive media figures and commentators, warning them that hiding past misdeeds behind a smokescreen of personal loyalty or strategic necessity is a betrayal of their audience. Yet many liberal speakers and online influencers have tie themselves in knots trying to rationalize the situation, telling supporters that the administration surely wouldn't partner with pro-Yoon prosecutors unless there was some unavoidable, high-stakes reason.

This dissonance has left rank-and-file supporters deeply cynical. Online political communities are increasingly flooded with a grim resignation: that the current ruling camp is simply recycling prosecutors who targeted past conservative figures while viewing those who targeted the liberal camp as "capable professionals" worth employing for their own political shielding. If true, prosecution reform has degenerated from a crusade for justice into a cynical turf war over who gets to hold the sword.

The Hidden Blueprint: Structural Majorities and Party Realignment

To understand why this is happening, one has to look beyond isolated personnel choices and examine the broader political strategy attributed to President Lee Jae-myung and his allies: the pursuit of a permanent "structural majority" and a comprehensive redevelopment of the Democratic Party.

As various political analysts have pointed out, this strategy relies on marginalizing or cleanly excising the traditional factions loyal to former Presidents Roh Moo-hyun and Moon Jae-in. A glance at official digital footprints reveals a systematic downplaying of the Roh and Moon legacies, reflecting an internal party friction that dates back years to fierce primary battles. The calculation appears to be that by shedding the traditional progressive core and replacing them with a mix of new loyalists and pragmatic conservatives, the party can lock in a stable, unbreakable parliamentary majority.

Yet evicting the people who built the party's foundation in pursuit of a top-down architectural redesign is a high-stakes gamble with terrible historical precedents. When a reform movement turns its guns inward and embraces the very establishment it was elected to dismantle, it courts political disaster.

What to Watch Next

As the Serious Crime Investigation Agency prepares to open its doors in October, the months ahead will serve as a definitive stress test for South Korea's judicial reform movement. Here are the concrete developments to monitor:

  • Final Agency Appointments: Watch closely to see whether controversial figures tied to the former Yoon administration actually secure regional bureau chief positions at the SCIA, or if public outcry forces a last-minute retreat.
  • Internal Party Fractures: Track how lawmakers aligned with the former Moon and Roh administrations respond to ongoing party realignments and whether organized internal dissent emerges within the ruling party.
  • The Legal and Political Fallout of High-Profile Cases: Observe how the SCIA handles major corruption investigations—its early case selections will immediately reveal whether it operates as an independent watchdog or a continuation of old prosecutorial habits.
  • Opposition Maneuvering: Keep an eye on how marginalized conservative figures, including figures like Han Dong-hwan, attempt to weaponize public disillusionment over these compromised reform efforts.

Reform built on cowardly silences and expedient compromises is nothing more than a sandcastle. Only the courage to face uncomfortable truths can break the cycle of recycled power.
