Boots, Ballots, and the Bench: Why South Korea’s Social Activism is Stepping Up in Seongsu-dong

October 31, 2026

In the narrow alleys of Seongsu-dong—a neighborhood once known as the gritty heart of Seoul’s shoemaking industry and now its trendiest "Brooklyn-style" hub—the rhythmic tapping of cobblers' hammers has been joined by a different kind of energy. At a boutique named "Dreaming Agio," the air recently buzzed not just with the scent of tanned leather, but with the fervent political discourse that defines modern South Korea. Here, two of the country’s most influential liberal voices, writer Rhyu Si-min and veteran anchor Byeon Sang-wook, traded their pens and microphones for aprons and measuring tapes.

The event was a "one-day salesman" promotion for Agio, a social enterprise that has become a symbol of disability empowerment in Korea. Agio is owned and operated by the "Landscape of Shoe Making" cooperative, a company where most of the master craftsmen are hearing-impaired. But in the current political climate under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung, a simple charity event rarely stays "simple." As citizens lined up to have their feet measured by a former cabinet minister and a legendary journalist, the conversation quickly shifted from the arch of a foot to the integrity of the Supreme Court. In Seongsu-dong, the act of buying a pair of shoes became a proxy for supporting social justice and a platform for demanding the impeachment of the nation’s top judge.

The Artisans of Silence and the "Social Win-Win"

To understand why a shoe store became a political flashpoint, one must understand Agio’s place in the Korean psyche. For years, the brand struggled in a market dominated by cheap mass-produced imports. It gained national fame only after a photograph went viral showing the worn-out, tattered soles of a pair of Agio shoes belonging to a former liberal leader. That image transformed the brand into a badge of honor for the "reform-minded" segment of the public—those who value the "Sang-saeng" (win-win coexistence) philosophy that Agio represents.

During the event, Rhyu Si-min—a former Minister of Health and Welfare and a prolific author who acts as a primary intellectual influencer for the ruling Democratic Party—spent six hours on his feet. This wasn't a mere celebrity cameo; Rhyu and Byeon Sang-wook (a former senior journalist at CBS) were deeply involved in the labor. They measured feet, signed autographs, and pitched the "Cinderella Sale," where discontinued models were sold for about $22 to $37 (30,000 to 50,000 KRW), with all proceeds going to support the employment of disabled workers.

The event also showcased the brand's commitment to Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) values. An upcycling class led by instructor Jeong Se-ah allowed participants to turn leather scraps into book covers, with hearing-impaired staff using hot-stamping machines to emboss initials alongside the signatures of Rhyu and Byeon. This "visible solidarity" is what draws thousands to Seongsu-dong. For the foreign observer, it might look like a standard pop-up store, but for the attendees, it was a communal reaffirmation of the "labor value" that they feel is often ignored by the corporate elite and the conservative opposition.

Riot Boots and the Satire of the "Search and Seizure" Era

The highlight of the product lineup was an item that Rhyu Si-min introduced with a heavy dose of political satire: the Agio functional boot, originally designed for supply to the national police force. These are not heavy, steel-toed combat boots, but lightweight, specialized footwear designed for officers who must stand or walk on city pavement for twelve hours a day.

"You shouldn't wear these when you come for a search and seizure," Rhyu joked to the crowd, drawing a mix of laughter and bitter sighs. The comment was a sharp jab at the previous era under Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, whose presidency was characterized by what critics called a "Republic of Prosecutors," marked by an unprecedented number of raids on political opponents and media outlets. While Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol is currently imprisoned following his impeachment, the trauma of that period remains a potent rallying cry for the liberal base.

The technical excellence of the shoes—their extreme lightness and anti-slip grip designed for sea fishing and urban patrol—served as a metaphor for the speakers. The message was clear: even the tools of state power (like police boots) should be crafted with the care and integrity of disabled artisans, rather than being used as instruments of oppression. Rhyu’s "pro-salesman" performance, where he honestly noted the boots weren't meant for rugged mountain climbing but were perfect for city walking, highlighted a level of transparency that his supporters find lacking in the current judicial system.

The Storm at the Supreme Court: Why Jo Hee-dae is the Target

As the "Dreaming Agio" store celebrated social cooperation inside, the atmosphere just a few miles away was far more confrontational. The event in Seongsu-dong took place against the backdrop of a growing movement to impeach Jo Hee-dae, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Korea. For many of the citizens who visited the store, the two issues are linked: you support Agio to build the world you want, and you impeach Jo Hee-dae to destroy the remnants of the world you reject.

Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae is increasingly viewed by activists—specifically the group "Candlelight Action"—as a blockade against the judicial reforms promised by President Lee Jae-myung’s administration. Activists accuse Jo of leading a "judicial coup" from within the bench, allegedly obstructing fair trials related to the insurrection charges against members of the former administration and stalling the truth-finding process for the Itaewon crowd crush tragedy.

The frustration peaked on the Monday following the Agio event, as protesters gathered in front of the National Assembly. Their grievance is twofold. First, they view Jo as a protector of the "old guard" who continues to issue warrants that hinder current investigations. Second, they are furious with their own representatives. Several Democratic Party (DP) lawmakers had reportedly promised to introduce an impeachment motion against Jo, only to backtrack at the last minute, opting for a "press conference only" approach that activists dismissed as "show-tong" (fake communication).

The "Backlash" Debate: A Ruling Party Under Pressure

The tension within the Democratic Party, the current ruling party, is palpable. Despite holding a commanding majority in the National Assembly, the leadership is wary of what they call "Yeok-pung" or a political backlash. In Korean politics, this is the fear that aggressive moves—like impeaching a Chief Justice—might be perceived as an overreach of power by moderate voters, potentially hurting the party in future local elections.

However, the voices from the Seongsu-dong crowd and the street protests suggest that the base has run out of patience with this caution. "When did we ever have a 'favorable wind'?" one activist asked, pointing out that the 170-plus seats held by the DP were won precisely because the people wanted them to use that "weapon" to purge the remnants of the Yoon Suk-yeol era. The criticism is that the party is "calculating" while the people are "bleeding" on the asphalt.

The current situation is a delicate balancing act for President Lee Jae-myung. While his government focuses on economic recovery and social welfare (symbolized by his supporters' patronage of brands like Agio), the more radical wing of his support base demands a "total clearing" of the judiciary. They argue that as long as Jo Hee-dae holds the gavel, the legal system will remain a "shield for the powerful" rather than a "support for the weak."

What to Watch: The Road to Judicial Reform

The Agio event in Seongsu-dong was more than a successful bake sale for shoes; it was a temperature check for the Korean liberal movement. The fact that an afternoon of shoe-selling could attract 120,000 live viewers online and thousands of physical visitors shows that the "Candlelight" spirit is far from extinguished; it has simply diversified into social enterprise and consumer activism.

In the coming weeks, there are three key developments to monitor:

  • The National Assembly’s Motion: Pressure is mounting on the Democratic Party leadership to move beyond "investigative committees" and actually file the impeachment papers against Chief Justice Jo Hee-dae. Watch for whether the party's "floor leader" bows to the pressure of the activist base.
  • The Itaewon and Insurrection Trials: The speed and transparency of these specific court cases will determine the level of public anger. If the courts continue to 기각 (dismiss) warrants related to the previous administration's alleged crimes, the calls for a "judicial sweep" will grow louder.
  • Agio as a Political Barometer: The success of social enterprises like Agio will continue to serve as a measure of the "moral economy" that the Lee Jae-myung administration promotes. If these brands thrive, it signals a consolidation of the liberal base’s economic power.

As Rhyu Si-min noted while packing up for the day, a good pair of shoes is supposed to support you from the lowest point—the ground. For the thousands who gathered in Seongsu-dong, the hope is that the law will eventually do the same, providing a firm footing for a society that is still trying to walk away from a turbulent past toward a more stable, "win-win" future.
