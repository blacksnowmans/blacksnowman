Slicing an Insurrection: Why South Korea’s Supreme Court Partially Cleared a Former Prime Minister Over Martial Law

November 20, 2026

Late on the night of December 3, inside the presidential office in the Yongsan district of Seoul, a frantic drama was unfolding that would shake South Korea’s constitutional foundation. Armed troops were being readied to storm the National Assembly, police units were fanning out across the capital, and top administration officials were summoned in haste. Among them was Han Duck-soo, then serving as Prime Minister—a technocrat with nearly 50 years of experience in public service, having previously held senior positions including Deputy Prime Minister for Economy and two separate stints as Prime Minister.

Within a narrow window of just one to two hours, South Korea was plunged into an unannounced emergency martial law declaration. Yet months later, when the legal reckoning reached the highest court in the land, South Korea’s Supreme Court performed a controversial piece of judicial surgery. Sitting en banc—a full panel of justices—the Supreme Court overturned key parts of a lower court ruling against former Prime Minister Han, remanding his case to the Seoul High Court with instructions of not guilty regarding his actions immediately preceding the decree.

Specifically, the Supreme Court majority concluded that Han’s decision to suggest and convene a Cabinet meeting—the chief executive body comprising senior ministers required to deliberate on major state decisions—just before the martial law announcement did not carry an explicit intent to subvert the constitution. The ruling has sparked widespread public outrage and fierce debate among legal scholars, who question how a contiguous two-hour sequence of events could be carved up to grant partial immunity to the executive branch’s second-in-command.

A Legal Dissection of a Two-Hour Window

To understand the controversy, one must examine how the Supreme Court majority drew its line. The court evaluated three specific charges against Han Duck-soo related to his participation in key tasks of an insurrection. In its decision, the majority separated Han’s actions into two distinct phases separated by barely an hour.

In the first phase—the moments before martial law was declared—Han met with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was later impeached and is currently serving a prison sentence. The majority reasoned that before this face-to-face meeting, Han did not know the specific, sweeping details of the martial law decree and had voiced mild concerns beforehand. Based on this, the justices interpreted Han’s suggestion to convene the Cabinet meeting (국무회의) as a standard administrative effort to make the president follow constitutional procedure, rather than an active attempt to assist in an illegal overthrow of the democratic order.

The majority went so far as to argue that penalizing a minister for advising procedural compliance could create a dangerous chilling effect, making public officials hesitant to offer administrative advice during future national emergencies. Under this logic, the court ruled that Han’s pre-declaration conduct lacked proven criminal intent to subvert the constitution.

This reasoning stood in sharp contrast to how lower courts viewed the timeline. The Seoul Central District Court had previously sentenced Han Duck-soo to 23 years in prison, evaluating his conduct as a single, uninterrupted effort to lend official cover to an illegal coup. Lower court judges noted that during those critical minutes, Han did not use his formidable authority to halt the unconstitutional move; instead, his primary action was securing the nine Cabinet members needed to fulfill the constitutional quorum so the declaration could proceed.

Critics argue that by isolating the pre-declaration hour, the Supreme Court created an artificial divide:

  • Pre-Declaration Phase: Suggesting and gathering the Cabinet to meet quorum requirements (Ruled Not Guilty due to lack of explicit intent).
  • Post-Declaration Phase: Discussing measures to cut electricity and water supplies to media outlets and processing decree signatures (Upheld as Guilty).

Slicing a tightly compressed sequence of events into separate windows of intent, observers contend, ignores how executive power operates in the heat of a crisis and creates a convenient legal refuge for high-ranking accomplices.

The Dissenting View: Anatomy of a Self-Coup

The majority opinion did not pass without fierce pushback from within the bench. Three Supreme Court justices—Justice Oh Kyung-mi, Justice Shin Sook-hee, and Justice Kim Seong-soo—issued a sharp dissenting opinion, accusing the majority of using arbitrary standards to obscure the reality of the crisis.

The three dissenting justices explicitly categorized the December 3 martial law declaration as a classic "self-coup" (친위쿠데타)—an insurrection launched from above by an elected chief executive using state force to neutralize the National Assembly’s constitutional check on presidential power. They emphasized that the judiciary had a duty to confront the overall nature of the event rather than retreat behind technical legal abstractions.

The dissent highlighted a critical one-hour gap: the window between when Han Duck-soo became aware of the martial law plan and when the Cabinet meeting formally commenced. During this period, Han had already seen the contents of Emergency Decree No. 1, which contained blatantly unconstitutional measures banning all political activities, imposing strict censorship on the press and media, and blocking the operations of Parliament.

As the second-highest official in the executive branch tasked with supervising government ministries, Han had a constitutional duty to resist. The dissenting justices noted that Han could have alerted fellow ministers to the illegality of the plan, refused to summon the meeting, or organized a joint statement of opposition. Instead, Han waited passively for ministers to arrive in Yongsan and immediately opened the meeting once quorum was reached. The gathering itself involved no meaningful debate or deliberation, lasting barely a minute in substance.

"The act of providing formal legitimacy to an unconstitutional martial law declaration while foreseeing the risk of becoming a mere tool for insurrection cannot be dismissed as a routine procedural recommendation." — Dissenting Opinion by Justices Oh Kyung-mi, Shin Sook-hee, and Kim Seong-soo

The dissent concluded that Han possessed at least "willful intent" (미필적 의사)—knowing the unconstitutional danger and choosing to facilitate the procedural facade anyway. By treating his pre-declaration actions as innocent advice, the dissent argued, the majority effectively provided a blueprint for high officials to evade accountability during future constitutional crises.

Armed Troops, Police Mobilization, and Moral Hazard

The gravity of Han’s procedural assistance becomes clearer when set against the sheer scale of military and police force deployed that night. Details from the 100-page indictment against Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol reveal a coordinated attempt to paralyze democratic oversight through armed force:

  • 1,605 Armed Soldiers: Deployed from elite units including the Special Warfare Command, the Defense Counterintelligence Command, and the Capital Defense Command.
  • 3,790 Police Officers: Mobilized from the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency and the Gyeonggi Nambu Provincial Police Agency.
  • Targeted Key Institutions: Armed forces were dispatched to seal off the National Assembly, political party headquarters, and three facilities of the National Election Commission (NEC)—its headquarters in Gwacheon, its regional office in Gwanak, and its election training center in Suwon. Plans were also uncovered to detain key political figures without warrants.

While troops were smashing windows at Parliament and citizens were stepping up to block military vehicles, the Cabinet meeting in Yongsan served as the single operational mechanism conferring an aura of legal authority on the operation. Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol himself received a confirmed 7-year prison sentence specifically for fabricating false declaration documents in connection with that meeting.

By clearing Han of treason charges for facilitating that vital first step, critics argue the Supreme Court has introduced a profound moral hazard into South Korean public administration. If a 50-year veteran of state governance can fulfill quorum requirements for an unconstitutional coup and later escape liability by claiming he offered mild verbal opposition beforehand, the legal duty of senior officials to uphold the Constitution is severely undermined.

What to Watch Next as the Retrial Begins

The legal battle now shifts back to the Seoul High Court, where a panel of appellate judges must conduct a retrial (파기환송심) under the parameters set by the Supreme Court’s majority ruling.

As the case proceeds, key legal and political developments to watch include:

  • Sentencing Adjustments at the Seoul High Court: How the appellate court will recalibrate Han Duck-soo’s punishment now that his pre-declaration Cabinet assembly charge has been remanded with a direction of acquittal, compared to his original 23-year sentence.
  • Impact on Co-Defendants: How this precedent will affect ongoing trials for other cabinet ministers and senior aides who claimed they merely attended or facilitated official meetings under executive pressure.
  • Refinement of Insurrection Standards: Whether South Korean jurisprudence will develop clearer legal frameworks specifically tailored to "self-coups" initiated by sitting executive leaders, as opposed to traditional armed rebellions from below.

South Korea’s democratic institutions ultimately survived the night of December 3 because ordinary citizens and lawmakers rushed to defend the National Assembly. But as the judicial process grinds on, the debate over Han Duck-soo’s verdict highlights an enduring tension: whether strict legal technicalities will override the fundamental duty of state leaders to protect the constitutional order when it comes under attack from within.
