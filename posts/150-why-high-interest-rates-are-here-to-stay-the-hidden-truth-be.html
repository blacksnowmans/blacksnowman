Why High Interest Rates Are Here to Stay: The Hidden Truth Behind the AI Investment Boom

September 04, 2026

If you spend any time in investor forums these days, you will inevitably run into the same tired question: "So, when are the interest rates finally coming down?" It is exhausting to watch market participants obsessively stare at charts, hanging on every word from the Federal Reserve (the U.S. central bank). They scramble to find excuses in every new inflation print or employment report, trapped in a cycle of anxiety that ignores a much larger, more structural shift.

For the last 20 years, we lived in an era defined by a glut of cheap capital. Back then, East Asian nations were hoarding U.S. Treasury bonds, fueling a "low-interest-rate party" that defined global finance. If you are still operating under that old playbook, you are making a grave mistake. The rules of the game have been rewritten. We are no longer living in a world of excess savings; we have entered an era where a massive, insatiable black hole—Artificial Intelligence—is consuming global capital at an unprecedented scale. Far from being a curse, today’s high interest rates may simply be the growing pains of a new economic epoch.

The Death of the Savings Era and the Rise of "Big AI"

To understand where we are, we must look at where we came from. In the past, export revenues from East Asia flowed into the U.S. Treasury market, keeping interest rates low and global liquidity abundant. Today, that flow has been diverted. Building a single AI data center now routinely costs trillions of won (billions of U.S. dollars). Companies are engaging in a cutthroat, capital-intensive race to establish "AI dominance," burning through astronomical amounts of capital expenditure (Capex).

The movement of capital has fundamentally changed. Where savings once flowed into real estate markets to inflate asset bubbles, today, every spare dollar is being sucked into the vacuum of AI infrastructure. Because this massive demand is being further bolstered by government deficit spending, high interest rates are not an anomaly—they are a physical inevitability. To look at these rates and conclude that "the stock market is dead" is akin to a farmer deciding to quit agriculture simply because it’s raining during the monsoon season.

  • The fundamental nature of global capital has shifted from "passive savings" to "active AI infrastructure investment."
  • High interest rates are not a sign of economic collapse, but rather a necessary cost of scaling the most significant industrial revolution of our time.
  • The winners of the next decade will be determined solely by who manages to secure control over this critical infrastructure.

Don't Be Fooled by Temporary Bottlenecks

The market’s frantic flipping back and forth ahead of Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meetings—the body responsible for setting U.S. interest rate policy—is missing the point. The absolute number of the interest rate matters far less than where that capital is being deployed. Just as the 1990s IT boom eventually triggered a massive surge in productivity, the current AI boom has the potential to push the ratio of investment to GDP well beyond 20%.

When you look at the yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury note, you are seeing a test of the market’s underlying strength. While the 5% threshold once caused widespread panic, it served as a crucible for the financial system. We need to stop overreacting to the "noise" created when legacy financial systems struggle to keep pace with this new speed. Once financial engineering catches up—such as when we see the securitization of infrastructure through products like "AI Mortgage-Backed Securities"—institutional capital will flood in, and rates will naturally stabilize. The current volatility is merely the opening chapter of this massive cycle.

We are still suffering from a "low-interest-rate hangover," holding onto the outdated belief that stocks only rise when money is cheap. In the new era of AI infrastructure, only companies that can grow while bearing the burden of high interest rates will survive. Any investment strategy that sits on the sidelines waiting for indiscriminate rate cuts is destined to underperform.

Korea’s Strategic Position in the AI Value Chain

While some point to Japan’s aggressive "state-led capitalism" as a model to emulate, the reality is that South Korea is already sitting on a gold mine. The most critical resources in the AI era are data centers, advanced semiconductors, and the massive power infrastructure—including nuclear energy—required to keep them running. How many countries in the world have the industrial capacity to provide all of these simultaneously? South Korea is already the core supply base for the global AI value chain.

The surge in investment from major Korean conglomerates starting next year will be about much more than just export numbers. The global financial system is being structured in a way that makes Korea an indispensable partner for AI infrastructure. While some investors are still fixated on real estate fluctuations or bank deposit rates, they are looking in the wrong direction. The smart money is already moving into the stock market, specifically targeting the firms at the very pinnacle of the AI supply chain.

The Bottom Line: What to Watch Next

I recently reviewed my own portfolio and had to ask myself: "Am I just waiting for the Fed Chair to speak?" The reality is that interest rate decisions are merely the downstream effects of macroeconomics; the real, fundamental changes in the world are happening on the ground, in the factories, and at the data center sites.

My advice is to stop looking for the "good old days." We are currently in a transition period. The moment financial systems fully integrate AI-driven infrastructure as a standard asset class, we will see the start of a true, massive rally. The fear currently induced by high interest rates is, for the forward-looking investor, simply a chance to buy into the future at a discount.

Will you retreat out of fear, or will you ride this monumental wave? The answer to that choice will become painfully obvious three years from now. I, for one, have chosen the latter.
