Why the Most Important Hiring Decisions Aren't About Skills

September 07, 2026

Walk into almost any modern office in Seoul today, and the scene is identical: rows of people tethered to their screens. The air is thick with the constant ping of Slack notifications, the relentless influx of automated reports, and the hum of AI tools that have quietly taken over significant chunks of our daily workload. In this hyper-efficient, digital-first environment, it is easy to lose sight of the most fundamental question: who is actually the right person for this team?

We are living in an era where technology promises to handle almost everything, yet this is precisely why human values and willpower have become the most critical variables in an organization’s success. The business theorist Jim Collins, famous for his research on what makes companies "great," argued that greatness isn't born from complex systems, but from the identity of the people within them. Lately, that reality has hit home with a renewed sense of urgency.

Skills Are Teachable; Attitudes Are Not

When searching for a "high performer," most managers prioritize immediate utility—how fast someone can use a new software tool or how quickly they can churn out a task. But in the real world, technical proficiency is a commodity. With the right training and motivation, almost any competent employee can reach a high level of technical mastery within three to six months. Once that "golden time" passes, the gap in technical output narrows significantly. What remains—and what truly differentiates the star players—is the difference in attitude.

The real issue lies in what cannot be taught: a philosophy toward work, the temperament shown when facing unexpected obstacles, and the basic humility required for genuine collaboration. Jack Welch, the former CEO of General Electric, famously prioritized weeding out those whose values clashed with the organization, even if they were technically skilled. He understood that a single person’s "negative contagion" can erode an entire team's culture.

The Paradox of Hiring

Jim Collins’ principle of "getting the right people on the bus" (hiring for character first) often faces sharp criticism in practice. Critics argue that this approach can stifle diversity and cement a "birds of a feather flock together" culture. In high-pressure, performance-driven environments, companies often become so obsessed with "cultural fit" that they inadvertently exclude the innovative "misfits" who are often the very people needed to drive real change.

When Mature Talent Opens the Window

The easiest way to spot a mature, high-value collaborator is to observe where they look when a project hits a wall. When things go well, everyone is happy to claim credit. But when a project crashes or an unexpected error occurs, a person’s true character is revealed.

Mature individuals look in the mirror. They ask, "What did I miss?" or "Was my direction flawed?" These are the people who lift those around them. Conversely, immature individuals look out the window, pointing fingers at external circumstances or other team members. Google’s internal study, Project Aristotle, famously highlighted the importance of "psychological safety"—a state that can only exist when mature team members treat mistakes not as opportunities to blame, but as opportunities to learn.

Why Talent Quits

Statistics suggest that the primary reason core talent leaves a company is not salary, but a "lack of growth." In a labor market where resignation rates are at historic highs, top performers rarely leave just because the work is hard. They leave when they feel they have nothing left to learn, or when they perceive their leader as someone who resists change. With turnover rates hovering around 2.9%, we have entered an era where companies don't just "hold" talent—leaders must grow themselves to keep their teams from walking out the door.

A Leader Who Stops Growing Kills the Organization

Ultimately, the health of an organization flows from the leader. If a leader is grappling with the exact same problems they faced a year ago, the organization is already in decline. Headhunters often report that the most common reason given by top-tier candidates for wanting to switch jobs is simply, "I have nothing left to learn here." It is a bitter pill for a leader to swallow, but it is a fact.

The recent rise of AI-driven recruitment and performance evaluation, particularly in the United States, carries a significant warning: as personnel management becomes increasingly automated, the leader’s "human touch" and personal philosophy become more important, not less. Who would want to share their values and dedicate themselves to a team led by someone who relies solely on an AI’s cold output? A leader is not just a supervisor checking off performance metrics; they must be an architect who builds a space where colleagues can safely make mistakes and grow.

A Routine for Identifying Core Talent

  • Over the past year, has this person been a catalyst for the team’s growth?
  • When facing failure, does this person look in the mirror or out the window?
  • If this person resigned tomorrow, how much would it actually disrupt my sense of security for the team?
  • Have I clearly distinguished between a person who is "bad" and a person who is simply in a "bad position"?

What to Watch Next

As we move further into an AI-integrated workplace, watch for the shift in how "leadership" is defined. We are likely to see a split in corporate culture: firms that double down on algorithmic efficiency, and firms that pivot toward radical human mentorship. The latter will be the ones that attract the talent that matters. If you are a leader, your task is clear: stop looking at the spreadsheets for a moment and look at the reflection in the glass. The growth of your team depends not on the tools you implement, but on whether you are providing a space where your people are empowered to become better versions of themselves.
