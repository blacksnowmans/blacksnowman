Beyond the Headlines: Why the Myth of China's Imminent Collapse is Dangerous for South Korea

October 29, 2026

Scroll through YouTube or online forums, and you will inevitably stumble upon sensational thumbnails teasing the "truth" behind rumors that Chinese President Xi Jinping has been ousted. These videos paint a vivid picture of an imminent internal coup within the Chinese Communist Party, leading many to believe that the regime is on the brink of total collapse. But how much of this digital chatter is grounded in reality?

The short answer is that these claims are closer to wishful thinking than objective analysis. By examining the sober insights of scholars like Professor Cho Young-nam, we can begin to strip away the emotional myths surrounding China and confront a much more uncomfortable reality: China's societal resilience is far stronger than many outsiders care to admit.

The Flashpoint of the Taiwan Strait and American Calculus

Anxieties over a potential military conflict in the Taiwan Strait remain a fixture of international headlines. However, assessing this risk requires a clear-eyed look at the stakes, particularly regarding the United States. When asked whether Washington would automatically intervene militarily if a conflict broke out, the answer is far from a simple yes or no—it entirely depends on a high-stakes calculation by American leadership.

If the U.S. decides against intervening, the security architecture of East Asia built over decades could unravel overnight, shattering alliances beyond repair. Conversely, if Washington chooses to fight, the conflict risks spiraling uncontrollably. China would likely strike U.S. bases in Okinawa and surrounding areas, threatening to drag South Korea and Japan into a devastating, full-scale regional war. Recent polling within Taiwan reveals that fewer than 20% of citizens genuinely believe the U.S. would automatically intervene. Yet the question persists because Washington must weigh whether defending Taiwan is worth the catastrophic risks involved.

Beijing views holding onto Taiwan not merely as a territorial ambition, but through three existential pillars: military security, political legitimacy, and maritime control. If Taiwan were to declare independence and host U.S. naval forces, China’s southern maritime access would be choked off, threatening its strategic depth. Furthermore, the Chinese Communist Party's claim to power rests on the historical narrative of revolutionary victory; leaving Taiwan's status unresolved undermines that core legitimacy. Finally, control over the South China Sea is directly tied to China's economic survival. Backed by its Anti-Secession Law, Beijing has codified the conditions under which it will use force, meaning the leadership will not let go of the Taiwan issue easily.

Deconstructing the Myth of the Chinese Coup

Rumors of a coup or Xi Jinping's downfall are continuously recycled across social media. Yet exhaustive tracking of major media outlets in the U.S., Japan, and Taiwan reveals zero credible evidence to support these claims. They are fueled less by facts and more by a collective hope that China will simply implode.

Observers often point to China’s stark internal economic disparities—where per capita GDP hovers around $30,000 in cosmopolitan hubs like Shanghai but lingers near $10,000 in the interior provinces—and ask why a popular uprising has not materialized. The answer lies in how Chinese citizens process discontent. Rather than blaming the overarching socialist system, people tend to localize their frustrations, blaming corrupt local party cadres or citing regional peculiarities. In a single-party state where systemic alternatives carry catastrophic costs, citizens choose adaptation over revolution. Over the past four decades, roughly 300 million migrant workers have moved from the countryside to cities in search of a better life, acting as a massive pressure-release valve for societal grievances.

For a true rebellion to happen, a population needs grievances, a viable alternative, and the conviction that the alternative will govern better. Right now, no alternative political force exists inside China. Moreover, compared to where they were five years ago, ordinary Chinese citizens still perceive incremental improvements in their personal lives. Projecting democratic models onto a sprawling continental state like China remains a profound analytical mistake.

The Geopolitical Chessboard and the Limits of U.S. Power

Ever since the 2008 global financial crisis, Beijing’s leadership has operated under the firm conviction that the United States is a declining power. Moving through phases of expanding core interests, pushing back against aggressive U.S. trade policies, and emphasizing a renewed "spirit of struggle" at recent Party Congresses, Beijing has steadily prepared for a long game, aiming to expand its nuclear arsenal significantly by 2030.

A Sharp Warning: Dismissing China's resilience or praying for its sudden collapse is a dangerous strategic trap. Ignoring objective facts in favor of emotional satisfaction is the quickest way for a nation to miscalculate its foreign policy.

The current U.S.-China rivalry is not a short-term squabble; it will define the global landscape for decades. Compounding this friction is a messy, multi-layered multipolar order. Driven in part by a more isolationist American foreign policy focused on domestic priorities, regional powers are carving out their own spheres of influence. From military deterrence to the race for artificial intelligence supremacy, the world is entering an era of fierce, uncompromising competition where state control will inevitably dictate corporate behavior.

South Korea’s Strategic Imperative in a Multipolar World

For roughly two decades, South public discourse on China has often swung between emotional hostility and groundless optimism. Neither approach helps Seoul navigate its complex neighborhood. With China running massive annual trade surpluses, the notion that this economic behemoth will collapse or become entirely isolated overnight is an economic fantasy.

Under the current administration of President Lee Jae-myung, South Korea faces a daunting diplomatic balancing act. Following the diplomatic isolation left in the wake of the impeachment and imprisonment of former leader Yoon Suk Yeol, Seoul must move past rigid partisan politics. Treating China with simplistic disdain or uncritical acceptance will prove equally fatal to South Korea's future.

As the international order fractures into competing poles, South Korea's survival depends on agile, pragmatic diplomacy. Seoul must cooperate where interests align and stand firm where they diverge, refusing to let wishful thinking obscure the harsh realities of great power competition.
