How South Korea's Ruling Party Lost Its Soul: Betrayal, Fandoms, and the Crisis of the Democratic Left

September 10, 2026

There is a peculiar silence that follows a hard-fought election victory. While the victors celebrate under flashing lights and confetti, a different kind of quiet settles over the foot soldiers—the volunteers, donors, and long-time believers who poured their own time and money into the campaign. When the power they fought to secure finally arrives, but brings with it cold indifference or outright scorn from the newly enthroned, a profound disorientation sets in. Watching political commentary in South Korea recently, a single, devastating phrase captured this sentiment: "We were conned." On the surface, the ruling progressive bloc looks like a dominant force. But beneath the institutional victories lies a rotting interior of betrayed supporters, plummeting trust, and a sharp, qualitative decay in political culture.

The Anatomy of Betrayal Among Progressive Supporters

In South Korean politics, the most lethal weapon is not a failed policy, but the emotional desolation of realizing you were used as a disposable tool. This cuts much deeper than a disagreement over real estate taxation or the pace of judicial reform. These are voters who passionately fought to elect Lee Jae-myung—now South Korea's president—only to find themselves suddenly branded as internal enemies or "anti-Lee" dissidents by the party's ruling clique. Without warning, loyal citizens find themselves targets of friendly fire, attacked by pro-government commentators and self-proclaimed loyalists.

At first, supporters rationalized the friction as a misunderstanding. But as weeks turned into months, a grim pattern emerged: President Lee's camp had used them as a ladder to power, and now that the ladder was no longer needed, it was being kicked away. The cruelty lies not just in the abandonment, but in the hostility of it. Every retweet and online jab serves as a digital paper-cut, leaving voters wondering why the party they bled for now seems to take pleasure in their pain. The same activists who once defended past liberal figures from malicious attacks now find themselves on the receiving end of insults from politicians who happily stepped over them to grab a title. The true driver behind the administration's plunging approval ratings isn't a statistical blip; it is a foundational fracture of trust.

Recent polling underscores this political gravity. President Lee's job approval rating recently entered a "death cross," marking five consecutive weeks of decline. The drop has been particularly steep across demographic pillars, spreading rapidly from voters in their 60s down to the 40s and younger. These danger signals, amplified by local election defeats, serve as a blunt notification from the public that patience has run out. Meanwhile, the Democratic Party of Korea (the DP, South Korea's main liberal party) remains deeply fractured. Following a national convention where Kim Min-seok was elected party leader without a clean majority, the leadership lineup reflects a tense, fragile standoff between pro-presidential factions. When a party has to scramble its top brass into emergency meetings just to keep internal factionalism from blowing the roof off, it becomes clear that shifting public sentiment cannot be pacified by backroom damage control alone.

The Rise of Raw Aspiration and the Loss of Political Decorum

For decades, the core of the South Korean progressive movement was sustained by voters who possessed a high degree of democratic consciousness—people who gave freely of their resources not out of blind obedience, but because they believed the DP stood for higher moral ideals and public good. Because these voters kept the party anchored, the center-left maintained a reputation as a moral force in politics.

That safety net has frayed. Eager to consolidate power, the party began welcoming figures from conservative backgrounds who brought ambition and opportunism rather than progressive values. The result is a coarsening of political language that leaves long-term supporters experiencing deep cognitive dissonance. When progressive lawmakers openly hurl venomous attacks at popular independent political commentators—such as recent public feuds involving figures like lawmaker Choi Hyuk-jin and broadcaster Kim Eo-jun—it exposes a culture where internal fratricide is rewarded. Supporters are left asking a painful question: Is this the democracy we broke our backs to build?

Online spaces have accelerated this rot. Platforms and party member communities, originally pitched as tools for grassroots electronic democracy, have devolved into digital colosseums where extreme fandoms hurl abuse at anyone who deviates from the party line. Nuanced debate has been choked out by a binary, cultish worldview: you are either an absolute loyalist or an existential enemy. When a political party strips away even the pretense of institutional decorum and reduces itself to naked, unvarnished ambition, it stops being a political home and becomes nothing more than an interest group wearing the clothes of a social movement.

Youth Alienation and the Epidemic of Opportunistic Loyalty

If older generations feel heartbroken, South Korea's youth look at the ruling party with a cold, piercing cynicism. Young voters are well aware that neither major party holds a monopoly on virtue, but the DP stings more because of its self-righteous posturing. Younger generations frequently mock the party's moral posturing, noting that at least the conservative opposition is transparent about its cynicism. When a political faction that built its brand on moral superiority discards its ethical armor to gorge on institutional power, the resulting disconnect drives young voters away from the ballot box entirely.

This opportunism is matched inside the halls of the National Assembly, where independent lawmakers scramble to jump on the ruling party bandwagon. Opportunists twist themselves into knots to prove their loyalty, remaining silent in the face of embarrassing scandals out of fear of missing out on the next nomination cycle. When lawmakers abandon basic principles for a guaranteed ballot spot, ordinary citizens feel a profound sense of disillusionment. To the political elite, the only thing that matters is the next badge of office; to the public, this naked careerism is dragging the entire political ecosystem into moral bankruptcy.

Looking Ahead: The Mirror Test for South Korea's Progressives

The internal warfare and voter hemorrhaging shaking South Korea's progressive camp are symptoms of a systemic institutional crisis. When reformist movements capture state power, the intoxicating nature of authority often blinds them to the ground-level reality outside the palace walls. The platforms built to empower rank-and-file members have instead become echo chambers of ideological purity tests and factional hatred.

For observers watching South Korean politics from afar, the current turbulence offers a clear lesson on the fragility of democratic governance when power replaces purpose. Reform that forgets its ethical core inevitably curdles into self-preservation. As the administration and its ruling party face mounting public skepticism, the path forward will depend entirely on whether they have the courage to look into the mirror—stop blaming shifting statistics, stop silencing internal critics, and confront how far they have drifted from the ideals that brought them to power. If they refuse, the political reckoning waiting at the next ballot box will be merciless.
