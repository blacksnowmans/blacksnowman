Inside the 23-Year Battle to Rebuild Seoul's Most Infamous Apartment Complex

October 05, 2026

For anyone watching South Korea's volatile housing market, the name Eunma Apartment carries an almost mythic weight. Located in Daechi-dong—the undisputed epicenter of Seoul's hyper-competitive private education industry—the sprawling complex has long been viewed as the ultimate symbol of Gangnam wealth, speculative fever, and bureaucratic gridlock. When news broke that Eunma had finally secured project implementation approval after a staggering 23-year standoff, it was greeted not with pure celebration, but with the collective, white-knuckled tension of people bracing for a shockwave. This is not just about tearing down old concrete; it is the opening salvo of a seismic shift that threatens to rattle South Korea's entire housing ecosystem.

Crossing the Highest Administrative Mountain

Originally approved for a promotion committee all the way back in December 2003, Eunma has taken longer to reach this milestone than even some of Seoul's most notoriously delayed redevelopment zones. South Korea's urban renewal process is a grueling gauntlet—spanning district designation, safety diagnoses, committee formation, union establishment, builder selection, project implementation, management disposal plans, tenant relocation, demolition, and final construction. Of all these hurdles, the project implementation phase is widely considered the steepest mountain.

By clearing this administrative hurdle, Eunma has effectively closed the door on its regulatory purgatory. The blueprint ahead envisions replacing the aging buildings with a sleek, 49-story mega-complex housing roughly 5,800 units, achieved by accepting conditional density bonuses that mandate public housing space. Yet, behind the gleaming architectural renderings lies a harsh financial reality. Due to skyrocketing construction costs, the projected return rate for current owners sits in the mid-to-high 80% range. Controversies surrounding integrated cost calculations for apartments and commercial spaces—with disputes hovering around 9.7 billion won (about $7 million)—have left union members sweating. The grandiose promise of a 49-story skyscraper means that the financial burden landing on individual owners will be heavier than expected.

The Looming Relocation Tsunami and Tenant Anxiety

The developer's roadmap aims to wrap up member apartment selections and hit management disposal approval in time to kick off tenant relocation by the first half of 2027. However, the logistics of clearing out over 4,400 households in a single stroke are staggering. Industry insiders view 2026 as a critical watershed year for Seoul's real estate sector, with an estimated 150,000 households preparing to move out across various urban renewal projects over a four-year window. Eunma's looming exodus threatens to trigger a severe supply crunch.

What makes Eunma uniquely delicate is its demographic makeup: roughly 60% to 70% of current residents are tenants, not owners. While homeowners are eager to cash in on future asset values, tenants face a cold reality. Renters in Daechi-dong are overwhelmingly families anchored to the neighborhood's famous cram schools (*hagwons*). For these parents, sudden eviction is an assault on their children's educational survival. Because South Korea's housing laws classify private reconstruction projects differently from public redevelopments, tenants in projects like Eunma are not legally entitled to mandatory moving expenses or relocation compensation. While the courts consistently rule that owners have the right to clear out tenants once management disposal plans are approved, wielding the law like a blunt instrument risks sparking bitter physical clashes and costly delays.

War in the Courtyard: The Commercial Complex and Legal Ghosts

Beyond the residential towers, Eunma harbors another massive obstacle right in its center: the Eunma Commercial Complex. Far from a standard corner market, this multi-story hub functions almost like a local shopping mall, hosting hundreds of operating businesses and hundreds of property-holding members. A sharp conflict of interest divides them: apartment owners fear that allocating new units to commercial owners will slash general sales revenue, while commercial tenants view redevelopment as a death sentence for their livelihoods.

Complicating matters further is a lingering legal ghost from South Korea's corporate history. Heirs of Jung Tae-soo, the disgraced former chairman of the collapsed Hanbo Group, have mounted a legal challenge over commercial ownership rights tied to basement bomb shelters registered under Jung's name back in 1979. Having won favorable rulings in lower courts, the family threatens to muscle its way into the union membership pool. If the courts ultimately side with them, the entire ownership structure of the commercial complex could be thrown into chaos, adding a high-stakes corporate scandal to an already volatile housing project.

What to Watch Next

As Eunma Apartment enters its next phase, several concrete milestones will dictate whether this multi-decade endeavor succeeds or dissolves into further chaos:

  • The 2026 Management Disposal Timeline: Watch for whether the union can successfully finalize member applications and push through management disposal without bogging down in court battles.
  • Commercial Settlement Outcomes: Keep an eye on negotiations between the apartment union and the 388 commercial property owners. Any escalation into lawsuits or extreme protests like those seen in other major Seoul rebuilds will instantly stall progress.
  • Daechi-Dong Jeonse (Lump-Sum Deposit) Prices: Monitor local rental market data. The exact moment Eunma's 4,400+ households receive final eviction notices, look for a sharp spike in lease prices across neighboring Daechi-dong as displaced families scramble for alternative housing.
  • Eviction Enforcement and Lawsuits: Pay attention to how the union handles holdout tenants. A heavy-handed reliance on forced eviction lawsuits will signal potential public relations disasters and physical blockades.

Eunma's long-awaited step forward proves that even the most entrenched urban symbols can eventually move—but the path from blueprint to reality is paved with staggering financial and social costs.
