Why South Korea's Opposition Is Squandering Its Chances as Presidential Ratings Stagnate

September 01, 2026

When you look at South Korean politics today, it is hard not to feel a sense of fatigue. Reports that presidential approval ratings are trapped in the 30 percent range no longer come as a shock. But the real crisis is not the numbers themselves—it is the qualitative erosion of the political base. Even unconditional fandoms are turning their backs or becoming openly critical, signaling a deeper alienation that goes far beyond simple policy disagreements.

The True Meaning of the 30 Percent Approval Ceiling

Is a 30 percent approval rating just another statistic? Far from it. It functions as a final warning light from the public. While politicians once comforted themselves by calling the 30 percent mark a "concrete base" of steadfast support, that foundation is now cracking. With core supporters shrinking to a fraction of their peak levels, the administration's momentum for governance is effectively running on empty.

This is no longer about minor grievances over real estate policy or the stock market. The core issue is that disappointment with leadership has hardened into a deep-seated veto sentiment. When an electorate operates under the pre-existing assumption that "nothing this government does will work," no policy initiative can break through the noise.

Internal Power Struggles and the Illusion of Control

Inside the Democratic Party of Korea—South Korea's primary liberal opposition party—the situation is even more chaotic. Rather than settling down after its national convention, internal conflict has only intensified. Under the leadership of party heavyweights like Kim Min-seok, the new leadership has swept through organizational, strategic, and administrative posts to tighten its grip on the apparatus. But is this consolidation actually building unity?

Instead, rank-and-file members view these unilateral appointments with suspicion rather than mere disappointment. Lawmakers watch their steps, rendering independent voices virtually non-existent, while internal sniping continues unabated. The party leadership has launched over 30 separate task forces and even its own TV broadcasting initiatives in the name of expanding its political footprint, but these flashy performances do little to win over a skeptical public.

The Politics of Control Versus True Communication

A closer look at how the Democratic Party structures its inner circle reveals an overwhelming tilt toward consolidating the party leader's authority. Replacing key staffers put in place by predecessors just months prior has prompted quiet grumbling among party insiders that the moves are too transparently about power accumulation rather than collective strategy.

On sensitive issues like judicial and prosecutorial reform, where diverse internal viewpoints should be filtered and debated, the party has closed its own communication channels in the name of maintaining a "single line" of command. This lack of internal tolerance raises a fundamental question: If a leadership cannot manage dissenting opinions within its own ranks, how can it hope to persuade the broader electorate in major national elections?

Prosecutorial reform is undoubtedly a historic task for the progressive movement, but the execution has misfired. When internal voices call for a more measured pace and are summarily ignored, it only accelerates internal fracturing. In politics, the legitimacy of the process matters just as much as the stated justification. Reforms that fail to convince the party's own members will ultimately only exhaust the public.

Moving Past Performative Politics to Rebuild Trust

Current South Korean politics expends an enormous amount of energy on "playing at work." Does creating dozens of new task forces or launching a proprietary television channel genuinely change people's daily lives? Rather than structural rebranding, what is required is a fundamental shift in attitude—dropping the monopoly on power and genuinely listening to the grassroots.

The Democratic Party's stated goal to recruit moderate and conservative talent to expand its big tent is a sound direction in theory. However, attempting to build a broad coalition without internal cohesion is like building a castle on sand. Externally, citizens are no longer fooled by glamorous party logos or a deluge of press releases. If an opposition cannot offer substantive answers on living costs, inflation, and job security, even the most elaborate institutional creations will be dismissed as political theater.

What to Watch Next

As political observers monitor the coming months in Seoul, several concrete indicators will show whether this crisis deepens or begins to turn around:

  • Shifts in Core Demographics: Watch for whether approval ratings break decisively below the 30 percent floor, or if critical defections spread from enthusiastic loyalists to broader voter blocs.
  • Internal Dissent and Legislative Clashes: Pay attention to how the party leadership handles upcoming votes on contentious legislation, particularly whether dissenting voices within the opposition are permanently sidelined or accommodated.
  • Policy Focus Over PR: Track whether the leadership actually scales back its performative initiatives—such as high-profile task forces and media ventures—in favor of legislative compromises on livelihood issues like housing and inflation.

True political leadership does not stem from fortifying internal control, but from relieving the anxieties of party members and the broader public. Until political elites realize that substance matters more than packaging, superficial changes will remain nothing more than background noise.
