The Han Dong-hoon Family Scandals and the Enduring Myth of South Korean Prosecutorial Immunity

October 09, 2026

To understand modern South Korean politics, you have to abandon the comforting western fiction that a country's legal system stands above its political class. Day after day, headlines out of Seoul remind citizens of a simple, grim reality: in the higher echelons of the state, the law is not a shield protecting the public, but a flexible instrument wielded by an interconnected elite to protect its own.

For decades, the South Korean prosecution service operated as a virtually unchecked leviathan, combining police, prosecutorial, and indictment powers into an institution so powerful it routinely intimidated presidents. When investigative journalist Chang In-soo recently unpacked the inner workings of this system, the resulting revelations exposed far more than individual misconduct. They laid bare a self-perpetuating prosecutorial cartel—a network where family ties, economic collusion, and systemic self-censorship allow powerful figures to evade accountability entirely.

When Enforcing the Law Becomes a Crime

To grasp how deeply compromised the system is, one only has to look at the bizarre legal ordeal of Jung Jin-woong, a senior prosecutor who once headed the 1st Criminal Division at the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office. In 2020, Jung was leading an investigation into Han Dong-hoon—then a high-ranking prosecutor and later a major conservative political figure—over a high-profile media-prosecution collusion scandal. Armed with a valid court-issued search warrant, Jung attempted to seize Han’s smartphone to preserve potential digital evidence.

What followed read like a black comedy about the absurdity of Korean power structures. As Jung moved to secure the phone to prevent data deletion, Han physically resisted. A scuffle ensued, and both men ended up on the floor. In any normal functioning state, an investigator preventing a suspect from destroying evidence during a lawful search is simply doing his job. Instead, the target of the search turned the tables, filing criminal charges of physical abuse against the investigator.

Miraculously, the state apparatus obliged. Jung Jin-woong was actually indicted, hauled into court, and subjected to a grueling prosecution—a stark illustration of the inverted morality of the Korean justice system, where doing your job right as a prosecutor becomes a crime if it targets the wrong person.

Years later, the Supreme Court finally acquitted Jung, ruling that his actions constituted lawful execution of duties. Yet the institutional scars remain. The indictment itself had been rubber-stamped by senior prosecutors like Kim Ji-woong, demonstrating how readily the legal machinery bends its own rules to shield favored insiders. An honest prosecutor faces ruin, while those who protect the powerful coast to promotion. This is the structural rot that makes true prosecutorial reform an existential necessity for South Korean democracy.

Family Ties and Cover-Ups: The Jin Dong-gyun Case

If the harassment of an honest investigator exposes the corruption of the prosecution's institutional culture, the private behavior of its elite reveals something far darker. In 2015, a junior female prosecutor came forward with a horrifying account of workplace sexual violence. Her abuser was Jin Dong-gyun, an elite prosecutor marked out for a stellar career.

The details of Jin's behavior are stomach-turning: forcing kisses in taxis, sliding hands under undergarments to grope victims, and even exposing himself in front of a colleague. Similar assaults took place in motels and karaoke bars. In a normal workplace, this would mean immediate arrest and public disgrace.

Inside the South Korean prosecution service, however, it meant a quiet exit. There was no disciplinary hearing, no public trial, and no criminal record. Jin was simply allowed to resign quietly, immediately walking out of the public sector to land a lucrative position as a legal executive at the CJ Group, one of South Korea's massive family-run conglomerates. Such an escape hatch is impossible without heavy protection from the top.

It was only years later, catalyzed by the global #MeToo movement and courageous whistleblowers like prosecutor Seo Ji-hyun, that the case was finally forced into a real courtroom. Jin was eventually sentenced to 10 months in prison and taken into custody. Yet even then, his arrogance remained intact. He petitioned the Constitutional Court, arguing bizarrely that forcible molestation laws were unconstitutional because "he hadn't used violence or threats, only surprise groping"—a semantic defense so grotesque it was summarily dismissed by the court.

Why was Jin so brazen? The answer lies in his pedigree. His father is Jin Hyung-goo, a former high-ranking prosecutor notorious for past political scandals, and his brother-in-law is none other than Han Dong-hoon. To this elite legal dynasty, the law was never a binding social contract; it was a family shield.

Stock Manipulation and the Economic Arm of the Cartel

The corruption of this prosecutorial aristocracy has never been confined to workplace misconduct or bureaucratic turf wars; it extends deep into financial crime. In 2022, investigative reports revealed explosive links between Han Dong-hoon's family and the "Newall Corp" stock manipulation scandal.

While shady operators were artificially inflating stock prices through shell companies, the names of Jin Hyung-goo and Jin Dong-gyun repeatedly popped up on corporate registries. The elder Jin was listed as a director of a company created by the stock manipulators, while the younger Jin—the disgraced former prosecutor—was registered as a director and auditor for another connected firm.

Most flagrantly, when these stock-manipulating entities executed third-party paid-in capital increases—a lucrative financial maneuver designed to funnel unearned wealth to chosen insiders—Jin Dong-gyun's name was right there on the beneficiary list. Think about that: a former prosecutor, sworn to uphold financial integrity, acting as an economic partner to professional stock manipulators. For ordinary citizens drowning in economic anxiety, watching the guardians of the law quietly enrich themselves through market fraud is a source of profound, systemic rage.

The same pattern of institutional protection appeared when police attempted to investigate allegations of resume padding and essay ghostwriting involving Han Dong-hoon’s daughter, which threatened to undermine public trust in South Korea's fiercely competitive education system. When the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency formally requested international legal cooperation from the Ministry of Justice to verify documents submitted to MIT, the paperwork simply vanished into a bureaucratic black hole, with the Ministry claiming no record of the request ever existed. The machinery of state quietly shifted into reverse to protect its own.

The Perils of Opportunism Under the Current Administration

As the administration of President Lee Jae-myung attempts to steer the country forward, these old ghosts continue to haunt the machinery of state. Reforming powerful investigative agencies is never simply a matter of passing new legislation or changing organizational charts; it requires placing individuals of unyielding integrity at the helm.

Yet politics often rewards the flexible. The recent controversy surrounding the nomination of figures like Kim Ji-yong to lead the newly reorganized Investigation Bureau has sparked deep anxiety among reformers. Observers of South Korean officialdom know the type well: opportunistic bureaucrats who bend with the political wind faster than grass in a gale, eager to please whoever holds the levers of state power. Placing such figures at the head of powerful new investigative bodies risks reproducing the exact authoritarian abuses past administrations spent years trying to dismantle.

This risk is compounded by institutional vacuums. With key security and law enforcement posts—including the heads of the National Police Agency and the Prosecutors' Office—remaining vacant for extended periods, the government sends deeply mixed signals. When the public sees politically compromised figures being pushed into powerful investigative slots while crucial oversight posts sit empty, it awakens a very old, very cold fear.

As veteran political commentator Yu Si-min has warned, the subtle scent of institutional arrogance is dangerous for any administration. South Korea's current progressive government owes its existence to the candlelight revolution that swept away the corrupt authoritarianism of the past. If it stumbles by tolerating old guard opportunists or shielding institutional decay, public patience will inevitably run out.

What to Watch Next

As this political and legal drama unfolds, several concrete milestones will indicate whether meaningful reform is actually taking place or simply being performed for the cameras:

  • The fate of investigative agency appointments: Watch closely to see whether controversial nominees with deep ties to the old prosecutorial establishment are successfully confirmed or quietly dropped in the face of public pushback.
  • Progress on pending structural reforms: Track how the National Assembly handles the actual reorganization and decentralization of prosecutorial powers—specifically whether independent oversight mechanisms are given real teeth or merely window dressing.
  • Handling of institutional backlogs: Observe whether long-stalled investigations into high-profile white-collar and family-connected financial scandals finally move forward or remain quietly buried in bureaucratic limbo.

True reform does not happen through grand slogans or cosmetic name-changes. It begins the moment a government decides that no family name, elite university pedigree, or prosecutorial badge is powerful enough to place someone above the law. If Seoul's current leaders fail to grasp this simple truth, the public's quiet fury will eventually transform into a wave they cannot control.

"The law must be an impartial mirror, but for decades, the prosecutor's blade always managed to miss its own neck."

The question remains: whose hand will finally hold the knife that breaches the fortress of entrenched privilege?
