How a 37-Year-Old Abandoned Korean Building Was Reborn as a Smart Multi-Family Home

September 27, 2026

Walking through the older alleyways of South Korea, you often find yourself wondering: When is someone finally going to tear that down and start over? That is usually the only thought that comes to mind when you encounter a decaying residential building that has weathered nearly four decades of freezing winters and humid monsoons. In a country obsessed with shiny new high-rise apartment complexes, buildings past the 30-year mark are typically written off as architectural dead ends—destined for the wrecking ball.

When you look closely at a multi-family housing unit built in 1990, the symptoms of age are everywhere: faded red bricks, crumbling decorative railings, a chaotic maze of exterior staircases, and, worst of all, the dreaded semi-basement floor that reliably floods every time a heavy summer rain hits. Logically, the standard playbook says you demolish it, clear the rubble, and build a brand-new box. But watching a total transformation unfold over the span of just four months completely shatters that assumption. This was not a quick coat of paint and some fresh wallpaper. Through structural reinforcement, clever vertical expansion, and smart engineering, a decrepit 37-year-old relic was given an entirely new lease on life.

From Blight to Modern Masterpiece: Exterior and Structural Makeover

Erasing Decades of Wear with Modern Design Choices

The past life of this 1990-built multi-family house was far from charming. The exterior staircases blurred the lines between the owner's unit and the rented apartments, compromising privacy while dragging down the visual appeal of the entire neighborhood. Worse still, the semi-basement level was a chronic flood risk. Yet, the owner and the construction team chose a much harder, more sustainable path: extending the building's lifespan through a massive remodeling project rather than opting for a carbon-heavy demolition and rebuild.

Executed over an intense four-month period, the exterior overhaul relied on a striking contrast of crisp white and warm brown tones to project a sophisticated urban aesthetic. Recognizing that parking is non-negotiable in modern South Korean cities, the design team even managed to carve out two dedicated parking spaces on a remarkably tight plot. At night, integrated, subtle lighting washes over the facade, making the 37-year-old framework look thoroughly contemporary. The old terracotta tiles and stained red bricks are completely gone, replaced by a clean, signature "slot design"—two thin, vertical recessed grooves stamped uniformly across the perimeter walls and gates to give rhythm and visual identity to an otherwise flat mass.

Solving the Privacy Puzzle Through Smart Zoning

One of the hardest parts of remodeling older South Korean multi-family homes is sorting out the circulation—who walks where, and how strangers and residents cross paths. Previously, scattered outdoor stairs meant everyone shared overlapping spaces. To fix this, the construction crew added targeted exterior partition walls that cleanly separate the owner’s private zone from the rental units. The ground floor, the mini-gate entry, and the semi-basement apartments now have entirely independent paths that never intersect.

Space was also aggressively maximized within the limits of South Korea’s strict floor area ratio and building coverage laws. Protruding sections of the exterior were legally registered as expansions, maximizing usable indoor square footage. Meanwhile, the second and third floors were knitted together via an internal main staircase, creating a duplex owner's unit that mimics the feel of a standalone suburban home.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • A 37-year-old multi-family house was completely modernized into a sleek, functional urban asset in just four months.
  • Strategic exterior partitions and legal expansions cleanly separated the owner's duplex from five separate rental units.
  • A unified "slot design" pattern was applied across the facade to establish a distinct architectural identity.

Defeating the Semi-Basement Flood Curse with Smart Engineering

Living Under the Constant Threat of Summer Monsoons

In low-lying urban neighborhoods across South Korea, semi-basement apartments (*banjiha*) carry a heavy psychological toll. During the heavy monsoon rains of June and July, water frequently backs up into these ground-floor units. As the project's lead contractor noted, getting control of water intrusion was the absolute make-or-break condition of the entire build. No amount of interior decorating matters if your living room floor turns into a swimming pool.

While local governments in cities like Seoul hand out subsidized flood barriers and backflow prevention valves, safety ultimately relies on a building's internal plumbing network. For the tenants of this building, past summers meant watching water seep through floorboards and pool around kitchen cabinets—a chronic source of anxiety.

The Secret Sump Pump System Hidden Inside a Kitchen Cabinet

To win the war against water, the construction team bypassed superficial surface sealants in favor of heavy-duty physical drainage mechanics. Inside the semi-basement units, right beneath the kitchen sink, lies a hidden weapon. To the casual observer, opening the cabinet doors reveals an ordinary storage space. But lifting a concealed panel on the floor exposes a dedicated drainage sump pump system.

When heavy rains cause groundwater to rise or back up through the pipes, water flows naturally along a sub-floor drainage board into this hidden basin. Once the water hits a specific threshold, a plugged-in automatic drainage pump kicks on and violently ejects the water outside. It is an ingenious, invisible piece of plumbing engineering that completely neutralizes flood risks without ruining the interior aesthetics.

🔥 Engineering Out the Semi-Basement Threat

In old-building renovations, aesthetic updates pale in comparison to structural and utility safety. Semi-basement flooding is a literal life-or-death hazard in Korean cities. Relying solely on municipal handouts is not enough; builders must integrate proactive drainage pits and automatic pumps right into the architectural blueprint.

True safety is never guaranteed by good intentions—it is secured through rigorous mechanical design.

Inside the Owner's Duplex: IoT Living Meets the 'Super Mirror'

Upgrading a 1990s Frame to Smart-Home Standards

Once the foundation and rental units were secured, the renovation shifted to the owner's duplex spanning the second and third floors. The guiding directive was simple: make a 37-year-old frame operate like a brand-new apartment where everything can be managed from a smartphone.

Step into the living room, and a modern ceiling fan spins beneath a fully integrated Internet of Things (IoT) environment. Every light, climate control unit, and ventilation hatch syncs seamlessly with wall pads and mobile applications. You can kill all the lights in the house, check air quality, and adjust the heating straight from bed. It proves that an old skeleton can easily house a futuristic nervous system if you are willing to completely gut and replace the internal wiring and communication lines.

Turning a Load-Bearing Obstacle into a Design Feature

Every renovation hits a brick wall—sometimes literally. When remodeling older floor plans, architects frequently encounter unmovable structural columns that chop up the space. In this house, a massive structural pillar sat squarely in the middle of the kitchen pathway.

Instead of trying to hide it, the design team embraced it. They wrapped the column in an L-shape and finished the entire surface in "Super Mirror"—a heavily polished, highly reflective stainless steel material. Rather than feeling blocked off, the kitchen suddenly feels twice as large as the mirror bounces light and surrounding reflections across the room. Paired with a sleek black-and-silver built-in kitchen layout and a continuous ceramic dining island, a fatal architectural flaw was turned into the home's most striking visual anchor.

💭 Turning Structural Limits Into Art

We all face unchangeable constraints in life, much like an immovable load-bearing column in an old house. But rather than fighting or lamenting the restriction, wrapping it in a super mirror to expand your world offers a profound design philosophy. Architecture, at its core, is not just about fixing physical rooms; it is about retraining how we perceive our limitations.

Micro-Zoning and Space Optimization: The Aesthetics of Storage

Squeezing Maximum Utility Out of Tight Quarters

Behind the glossy main kitchen lies a masterclass in space efficiency. Sliding open a sleek black louvered door reveals a compact utility room that packs an astonishing amount of function into a tiny footprint. Built-in refrigerators, deep storage closets for bulky vacuum cleaners, a hand-washing basin, and the central IoT hub are slotted tightly together.

Above it all, a heat-recovery ventilator ensures fresh air circulates continuously even when the doors remain sealed shut. It is a textbook example of how to handle tight layouts: instead of sacrificing features, you chop storage requirements down into micro-units and maximize density.

Plumbing Access Hatches and 200-Kilogram Shower Benches

In compact Korean homes, routing pipes for stacked washer-dryer towers usually creates an ugly visual mess. Here, the contractors built a discreet access hatch beneath the main bathroom to centralize and service complex plumbing lines without exposing them.

Another standout feature is the bathroom's universal design: a reinforced concrete shower bench built to handle up to 200 kilograms (about 440 lbs). Designed to accommodate aging residents or anyone needing to sit down safely while washing, it proves this home was built around the physical realities of human bodies, not just real estate trends.

💡 Space Optimization Checklist

  • A secondary kitchen packs sub-appliances, cleaning gear, an IoT panel, and ventilation systems behind compact louver doors.
  • A concealed bathroom access hatch keeps essential plumbing serviceable without ruining tight spatial layouts.
  • A heavy-duty concrete shower bench introduces true universal design capable of supporting massive loads safely.

Roof Waterproofing and the Financial Reality of a 7-Unit Transformation

The Grid Waterproofing Panel System

The ultimate test of any residential renovation in South Korea happens on the roof. Traditional polyurethane or sheet waterproofing coats inevitably crack and peel over time, leading to dreaded ceiling leaks. For this project, the team bypassed conventional methods entirely, installing an advanced grid-pattern waterproofing panel system across the entire roof.

While these panels carry a higher upfront cost than standard coatings, their long-term durability completely eliminates the peeling failures that plague older buildings. It highlights an essential rule of property rehabilitation: structural health and weatherproofing must always take financial priority over decorative interior finishes.

The Math Behind 7 Units and a 600 Million Won Budget

So, what was the bottom line? The completed building now houses five rental units and two owner-occupied levels, totaling seven distinct households. The entire overhaul—including structural reinforcement, gut-renovation, exterior re-cladding, and roof paneling—came out to a total estimated budget in the early 600 million won range (roughly $450,000 USD).

While that is a substantial sum, it represents a remarkably pragmatic investment when compared to the millions required to completely demolish and rebuild a multi-family property from scratch in the greater Seoul area. By dramatically lifting rental yields and asset valuations simultaneously, the project turned a deteriorating eyesore into a profitable urban housing model.
