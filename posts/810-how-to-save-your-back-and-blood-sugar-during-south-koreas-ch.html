How to Save Your Back and Blood Sugar During South Korea's Chuseok Holidays

September 18, 2026

For millions of people living in South Korea, the Chuseok autumn harvest festival means packing into cars for the annual migration to their hometowns. It also means hours of gridlock on the expressways, followed by days of heavy traditional food, family reunions, and ultimately, a familiar kind of physical misery. While the rest of the world looks at major cultural holidays as a time for rest, Korean media and health experts spend the days leading up to the break warning citizens about the impending physical toll: locked hips, screaming lower backs, and sudden weight gain.

Recently, a special broadcast hosted by prominent media figure Kim Ou-joon tackled this exact national ritual. Joined by guests including Moon Gyo-hoon, Baek So-dam, and commentator Park Moon-sung, the studio transformed into an impromptu physical therapy session. Far from a dry medical segment, the program highlighted a very real cultural anxiety: why do we willingly subject our bodies to hours of immobility and overeating every single year, and how can a few minutes of roadside stretching save us from the post-holiday hospital visit?

Surviving the Expressway: Hip and Spine Routines for Long Drives

The first major casualty of a multi-hour drive is the hip joint, the critical nexus connecting the pelvis to the leg bones. When drivers grip steering wheels for hours, locking their feet between the accelerator and brake pedals, the pelvis shifts out of alignment and the spine absorbs relentless compression.

During the broadcast, guest Baek So-dam demonstrated targeted internal and external rotation movements, mimicking the sweeping action of a windshield wiper. While the studio audience and guests joked around—with Kim Ou-joon poking fun at panelist Park Moon-sung's stiff posture—the underlying message carried severe weight. Ignoring your body's early warning signals in favor of rushing to the next highway rest stop leaves the spine on the verge of collapse.

The prescription is deceptively simple: instead of just using rest stops to grab snacks or use the restroom, drivers need to spend a few minutes moving their hips right by the car door. Simple pelvic tilts and windshield-wiper leg rotations can relieve the built-up pressure that leads to chronic lower back pain. As the cast members groaned in relief while trying out the movements, it became clear that a ten-minute investment at a highway rest stop cuts travel fatigue in half.

The Anatomy of Posture: Twists and Side Stretches

Once the hips are somewhat loosened, addressing the lower back requires more deliberate mechanics. The studio session moved into deeper anatomical corrections, led by a female guest who introduced a routine combining internal hip rotation with small, hula-hoop-like pelvic circles.

By pointing the toes inward and tracing circles, participants were forced to diagnose where their bodies had grown stiff over months of sedentary habits. The routine escalated with a more challenging posture: stepping the left leg behind the right, raising both arms in a "surrender" position, and driving the pelvis to the side to pull the oblique muscles.

Watching media personalities and guests stumble, lose their balance, and let out audible sighs of discomfort served as a mirror for the audience at home. Modern office workers and drivers spend so much of their lives rigid that stretching the side body feels like an extreme sport. Yet, this minor discomfort is the only antidote to the systemic stiffness generated by modern Korean work and transit culture.

Battling Holiday Bloat with the 'Second Heart'

Physical pain is only half the battle during Chuseok. Once drivers finally reach their destinations, they face an endless spread of holiday delicacies: songpyeon (half-moon rice cakes), jeon (savory Korean pancakes), and various fried dishes. While eating brings immediate comfort, the real terror sets in when unmanaged blood sugar converts directly into stubborn body fat.

To combat this, the broadcast introduced a simple metabolic hack focusing on the calves, often referred to in Korean health circles as the "second heart." By performing rhythmic heel raises—standing on tiptoes and lowering back down while pumping the arms—exercisers can stimulate full-body blood circulation before the calories from holiday meals have a chance to settle into fat.

The panel demonstrated the calf-raise routine to music, turning the TV studio into a makeshift aerobic gym. The segment served as a direct critique of the traditional post-meal routine of lying on the living room floor watching television. Activating the calf muscles keeps the metabolism running, offering a simple way to protect blood sugar levels without needing an expensive gym membership.

Core Control and the Road Ahead

The session concluded with a final high-intensity movement aimed directly at abdominal fat: a combination of wide arm movements and high knee raises designed to violently contract the rectus abdominis. Amidst laughter and clumsy attempts by the cast to keep the beat, the underlying lesson was clear. Survival during South Korea's high-stress holiday seasons requires active maintenance, not passive endurance.

As the broadcast wrapped up, the final takeaway was not about complicated fitness equipment or strict diets. It was about overcoming the social awkwardness of stretching in public spaces and refusing to let holiday hospitality destroy your physical health.

    What to watch for next:
  • Highway rest stop infrastructure and whether travel safety campaigns begin incorporating physical wellness tips.
  • Public health announcements in South Korea regarding post-holiday physical therapy demand and metabolic tracking after major holiday periods.
  • The growing popularity of micro-workouts and desk/travel stretching routines across Korean digital media platforms.

This Chuseok, the choice is simple: trade groans of pain for light breathing, and take a few minutes at the rest stop to keep your body moving.
