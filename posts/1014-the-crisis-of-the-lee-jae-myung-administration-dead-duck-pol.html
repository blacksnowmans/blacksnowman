The Crisis of the Lee Jae-myung Administration: Dead-Duck Politics and the Shadow of the Past

October 12, 2026

Barely a year and three months have passed since President Lee Jae-myung took office, yet the political landscape in Seoul feels exhausted, anxious, and deeply unsettled. In South Korean politics, administrations enjoy a honeymoon period, but the current reality looks entirely different. Staff positions remain empty, ministerial nominees repeatedly decline posts, and political insiders in the Yeouido district—South Korea’s equivalent of Capitol Hill—are already whispering about a "dead duck" presidency, bypassing the usual stage of lame-duck decline.

Faced with a rapidly evaporating grip on state affairs, the administration has resorted to peculiar tactics. Rather than offering a coherent vision for economic recovery or administrative competence, it has begun dragging past progressive icons into current debates and stoking anxieties with unverified rumors of an imminent overseas troop deployment. For outside observers watching Seoul, this is a glaring symptom of a government struggling to govern under its own weight.

Invoking Roh Moo-hyun as a Shield

President Lee has made a habit of taking to X (formerly Twitter) on weekends to engage in direct, combative exchanges with critics. To justify this unorthodox style, the administration has repeatedly invoked the memory of Roh Moo-hyun, the progressive president who served from 2003 to 2008 and is revered by many on the South Korean left. Supporters of the current president argue that just as Roh faced vicious attacks from the conservative establishment for his plainspoken, anti-authoritarian demeanor, President Lee’s digital sparring matches are simply a modern extension of that same grassroots communication.

Yet this comparison strikes many critics as a hollow stretch. Roh Moo-hyun never spent his weekends trading barbs in real-time social media comment sections, nor did he lash out defensively when his public approval dipped. The invocation of Roh looks less like ideological continuity and more like an emotional reflex—an attempt to insulate the current leader from criticism by suggesting that an attack on Lee is inherently an attack on the legacy of Roh himself. When public criticism mounts over falling poll numbers or a sharp drop in the president's traditional market walkabouts, dragging a beloved former leader into the fray exposes a profound political insecurity.

The Rumor of Imminent Troop Deployment

More alarming than social media spats are the swirling rumors within political circles regarding an imminent overseas military deployment. Whispers have spread through Yeouido that the administration is preparing a major troop dispatch or comparable military action. This immediately evokes bitter historical parallels to the Roh Moo-hyun administration’s controversial decision to send thousands of troops to Iraq, a move that cost the government dearly—including roughly 723.8 billion won (about $520 million)—and deeply divided the progressive camp.

The logic being floated within ruling-circle rumor mills is transparent: because Roh Moo-hyun once authorized a controversial deployment in the name of the South Korea-U.S. alliance, the current administration should be granted the same leeway. By wrapping a deeply sensitive national security decision in the rhetoric of alliance duties and historical precedent, the government appears to be pre-emptively insulating itself against anticipated backlash. Under South Korea’s constitution, sending troops abroad requires strict parliamentary consent. If the administration attempts to bypass a genuine public debate by leaning on historical nostalgia, it risks fracturing its own base and plunging the country into an avoidable constitutional crisis.

Re-enacting Past Controversies Through Park Won-soon

The administration’s reflex to shield itself through association does not stop with Roh Moo-hyun. Officials have also invoked the late Park Won-soon, the long-serving mayor of Seoul who died in 2020, to justify the president’s aggressive social media moderation and blocking of critical accounts. By recalling Park’s frustrations with online abuse, the administration frames its refusal to engage with dissenting voices not as political intolerance, but as a noble defense of a healthy public square.

This framing collapses under scrutiny. While the president’s digital defenders ruthlessly block critical citizens as malicious trolls, they routinely turn a blind eye—or offer implicit encouragement—to vitriolic attacks directed at political opponents. When sympathetic YouTubers and hardline factions circulate crude memes or mocking imagery targeting political rivals, the administration's commitment to civil discourse vanishes. This double standard reveals a governing style that equates criticism with illegitimacy.

Compounding this defensiveness is the language emanating from the top office. Observers have noted an alarming shift toward internet subculture slang—tones historically associated with radical online communities known for misogyny and historical revisionism. When legitimate political critiques are dismissed with juvenile sarcasm, the presidency lowers its own institutional dignity. A leader struggling with plunging approval ratings and administrative vacancies cannot afford to communicate like an anonymous message-board troll.

What to Watch Next

As this administration stumbles toward the midpoint of its term, the gap between political rhetoric and the daily struggles of ordinary citizens continues to widen. Several concrete developments will signal where South Korea's political trajectory is heading next:

  • Parliamentary Moves on Security: Any official motion or budget submission regarding overseas troop deployments will immediately test the ruling Democratic Party’s discipline and its relationship with the conservative opposition in the National Assembly.
  • Cabinet and Staff Appointments: Watch whether the president can successfully fill critical vacancies, such as the Chief of Staff position, or if the administration remains trapped in a hiring stalemate that deepens perceptions of administrative collapse.
  • Economic Indicators and Public Sentiment: As persistent inflation and high interest rates weigh heavily on household budgets, watch how voters react to deteriorating livelihood conditions independent of political spin.
  • Shifts Within the Ruling Bloc: Pay attention to whether rank-and-file lawmakers in the ruling party continue to maintain a silent consensus or if growing panic over future elections forces them to publicly distance themselves from the presidential office.

History suggests that when a government loses its internal momentum, invoking the ghosts of past leaders cannot stave off political gravity. For President Lee, the time for defensive framing and historical borrowed authority is running out; the public is increasingly looking past the rhetoric to examine the naked reality of governance beneath.
