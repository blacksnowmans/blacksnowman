Surviving the Camino de Santiago: A Realist's Guide to Preparation and Pain Management

September 07, 2026

For thousands of travelers each year, the Camino de Santiago—the ancient network of pilgrim routes leading to the shrine of Saint James in Santiago de Compostela, Spain—occupies a sacred space on the bucket list. South Koreans, in particular, have taken to the trail in droves, ranking among the top non-European nationalities tackling the trek. Yet the romanticized image of a soul-searching stroll across the Spanish countryside often shatters by the end of the first week, when reality sets in for blistered feet and screaming knees. Local authorities in Spain frequently issue warnings about ill-prepared tourists needing rescue, particularly along treacherous stretches like the Pyrenees crossing on the French Way (Camino Francés). Surviving the Camino requires shedding grand romantic illusions in favor of cold, tactical preparation. Here is how to make it to the cathedral without breaking your body.

The Feet Are Your Engine: Managing Shoes, Socks, and Blisters

On the Camino, your feet are your only mode of transportation, and treating them right is the single most important factor between finishing the journey and calling a taxi from a ditch. The most common casualties are blisters and plantar fasciitis, and prevention starts long before you take your first step.

When buying footwear, size up drastically. Even if you normally wear a women's or men's size 230mm (roughly a US 5 or 6), you should be looking at a 245mm (roughly a US 7.5 or 8) for the trail. Feet swell dramatically over hours of continuous walking, and a snug fit quickly transforms shoes into medieval torture devices. Choose stiff soles; the rocky terrain of the Spanish tracks will punish your arches if your soles are too thin and flexible. Waterproofing is non-negotiable given Europe's fickle weather, but make sure your boots are thoroughly broken in before departure.

Socks, counterintuitively, do not benefit from layering. Wearing three pairs of toe socks and heavy wool hiking socks at once will only generate friction and cook your feet. Two pairs maximum—and a good application of Vaseline over your entire foot before putting them on—will dramatically reduce chafing. Throughout the day, the golden rule is to stop every one to two hours, take off your shoes and socks, and let your feet dry out completely. Walking with damp feet is a direct express train to blister city.

A Note on Gear Fetishes: Drop the obsession with knee braces and trekking poles. Wearing a knee brace all day cuts off circulation, causing painful heat rashes rather than relieving joint stress; save it strictly for steep descents. Trekking poles are helpful at first, but many pilgrims find them a nuisance after ten days and discard them. Do not pack out of panic.

Pack Light, Live Light: The Philosophy of the Backpack

Your pack will quickly become an extension of your body, and its weight is a direct reflection of your psychological baggage. A 29-liter backpack equipped with an internal steel frame is often the sweet spot. Packs without frames collapse against your spine, cutting off ventilation and pulling your center of gravity backward, which ruins your posture over long distances. A framed pack forces you to stand upright, protecting your neck and back from strain.

When it comes to clothing, functionality reigns supreme, and you only need two sets of functional wear. Assuming you will wash your clothes daily—which you must—keeping your wardrobe down to the absolute minimum is essential. Do not indulge in the luxury of separate pajamas; sleep in the clothes you plan to wear the next morning to shave precious minutes off your dawn routine. As for rain gear, cheap plastic ponchos will fail you in heavy downpours, especially if your backpack makes them bunch up in the back. On torrential days, outsourcing your pack to a luggage delivery service that shuttles bags to the next albergue (pilgrim hostel) is well worth the peace of mind.

Electronics and small items require similar minimalism. Wall outlets in albergues are shared by dozens of tired pilgrims, making your charger vulnerable to being accidentally unplugged. Carrying a modest power bank—charging your phone via the power bank rather than directly into the wall—is much safer. Headlamps are indispensable for pre-dawn departures, and cheap sunglasses (like a $4 pair from Decathlon) are entirely sufficient, as expensive shades invariably get scratched or lost in the bottom of your pack.

Decoding Spain on the Fly: Language, Food, and Local Etiquette

Language barriers on the Camino cause far less friction than newcomers fear. You do not need fluent Spanish; a cheerful "Hola" and "Buen Camino" will get you through the first half of your interactions, and knowing numbers one through three is plenty for ordering food or checking into a bed.

When stopping at a local café for a morning pick-me-up, a simple "Hola, café con leche uno, please" will earn you a warm smile from the barista. For about $1.50 to $2, that café au lait provides a moment of grace that washes away the early morning chill. However, you must respect the rhythm of Spanish life: siesta culture is real. Between 2:00 PM and 5:00 PM, nearly all shops shutter their doors, meaning lunch must be planned in advance. Keep in mind that large supermarkets often close on Sundays, requiring careful pantry management so you do not get caught hungry.

When navigating logistics, forget Google Maps. The most reliable bus schedules, stop locations, and taxi numbers come from one definitive source: your albergue host. If you need a taxi or local advice, walk into the nearest bar or hostel, open a translation app, and ask the staff. They are almost universally glad to help.

Mental Resilience and Daily Maintenance

Bedbugs loom large in the folklore of the Camino, casting a shadow of anxiety over many first-time travelers. In reality, modern albergues have largely mitigated this threat by wrapping mattresses in protective, wipeable materials, and seasoned pilgrims rarely encounter them. Simple hygiene habits—like keeping your pack off the floor and away from the bed—will ward off most issues without letting paranoia ruin your trip.

Physical maintenance should be viewed as an evening ritual rather than a chore. Taking multivitamins every few days helps fend off cumulative fatigue, and keeping a basic supply of ibuprofen and cold medicine on hand covers most minor ailments. Every evening after washing up, take time to massage your legs and apply muscle relief gels. This daily maintenance is not merely about hygiene; it is a ritual of self-compassion that acknowledges the physical toll of the road.

Finally, do not skip recording your journey. Between the exhaustion of walking and the weight of your pack, it is tempting to collapse into sleep immediately upon arrival. Yet keeping a small notebook or digital log of the day's pain, the landscape, and the strangers who became friends is what cements the experience. When the trail is over, those scribbled notes will remain the only permanent souvenir of a transformative walk.
