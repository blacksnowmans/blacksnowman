The Dual Scales of Justice: South Korea’s Judiciary Under Fire for Privilege and Paralysis

October 07, 2026

Picture a courtroom where a bus driver loses his job over the equivalent of 60 cents, or where logistics workers face criminal fines for eating a few dollars' worth of snacks. Now, shift your focus to the high-vaulted, polished chambers of the Supreme Court of Korea. As recent National Assembly audits have revealed, while the judiciary has been mercilessly pedantic toward the financial missteps of the working class, it has been running a massive, opaque, and entirely unauthorized cash slush fund for its own elite.

The annual government audit, a rigorous process where the National Assembly oversees the executive and judicial branches, recently became a theater of raw frustration. For those watching outside of Korea, this drama is more than just a squabble over accounting; it is a fundamental test of the rule of law in a country where the judiciary—the final arbiter of justice—is currently embroiled in a scandal of its own making.

The Two Faces of South Korean Law

The audit session, led by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee, was punctuated by searing criticism from lawmakers like Suh Young-kyo, a senior member of the Democratic Party of Korea (the current ruling party). She drew attention to a hauntingly familiar case: the "800-won (roughly 60 cents) bus driver incident." In this case, a driver was dismissed from his job after a CCTV camera caught him taking the equivalent of a few cents to buy coffee. The court ruled in favor of the employer, effectively stripping a man of his livelihood over pennies.

This callousness stands in stark contrast to the judiciary’s internal culture. During the audit, it came to light that for years, the National Court Administration—the body that manages the judiciary’s administrative affairs—has been distributing "encouragement money" to Supreme Court Justices and high-ranking officials. We are talking about monthly payments of 2 million won (about $1,500) for Justices and 3 million won (about $2,250) for the Minister of Court Administration. This money was distributed regularly, despite the fact that no budget item for such "encouragement" exists.

When questioned, top judicial officials engaged in a clumsy game of finger-pointing, with the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, Cho Hee-dae, claiming ignorance and his administrators claiming it was all authorized. To the public, the message was clear: the judiciary applies a microscope to the survival-level mistakes of the poor while treating taxpayer funds like an internal piggy bank.

A Constitutional Crisis: The Great Vacancy

Beyond the ethical rot of slush funds, the Supreme Court is currently facing an unprecedented administrative paralysis. For over six months, several seats on the Supreme Court bench have remained vacant. Under South Korean law, the Chief Justice is responsible for recommending candidates to the President, who then appoints them. This process has devolved into a bitter tug-of-war.

The current standoff involves the Chief Justice resisting the President’s objections to certain candidates, citing judicial independence. However, critics—including legal scholars and reform advocates—argue that this "independence" is being used as a shield to shirk constitutional duty. By allowing these vacancies to persist, the Supreme Court has effectively crippled its own ability to function, as the "Full Bench" (the body that hears the most critical constitutional cases) cannot operate at full capacity. The losers in this power game are the ordinary citizens whose cases are delayed indefinitely while the country’s top jurists engage in a political standoff.

The irony is not lost on observers. While the judiciary defends its right to be free from government interference, it has simultaneously failed to perform its basic administrative function. As Kim Kyung-ho, a witness and lawyer who testified at the audit, noted, the systematic failure to fill these seats without a valid reason constitutes a clear dereliction of duty under the Constitution.

The Culture of Waste: Wine and Warehouse Hoarding

The audit also pulled back the curtain on the judiciary’s broader spending habits. The National Court Administration was found to have spent over 100 million won (about $75,000) on wine using corporate cards, often omitting the required details on who was present or why the purchase was made. In one instance, nearly 7 million won (about $5,250) was spent on alcohol late at night without proper documentation.

Furthermore, the judiciary appears to have engaged in a "budget-burning" exercise. To ensure their budget wasn't cut for the following year, they purchased thousands of PCs, printers, and scanners that were never put to use, leaving them to collect dust in storage warehouses. For a branch of government that frequently lectures the public on the necessity of integrity and austerity, the reality of their internal ledger—filled with unaccounted-for cash payments and extravagant, unnecessary equipment—is a profound betrayal of public trust.

What to Watch Next

The revelations from this audit have put the judiciary on the defensive, but the real question is whether this will lead to meaningful change. Here is what to monitor in the coming months:

  • The Constitutional Court’s Intervention: The opposition People Power Party has taken the matter of the Supreme Court vacancies to the Constitutional Court, seeking a ruling on whether the deadlock violates the Constitution. Any decision here will set a precedent for how much authority a Chief Justice can exert in refusing presidential appointments.
  • Internal Reform or Entrenchment: Will the Supreme Court leadership offer a sincere apology and move to abolish the "encouragement money" system, or will they attempt to ride out the controversy by blaming the audit as an infringement on judicial independence?
  • Public Pressure and Legislative Action: The Democratic Party is likely to push for stricter oversight laws, specifically regarding the use of "special activity funds" and other opaque budgetary categories. Keep an eye on new legislation aimed at forcing transparency in judicial spending.

The judiciary in South Korea is currently at a crossroads. It can either continue to hide behind the abstract concept of "judicial independence" to maintain its status as an untouchable, self-serving cartel, or it can submit to the same level of democratic control and transparency that it demands of the rest of society. The "800-won incident" may have been a minor case years ago, but it has become a powerful symbol: in the eyes of the public, the scales of justice are finally being weighted—and the judiciary is finding itself on the lighter side.
