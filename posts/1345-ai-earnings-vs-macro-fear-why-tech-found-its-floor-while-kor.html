AI Earnings vs. Macro Fear: Why Tech Found Its Floor While Korea Struggles to Keep Up

October 20, 2026

Imagine standing on a trading floor when the screens are a sea of red. Just days ago, the atmosphere in the financial markets was one of pure, unadulterated panic. Retail investors were frozen, watching their portfolios bleed, while the headlines screamed about "higher for longer" interest rates and a looming global recession. In that moment, a sentiment survey of market participants hit a staggering 73% negativity rate. When seven out of ten people are convinced the sky is falling, the instinct to flee becomes a physical weight. Yet, as history often reminds us, it is precisely when the collective terror reaches its zenith that the market quietly finds its footing.

The recent rebound in the Nasdaq wasn't driven by a sudden change in the Federal Reserve's stance or a magical drop in inflation. It was driven by a cold, hard realization: the AI revolution is actually making a lot of money. While macro-economic fears were dominating the narrative, the underlying cash-generating power of Big Tech remained not just intact, but formidable. For investors who can look past the noise of bond yields and central bank rhetoric, the current landscape offers a masterclass in how "Factfulness"—the practice of basing opinions on data rather than emotion—separates the survivors from the casualties in the world of high-stakes finance.

The Psychology of the Bottom: Why 73% Negativity Matters

To understand the current market, one must first understand human frailty. Behavioral economics, specifically Prospect Theory, teaches us that the pain of losing money is felt twice as intensely as the joy of gaining it. This "loss aversion" creates a cycle where investors dump assets at the exact moment they should be buying. When negativity hit that 73% threshold recently, it signaled that the "weak hands" had been shaken out. The Nasdaq’s subsequent surge of over 1.1%—a gain of nearly 300 points—was a violent correction of that emotional overshooting.

Local Korean market analysts often emphasize that the secret to long-term wealth isn't hitting "home runs" during a bull market; it’s "losing less" during the inevitable crashes. If a portfolio drops by 50%, it requires a 100% gain just to get back to zero. However, a defensive strategy that limits a drop to 20% only needs a 25% recovery to reach new highs. This arithmetic of survival is what separates professional institutional players from the retail crowd that panics at the sight of a "blue screen" (the color of falling stocks in the Korean trading interface).

In this environment, the most dangerous thing an investor can do is "colorize" the market with their own anxiety. When you project your fears onto the data, you stop seeing the companies and start seeing a monster. The recent bounce-back was a reminder that while the macro-economic waves are rough, the ships with the strongest engines—the AI-driven tech giants—are still making headway. The recovery was a textbook example of mean reversion: prices had fallen so far below the actual value of the companies' earnings that "smart money" simply couldn't ignore the discount anymore.

European Cracks and the Sticky Inflation Trap

While the U.S. tech sector found its floor, the global macro picture remains deeply fractured, with the newest tremors coming from Europe. France, the symbolic heart of European social democracy, is currently grappling with a fiscal nightmare. To combat a ballooning deficit, the government has moved to slash pensions and tighten budgets, sparking riots in major cities from Paris to Marseille. The social unrest has even forced night-time curfews in some districts, a level of instability that has sent shockwaves through the bond markets.

The financial fallout was immediate. The yield on 10-year French government bonds spiked by 14 basis points (0.14 percentage points), nearing the 5.0% mark. Even more telling is the "spread"—the difference in interest rates—between French bonds and German bonds (the gold standard for European stability), which has widened to 1.5%. In the eyes of the credit markets, France and Germany are no longer in the same league of reliability. This European fiscal "seizure" acts as a major headwind for global risk appetite, keeping investors on edge even as individual companies perform well.

Adding to this tension is the "sticky" nature of energy inflation. As winter approaches, the demand for heating oil and diesel is surging, but supply remains constrained. Refined oil prices are stubbornly high, with Brent crude hovering around $109 per barrel and WTI near $98. This isn't just a number on a screen; it’s a cost that permeates every layer of the economy, from shipping to manufacturing. It creates a "cost-push" inflation that central banks find difficult to fight with interest rates alone. For the U.S. administration, this has led to quiet, unofficial diplomatic maneuvers to potentially ease sanctions on Russian energy—a desperate attempt to bring down domestic gasoline prices before they further erode the purchasing power of the American consumer.

The AI Revaluation: Earnings Power vs. High Rates

Despite these macro headwinds, the Nasdaq's resilience comes down to a simple shift in valuation: AI tech stocks became "cheap" again. During the recent sell-off, while stock prices were falling, the earnings estimates for companies leading the AI revolution were actually being revised upward. This created a compression in the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio. For a leader like NVIDIA, the "Salt P/E" (a measure of valuation relative to growth) dropped to historically attractive levels.

The market is currently witnessing a fierce tug-of-war between two realities. On one side, you have the explosive profit margins of data center operators. Amazon Web Services (AWS), for instance, recently reported an operating margin of 39%—a staggering figure that proves the cloud and AI infrastructure business is a literal cash cow. On the other side, you have the reality of borrowing costs. For new entrants, the cost of capital is now 10% to 15%, a "hurdle rate" that makes it nearly impossible to compete with the established giants who built their infrastructure when money was free.

A Snapshot of Tech Resilience:

  • Amazon: Surged 4% on the back of AWS dominance.
  • Tesla: Rebounded 3% as its AI and autonomous driving ecosystem continues to expand.
  • Micron Technology: Gained nearly 3% due to the persistent shortage of high-end memory chips.
  • NVIDIA: Maintained its lead as the primary hardware provider for the AI era, seeing its valuation reset to more sustainable levels.

The "Dead Cross"—the point where high interest costs finally overwhelm AI profits—may come eventually, but the data suggests we are nowhere near it yet. As long as the cash flow from these tech giants continues to outpace the cost of their debt, they will remain the "safe havens" of the modern era, attracting capital every time the broader market dips.

The Korea Discount: Politics, Governance, and the China Threat

For those looking at the South Korean market, the story is far more somber. While the U.S. and even parts of Europe have seen rallies, the KOSPI and KOSDAQ have suffered from a painful "decoupling," consistently ranking as some of the worst-performing indices globally this year. This "Korea Discount" is rooted in a mix of external competition and internal structural failures.

On the competitive front, China’s CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies) recently shocked the industry by swinging to a massive profit. In the second quarter of 2026, CXMT reported an operating profit of 81.7 billion yuan (about $11.3 billion), a thirteen-fold increase from the previous year. While Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (Korea’s leading semiconductor manufacturers) have shifted their focus to high-margin HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) for AI, CXMT has aggressively filled the void in the "legacy" DRAM market used in basic PCs and electronics. If the margins on this legacy tech continue to slide while HBM competition heats up, the Korean semiconductor giants could face a pincer movement that threatens their long-term dominance.

Domestically, the political and corporate environment under the administration of President Lee Jae-myung (of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea) has struggled to restore investor confidence. The legacy of the previous era—marked by the impeachment and imprisonment of Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol—left a fractured regulatory landscape. Today, the Korean market is plagued by "split-off listings" and corporate governance issues that often prioritize founding families over minority shareholders. When a company like SK Hynix contemplates listing its subsidiaries separately (such as the Solidigm issue), it dilutes the value for existing shareholders, driving global capital away from Seoul and toward New York or Tokyo.

"Investment is a long-distance race, much like the 1,200km Paris-Brest-Paris cycling event. There are moments on a steep hill where you wonder why you ever started. But the secret is to keep chewing on your bread, minimize the pain, and trust that growth is a law of nature."

What to Watch Next

As we move into the final quarter of the year, the "Factfulness" of the market will be tested by three key indicators. First, watch the HBM profit margins of SK Hynix and Micron; if they can successfully offset the decline in legacy chip prices, the semiconductor sector will see a massive "buyback" rally. Second, keep a close eye on European bond spreads. If the gap between French and German yields continues to widen, it could trigger a broader "risk-off" sentiment that even AI earnings can't ignore.

Finally, for the Korean market, the focus remains on Corporate Value-up initiatives. Unless the Lee Jae-myung government and the Democratic Party can push through meaningful reforms that protect minority shareholders and end the practice of predatory split-listings, the KOSPI will likely remain a "trap" for global investors, regardless of how well the underlying tech companies perform. In a world of high interest rates, the market only has room for the efficient, the transparent, and the profitable. Everything else is just noise.
