A Party at War with Its Own History: Inside the Leaked Recording Crippling South Korea’s Ruling Party

November 15, 2026

On November 14, South Korea’s political establishment was rocked by a single audio file circulating through newsrooms in Seoul. In the recording of a closed-door meeting with party members, Choi Min-hee, a member of the Supreme Council of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), uttered words that struck at the core of the ruling party's political identity: she claimed that sitting President Lee Jae-myung had actually opposed the impeachment of Yoon Suk-yeol during the height of the crisis that eventually brought down the former leader.

Within hours, what might have been brushed off as a rogue politician’s slip of the tongue erupted into a full-scale political civil war. The fallout exposed deep factional fault lines within the ruling party, pitting loyalists of President Lee against internal reformists, crippling party leadership meetings, and handing the conservative opposition a powerful political weapon. For an administration navigating its crucial first year in power, the spectacle revealed a party consumed by internal power struggles at the very moment it needs unity to govern.

The Spark: A Leaked Tape and the Rewriting of Impeachment History

To understand why Choi Min-hee’s leaked remarks sparked such immediate outrage, one has to understand the political sanctity of the impeachment movement in South Korea. The downfall and ultimate imprisonment of Yoon Suk-yeol—who was removed from office following widespread public protests—serves as the foundational mandate for President Lee Jae-myung and the ruling Democratic Party of Korea. For a high-ranking party official to suggest that Lee himself hesitated or opposed that movement undermines the very narrative of moral clarity upon which the current administration built its legitimacy.

According to transcripts published by Korean media, Choi told party members: "President Lee Jae-myung also opposed Yoon Suk-yeol’s impeachment; have you all forgotten?" She went further, alleging that Lee had attempted to coordinate with then-Prime Minister Han Duck-soo to maneuver toward Yoon Suk-yeol’s resignation rather than pressing for full impeachment.

The backlash was instant, forcing Choi into damage control. Within twenty-four hours, she issued a public retraction, claiming she had simply misspoken. Choi insisted that President Lee had consistently supported impeaching Yoon Suk-yeol and that her remarks were intended to describe past internal debates regarding whether to delay the impeachment of Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. She argued that during the frantic negotiations of the impeachment crisis, party leaders had legitimately debated political tactics, and her memory had conflated those discussions.

However, her explanation failed to calm the storm, largely because her leaked account dragged other heavyweights into the fray. Choi had cited veteran DPK lawmaker Park Ji-won as the figure who advised partnering with Prime Minister Han. Park immediately fired back, expressing fury at being pulled into the controversy. Park flatly denied ever advocating for such a compromise, questioning why his name was being weaponized in a private meeting and accusing Choi of fabricating internal party history.

Meanwhile, Han Dong-hoon, leader of the People Power Party (PPP)—South Korea’s main conservative opposition party—seized on the chaos. Han publicly mocked Choi’s retraction, stating that attempting to deny explicit audio recordings was simply "ugly." As the controversy dominated headlines, the DPK found itself stuck defending its historical record against its own leaders.

The Loyalists Counterattack: "Arrows Shot from Behind"

As the afternoon of November 14 progressed, the crisis shifted from a dispute over historical facts to a display of political force. Pro-Lee loyalists within the party mobilized aggressively against Choi Min-hee, signaling that misrepresenting the president’s legacy would carry severe consequences.

The vanguard of this pushback was the Democratic Innovation Conference of Gyeonggi, a powerful outside organization closely aligned with President Lee Jae-myung. The group issued a blistering public statement asserting that Choi’s remarks had "crossed a line that should never be crossed" and demanding her immediate expulsion from the party. By the next day, angry party members and loyalist activist groups gathered outside Choi’s constituency office, holding banners demanding her resignation and stripping of party membership for defaming the president.

"Arrows shot from behind hurt the most."

The defining moment came late that evening when media outlets reported a poignant reaction attributed to President Lee Jae-myung himself. Speaking privately about the situation, Lee reportedly remarked that "arrows shot from behind hurt the most." In South Korean political parlance, such a statement from a sitting president functions as an unequivocal message of betrayal. It signaled to the party rank-and-file that the executive office viewed Choi’s comments not as an innocent mistake, but as an act of political sabotage.

With presidential displeasure made clear, the pro-Lee faction tightened the screws. For these loyalists, protecting the legitimacy of Lee's presidency required demonstrating absolute zero tolerance for internal leaks or historical revisionism. What began as a debate over an audio tape transformed into a coordinated movement to purge Choi from the leadership ranks.

Ethics Audits, Meeting Boycotts, and Tabloid Accusations

Rather than quelling the feud, the decision by DPK Chairman Kim Min-seok to launch an official ethics audit into Choi Min-hee triggered an outright mutiny within the party's supreme decision-making body.

On the morning of the audit announcement, three members of the Supreme Council—Choi Min-hee, Han Min-soo, and Lee Sung-yoon—announced a complete boycott of leadership meetings. Representing the internal reformist faction (often associated with rival internal power centers), the trio skipped official business and instead held a dramatic joint press conference at the National Assembly.

Their message was an open declaration of war against Chairman Kim Min-seok:

  • Targeting the Leadership: The boycotting members declared that the ethics committee should be auditing Chairman Kim Min-seok, not Choi Min-hee, accusing the leadership of conducting targeted witch hunts to silence internal critics.
  • Claims of Political Manipulation: They labeled the audit a "fabricated disciplinary ambush" designed to consolidate control over the party apparatus.
  • Escalating Religious Allegations: The fight turned remarkably ugly as both sides traded bizarre accusations involving fringe religious groups. Fragments of Choi’s leaked audio contained claims that the Unification Church had backed Kim Min-seok's leadership bid. In response, critics brought up past unproven rumors surrounding Kim's alleged ties to Shincheonji—claims Kim's office had previously clarified and dismissed for lack of evidence.

By bringing raw factional warfare into the national media spotlight, the ruling party’s leadership council effectively paralyzed itself. Political analysts observed that the party supreme council had descended into mudslinging, trading unsubstantiated rumors about religious influence rather than focusing on legislative priorities.

Opposition Special Counsel Demands and the Road Ahead

The primary beneficiary of the ruling party’s self-inflicted crisis has been the opposition People Power Party (PPP). Seizing on the admissions leaked from within the DPK, conservative leadership launched a fierce counter-offensive, demanding an independent special counsel investigation.

PPP leaders argued that if high-ranking DPK officials were openly accusing each other of cult affiliations and manipulating internal party discipline, a neutral special prosecutor was necessary to uncover the truth. While the PPP has faced its own share of internal divisions and public pressure, the DPK’s civil war provided conservatives with a golden opportunity to shift the public narrative away from their own vulnerabilities and onto the ruling party’s administrative dysfunction.

The tangible cost of this political infighting is already reflected in public opinion. Internal party leaks indicate growing panic among DPK strategists, as recent media reports highlighted a drop of nearly 5 percentage points in party approval ratings amid the leadership turmoil and controversial policy debates. At a time when the administration needs strong public support to push through economic legislation, energy spent on factional warfare is dragging down the government's momentum.

What to Watch Next

As the Democratic Party of Korea navigates this spiraling crisis, several key indicators will determine whether Chairman Kim Min-seok can restore order or if the ruling coalition faces further fragmentation:

  • The Ethics Committee Decision: Watch whether the party ethics panel bows to pro-Lee loyalists by expelling Choi Min-hee or opts for a milder penalty to avoid alienating the reformist faction. A severe punishment could prompt further boycotts or legal challenges from within.
  • Functionality of the Supreme Council: Observe whether boycotting members return to official leadership meetings. Continued absences will leave Chairman Kim Min-seok heading a broken leadership structure, crippling the party's ability to coordinate key votes in the National Assembly.
  • Legislative Pressure from the Opposition: Keep an eye on how aggressively the People Power Party pushes its special counsel motions regarding the leaked claims, using parliamentary maneuvers to keep the ruling party's dirty laundry in the headline cycle.

The events of mid-November have demonstrated that even with control of the presidency and a legislative majority, a ruling party remains vulnerable to its own internal history. How the Democratic Party of Korea resolves this clash between loyalty to President Lee Jae-myung and internal party dissent will shape South Korea’s domestic political landscape for months to come.
