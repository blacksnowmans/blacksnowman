The Grim Reality of Caregiving: Why 'Becoming Adult' (Cheoldeul Muryeob) Is Resonating in Korea

September 16, 2026

There are moments when a film hits closer to home than any grand political discourse ever could. It is that chilling, visceral realization when a line of dialogue on screen perfectly mirrors a moment of quiet despair you once lived through. Recently, the independent Korean film Becoming Adult (known in Korean as Cheoldeul Muryeob) has sparked a profound conversation in South Korea, moving past the tired tropes of "tear-jerker" family dramas to expose the heavy, unacknowledged burden of caregiving that many Korean families suffer in silence.

The film was recently discussed on a popular YouTube channel featuring director Jung Seung-o, actress Yang Mal-bok, film critic Yoon Sung-yoon, veteran director Lee Myung-se, and a prominent film YouTuber known as "Almost None" (Geoui-eopda). Their discussion stripped away the societal facade of the "perfect, self-sacrificing family," revealing instead the raw friction of caregiving in a country where the state often leaves the most vulnerable to fend for themselves.

The Origins of a Seven-Year Struggle

In Korean culture, the family is traditionally viewed as an impenetrable sanctuary of unconditional love. However, for director Jung Seung-o, that sanctuary became a claustrophobic reality when he began caring for his ailing father. The director candidly shared that the film’s narrative was born from his own grueling seven-year journey as a caregiver. Having lived apart from his father for two decades, suddenly being thrust into a hospital room together created a pressure cooker of resentment, exhaustion, and frustration that he felt he couldn't share with the outside world.

This is the core of the film’s power: it rejects the notion that caring for a parent is purely a "sacred sacrifice." Instead, it gives voice to the unspoken reality—the irritation, the desire to run away, and the deep, dark exhaustion that comes when your daily life is consumed by someone else’s decline. By treating these feelings as valid rather than shameful, the film challenges the violent expectation that family members must simply endure, no matter the toll on their mental health.

As the film illustrates, when a society offloads the duty of care entirely onto individuals, it creates a "hell" where the patient loses their dignity and the caregiver loses their soul. Becoming Adult tackles this not through somber preaching, but through a mix of biting black comedy and deep pathos, forcing the audience to laugh at the absurdity of their own pain.

The "Factual Relationship" and the Rise of the Filial Contract

One of the most striking concepts discussed by the cast is the idea of a "factual relationship" (a term often used in Korea for couples who live together but are not legally married, though here it refers to families who are estranged but forced to live together). Many families today function like long-divorced couples: the love has evaporated, replaced by habit, resentment, and a cold sense of duty. The children, even as adults, are trapped in the middle, performing the role of the "filial child" while navigating the emotional minefields of their parents' dysfunction.

The film introduces the unsettling device of a "Filial Contract"—a legal document detailing exactly what a child is obligated to do when a parent becomes infirm. While it sounds grotesque, it is a chillingly accurate reflection of contemporary Korean society. As the younger generation struggles to maintain their own lives, they turn to legal logic to protect their boundaries, while parents seek insurance against loneliness. It is a transactional, cold, and entirely modern way of dealing with the decay of the traditional family structure.

This approach transforms the film from a standard drama into a critique of how the "myth of the family" is used to exploit individuals. By introducing the language of contracts into the bedroom and the hospital ward, the film exposes the failure of the social safety net in South Korea.

Why This Matters for the Future

Becoming Adult, which won two awards at the 30th Busan International Film Festival (the largest film festival in South Korea), serves as a mirror for a generation exhausted by the demands of traditional Confucian duties. It highlights that the burden of care is not merely an individual problem but a systemic one. When the state fails to provide adequate support, families are left to cannibalize their own mental health to bridge the gap.

The film’s release in theaters and its accompanying discussions in venues like the Apgujeong CGV Art House—a specialized theater in Seoul known for screening independent and art-house films—suggest a growing hunger for stories that don't shy away from the ugly, messy truths of life.

What to Watch Next

  • The Shift in Discourse: Keep an eye on how Korean media covers "caregiver burnout." As the population ages rapidly, the conversation is shifting from individual "filial piety" to systemic demands for state-provided care.
  • The Independent Cinema Wave: Look for more films like Becoming Adult that utilize black comedy to dissect social taboos. These films are becoming the most effective tools for social critique in a landscape dominated by corporate blockbusters.
  • The "Adulting" Struggle: Notice the trend of "contractual relationships" in Korean media. As traditional marriage and family models falter, expect to see more stories about how young people are redefining their relationships with their parents through boundaries, legalism, and, occasionally, distance.

Ultimately, Becoming Adult leaves us with a difficult question: How long are we going to let the "name of family" remain an excuse for neglecting our collective wounds? Sometimes, the most mature thing one can do is admit that the "sanctuary" has become a prison, and that looking after oneself is not an act of selfishness, but of survival.
