The Dignity of the Digital Pulpit: President Lee Jae-myung’s Social Media Dilemma and the Ghost of Prosecution Past

October 30, 2026

Late at night, while most of Seoul is asleep, a blue light often flickers in the presidential residence. It is not the light of a midnight strategy meeting or a crisis briefing, but the glow of a smartphone. President Lee Jae-myung, known for his "direct communication" style, is on social media. For his core supporters, these posts are a breath of fresh air—a leader bypassing the "biased" traditional media to speak directly to the people. But for a growing number of critics within his own party and a majority of the South Korean public, those late-night posts have become a symbol of a presidency that struggles to transition from the combativeness of a candidate to the dignity of a head of state.

The Democratic Party of Korea (DPK), the country's current ruling party, is increasingly uneasy. What was once seen as a strategic asset—President Lee’s ability to mobilize his base via "finger play" (SNS activity)—is now being viewed as a "leadership risk." This tension is not just about etiquette; it is a battle over the soul of the party and its strategy for governance. The internal friction is exacerbated by the lingering shadows of past prosecutorial investigations, specifically the SillaJen scandal and the targeting of liberal intellectual Rhyu Si-min, as well as a controversial proposed merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party, the hardline liberal splinter group led by Cho Kuk.

The Risks of Late-Night Leadership and the "Dignity Gap"

To understand the current friction, one must look at the data. A recent poll by Public Opinion Research Gongjeong found that 62.5% of South Koreans evaluated President Lee’s frequent social media activity negatively, describing it as "lacking dignity." Only 31.9% viewed it positively. Perhaps most alarming for the Blue House (the presidential office) is that the highest negative rating came from the under-30 demographic, at 69.8%. This generation, which prized Lee's "cool" and "direct" persona during the election, now appears fatigued by a president who seems unable to log off.

Within the DPK leadership, the concern is being voiced as a fear of "Rhyu Si-min-ization." Rhyu Si-min, a former health minister and a towering figure in liberal intellectual circles, is known for his sharp, uncompromising logic. While highly respected by the base, Rhyu is also a polarizing figure who critics say burns bridges with the political center. When DPK lawmakers warn that the President’s SNS activity is turning him into "another Rhyu Si-min," they are expressing a fear that the President is narrowing his own path, speaking only to the echo chamber of his most fervent supporters while alienating the moderate voters needed to govern effectively.

The criticism isn't just about frequency but also about the language used. President Lee has occasionally used internet slang and aggressive rhetoric that critics say demeans the office. Opposition lawmaker Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party recently quipped that he wished the President would "get at least 52 hours of sleep a week," contrasting the current President’s late-night SNS habit with the late-night drinking sessions associated with Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol’s presidency. For the middle-of-the-road voter, the choice between a president who drinks all night and one who argues on social media all night feels like a false dilemma they shouldn't have to face.

The "Cabinet" and the Ghost of the SillaJen Scandal

Why is the President so insistent on this combative stance? The answer lies in the deep-seated trauma the DPK holds regarding the South Korean prosecution service. The term "cabinet investigation" is a chilling one in Korean politics. It refers to the alleged practice of prosecutors keeping files on political figures "in the cabinet," ready to be pulled out and used as leverage or "ammunition" whenever the political winds shift.

The SillaJen scandal is the primary example of this fear. SillaJen, once a star of the K-bio industry with a market cap reaching about $5.8 billion (8 trillion won), collapsed after its flagship cancer drug failed clinical trials in 2019. However, the legal fallout became highly politicized. During the administration of the now-imprisoned Mr. Yoon Suk-yeol, who was then Prosecutor General, the prosecution was accused of trying to link the company’s downfall to Rhyu Si-min and other figures close to the liberal establishment.

The case of Lee Chul, the former head of Value Invest Korea (VIK), is particularly illustrative. Lee Chul was pressured by prosecutors and even certain journalists—most notably in the "media-prosecution collusion" scandal involving a reporter from Channel A—to provide dirt on Rhyu Si-min. Prosecutors reportedly used the threat of additional charges against Lee Chul’s family to squeeze him. This "dust-shaking" investigation style, where prosecutors keep digging until they find anything to justify an arrest, is what President Lee and his supporters believe they are still fighting against. They see the SNS pulpit as their only shield against a prosecutorial "deep state" that never truly closed its cabinets.

To combat this, the DPK is pushing for the "Law on Distortion of Law," a piece of legislation that would punish prosecutors who intentionally misinterpret the law or manipulate evidence. While the judiciary remains skeptical, the DPK views this as a necessary survival tool to ensure that no more "planned investigations" like the SillaJen-Rhyu Si-min saga can occur.

The Cho Kuk Factor: A Merger of Strength or Weakness?

The internal DPK strife has found a new focal point: the potential merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party. Led by former Justice Minister Cho Kuk, the party represents the most aggressive wing of the liberal camp. On paper, a merger makes sense—it would solidify a super-majority in the National Assembly and streamline the opposition to the conservative People Power Party (PPP).

However, the move has sparked a "text bomb" campaign from DPK party members. Thousands of messages have flooded the phones of party leaders, with many arguing that merging with Cho Kuk’s party would be "political suicide." The concern is twofold. First, there is the issue of "brand purity." Cho Kuk remains a deeply divisive figure due to past scandals involving his family’s academic credentials. Second, there is the strategic worry that a merger would permanently paint the DPK as a "hardline-only" party, making it impossible to win over the 30% to 40% of voters who identify as centrists.

Even President Lee’s own base is divided. Polls show that 41% of DPK supporters oppose the merger, outstripping the 32% who support it. Critics within the party argue that instead of looking for "lifeboats" like Cho Kuk’s party, the President should focus on broadening his own appeal. "True communication isn't saying what you want to say; it's hearing what you don't want to hear," one senior lawmaker noted. The fear is that a merger would act as a "nail that pops the lifeboat," dragging the DPK down into a permanent cycle of vendetta politics rather than national governance.

Beyond the Screen: What to Watch Next

President Lee Jae-myung stands at a crossroads. He is the head of state, yet he often acts like an insurgent. The coming months will determine whether his government can move beyond the "combat mode" that characterized his rise to power. There are three key areas to watch as this drama unfolds:

  • The "Law on Distortion of Law" Debate: If the DPK successfully passes this law, it will mark the most significant curtailment of prosecutorial power in modern Korean history. However, it also risks a constitutional showdown with the judiciary.
  • The Presidential Communication Shift: Watch for whether the Blue House reduces the frequency of the President’s personal SNS posts in favor of more formal press conferences or multi-partisan meetings. A shift here would signal that the "dignity" criticism is being taken seriously.
  • The Merger Stalemate: The fate of the merger with the Rebuilding Korea Party will be a litmus test for President Lee’s control over his own party. If he pushes it through despite the "text bombs," it suggests a commitment to a hardline, base-first strategy. If he retreats, it may signal an opening for the "moderates" within the DPK.

Governance is often described as the art of the possible. For President Lee, the challenge is to prove that his vision for Korea is larger than his smartphone screen. The public’s demand for "dignity" is not just a call for better manners; it is a request for a president who can represent the entire nation, including those who do not follow him on social media. Whether he can close the "cabinet" of the past and open a new door for the future remains the defining question of his presidency.
